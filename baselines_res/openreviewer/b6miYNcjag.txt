# Review

## Summary
The paper proposes a method to score the reliability of a dataset without access to the ground truth. The proposed method leverages an auxiliary observable dataset to define a Gram matrix, which is then used to calculate the Gram determinant score. This score is shown to preserve several ground-truth-based reliability orderings and yields the same reliability ranking of datasets regardless of the experiment. Experiments on synthetic noise models, CIFAR-10 embeddings, and real employment data demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

## Strengths
1. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
2. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the proposed Gram determinant score, demonstrating its ability to preserve several ground-truth-based reliability orderings and exhibit invariance properties.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper lacks comparisons with existing methods for evaluating dataset reliability, such as those based on influence functions [1] and Shapley values [2]. The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess the advantages of the proposed method over existing approaches.
2. The proposed method requires access to an auxiliary dataset that is indirectly related to the target dataset, which may not always be available in practice. This limitation reduces the applicability of the proposed method in scenarios where such auxiliary data is not accessible.
3. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. Given that the method involves calculating the Gram matrix and its determinant, it is important to understand the scalability of the approach for large datasets.

[1] Koh, P. W., & Liang, P. (2017). Understanding black-box predictions via influence functions. In International conference on machine learning (pp. 1885-1894). PMLR.

[2] Ghorbani, B., & Zou, J. (2019). Data shapley: Equitable valuation of data for machine learning. arXiv preprint arXiv:1909.12292.

## Questions
1. How does the Gram determinant score compare to other methods for evaluating dataset reliability, such as those based on influence functions and Shapley values? Can you provide experimental comparisons to demonstrate the advantages of your proposed method?
2. How does the proposed method perform when the auxiliary dataset is not available or when the indirect relationship between the auxiliary and target datasets is weak? Have you explored scenarios where the auxiliary dataset is generated through a different distribution than the target dataset?
3. What is the computational complexity of the proposed method, especially when dealing with large datasets? How does the running time of your method compare to existing approaches?
