# Review

## Summary
This paper presents a method called Weight-Activation Subspace Iteration (WASI) for efficient training of transformer models on resource-constrained edge devices. The key idea is to perform training in a low-rank subspace to reduce memory and computational costs while maintaining accuracy. The authors propose to use SVD and subspace iteration to decompose both model weights and activation maps into low-rank representations. This allows for substantial savings in memory usage and computation (up to 62x and 2x respectively) while achieving comparable accuracy to vanilla training. The method is evaluated on vision transformer models (ViT, Swin Transformer) and a small language model (TinyLlama) across various datasets and a real-world deployment scenario on a Raspberry Pi 5.

## Strengths
1. The paper addresses an important problem of enabling efficient training of transformer models on resource-constrained edge devices which is becoming increasingly relevant for on-device learning scenarios.

2. The proposed method of decomposing both weights and activations into a low-rank subspace is novel and well-motivated by the hypothesis that a model's essential information resides in a stable subspace.

3. The experimental evaluation is comprehensive, covering multiple transformer architectures (ViT, SwinT, TinyLlama) and datasets. The results demonstrate significant memory and computational savings while maintaining accuracy. The on-device evaluation on Raspberry Pi 5 provides practical evidence of the method's efficiency.

4. The paper is well-written and easy to follow. The method is clearly explained with helpful diagrams and the experimental results are presented in a clear and organized manner.

## Weaknesses
1. While the paper shows promising results on vision transformer models, it is unclear how well the method generalizes to other types of transformer models such as decoder-only models like Llama or encoder-decoder models like T5. The authors should provide more discussion on this aspect and ideally include experiments on a wider range of transformer architectures.

2. The paper lacks a thorough comparison with other state-of-the-art methods for efficient training of transformer models. While some baselines are included, a more comprehensive comparison with methods like LoRA and its variants, as well as other low-rank decomposition techniques, would strengthen the paper.

3. The paper could benefit from more detailed analysis of the trade-offs involved in the proposed method. For example, while the method reduces memory usage, there may be an increase in training time due to the subspace iteration operations. A more comprehensive analysis of the computational trade-offs would be valuable.

4. The paper could provide more insights into the stability of the low-rank subspaces during training. While the authors show that the ranks remain relatively stable, a more detailed analysis of any potential instability or drift during long training runs would be valuable.

## Questions
1. How does the proposed method perform on other types of transformer models such as decoder-only models (e.g. Llama) or encoder-decoder models (e.g. T5)? Have the authors considered any adaptations to the method for these architectures?

2. Can the authors provide more details on the computational overhead of the subspace iteration operations? How does the training time compare to vanilla training and other baselines?

3. How does the method perform on larger-scale models? Have the authors considered any strategies to further improve efficiency for very large models?

4. How does the method compare to other state-of-the-art approaches for efficient training of transformers such as LoRA and its variants?
