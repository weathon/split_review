# Review

## Summary
This paper proposes a new method for learning the minimum action distance (MAD) between states in a Markov Decision Process (MDP) from state trajectories alone, without requiring reward signals or action information. The MAD, defined as the minimum number of actions needed to transition between two states, provides a meaningful geometric measure of state similarity that can support tasks like goal-conditioned reinforcement learning. The authors introduce a framework for self-supervised MAD learning that supports both symmetric and asymmetric distance approximations, using a computationally efficient quasimetric that outperforms more complex alternatives. They evaluate their approach on a variety of environments with known MAD values, including deterministic and stochastic dynamics, discrete and continuous state spaces, and environments with noisy observations. The empirical results show that their method efficiently learns accurate MAD representations and outperforms existing state representation techniques in terms of representation quality.

## Strengths
The paper addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on learning the MAD without relying on reward signals or actions, which is a unique and potentially valuable contribution to representation learning in RL. The proposed algorithms are simple and computationally efficient, making them practical for a wide range of applications. The empirical evaluation is thorough, covering multiple types of environments and comparing against relevant baselines. The paper is well-structured and clearly written, with helpful visualizations and a detailed explanation of the MAD concept and its importance.

## Weaknesses
The paper lacks a strong motivation for learning the MAD specifically. While it is presented as a useful measure, it is unclear how it compares to other state similarity metrics or why it is particularly beneficial for downstream tasks. The experimental evaluation is limited to environments with known MAD values, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. It is unclear how well the method would perform in environments where the MAD is not known or cannot be easily computed. The paper does not provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed algorithms or their convergence properties. A deeper understanding of the theoretical foundations would strengthen the contribution. The choice of quasimetric seems crucial to the performance of the method, but the paper does not provide a principled way to select the best quasimetric for a given environment. The ablation studies are limited and do not explore the sensitivity to important hyperparameters like the quasimetric choice or the regularization factors.

## Questions
- Can you provide a more detailed comparison between MAD and other state similarity metrics? How does it relate to concepts like bisimulation or successor features?
- How does the computational complexity of your method compare to existing approaches for learning state representations in RL?
- Can you provide theoretical guarantees or analysis for your proposed algorithms? What are their convergence properties?
- How does the choice of quasimetric affect the performance of your method? Is there a principled way to select the best quasimetric for a given environment?
- How does your method perform in environments where the MAD is not known or difficult to compute? Can you provide examples or case studies demonstrating its applicability?
