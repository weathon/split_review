# Review

## Summary
This paper provides a geometric interpretation for GPTQ, which implies a layer-wise global error bound. The main results are (i) the GPTQ optimization problem is equivalent to the closest vector problem (CVP) with L2 distance; (ii) the GPTQ algorithm executed from the last to the first dimension is the same as Babai’s nearest plane algorithm on the basis of the factorized Hessian matrix; (iii) the worst-case layer-wise error in the no-clipping setting is bound tightly by the trace of the diagonal matrix of the LDL decomposition of the Hessian matrix. Leveraging this bound, the authors design post-training quantization methods that avoid clipping, and outperform the original GPTQ. In addition, the authors provide efficient GPU inference kernels for the resulting representation.

## Strengths
- The authors establish the equivalence between GPTQ and Babai’s nearest plane algorithm, which is a valuable contribution to the understanding of quantization methods for LLMs.
- The paper provides a geometric interpretation for GPTQ, offering a new perspective on the quantization process and its implications for error bounds.
- The authors design post-training quantization methods that avoid clipping, which can potentially improve the accuracy of quantized models.
- The paper includes efficient GPU inference kernels, which can accelerate the deployment of quantized models in practical applications.

## Weaknesses
- The theoretical results rely on the assumption that no weights are clipped, which may not hold in practical scenarios where clipping is necessary to fit the data within the given bitwidth.
- The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed methods, which could be a concern for large-scale applications.
- The authors do not provide a detailed analysis of the robustness of their methods to different types of data or models, which could affect their generalizability.

## Questions
- How does the assumption of no clipping affect the practicality of the proposed methods, and are there ways to mitigate the impact of clipping on the results?
- Can the authors provide more details on the computational complexity of their methods and how they compare to existing approaches?
- How do the proposed methods perform on different types of data or models, and are there any specific scenarios where they are particularly effective or less effective?
