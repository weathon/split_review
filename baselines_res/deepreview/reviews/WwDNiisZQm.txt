## Summary:

This paper introduces Content-Aware Mamba (CAM), a novel state-space model (SSM) designed to enhance learned image compression (LIC) by adapting processing to image content. The core idea revolves around addressing the limitations of standard Mamba models, which typically employ rigid, content-agnostic scans. The authors propose two key mechanisms to overcome these limitations: a content-adaptive token permutation (CTP) strategy and a global-prior prompting (GPP) mechanism. The CTP module dynamically reorders tokens based on their feature similarity, aiming to group semantically related tokens together, thus facilitating more efficient compression. This is achieved through a codebook-based clustering approach, where tokens are assigned to clusters based on their cosine similarity to learned centroids. The GPP module, on the other hand, injects sample-specific global priors into the SSM, relaxing its strict causality and enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies more effectively. The authors integrate these mechanisms into a Mamba-based LIC model, termed CMiC, and demonstrate its performance on several benchmark datasets. The experimental results presented in the paper show that CMiC achieves state-of-the-art rate-distortion (RD) performance, outperforming existing models, including VTM-21.0, by significant margins. Specifically, the paper reports improvements in BD-rate of 15.91%, 21.34%, and 17.58% on the Kodak, Tecnick, and CLIC datasets, respectively. The authors also conduct ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of each component, showing that both CTP and GPP contribute to the overall performance gains. The paper also includes visualizations of the effective receptive fields (ERFs) of the proposed model, which are used to illustrate how the CTP and GPP mechanisms enable the model to capture global redundancy. Overall, the paper presents a novel approach to adapting Mamba models for image compression, with promising empirical results. However, as I will discuss in detail, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened, particularly in terms of a more thorough analysis of computational complexity, memory usage, and the practical limitations of the proposed method.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to adapting Mamba models for learned image compression. The introduction of Content-Aware Mamba (CAM) with its two key mechanisms, content-adaptive token permutation (CTP) and global-prior prompting (GPP), represents a significant step forward in addressing the limitations of standard Mamba's fixed scanning order and causality in the context of image compression. The CTP module, which dynamically reorders tokens based on their feature similarity, is a novel idea that allows the model to prioritize interactions between content-similar tokens, thereby enhancing the capture of global redundancy. The use of a codebook-based clustering approach for token permutation is also a technically sound and well-motivated choice. The GPP module, which injects sample-specific global priors into the SSM, is another innovative contribution that effectively relaxes the strict causality of Mamba, enabling it to capture long-range dependencies more effectively. The experimental results presented in the paper are compelling, demonstrating state-of-the-art rate-distortion performance on multiple benchmark datasets. The authors provide thorough ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of each component, showing that both CTP and GPP contribute to the overall performance gains. The paper also includes visualizations of the effective receptive fields (ERFs) of the proposed model, which are used to illustrate how the CTP and GPP mechanisms enable the model to capture global redundancy. The paper is generally well-written and clearly explains the proposed method and its technical details. The figures and tables are informative and effectively illustrate the key concepts and results. The authors also provide implementation details and code, which enhances the reproducibility of the work. The paper's focus on addressing the limitations of Mamba's fixed scanning order and causality in the context of image compression is a significant contribution to the field. The proposed CAM model, with its CTP and GPP mechanisms, offers a promising approach to enhancing the performance of Mamba-based LIC models. The empirical results, which demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmark datasets, further support the significance of this work.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other efficient methods, such as those using quantization, pruning, or knowledge distillation. While the paper compares against several state-of-the-art LIC models and reports some efficiency metrics (Table 1), it does not directly address the efficiency gains relative to models optimized with these specific techniques. This omission makes it difficult to assess the true efficiency gains of the proposed method relative to readily deployable alternatives. For instance, the absence of comparisons against models optimized with techniques like post-training quantization (e.g., 8-bit weight quantization) makes it hard to understand the practical trade-offs. Furthermore, comparisons against pruned models, which can achieve significant speedups and memory reductions, are needed to fully contextualize the efficiency of the proposed approach. This is a significant limitation, as these techniques are commonly used in practical deployments. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's experimental section (Table 1) clearly lacks these comparisons. Second, the paper provides decoding latency but omits encoding time, which is a significant oversight. Encoding is often a more computationally intensive process in learned image compression, and without this data, it's impossible to fully evaluate the practical applicability of the method, especially in scenarios where real-time encoding is required. A comparison against VTM-21.0, a widely used standard, would provide a crucial benchmark for understanding the practical trade-offs of the proposed method. The paper explicitly labels the reported latency as "decoding latency" in Table 1, and does not provide encoding latency for CMIC or the baselines. My confidence in this weakness is also high. Third, the paper does not include a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with the content-adaptive token permutation (CTP) and global-prior prompting (GPP) mechanisms. While the paper claims that these modules introduce negligible overhead (Section 4.5), it does not provide a breakdown of the computational cost of each component. This makes it difficult to assess their individual impact on overall efficiency. Furthermore, the memory footprint of the model, including the storage requirements for the learned codebook and intermediate feature maps, is not clearly quantified, which is crucial for practical deployment. Table 1 provides overall complexity metrics, but lacks a detailed breakdown of the computational cost of CTP and GPP, and a specific quantification of the codebook's memory usage. Fourth, the paper lacks a discussion on the potential limitations and trade-offs of the proposed method, such as increased complexity or computational requirements. While the paper highlights the performance gains, it does not fully address the potential increase in computational cost associated with the content-aware mechanisms. A detailed analysis of the computational overhead, including memory usage and energy consumption, is necessary to understand the practical limitations of the proposed method, especially for deployment on resource-constrained devices. While the paper provides some complexity metrics, it lacks a detailed breakdown of the overhead of CTP and GPP and a discussion on energy consumption. My confidence in this weakness is medium. While the paper provides overall decoding latency, a detailed analysis of the inference time complexity of the individual CTP and GPP modules is missing. My confidence in this weakness is medium. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a comprehensive comparison with other efficient methods, including those using quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation. Specifically, they should compare their method against models that have undergone post-training quantization to 8-bits or even 4-bits, as these are widely used in practical deployments. This would provide a clearer picture of the performance-efficiency trade-offs. Furthermore, comparisons against pruned models, which can achieve significant speedups and memory reductions, are essential to fully contextualize the efficiency of the proposed approach. The authors should also consider including models optimized with knowledge distillation, as this technique can produce highly efficient student models that maintain good performance. These comparisons should be performed on the same datasets and using the same evaluation metrics to ensure a fair comparison. The results should include not only rate-distortion performance but also encoding and decoding speeds, memory usage, and energy consumption. Second, the authors must include a detailed analysis of both encoding and decoding times. The encoding process is often the bottleneck in image compression, and without this data, it's impossible to fully evaluate the method's applicability, especially in real-time scenarios. The authors should compare their encoding and decoding speeds with those of VTM-21.0, a widely used standard, to provide a clear benchmark. This comparison should be performed on the same hardware and using the same evaluation metrics. Furthermore, the authors should provide a breakdown of the computational cost of each component of their method, including the content-adaptive token permutation and global-prior prompting. This would help to identify the bottlenecks and potential areas for optimization. The authors should also report the memory footprint of their model, as this is a critical factor for deployment on resource-constrained devices. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with training the clustering codebook. This should include the number of iterations required for convergence, the computational resources needed (e.g., GPU hours, memory), and the sensitivity of the results to different training parameters. It would also be beneficial to compare the training time of the proposed method with other state-of-the-art methods to provide a clearer picture of its computational overhead. Furthermore, the authors should investigate techniques to reduce the training time, such as using more efficient clustering algorithms or pre-training the codebook on a large dataset. Fourth, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the inference time complexity of each module in their proposed method. This should include the time required for token permutation, global prior prompting, and the Mamba model itself. The analysis should also consider the impact of different image resolutions on the inference time. It would be helpful to compare the inference time of the proposed method with other state-of-the-art methods, especially those designed for real-time applications. The authors should also explore techniques to optimize the inference process, such as using model quantization or pruning, to reduce the computational overhead. Fifth, the authors should conduct a more thorough evaluation of their method on diverse image datasets, including medical and satellite imagery, to assess its generalization capabilities. This evaluation should include a quantitative analysis of the compression performance on different types of images, as well as a qualitative assessment of the reconstructed image quality. Additionally, the authors should investigate the robustness of their method to different types of noise and artifacts, such as Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper noise, and compression artifacts. This analysis should include a discussion of how these factors affect the compression performance and reconstructed image quality. The authors should also discuss the potential challenges of extending their method to video compression, including the need for temporal modeling and motion compensation. This discussion should include a preliminary analysis of how the proposed token permutation and global-prior prompting mechanisms could be adapted to handle temporal dependencies in video data. Finally, the authors should include experiments on datasets that exhibit significant variations in image characteristics, such as those with complex textures, varying lighting conditions, and diverse object types. For example, datasets like the Set5, Set14, and BSD100, which are commonly used in image processing research, could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's generalizability. Additionally, the authors should analyze the performance of the proposed method on images with different resolutions, including both upscaled and downscaled versions of the existing datasets, to understand its resolution-dependent behavior. This would provide a more complete picture of the method's strengths and weaknesses across a wider range of image types and resolutions.
