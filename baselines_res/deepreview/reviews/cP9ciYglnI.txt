## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel interactive method for cortical sulcal labeling, leveraging spherical convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and a shape-adaptive guidance signal. The core idea revolves around preserving the intricate anatomical details of the cortical surface by mapping it onto a sphere, thus avoiding the distortions inherent in planar projections. The method encodes user clicks as weighted geodesic distance transforms (WGDT), which are then used to guide the spherical CNN. The WGDT is designed to propagate faster along sulcal regions and slower in gyral regions, effectively capturing the underlying cortical folding patterns. This is achieved by solving the eikonal equation with a speed function based on the mean curvature of the cortical surface. The authors demonstrate that even a single click using this encoding scheme can significantly improve segmentation accuracy compared to fully automatic methods and equidistance schemes. The experimental evaluation, conducted on a subset of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) dataset, shows that the proposed method achieves both high accuracy and efficiency, particularly in labeling small and variable sulci in the lateral prefrontal cortex. The results indicate that the proposed interactive approach, with its curvature-aware guidance signal, offers a promising direction for improving the precision and reliability of cortical sulcal labeling, which is a critical task in neuroimaging analysis. The method's ability to incorporate user input through clicks, combined with the shape-adaptive guidance signal, allows for a more refined and accurate segmentation process, addressing some of the limitations of existing automatic methods. The paper's contribution lies in its innovative use of spherical CNNs, the WGDT encoding scheme, and the integration of geometric information to enhance interactive segmentation of cortical sulci. The findings suggest that this approach could be valuable for various applications in neuroscience and clinical research, where accurate delineation of cortical structures is essential. However, the study also has some limitations, particularly regarding the scope of evaluation and the reliance on a pre-reconstructed cortical surface, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most significant is the introduction of a shape-adaptive guidance signal for interactive cortical sulcal labeling. This approach, which leverages spherical convolutional neural networks, effectively preserves the structural information of the cortical surface, avoiding the loss of anatomical details that can occur with planar projections. The use of a spherical mapping is a notable technical innovation, allowing the method to better capture the complex geometry of the cortex. Furthermore, the encoding of user clicks using a weighted geodesic distance transform (WGDT) is a clever way to incorporate user input into the segmentation process. The WGDT is designed to propagate faster along sulcal regions and slower in gyral regions, effectively capturing the underlying cortical folding patterns. This curvature-aware signal, derived from the mean curvature of the cortical surface, allows the neural network to focus on the intended refinement regions, leading to improved accuracy. The experimental results, obtained on a dataset of 72 subjects with 17 sulci in the lateral prefrontal cortex, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The authors show that even a single click using the WGDT signal can significantly improve segmentation accuracy compared to fully automatic methods and equidistance schemes. This highlights the efficiency of the proposed approach, as it requires minimal user interaction to achieve high accuracy. The method's ability to handle small and variable sulci, which are often challenging for automatic methods, is another notable strength. The paper's focus on interactive segmentation, allowing for user-guided refinement, is a valuable contribution, as it addresses the limitations of fully automatic methods that often require manual correction. The combination of spherical CNNs, the WGDT encoding scheme, and the integration of geometric information represents a significant advancement in the field of cortical sulcal labeling. The paper is also well-written and easy to understand, making the technical details accessible to a broad audience. Overall, the strengths of this paper lie in its innovative methodology, strong empirical results, and clear presentation.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the proposed method is limited by its evaluation on a single dataset, the Human Connectome Project (HCP). As the authors themselves state, "We used a subset of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) (Van Essen et al., 2012). 72 participants were randomly chosen (aged 22-36 years old; 36 males and 36 females)." (Section 3.1). This reliance on a single dataset raises concerns about the generalizability of the method to other datasets or populations. The HCP dataset is known for its high quality and specific acquisition parameters, and the method's performance may not be consistent across datasets with different characteristics, such as varying scanner types, resolutions, or subject populations. This limitation is significant because it restricts the confidence in the method's applicability to real-world scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the use of only the HCP dataset for evaluation. Second, the proposed method requires multiple user clicks to achieve optimal performance. While the paper demonstrates that a single click can improve accuracy compared to automatic methods, the results show that performance generally improves with subsequent clicks. For example, Figure 4 shows that while the WGDT signal performs well with a single click, performance generally improves with subsequent clicks for all guidance signals. Figure 5 also shows that the WGDT signal with 3 clicks generally achieves higher Dice scores compared to 1 or 2 clicks, especially for large sulci. The paper uses 3 clicks in its experiments, and the results show improvement with more clicks, suggesting that achieving "good performance" might require multiple interactions. This reliance on multiple clicks could limit the practicality of the method in real-world applications, where user effort and time are critical factors. The paper does not explicitly address the efficiency in terms of the number of clicks required for acceptable performance. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the experimental setup uses 3 clicks, and the results show improvement with more clicks, suggesting that achieving "good performance" might require multiple interactions. However, the definition of "large number" is subjective. Third, the method involves several hyperparameters that require manual tuning, which may limit its generalizability to new datasets. The authors mention that they used WGDT signal of  $k \in [6, 8, 10]$  and  $\sigma = \pi/32$ ; the optimal value of  $\sigma$  for WGDT signal was determined by evaluating performance across multiple configurations as detailed in Appendix A.1. (Section 3.2). For comparison, they used ADT and Disk with  $\sigma \in [\pi/32, 3\pi/64, \pi/16]$ , which are small enough to capture fine-grained sulcal branches. (Section 3.2). They also simulated 3 clicks per subject, accumulating the losses from each of the clicks with  $\beta_i \in [1/6, 1/3, 1/2]$ . (Section 3.2). The paper explicitly mentions trying different values for hyperparameters like k, sigma, and beta, and the need to determine the "optimal" value for sigma. This indicates a sensitivity to these choices. The lack of an automated hyperparameter optimization strategy means that the method's performance may be sensitive to the choice of these parameters, and optimal values may vary across different datasets or even within the same dataset for different subjects. This manual tuning process can be time-consuming and requires expert knowledge. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper describes the process of selecting hyperparameter values and the range of values explored, indicating a sensitivity to these choices. Fourth, the proposed method is limited to single-sulcus segmentation. The authors state, "Our interactive sulcal labeling model is trained to perform binary segmentation of a cortical surface for individual sulci with support for iterative refinement." (Section 2.1). They also mention, "Although cortical surfaces contain multiple sulci, incorporating multiplication sulci in interactive segmentation can introduce several challenges. These include an increased number of possible user click sequences, arbitrary shifts in user focus across sulci, and difficulty in ensuring balanced training for each sulcus. While a few previous multi-object interactive segmentation studies (Rana et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2024) have discussed these challenges, a widely-adopted protocol for multi-object segmentation has not yet been established. In addition, we train a separate supervised model for each sulcus to account for the distinct morphological characteristics of LPFC sulci, including size, branching pattern, and overall shape. This per-sulcus modeling approach is consistent with common practices in medical image interactive segmentation (Wang et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2021; Diaz-Pinto et al., 2022) and allows each model to specialize in the characteristics of each individual sulcus." (Section 2.1). This focus on single-sulcus segmentation is a significant limitation because the human cortex contains numerous sulci that are often intertwined and vary greatly in size and shape. The current approach would require separate models for each sulcus, which is inefficient and does not leverage potential shared features or relationships between sulci. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the single-sulcus focus and the use of separate models. Finally, the method relies on the availability of a cortical surface reconstruction, which may not always be accurate or feasible. The authors state, "Since the cortical surface is genus-zero, its geometric features can be mapped onto the unit sphere." (Section 2.1). They also mention, "We used geometric features (Fischl, 2012): mean curvature of the white-matter surface (*curv*), average convexity (*sulc*), and mean curvature of the inflated surface (*inflated.H*)." (Section 3.1). They also state, "We simulated user clicks and encoded guidance signals on the unit spherical mesh obtained from the FreeSurfer reconstruction pipeline." (Section 3.3). The method description heavily relies on the cortical surface and its properties (curvature, sulc). The experimental setup explicitly mentions using the FreeSurfer reconstruction pipeline. This confirms the reviewer's point about the reliance on surface reconstruction. The accuracy of the cortical surface reconstruction directly impacts the performance of the proposed method. Any errors or inaccuracies in the surface reconstruction will propagate through the subsequent processing steps, potentially leading to incorrect sulcal labeling. Furthermore, the method's applicability is limited to datasets where a cortical surface can be reliably extracted, excluding volumetric data where surface reconstruction is not straightforward or possible. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the method description and experimental setup clearly indicate the dependence on pre-reconstructed cortical surfaces.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. First, to mitigate the limitation of evaluating the method on a single dataset, the authors should expand their evaluation to include multiple datasets with varying characteristics. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's generalizability. Specifically, the inclusion of datasets with different scanner types, resolutions, and subject populations would be beneficial. Furthermore, the inclusion of datasets with known pathologies or developmental variations could reveal potential limitations of the proposed approach. A quantitative analysis of the performance across these datasets, including metrics such as Dice score and Hausdorff distance, would be essential. This would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses in different contexts. The authors should also consider comparing their method against other state-of-the-art interactive segmentation techniques, not just fully automatic methods, to better contextualize their performance gains. Second, to address the concern about the number of user clicks, the authors should investigate methods to reduce the required interactions. This could involve exploring more efficient guidance signal encoding strategies or incorporating user feedback mechanisms that allow for more targeted corrections. For example, the authors could explore the use of adaptive click placement, where the system suggests optimal click locations based on the current segmentation state. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the relationship between the number of clicks and the resulting segmentation accuracy. This would help to determine the practical trade-off between user effort and segmentation quality. The authors should also consider the potential for automating some of the click-based refinement process, perhaps by incorporating a confidence map that highlights areas where user input is most needed. Third, to address the sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, the authors should develop an automated hyperparameter optimization strategy. This could involve using techniques such as grid search, random search, or Bayesian optimization to find the optimal values for these parameters. The optimization process could be guided by a validation set, or by using a surrogate model to predict the performance of the method for different parameter values. The method should also be made less sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, possibly by incorporating adaptive mechanisms that can automatically adjust the parameters during the segmentation process. This would make the method more user-friendly and improve its generalizability to new datasets. Fourth, to overcome the limitation of single-sulcus segmentation, the authors should explore a framework that can handle multiple sulci simultaneously. This could involve developing a multi-label classification approach where each voxel on the cortical surface is assigned to a specific sulcus or background. This would require a more sophisticated network architecture capable of differentiating between adjacent or overlapping sulci, possibly by incorporating contextual information or attention mechanisms. Furthermore, the method should be extended to handle cases where the boundaries between sulci are ambiguous or poorly defined. This could be achieved by incorporating a soft assignment of voxels to sulci, rather than a hard assignment, allowing for uncertainty in the segmentation boundaries. The training process would also need to be adapted to handle multi-label data, potentially using a weighted loss function to account for class imbalance. Finally, to address the reliance on a pre-reconstructed cortical surface, the authors should extend the method to directly process volumetric data, such as T1-weighted MRI scans. This would require integrating a surface reconstruction module into the framework, or developing a method that can directly segment the sulci from the volumetric data. This could involve using 3D convolutional neural networks or other techniques that can handle the high dimensionality of volumetric data. The method should also be made robust to variations in image quality and noise, which are common in clinical datasets. This could be achieved by incorporating data augmentation techniques during training or by using robust loss functions that are less sensitive to outliers. Furthermore, the method should be evaluated on a wider range of datasets, including those with varying image quality and noise levels, to assess its robustness and generalizability.
