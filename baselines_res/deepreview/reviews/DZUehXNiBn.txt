## Summary:

This paper introduces VISTA (Voting-based Integration of Subgraph Topologies for Acyclicity), a novel modular framework for large-scale causal discovery. The core idea behind VISTA is to decompose the complex problem of learning a global causal structure into smaller, more manageable subproblems by focusing on local Markov Blankets (MBs). The method begins by identifying the MB for each variable, effectively reducing the global causal structure into a set of local subgraphs. These subgraphs are then learned using a chosen base learner, and the resulting local causal structures are integrated through a weighted voting mechanism. This voting process assigns weights to edges based on their frequency and a tunable parameter that penalizes edges with low support, using an exponential decay function. The final step involves enforcing acyclicity using a Feedback Arc Set (FAS) algorithm, specifically a GreedyFAS heuristic. The authors claim that VISTA is model-agnostic, imposing no assumptions on the inductive biases of base learners, and is compatible with arbitrary data settings. They provide theoretical guarantees for the weighted voting scheme, including finite-sample error bounds and asymptotic consistency, and demonstrate the effectiveness of their method through experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The empirical results suggest that VISTA achieves improvements in both accuracy and efficiency, particularly for large-scale datasets, when compared to several baseline methods. The authors emphasize the modularity of their approach, allowing for flexibility in the choice of base learners and the potential for parallelization. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to scalable causal discovery, leveraging local structure learning and a novel aggregation mechanism. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of this framework. The paper's focus on modularity and scalability is commendable, but the lack of detailed analysis in certain areas, particularly regarding the novelty of the aggregation method, the assumptions underlying the theoretical guarantees, and the computational complexity, limits the impact of the work. Furthermore, the paper's discussion of the Markov Blanket learning process and the limitations of the FAS algorithm are also areas that require further attention. Despite these limitations, the paper provides a valuable contribution to the field of causal discovery by proposing a practical and scalable framework that can be applied to a wide range of data settings.
