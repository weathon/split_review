## Summary:

This paper presents a novel investigation into the conversion of dynamically sparsely trained Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) into Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), focusing on the potential for improved energy efficiency and maintained accuracy. The authors introduce Cannistraci-Hebb Training (CHT), a brain-inspired dynamic sparse training method, to create sparse ANNs. These sparse ANNs are then converted into SNNs using various conversion approaches. The core contribution of this work lies in demonstrating that sparse SNNs, derived from CHT-trained ANNs, can achieve accuracy levels comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, those of dense SNNs, while simultaneously offering substantial reductions in theoretical energy consumption. The authors explore the impact of connectivity sparsity on both accuracy and theoretical energy efficiency across different conversion methods and ANN architectures, including Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), VGG-16, and Vision Transformers (ViT-B), using datasets such as CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet. A key finding of this research is the identification of a significant time lag between the saturation of firing rates and the saturation of accuracy in SNNs. This time lag is shown to be different between sparse and dense networks, with sparse SNNs exhibiting a longer time lag. This observation provides insights into the temporal dynamics of SNNs and their relationship to accuracy. The paper's significance lies in its exploration of a novel approach to ANN2SNN conversion, highlighting the potential of sparse networks for energy-efficient SNNs. The use of CHT, a biologically inspired training method, adds an interesting dimension to the research. The comprehensive analysis of the impact of connectivity sparsity on accuracy and theoretical energy efficiency, along with the investigation of the temporal relationship between firing rate and accuracy saturation, contributes valuable insights to the field. The results suggest that sparse SNNs offer a promising avenue for developing more efficient and accurate neural networks, with potential implications for neuromorphic computing and other applications where energy efficiency is paramount. However, the paper also acknowledges certain limitations, particularly the reliance on theoretical energy calculations rather than empirical hardware measurements, and the focus on specific datasets and network architectures. These limitations, along with the lack of comparison with other sparse training methods, will be discussed in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths:

This paper makes several notable contributions to the field of ANN-to-SNN conversion and sparse neural network research. First and foremost, the application of Cannistraci-Hebb Training (CHT) to create sparse ANNs for subsequent conversion to SNNs is a novel approach. This method, inspired by synaptic turnover in biological brains, allows for the dynamic learning of both weights and topology, resulting in sparse network structures. This is a significant departure from traditional methods that often rely on static sparsity patterns or pruning techniques. The use of CHT adds an interesting dimension to the research, bridging the gap between artificial and biological neural networks. The paper's exploration of the impact of connectivity sparsity on both accuracy and theoretical energy efficiency is another key strength. The authors systematically analyze the performance of sparse SNNs across different conversion approaches and ANN architectures, providing a thorough evaluation of their proposed method. This comprehensive analysis demonstrates that sparse SNNs can achieve accuracy comparable to, and in some cases surpassing, that of dense SNNs, while simultaneously offering substantial reductions in theoretical energy consumption. The results, which show up to 99% theoretical energy reduction, are compelling and highlight the potential of sparse SNNs for energy-efficient computing. Furthermore, the investigation into the temporal relationship between firing rate saturation and accuracy saturation is a valuable contribution. The authors reveal a significant time lag between these two phenomena, and they demonstrate that this time lag differs between sparse and dense networks. This finding provides new insights into the temporal dynamics of SNNs and their relationship to accuracy, which could be crucial for the design of more efficient SNN-based systems. The paper's focus on the theoretical energy efficiency of SNNs is also a strength. While the analysis is based on theoretical calculations, it provides a framework for understanding the potential energy savings of sparse SNNs. The authors use specific energy values for MAC and AC operations, which allows for a quantitative comparison of the energy efficiency of different network architectures and sparsity levels. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain their methodology, results, and conclusions, and they provide sufficient detail for others to replicate their work. The use of figures and tables effectively presents the experimental results, further enhancing the clarity of the paper. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach, comprehensive analysis, valuable findings, and clear presentation.


## Weaknesses:

As I've verified, the paper focuses exclusively on comparing sparse SNNs, derived from CHT-trained ANNs, with dense SNNs. While this comparison is valuable, it does not provide a complete picture of the relative effectiveness of CHT. The absence of a comparison with other sparse training techniques, such as magnitude-based pruning, the lottery ticket hypothesis, or other dynamic sparse training methods, limits the understanding of CHT's unique advantages and disadvantages. This omission is significant because it prevents a clear assessment of whether CHT offers a superior approach to sparsity compared to existing methods. The experimental design consistently uses dense ANNs as baselines, and there is no mention of other sparse training methods in the experimental setup or results. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to determine if the observed benefits of sparse SNNs are specific to CHT or if they would generalize to other sparse training techniques. This is a high-confidence weakness, as it is clearly evident from the paper's experimental design and the absence of any discussion of alternative sparse training methods. This comparison should not only focus on final accuracy but also on other critical aspects such as the sparsity level achieved, the computational cost of training, and the energy efficiency of the resulting SNNs. For example, the authors could compare the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) required to achieve a certain level of accuracy with different sparse training methods. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the convergence speed of different sparse training methods during the ANN training phase, as this can significantly impact the overall training time and resource consumption. A detailed analysis of these factors would provide a more complete picture of the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed CHT-based approach compared to other state-of-the-art sparse training techniques. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different sparsity patterns on the performance and energy efficiency of the SNNs. For example, they could investigate the effect of structured sparsity, where groups of connections are removed together, versus unstructured sparsity, where individual connections are removed randomly.
