## Summary:

This paper introduces a theoretical framework for understanding the conditions under which data curation can improve the performance of machine learning models, challenging the traditional notion that 'more is always better' when it comes to training data. The authors leverage random matrix theory to derive exact scaling laws for the test error of linear classifiers trained on curated datasets. Their framework considers a high-dimensional Gaussian data model and analyzes the impact of pruning data based on difficulty and correctness. The core finding is that, under certain conditions, training on a small, carefully curated dataset can outperform training on a larger, uncurated dataset. This is particularly true when the data generator is strong and the data is abundant. The authors validate their theoretical results using synthetic data experiments and also demonstrate the applicability of their framework to real-world scenarios, specifically in the context of large language models (LLMs) trained for mathematical reasoning and image classification on ImageNet. The paper's key contribution lies in providing a theoretical justification for the 'less is more' phenomenon observed in some empirical studies, offering a principled approach to data curation. The authors show that the optimal pruning strategy depends on the quality of the data generator and the amount of available data. They also show that their theory can explain some recent empirical findings in LLMs, where curated datasets have been shown to improve performance on specific tasks. The paper's analysis is primarily focused on linear models, which is a limitation, but the authors provide a solid foundation for future work in this area. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field by providing a theoretical framework for understanding data curation, which is a critical problem in the era of large datasets and models. The authors' work provides a valuable tool for understanding and solving this problem, and their findings have important implications for data-centric machine learning.

This approach provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for the empirical observations that have been made about the 'less is more' phenomenon. The paper's core strength lies in its ability to provide a theoretical explanation for why, under certain conditions, training on a smaller, curated dataset can outperform training on a larger, uncurated dataset. This is a crucial insight that challenges the conventional wisdom of simply using more data. The paper's focus on data-centric machine learning is timely and relevant, as the field moves towards more efficient and effective use of data. ## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a compelling theoretical framework, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the empirical validation, while valuable, is somewhat limited in scope. The paper primarily relies on synthetic data and ImageNet for empirical validation. While ImageNet is a large and diverse dataset, the paper lacks experiments on a wider range of real-world datasets, particularly those from different modalities such as text, code, or speech. This limitation is noted by multiple reviewers, and I concur that it restricts the generalizability of the findings. The paper's analysis is primarily focused on image classification, and it is unclear how the proposed curation strategies would perform on other types of data. For instance, the effectiveness of the curation strategy might vary significantly when applied to sequential data like text or time-series data, which exhibit different statistical properties compared to image data. This lack of empirical diversity is a significant weakness that needs to be addressed in future work. Second, the theoretical framework is based on several assumptions that may not hold in real-world scenarios. The most significant of these is the assumption of a high-dimensional Gaussian data model. As stated in Section 2.1, the data is assumed to be drawn from a Gaussian distribution, which is a simplification of real-world data complexities. Real-world data often exhibits non-Gaussian distributions, heavy tails, and multimodal structures, which are not captured by the Gaussian assumption. This limitation is also noted by multiple reviewers, and I agree that it raises concerns about the applicability of the theoretical results to more complex data distributions. Furthermore, the theoretical analysis is based on linear models, as explicitly defined in Section 2.1. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the 'Future Directions' section, but the lack of analysis for non-linear models is a significant weakness. The behavior of data curation in the context of non-linear models, which often exhibit different optimization landscapes and generalization properties, is not addressed by the current theoretical framework. This is a major limitation, as non-linear models, such as neural networks, are widely used in practice. The paper also assumes binary classification, which is a simplification of many real-world tasks. The theoretical framework should be extended to handle multi-class problems, which are more common in practical applications. The paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the theoretical results, particularly in cases where the assumptions are not fully met. While the 'Concluding Remarks' section mentions limitations and 'Future Directions,' a dedicated section thoroughly exploring the implications of violated assumptions is absent. This lack of a detailed exploration of limitations is a weakness that needs to be addressed. Finally, the paper lacks detailed guidance on the practical implementation of the proposed curation strategies. While the paper defines label-agnostic and label-aware curation rules in Section 2.2, it does not provide specific algorithms or heuristics for implementing the pruning function *q* in practice, especially for complex models. The paper does not address the computational cost of identifying the 'hard' or 'easy' examples, which could be significant for large datasets. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss how the curation strategy should be adapted when the data is not independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.), which is often the case in real-world scenarios. This lack of practical implementation details is a weakness that limits the applicability of the paper's findings. The paper also assumes some familiarity with random matrix theory (RMT), which may not be common among all machine learning researchers. While the paper mentions RMT concepts like the Stieltjes transform and Marchenko-Pastur law, it does not provide detailed explanations of these concepts. This lack of explanation makes the paper less accessible to a wider audience. In summary, while the paper's theoretical framework is a valuable contribution, the limitations discussed above need to be addressed in future work to enhance the generalizability and practical applicability of the findings. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and have been independently validated by multiple reviewers.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the empirical validation of their theoretical framework. This should include experiments on a wider range of real-world datasets, covering diverse modalities such as text, code, and speech. For text data, the authors could explore datasets from natural language processing tasks, such as sentiment analysis or question answering. For code, they could use datasets of code snippets or programming languages. For speech, they could use datasets of audio recordings or speech transcripts. These experiments should explore the impact of different data curation strategies on the performance of various models, including non-linear models such as neural networks. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the practical implications of their theoretical framework. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of their findings to different hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and batch size, to ensure the robustness of their results. Second, the authors should explore ways to relax the assumptions of the theoretical framework, particularly the assumption of a high-dimensional Gaussian data model and the use of linear models. This could involve extending the analysis to more general data distributions, such as those with heavy tails or multimodal structures. The authors could also investigate how the theoretical results can be adapted to multi-class classification problems, which are more common in practice. This might require developing new mathematical tools or adapting existing ones to handle the complexities of multi-class settings. Furthermore, the authors should consider the impact of model architecture on the effectiveness of data curation. For example, the optimal pruning strategy might depend on the specific type of model being used, and the authors should explore how to adapt their framework to different model architectures. Third, the authors should provide more concrete guidance on the practical implementation of the proposed curation strategies. This could involve developing specific algorithms or heuristics for identifying 'hard' or 'easy' examples, as well as providing practical recommendations for setting the parameters of the curation strategy. The authors should also address the computational cost of data curation and explore ways to make the process more efficient. This might involve using approximations or sampling techniques to reduce the computational burden. Furthermore, the authors should discuss how the curation strategy should be adapted when the data is not i.i.d., which is often the case in real-world scenarios. This might involve developing methods for identifying and handling non-i.i.d. data, as well as exploring the impact of non-i.i.d. data on the effectiveness of data curation. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the random matrix theory (RMT) underpinnings of their framework. This should include a more detailed discussion of the key concepts from RMT that are used, such as the Marchenko-Pastur law and the Stieltjes transform. It would be helpful to explain how these concepts are used to derive the scaling laws for the test error of linear classifiers trained on curated data. Furthermore, the authors should provide more intuition behind the assumptions they make, such as the isotropic setting, and discuss how these assumptions might affect the applicability of their results in real-world scenarios. A more detailed explanation of the RMT framework would make the paper more accessible to a wider audience and would strengthen the theoretical contributions of the paper. Finally, the authors should include a dedicated section that thoroughly explores the limitations of their theoretical results, particularly in cases where the assumptions are not fully met. This section should discuss the potential implications of violated assumptions on the generalizability of their findings. A more thorough discussion of these limitations would also enhance the paper's credibility and provide a more balanced perspective on the topic. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make it more impactful and relevant to a wider audience.
