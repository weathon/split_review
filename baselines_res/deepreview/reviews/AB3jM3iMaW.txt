## Summary:

This paper introduces Reasoning-Enhanced Learning for Temporal Graphs (Real-TG), a novel reinforcement learning framework designed to fine-tune large language models (LLMs) for explainable link forecasting on real-world temporal graphs. The core idea is to leverage LLMs' inherent reasoning capabilities to not only predict future links but also to generate human-understandable explanations for these predictions. The authors address the limitations of traditional temporal graph neural networks (TGNNs), which often lack interpretability and struggle to generalize to unseen graphs. Real-TG employs a Temporal Context Graph Selection (T-CGS) algorithm to construct relevant subgraphs, which are then verbalized and used as prompts for the LLM. The LLM is fine-tuned using Grouped Regularized Policy Optimization (GRPO) with an outcome-based reward function that encourages the model to produce accurate predictions and logically sound explanations. A key contribution of this work is the introduction of a new evaluation protocol that combines traditional ranking metrics with an LLM-as-a-Judge system. This system assesses the quality of the generated reasoning traces based on three criteria: faithfulness, logical consistency, and answer-explanation alignment. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of Real-TG through extensive experiments on multiple real-world temporal graph datasets. They show that Real-TG-4B, obtained by fine-tuning Qwen3-4B, outperforms much larger frontier LLMs, including GPT-5 mini, on prediction accuracy while producing high-quality explanations validated by both the LLM judge and human evaluations. The paper's findings suggest that reinforcement learning can be a powerful tool for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs for temporal graph tasks, leading to more interpretable and trustworthy predictions. The authors also highlight the importance of evaluating the reasoning process itself, not just the prediction accuracy, which is a significant step forward in the field of explainable graph learning. The work is significant because it addresses the critical need for interpretability in graph-based predictions, which is crucial for building trust in AI systems, especially in domains where understanding the reasoning behind a prediction is as important as the prediction itself. By combining the power of LLMs with reinforcement learning and a novel evaluation framework, this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of temporal graph learning and explainable AI.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the introduction of the Real-TG framework is a significant contribution. The idea of using reinforcement learning to fine-tune LLMs for explainable link forecasting on temporal graphs is both novel and impactful. This approach directly addresses the limitations of traditional TGNNs, which often lack interpretability and struggle to generalize to unseen graphs. The use of LLMs to generate reasoning traces is a clever way to achieve explainability, and the authors' choice of GRPO for fine-tuning is well-justified. Second, the development of a new evaluation protocol that combines ranking metrics with an LLM-as-a-Judge system is a major strength. This dual approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of both prediction accuracy and the quality of reasoning. The LLM-as-a-Judge system, which evaluates reasoning traces based on faithfulness, logical consistency, and answer-explanation alignment, is a valuable contribution to the field of evaluation and can be used to assess the quality of reasoning and the impact of hallucinations in other applications. The authors' validation of this system through human evaluations, which shows a strong correlation with the LLM judge's scores, further strengthens its credibility. Third, the experimental results are compelling. The authors demonstrate that Real-TG-4B outperforms much larger LLMs on multiple real-world temporal graph datasets, which is a significant achievement. The model's ability to generalize to unseen graphs and produce high-quality explanations, as confirmed by both LLM judges and human evaluations, underscores the practical impact of the proposed framework. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivations, methods, and findings, and the paper is well-structured and organized. Finally, the authors have made their code and data publicly available, which promotes reproducibility and further research in this area.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper acknowledges prior work on using LLMs for graph reasoning, including LLM4DyG, the core idea of using LLMs for temporal graph reasoning is not entirely novel. The authors state that their method extends LLM4DyG by incorporating reinforcement learning, which improves the quality of the reasoning trace. However, the core concept of applying LLMs to temporal graph reasoning has been explored in prior studies. This incremental contribution, while valuable, should be more clearly articulated. Second, the paper claims that its method is explainable, but it does not provide a rigorous comparison with existing methods in traditional link prediction, such as TGTalker. While the authors mention TGTalker in the related work, they do not provide a direct comparison of the explainability mechanisms and quality. This omission makes it difficult to assess the relative strengths and weaknesses of Real-TG's explainability compared to existing methods. The paper needs to not only compare the quality of the explanations but also the computational cost and the impact of the explanations on the prediction accuracy. Third, the paper's evaluation protocol, while comprehensive, lacks sufficient justification for using LLMs to evaluate the quality of reasoning traces. While the authors compare the LLM-as-a-Judge system with human evaluations, showing a strong correlation, they do not provide examples of reasoning traces generated by different models to illustrate the effectiveness of their proposed evaluation protocol. This makes it harder to understand how the evaluation protocol distinguishes between different qualities of reasoning. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed Real-TG framework. It lacks a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each stage of the pipeline, including the temporal context graph selection, prompt construction, and the reinforcement learning fine-tuning process. This analysis should not only consider the overall cost but also the scalability of the approach with respect to the size of the temporal graphs. The absence of this analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the framework, especially in resource-constrained settings. Fifth, the paper does not include a detailed sensitivity analysis of the hyperparameters used in the Real-TG framework. The absence of a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to understand the robustness of the results and how performance might vary with different settings. Sixth, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the Real-TG framework. While the authors briefly mention the limitations related to LLM hallucinations in the ethics statement, they do not delve into specific challenges that might arise in more intricate graph structures or in the presence of significant noise. For instance, the paper does not discuss how the model performs when the temporal dependencies are long-range or when the graph exhibits rapid changes in structure. This omission leaves a gap in understanding the robustness and applicability of the framework in challenging real-world scenarios. Seventh, the paper's evaluation could be strengthened by including a more diverse set of baseline models, particularly those that are specifically designed for temporal graph reasoning. While the paper compares against several traditional methods, it could benefit from including models that utilize attention mechanisms or graph convolutional networks tailored for temporal data. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of Real-TG's performance relative to the state-of-the-art in temporal graph reasoning. Finally, the paper does not provide specific examples of the explanations generated by the model in the main text. This makes it difficult for the reader to fully assess the quality and interpretability of the explanations generated by the model. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should more clearly articulate the specific challenges in temporal graph reasoning that their approach uniquely addresses. While the use of reinforcement learning to refine reasoning traces is a valuable addition, the core concept of applying LLMs to temporal graph reasoning has been explored in prior studies. The authors should investigate the model's ability to handle complex temporal dependencies, such as long-range relationships or cyclic patterns, which may not have been thoroughly addressed in previous work. Furthermore, a more detailed comparison with LLM4DyG, highlighting the specific limitations of that approach which are overcome by the proposed method, would be beneficial. This could include an analysis of the types of reasoning errors made by LLM4DyG and how the reinforcement learning framework mitigates these errors. The authors should also consider exploring the use of different reward functions in their reinforcement learning framework, as the choice of reward function can significantly impact the quality of the learned reasoning strategies. Second, the paper needs a more rigorous comparison with existing methods in traditional link prediction. The authors should not only compare the quality of the explanations but also the computational cost and the impact of the explanations on the prediction accuracy. A quantitative analysis of the explanations, such as the length and complexity of the reasoning traces, would also be valuable. Furthermore, the authors should investigate whether the explanations provided by their method are faithful to the model's decision-making process. This could be done by perturbing the input graph or the reasoning trace and observing how the predictions change. The authors should also consider using established metrics for evaluating the quality of explanations, such as those used in the interpretability literature. Third, the evaluation protocol needs further justification. The use of an LLM-as-a-Judge system is convenient, but it introduces potential biases and limitations. The authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the reliability of this evaluation method, including a comparison with human evaluations. This comparison should not only focus on the correlation between LLM and human scores but also on the specific cases where they disagree. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the LLM judge to different prompts and evaluation criteria. Additionally, the authors should provide more examples of reasoning traces generated by different models, including both successful and unsuccessful cases, to illustrate the effectiveness of their evaluation protocol. Fourth, the paper should include a more thorough analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed Real-TG framework. Specifically, the authors should provide a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each stage of the pipeline, including the temporal context graph selection, prompt construction, and the reinforcement learning fine-tuning process. This analysis should not only consider the overall cost but also the scalability of the approach with respect to the size of the temporal graphs. This should include a systematic exploration of the hyperparameter space, focusing on parameters such as the learning rate, batch size, and the number of training epochs. The analysis should not only report the best-performing hyperparameter settings but also provide insights into the sensitivity of the framework to these parameters. For example, are there any parameters that have a particularly large impact on performance, and are there any parameters that are relatively insensitive to changes? This analysis should be presented in a clear and concise manner, possibly using visualizations such as heatmaps or sensitivity plots, to allow readers to understand the robustness of the framework to different hyperparameter settings. Sixth, the paper should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the Real-TG framework. This should include an analysis of the scenarios where the framework may not perform well, such as graphs with very long-range dependencies or graphs with highly complex temporal dynamics. The authors should also discuss the potential biases that may be introduced by the LLM-as-a-Judge system, and how these biases may affect the evaluation results. For example, does the LLM judge favor certain types of reasoning patterns over others, and how can these biases be mitigated? Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of the outcome-based reward function, and whether it may lead to suboptimal reasoning strategies. This discussion should be presented in a balanced and objective manner, acknowledging the limitations of the framework while also highlighting its strengths. Seventh, the authors should include a more diverse set of baseline models, particularly those that are specifically designed for temporal graph reasoning. Comparing Real-TG against these models would provide a more comprehensive understanding of its relative strengths and weaknesses. Finally, to enhance the paper's focus on explainability, the authors should include concrete examples of the explanations generated by the model in the main text.
