## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to support vector classification (SVC) in non-Euclidean spaces, addressing a perceived limitation of traditional SVMs that are typically formulated within a Euclidean framework. The core idea revolves around incorporating data covariance into the optimization problem, effectively transforming the data from a non-Euclidean statistical space to a Euclidean space using the Cholesky decomposition of class covariance matrices. This transformation allows the application of standard SVM techniques in a space where the data's underlying structure is better represented. The authors propose an iterative algorithm, termed the SM Algorithm, to estimate the population covariance-adjusted SVM classifier from sample covariance matrices, making the method applicable to real-world scenarios where the true population covariance is unknown. The method aims to separate the margin space in a ratio proportional to class population covariance, which is a key departure from traditional SVMs that aim for equal margin separation. The empirical evaluation of the proposed covariance-adjusted SVC (CSVM) is conducted on five standard datasets, demonstrating improved performance over traditional linear and kernel SVMs, as well as whitening algorithms. The authors argue that traditional SVMs, which rely on the max-margin principle and Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, are only valid in Euclidean spaces, and that their method provides a more appropriate framework for non-Euclidean data. The paper's main contribution lies in the specific methodology of using Cholesky decomposition to transform the data and the iterative algorithm to estimate the covariance-adjusted classifier. The experimental results, while promising, are limited in scope and lack a thorough comparison with other state-of-the-art methods for handling non-Euclidean data. Overall, the paper presents an interesting approach to adapting SVMs for non-Euclidean spaces, but it requires further refinement and validation to fully establish its practical significance and theoretical grounding.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The core idea of adapting SVMs for non-Euclidean spaces by incorporating data covariance is both novel and intuitively appealing. The authors correctly identify that traditional SVMs, which are based on Euclidean distance, may not be optimal for data exhibiting complex covariance structures. The use of Cholesky decomposition to transform the data into a space where standard SVM techniques can be applied is a clever and effective approach. The proposed SM Algorithm, which iteratively estimates the population covariance-adjusted SVM classifier from sample covariance matrices, is a practical contribution that makes the method applicable to real-world datasets. The experimental results, while limited in scope, do demonstrate that the proposed CSVM outperforms traditional SVM kernels and whitening algorithms on the selected datasets. This provides initial empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper also provides a theoretical motivation for the proposed algorithm, arguing that the underlying principle of max-margin classification and KKT conditions are only valid in Euclidean vector spaces. This theoretical justification, while not fully comprehensive, adds weight to the proposed methodology. The paper's focus on addressing the limitations of traditional SVMs in non-Euclidean spaces is a significant contribution, and the proposed method offers a potential solution to this challenge. The authors' attempt to link the margin separation to the class covariance is also a valuable contribution, as it provides a more principled approach to classification in such spaces. The paper's overall contribution lies in the specific methodology of using Cholesky decomposition and the iterative algorithm to estimate the covariance-adjusted classifier, which is a novel combination of existing techniques. ## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several significant weaknesses that need to be addressed. First, the paper overclaims the limitations of standard SVMs. The authors assert that traditional SVMs are only valid in Euclidean spaces due to their reliance on Euclidean distance for margin and KKT conditions. While it is true that standard linear SVMs rely on Euclidean distance, SVMs can be adapted to use other distance metrics, including those derived from the Mahalanobis distance. The paper fails to acknowledge this flexibility, which undermines the motivation for the proposed method. This overclaim is evident in the abstract, which states that the study demonstrates limitations of performing SVM classification in non-Euclidean spaces, and in Lemma 2.1, which states that the principles of support vector classification are valid only in Euclidean vector spaces. This is a significant weakness because it misrepresents the capabilities of standard SVMs and overstates the novelty of the proposed approach. Second, the paper lacks a thorough comparison with existing methods for handling non-Euclidean data in SVM classification. While the paper compares against traditional SVM kernels and whitening algorithms, it fails to compare against methods explicitly designed for non-Euclidean geometry, such as those based on Riemannian geometry or information geometry. Furthermore, the paper does not compare against methods using kernel functions derived from geodesic distances on manifolds. This lack of comparison is a major weakness because it makes it difficult to assess the true advantage of the proposed method over existing state-of-the-art techniques. The experimental section only includes comparisons with 'Traditional SVM kernels (linear, RBF, sigmoid, polynomial) and whitening algorithms (PCA and ZCA)', which is insufficient to establish the superiority of the proposed method. Third, the experimental evaluation is limited in scope. The paper uses only five datasets, and it does not provide detailed information about the dimensionality of each dataset or the class distributions. The absence of this information makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the performance of the proposed method on high-dimensional datasets or datasets with complex non-linear structures. It is also unclear how the method performs on datasets with varying levels of class imbalance. This limited evaluation is a significant weakness because it does not provide a comprehensive assessment of the method's effectiveness. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm. The paper describes the steps of the algorithm, including covariance calculation, Cholesky decomposition, and SVM optimization, but it lacks a formal analysis of the computational complexity of these steps and how they scale with the number of samples and dimensions. This lack of analysis is a significant weakness because it makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the method for large-scale datasets. Fifth, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the paper mentions some limitations, such as the need for population covariance and higher computational complexity, it does not discuss sensitivity to parameter settings, convergence criteria, or the impact of noisy or outlier data points. This omission is a weakness because it does not provide a balanced and realistic assessment of the method's applicability. Sixth, the experimental results are presented without any measure of variability or uncertainty. The paper presents single values for accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC for each method on each dataset, without reporting standard deviations or confidence intervals. This lack of variability measures is a significant weakness because it makes it difficult to assess the statistical significance of the observed improvements. Seventh, the paper lacks a dedicated discussion section that interprets the experimental results in the context of the proposed method's theoretical properties. The paper presents results and makes comparisons, but it lacks a deeper discussion linking the performance improvements to the theoretical properties of CSVM or the specific characteristics of the datasets. This omission is a weakness because it does not provide a deeper understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses. Finally, the proposed algorithm is only defined for two classes, and the paper does not discuss how to extend it to multi-class problems. This limitation is a weakness because it restricts the applicability of the method to binary classification tasks.

This would provide a more accurate and nuanced perspective on the existing literature. Second, the authors should significantly expand the experimental evaluation to include a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods for handling non-Euclidean data in SVM classification. This should include comparisons with SVMs using Mahalanobis distance kernels, kernel methods designed for non-Euclidean spaces, and manifold learning techniques. This would provide a more robust assessment of the proposed method's performance and its position within the existing literature. Third, the authors should provide more detailed information about the datasets used in the experimental evaluation, including the number of samples, dimensionality, and class distributions. This information is crucial for assessing the validity and generalizability of the results. The authors should also include experiments on high-dimensional datasets and datasets with complex non-linear structures. Fourth, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm, including a breakdown of the computational cost of each step and how it scales with the number of samples and dimensions. This analysis should also include empirical results on the running time of the algorithm on different datasets. Fifth, the authors should include a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, including sensitivity to parameter settings, convergence criteria, and the impact of noisy or outlier data points. This discussion should also address the limitations of the SM Algorithm and its convergence properties. Sixth, the authors should report measures of variability, such as standard deviations or confidence intervals, for all performance metrics in the experimental results. This would provide a more robust assessment of the statistical significance of the observed improvements. Seventh, the authors should include a dedicated discussion section that interprets the experimental results in the context of the proposed method's theoretical properties. This discussion should explain why incorporating covariance information leads to improved performance and under what conditions this approach is most effective. Eighth, the authors should discuss how the proposed method can be extended to multi-class classification problems. This discussion should include a detailed explanation of the proposed approach and its theoretical justification. Finally, the authors should ensure consistent terminology throughout the paper, particularly when using terms like 'input space' and 'statistical space'.
