## Summary:

This paper introduces CorreGen, a novel generative framework for multi-view clustering (MVC) designed to address the challenges posed by noisy correspondences in real-world datasets. The authors identify two critical types of noisy correspondences: category-level mismatch, where semantically similar instances across views are incorrectly treated as negatives, and sample-level mismatch, which includes both misaligned pairs and unaligned samples. CorreGen formulates the problem as a maximum likelihood estimation over latent cross-view correspondences, which are modeled as unobserved variables. This approach contrasts with traditional discriminative methods that rely on pre-defined pairs. The framework employs an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to iteratively infer these latent correspondences. In the E-step, soft correspondence distributions are inferred across views using Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)-guided marginals. Specifically, a GMM is fitted to the embedding space of each view, and the posterior responsibilities are used to estimate the marginal likelihood of each sample. These marginals serve as constraints in an optimal transport formulation, which yields a soft many-to-many assignment capturing category-level relationships. In the M-step, the embedding network is updated to maximize the expected log-likelihood, effectively pushing semantically consistent pairs closer in the embedding space. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of CorreGen through extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world noisy datasets, showing that it outperforms state-of-the-art MVC methods. The paper also provides a theoretical connection by showing that the standard InfoNCE contrastive loss is a special case of their formulation under specific assumptions. The core contribution of this work lies in its novel generative approach to MVC, which shifts away from discriminative objectives and offers a new perspective on handling noisy correspondences. By modeling latent correspondences and using GMM-guided marginals, the method aims to uncover underlying semantic relationships without relying heavily on potentially noisy pre-defined pairs. The experimental results support the claim that CorreGen is robust to noisy correspondences and achieves superior performance compared to existing methods, particularly on challenging datasets with high levels of noise. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-view clustering by introducing a principled generative approach that effectively addresses the challenges of noisy correspondences.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of shifting from a discriminative contrastive objective to a generative one is a significant contribution. By formulating noisy correspondence learning as a maximum likelihood estimation problem over latent cross-view correspondences, the authors offer a fresh perspective on handling noisy data in multi-view clustering. This approach allows the model to uncover underlying semantic relationships without relying heavily on potentially noisy pre-defined pairs, which is a common limitation of existing methods. The use of an EM algorithm to iteratively infer these latent correspondences is a principled approach that aligns well with the generative formulation. Second, the paper's identification and formalization of two critical types of noisy correspondence—category-level mismatch and sample-level mismatch—are insightful. This work advances the understanding of challenges in real-world multi-view datasets and motivates the need for robust clustering methods. By explicitly addressing these two types of mismatches, the proposed method is better equipped to handle the complexities of real-world data. Third, the GMM-guided marginals in the E-step are a novel and effective way to estimate the posterior distributions of latent variables. The use of GMM to model the embedding space and the subsequent use of posterior responsibilities to guide the optimal transport formulation is a clever technique that allows the model to capture category-level relationships and down-weight noisy samples. This approach is a key component of the method's robustness to noisy correspondences. Finally, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate that CorreGen consistently outperforms state-of-the-art MVC approaches across various datasets and mismatch ratios. The significant performance improvements on the UMPC-Food101 dataset, which is known to be challenging due to its noisy nature, further highlight the robustness of the proposed method. The inclusion of an ablation study, although not exhaustive, provides some evidence for the effectiveness of the GMM marginals and the virtual sample mechanism. The theoretical connection showing that InfoNCE is a special case of their formulation is also a notable strength, providing a theoretical grounding for their approach.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the CorreGen framework. While the authors describe the E-step involving solving an optimal transport problem with entropy regularization and fitting a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), and the M-step involving maximizing a log-likelihood function, there is no explicit discussion of the time and memory requirements for training and inference. This is a significant omission, as the EM algorithm, especially with the added complexity of optimal transport and GMM, can be computationally expensive. Without a detailed complexity analysis, it is difficult to assess the scalability of the proposed method for large-scale datasets. Furthermore, the paper does not provide any empirical results on the convergence speed of the EM algorithm, which is crucial for practical applications. The absence of convergence curves or analysis of the number of iterations makes it difficult to understand the practical implications of using the method. This lack of analysis is a major limitation, as it prevents a clear understanding of the computational cost and practical feasibility of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lacks any discussion or empirical results related to computational complexity and convergence speed. Second, the paper's ablation study is not comprehensive enough to fully evaluate the contribution of individual components of the CorreGen framework. While the authors include an ablation study in Appendix F, which examines the impact of removing the GMM marginals and the virtual sample, it does not explore simpler alternatives to the GMM marginals or isolate the effect of the specific 'robust correspondence learning' aspect in the M-step. For example, it is unclear how much performance is lost if the GMM marginals are replaced with simpler alternatives, or if the robust correspondence learning is not used. A more thorough ablation study is needed to understand the importance of each component and whether the overall performance gain is due to a specific part of the framework or the synergistic effect of all components. This lack of a detailed ablation study makes it difficult to fully understand the underlying mechanisms of the method and the contribution of each component. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does include some ablation experiments, but they are not exhaustive. Third, the paper lacks a comprehensive discussion of the sensitivity of the CorreGen framework to hyperparameter settings. While Appendix E provides a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for $\rho$, $\epsilon$, and $m$, it does not cover all relevant hyperparameters, such as the number of GMM components, the learning rate, and other regularization parameters used in the neural network training. Without a detailed analysis of the sensitivity to these hyperparameters, it is difficult to determine the robustness of the method and provide guidance for practitioners on how to choose appropriate hyperparameter values for different datasets. The paper should also discuss the potential impact of these hyperparameters on the convergence and stability of the EM algorithm. This omission is a significant weakness, as it limits the practical applicability of the method and makes it difficult to reproduce the results. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does include some hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, but it is not comprehensive. Finally, while the paper claims that the proposed method can handle category-level mismatch and sample-level mismatch, the method does not explicitly address these two problems. The paper argues that by formulating the problem as maximum likelihood estimation over latent correspondences, it implicitly addresses both types of mismatches. However, a more explicit explanation of how each component directly addresses these specific types of mismatches would be beneficial. While the GMM-guided marginals in the E-step are designed to down-weight noisy samples and capture category-level relationships, the paper does not provide a clear theoretical justification for how this specifically handles category-level mismatches. This lack of explicit modeling of these mismatches is a limitation, as it makes it difficult to fully understand how the method achieves robustness to these types of noise. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper's formulation and the description of the E-step suggest an implicit handling of these mismatches, but a more explicit explanation would be beneficial.
