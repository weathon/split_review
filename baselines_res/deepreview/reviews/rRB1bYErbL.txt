## Summary:

This paper introduces R-HORIZON, a novel method designed to enhance and evaluate the long-horizon reasoning capabilities of large reasoning models (LRMs). The core idea behind R-HORIZON is to construct complex, multi-step reasoning tasks by composing simpler, single-horizon problems. This is achieved through various composition methods, including direct, sequential, and graphical composition, which are applied to mathematical, code, and agentic tasks. The authors demonstrate that current LRMs struggle with these long-horizon tasks, exhibiting significant performance degradation as the reasoning horizon expands. To address this, the paper leverages R-HORIZON to generate training data for reinforcement learning with verified rewards (RLVR). The results indicate that training with R-HORIZON-generated data not only improves performance on multi-horizon reasoning tasks but also enhances accuracy on standard reasoning benchmarks. The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of 26 LRMs across six datasets, providing valuable insights into the limitations of current models and the potential of R-HORIZON for advancing the field. The authors also analyze the allocation of 'thinking budgets' across different problems, revealing that models tend to allocate more tokens to early reasoning stages, indicating a difficulty in distributing resources effectively across extended reasoning sequences. The work is significant because it addresses a critical gap in current evaluation paradigms for LRMs, which often focus on isolated, single-horizon tasks. By introducing a method to construct complex, multi-step reasoning tasks, the authors provide a more realistic and challenging benchmark for evaluating and training LRMs. The use of R-HORIZON for RLVR also demonstrates a promising approach for improving model performance on both multi-horizon and standard reasoning tasks. Overall, this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field by providing a novel method for constructing challenging benchmarks, revealing limitations of current models, and demonstrating a path towards improving long-horizon reasoning capabilities.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, while the paper introduces R-HORIZON as a method applicable to various reasoning tasks, the experimental validation is heavily skewed towards mathematical problems. Although the authors mention and provide methods for code and agentic tasks, the main text focuses almost exclusively on mathematical examples, and the analysis and results for these other domains are comparatively less detailed. For instance, Section 3.1 provides a detailed description of the problem filtering and composition process specifically for mathematical tasks, while the details for code and agentic tasks are relegated to Appendix A. This emphasis on mathematical tasks raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings and the effectiveness of R-HORIZON for a broader range of reasoning tasks. The paper does not provide a thorough discussion on how R-HORIZON would perform on tasks involving natural language understanding, commonsense reasoning, or complex planning scenarios. This lack of discussion limits the practical utility of the R-HORIZON benchmark and raises questions about its applicability to real-world reasoning scenarios beyond the specific domains explored in the paper. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is clearly supported by the paper's structure and the distribution of experimental results. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with R-HORIZON, particularly for long-horizon tasks. The evaluation primarily focuses on accuracy metrics, but the computational resources required for training and inference, especially with increasing horizon lengths, are not thoroughly examined. The paper mentions setting a generation length to avoid truncation, but there is no explicit discussion or measurement of computational resources such as time, memory, or GPU usage. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the approach, especially for resource-constrained environments. Without a clear understanding of the computational cost, it is challenging to determine the scalability of R-HORIZON and its suitability for real-world applications. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper provides no evidence to counter this claim. Finally, while the paper describes multiple composition methods, the experimental evaluation heavily relies on the 'Sequential Compose' method, particularly for mathematical tasks. The impact of different composition strategies on the difficulty and characteristics of the generated benchmarks is not systematically explored. For example, the paper does not investigate how variations in the composition process, such as the order of sub-problems or the introduction of dependencies, might affect the resulting benchmark's difficulty and the models' performance. This lack of exploration limits the understanding of the benchmark's properties and its ability to capture the nuances of long-horizon reasoning. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the experimental results section clearly demonstrates the focus on the sequential composition method, with other methods mentioned but not explored in detail.
