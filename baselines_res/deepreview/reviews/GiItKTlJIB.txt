## Summary:

This paper investigates the faithfulness of chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs) when applied to physics problem-solving. The authors introduce a novel deletion-based probing framework to assess how much models rely on their CoT traces versus their ability to 'cram' answers without genuine reasoning. This framework involves systematically intercepting the CoT during generation, removing tokens, and measuring the downstream effects on the final answer. The core idea is to see if models can still produce accurate answers even when their intermediate reasoning steps are disrupted. The authors apply this framework to three open-source models—Magistral, Phi-4, and Qwen-A3B—across multiple physics benchmarks of varying difficulty, including UG Physics, PhyBench, and PhysReason. The main empirical finding is that these models can maintain high accuracy even with significant deletions (40-60%) of intermediate reasoning steps. This is achieved through a process the authors term 'cramming,' where the models reconstruct the missing reasoning steps directly into the final answer. Overlap analyses reveal that deleted equations and facts often reappear in the final answers, but inconsistently across different deletion strategies, suggesting a shallow and opportunistic reliance on CoT. The paper highlights the need for evaluation methods that go beyond accuracy to assess the faithfulness of reasoning in scientific domains. The authors argue that current accuracy-based evaluations are insufficient for scientific domains and point toward the need for methods that assess reasoning faithfulness as a core requirement for advancing AI for science. The study's significance lies in its novel approach to evaluating the reasoning process in LLMs, particularly in the context of scientific problem-solving. By demonstrating that models can achieve high accuracy through 'cramming' rather than genuine reasoning, the paper raises important questions about the reliability of CoT as a measure of true understanding. This work underscores the need for more robust evaluation methods that prioritize reasoning faithfulness, especially in high-stakes scientific applications. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their study, particularly the focus on physics and the lack of exploration into the internal mechanisms of the models. Overall, this paper provides a valuable contribution to the field by introducing a new methodology for evaluating reasoning dependence in LLMs and highlighting the need for more rigorous evaluation standards in AI for science.

## Strengths:

This paper's primary strength lies in its introduction of a novel deletion-based probing methodology to evaluate the faithfulness of chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs), specifically within the context of physics problem-solving. This approach is original and addresses a critical gap in the evaluation of reasoning in scientific domains, where accuracy alone is insufficient to ensure the reliability of the reasoning process. The authors' systematic deletion framework, which involves intercepting the CoT during generation, removing tokens, and measuring the downstream impact on the final answer, provides a valuable tool for assessing the extent to which models rely on their intermediate reasoning steps. Furthermore, the study provides compelling empirical evidence of 'cramming' behavior in LLMs. The finding that models can maintain high accuracy even with significant deletions of intermediate reasoning steps, by reconstructing the missing steps directly into the final answer, is a significant contribution to our understanding of how these models operate. This insight is crucial for understanding the limitations of current LLMs in scientific reasoning and highlights the need for more robust evaluation methods that prioritize reasoning faithfulness. The use of multiple physics benchmarks and open-source models strengthens the robustness of the findings. The authors' choice of diverse benchmarks, including UG Physics, PhyBench, and PhysReason, allows for a more comprehensive assessment of the models' reasoning abilities across different levels of difficulty. The inclusion of three open-source models—Magistral, Phi-4, and Qwen-A3B—further enhances the generalizability of the results. The paper is also well-organized, with clear explanations of the methodology, experiments, and results. The authors provide a detailed description of their deletion framework, the experimental setup, and the analysis of the results. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key findings and facilitates understanding. The paper's focus on the faithfulness of reasoning in scientific domains is particularly relevant, as it highlights the need for evaluation methods that go beyond accuracy to ensure the reliability of AI systems in high-stakes scientific applications. The authors' emphasis on the importance of reasoning faithfulness as a core requirement for advancing AI for science is a significant contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

One of the most significant limitations of this study is its exclusive focus on physics problem-solving. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the experiments are conducted using three physics benchmarks: UG Physics, PhyBench, and PhysReason. This narrow focus raises serious concerns about the generalizability of the findings to other scientific domains or general reasoning tasks. The specific structure and language of physics problems, which often involve well-defined equations and numerical solutions, might lead to different behaviors in models compared to domains like biology or chemistry, which rely more on descriptive reasoning and qualitative analysis. The observed 'cramming' behavior might be amplified or diminished in other domains, making it difficult to draw broad conclusions about the faithfulness of CoT reasoning across diverse tasks. The paper does not include any experiments or discussions on other scientific domains, further limiting the scope of its conclusions. This lack of generalizability is a major weakness, as it restricts the applicability of the findings to a specific domain. Another critical limitation is the paper's lack of exploration into the internal mechanisms that enable models to 'cram' missing information. While the authors observe that models can reconstruct deleted reasoning steps in the final answer, they do not delve into the underlying processes that facilitate this behavior. It is unclear whether this behavior arises from the model's ability to memorize common problem-solving patterns or if it reflects a more sophisticated form of reasoning. The paper acknowledges this limitation, stating, 'While we do not probe internal mechanisms directly, these results suggest that LLMs may draw on internalized physics knowledge or learned solution templates to regenerate missing reasoning steps during answer decoding.' However, this acknowledgment is not accompanied by any attempt to investigate these mechanisms. Analyzing the model's attention patterns or hidden states during the 'cramming' process could reveal whether the model is genuinely reconstructing the reasoning steps or simply retrieving memorized solutions. Without this analysis, the conclusions about the faithfulness of CoT remain somewhat superficial. The paper's reliance on token overlap metrics to assess reasoning faithfulness is also a significant weakness. While these metrics can indicate the presence of similar tokens or phrases, they do not assess the semantic correctness or logical coherence of the reconstructed reasoning. For example, a model might generate an equation that is syntactically similar to the original but semantically incorrect, which would not be captured by simple overlap metrics. The paper uses Jaccard similarity and Manhattan distance, which are established metrics, but their application in this context is limited. A more nuanced evaluation would require metrics that assess the logical validity and scientific correctness of the reconstructed reasoning steps. The authors do not discuss or use metrics that assess semantic correctness or logical coherence beyond token overlap, further limiting the robustness of their conclusions. The paper also lacks concrete, actionable solutions or strategies to improve reasoning faithfulness in LLMs. While the authors identify the issue of unfaithful CoT and highlight the need for new evaluation methods, they do not propose specific mitigation strategies. The conclusion states, 'For prompting and system design, it highlights the need to explore strategies that promote reliance on genuine intermediate reasoning rather than heuristic reconstruction,' but it does not offer any concrete proposals. The paper does not explore potential mitigation strategies, such as incorporating constraints on the reasoning process or using techniques like knowledge distillation to encourage more faithful reasoning. This lack of discussion on potential solutions limits the practical impact of the study. Finally, the study's focus on open-source models also limits the generalizability of the findings. The paper explicitly states that it evaluates three open-source LLMs: Phi-4, Qwen-A3B, and Magistral. The performance of open-source models can vary significantly in terms of their training data and architecture, making it difficult to generalize the findings to the broader landscape of LLMs. The absence of results from state-of-the-art closed-source models, such as GPT-4 or Claude-3.5, limits the scope of the conclusions. The paper does not include experiments with closed-source models, further restricting the applicability of its findings to a specific subset of LLMs. These limitations, taken together, significantly impact the overall conclusions of the paper and highlight the need for further research in this area.

For example, in biology, the reasoning might involve more descriptive and qualitative analysis, while in mathematics, it might involve more symbolic manipulation. By comparing the model's behavior across these diverse domains, it would be possible to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the generalizability of the observed 'cramming' behavior and the faithfulness of CoT reasoning. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to investigate the impact of different problem formats and question types on the model's reliance on CoT, as this could reveal potential biases or limitations of the approach. This expansion would require careful selection of datasets and potentially modifications to the deletion framework to account for the different types of reasoning involved in each domain. Second, future research should focus on analyzing the internal mechanisms behind the 'cramming' behavior. This could involve techniques such as attention analysis, which would reveal which parts of the input and intermediate reasoning steps the model is focusing on when generating the final answer. Additionally, probing the hidden states of the model could provide clues about the information that is being stored and retrieved during the 'cramming' process. For example, it would be interesting to investigate whether the model is accessing memorized problem-solving patterns or generating novel reasoning steps. This analysis could be further enhanced by comparing the internal states of the model when it is generating faithful reasoning traces versus when it is 'cramming' information. Such an analysis would provide a more nuanced understanding of the model's reasoning process and the limitations of CoT. This would require access to the internal states of the models and the development of appropriate analysis techniques. Third, to overcome the limitations of token overlap metrics, future work should explore more sophisticated evaluation methods that can assess the semantic correctness and logical coherence of the reconstructed reasoning. This could involve the use of domain-specific knowledge graphs to verify the scientific validity of the generated equations and facts. Additionally, it would be beneficial to develop metrics that can assess the logical flow of the reasoning steps, ensuring that the reconstructed reasoning is not only syntactically similar to the original but also logically sound. For example, one could use a formal logic framework to represent the reasoning steps and then evaluate the logical validity of the reconstructed reasoning. This would provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of the faithfulness of CoT reasoning and help to identify the specific areas where current models are lacking. This would require the development of new evaluation metrics and potentially the use of external knowledge sources. Fourth, the paper should include experiments with state-of-the-art closed-source models, such as GPT-4 or Claude-3.5, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon across different types of LLMs. This would involve comparing the performance of these models with the open-source models used in the study, focusing on the degree to which they exhibit the 'cramming' behavior. The authors should also investigate whether the observed behavior is correlated with model size or training data, which could provide insights into the underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different prompting strategies on the faithfulness of CoT reasoning. For example, they could compare the performance of models when prompted with explicit instructions to provide step-by-step reasoning versus when prompted with more concise instructions. This would help determine whether the observed behavior is an inherent property of the models or a consequence of the prompting strategy. Finally, the paper should propose concrete solutions or strategies to improve reasoning faithfulness in LLMs. This could involve exploring techniques such as incorporating constraints on the reasoning process, using knowledge distillation to encourage more faithful reasoning, or developing new evaluation metrics that specifically assess the faithfulness of CoT. For example, the authors could investigate the use of constraint-based reasoning frameworks that force the model to follow specific reasoning steps, or they could explore the use of adversarial training to make the model more robust to deletions of intermediate reasoning steps. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their proposed solutions and identify areas for future research. This would make the paper more impactful and provide a clear roadmap for future work in this area. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.
