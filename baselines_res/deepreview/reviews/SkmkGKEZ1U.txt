## Summary:

The paper introduces O-Forge, an innovative tool designed to assist in proving asymptotic inequalities by combining the creative problem-solving capabilities of large language models (LLMs) with the rigorous verification power of a computer algebra system (CAS), specifically Mathematica. The core contribution of O-Forge lies in its ability to decompose complex mathematical problems into simpler subproblems, which are then verified using Mathematica's `Resolve` function. This approach addresses a significant challenge in AI-assisted mathematics: ensuring the correctness of proofs generated by LLMs. The tool's effectiveness is demonstrated through case studies, including problems posed by mathematician Terence Tao, which highlight its potential to handle complex, research-level mathematical problems. However, the paper's evaluation is limited, and it does not provide a detailed analysis of the LLM's role in domain decomposition or the limitations of the `Resolve` function. These gaps raise questions about the tool's reliability and generalizability, particularly in high-stakes mathematical research.

## Strengths:

The paper presents a novel and valuable approach to automating the proof process for asymptotic inequalities, a common and challenging task in fields such as analysis, number theory, and theoretical computer science. The integration of LLMs with a CAS, specifically Mathematica, is innovative and has the potential to significantly accelerate mathematical research. The LLM's role in proposing domain decompositions leverages its creative problem-solving abilities, while the CAS ensures the rigor and correctness of the proofs, addressing a key issue in AI-assisted mathematics. The case studies, including problems posed by Terence Tao, provide strong evidence of the tool's capabilities and its potential to handle complex, research-level mathematical problems. The paper also highlights the practical benefits of O-Forge, such as reducing the manual effort required by mathematicians and making the proof process more accessible. The authors' choice to use Mathematica's `Resolve` function is well-justified, as it can handle a wide range of functions, including transcendental ones, which are often challenging for other automated theorem provers. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of automated mathematical reasoning and demonstrates the potential of hybrid AI systems in advancing mathematical research.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the promising contributions of O-Forge, several limitations and areas for improvement are evident. One of the most significant concerns is the lack of transparency in the verification process. The tool relies on Mathematica's `Resolve` function, which operates as a 'black box' and does not produce formal proof objects that can be independently verified. This limitation is explicitly acknowledged in the paper (Section 1.1 and Section 7), but the implications for high-stakes mathematical research are not thoroughly discussed. The reliance on a closed-source system like Mathematica raises concerns about the reliability of the proofs, especially since the correctness of the symbolic manipulations cannot be easily verified. This is a critical issue, as the trustworthiness of mathematical proofs is paramount.

Another limitation is the lack of a detailed analysis of the LLM's role in domain decomposition. The paper mentions that the LLM is guided by cues such as dominant terms and monotonic regimes (Section 2), but it does not provide a systematic evaluation of the quality of these decompositions. For instance, the paper does not discuss how often the LLM proposes incorrect or suboptimal decompositions, nor does it identify specific types of inequalities or functions for which the LLM struggles. This gap in the analysis makes it difficult to understand the strengths and weaknesses of the LLM-based decomposition strategy. A more in-depth exploration, including both successful and unsuccessful decompositions, would enhance the paper's transparency and reproducibility.

The paper also lacks a comprehensive evaluation of O-Forge's performance. The authors present two case studies and mention testing on a set of 40-50 easier problems (Section 5), but the results are not detailed. There is no comparison to other existing tools or methods for proving asymptotic inequalities, such as manual proof techniques or other automated theorem provers. This omission makes it challenging to assess the true value and effectiveness of O-Forge. A more rigorous evaluation, including a diverse set of problems from the literature and a comparison to other methods, would provide a more objective measure of the tool's capabilities and limitations.

The computational complexity of the verification process is another area that needs more attention. The paper briefly mentions the use of `Resolve` for symbolic verification (Section 2 and Section 5), but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost, especially for large or complex inequalities. Understanding the time and resources required for verification is crucial for practical applications of O-Forge. The authors should measure the time taken by `Resolve` to verify different types of inequalities and analyze the factors that affect its performance. This would help users to understand the practical limitations of the tool and to use it more effectively.

Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of O-Forge. While the authors acknowledge that the tool is not designed to produce formal proof objects (Section 1.1), they do not explore alternative approaches or tools that might address this limitation, such as integrating with open-source theorem provers like Lean or Coq. These systems offer more transparent proof verification, which could enhance the reliability and trustworthiness of O-Forge. The authors should also discuss the sensitivity of O-Forge to the choice of LLM and the prompts used to generate decompositions, as well as the types of asymptotic inequalities that are particularly challenging for the tool. Addressing these limitations would provide a more balanced and comprehensive view of O-Forge's capabilities and guide future research directions.


## Suggestions:

To enhance the paper and address the identified limitations, several concrete and actionable improvements are recommended. First, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the LLM's role in domain decomposition. This should include a discussion of the specific prompts used to guide the LLM, along with examples of both successful and unsuccessful decompositions. A quantitative analysis of the LLM's performance, such as the percentage of correct decompositions or the average number of iterations required to achieve a valid decomposition, would be valuable. The authors should also explore the limitations of the LLM-based decomposition strategy, identifying specific types of inequalities or functions for which it struggles. This could involve testing the tool on a wider range of problems, including those known to be challenging for LLMs, and analyzing the patterns in the LLM's failures. Comparing the LLM-based approach to other domain decomposition techniques, such as those based on adaptive partitioning or interval arithmetic, would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method.

Second, the paper should include a more detailed explanation of the symbolic verification process using Mathematica's `Resolve` function. This should cover the underlying mathematical techniques, such as quantifier elimination, and how they are applied to asymptotic inequalities. The authors should also address the limitations of `Resolve`, such as its inability to handle certain types of functions or inequalities, and how these limitations might affect the applicability of O-Forge. A thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the verification process, especially for large or complex inequalities, would be beneficial. This could involve measuring the time taken by `Resolve` to verify different types of inequalities and analyzing the factors that affect its performance. The authors should consider exploring alternative verification methods, such as those based on interval arithmetic or numerical analysis, to provide a more robust and versatile tool.

Third, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive evaluation of O-Forge. This should include testing the tool on a diverse set of asymptotic inequalities from the literature, with problems of varying difficulty and complexity. The authors should report the success rate of O-Forge on each problem and compare its performance to that of other tools or methods, such as manual proof techniques or other automated theorem provers. The comparison should not only focus on the success rate but also on the time taken to solve the problems and the complexity of the decompositions generated by O-Forge. A detailed analysis of the cases where O-Forge fails, including the reasons for the failure and the types of inequalities that are particularly challenging, would help to identify the limitations of the current approach and guide future research directions.

Fourth, the authors should address the lack of formal proof objects by exploring integration with open-source theorem provers like Lean or Coq. While the current approach using Mathematica's `Resolve` function is effective, the lack of transparency in the verification process is a significant limitation. The authors should investigate how the LLM-generated decompositions and the subsequent proofs can be translated into a formal language that can be verified by these theorem provers. This would not only enhance the reliability of the tool but also make it more accessible to the broader mathematical community. The authors could start by exploring existing tools and libraries that facilitate the export of proofs from Mathematica to formal proof languages or by developing custom scripts to translate the proof steps into a format that can be understood by Lean or Coq.

Finally, the authors should consider expanding the scope of O-Forge to handle a wider range of mathematical problems. While asymptotic inequalities are an important area of research, the tool could potentially be applied to other types of problems, such as series convergence or differential equations. This would increase the usefulness of O-Forge and make it a more valuable tool for the mathematical community. The authors should also explore the possibility of integrating O-Forge with other mathematical software packages, such as Mathematica or Maple, to provide a more seamless workflow for users. This would allow users to easily incorporate O-Forge into their existing research workflows and take advantage of its capabilities.
