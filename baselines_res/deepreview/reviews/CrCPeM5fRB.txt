## Summary:

This paper introduces the Signal Dice Similarity Coefficient (SDSC), a novel metric designed to enhance time-series self-supervised representation learning by focusing on local waveform consistency. The authors argue that traditional distance-based metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE) are inadequate for capturing the semantic structure of time-series data due to their sensitivity to amplitude variations and polarity, as well as their unbounded nature. SDSC, inspired by the Dice Similarity Coefficient used in image segmentation, quantifies structural agreement between temporal signals based on signed amplitude intersections, effectively capturing local waveform shapes rather than global temporal alignments. The authors propose using SDSC as a loss function by subtracting it from 1 and applying a differentiable approximation of the Heaviside step function, enabling its integration into neural network training. They also introduce a hybrid loss that combines SDSC with MSE to balance structural fidelity with amplitude accuracy. The core of the method lies in the definition of SDSC, which uses the minimum of the absolute values of the ground truth and reconstructed signals at each time point to calculate the intersection, and the sum of the absolute values to calculate the union. This approach is intended to capture the local structural similarity of waveforms, focusing on sign and magnitude overlap. The authors demonstrate the utility of SDSC through experiments on forecasting and classification tasks, using a self-supervised learning framework called SimMTM. They show that pre-training with SDSC-based losses achieves comparable or improved performance relative to MSE, particularly in in-domain and low-resource scenarios. The paper also explores the impact of a hybrid loss, which combines SDSC and MSE, and finds that this approach often provides the best balance between structural fidelity and amplitude accuracy. The authors provide a detailed analysis of SDSC, including its mathematical definition, properties, and a comparison with other metrics. They also discuss the computational complexity of SDSC, highlighting its linear complexity compared to the quadratic complexity of other metrics like Dynamic Time Warping (DTW). Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of structure-aware metrics like SDSC in time-series representation learning, suggesting that enforcing structural fidelity can enhance semantic representation quality and motivate a shift away from conventional distance-based losses. However, the paper also acknowledges the limitations of SDSC, particularly its intolerance to global shifts and warping, and its focus on local structural similarity rather than global temporal alignment. The authors' work is a significant step towards addressing the limitations of traditional metrics in time-series analysis and opens up new avenues for research in self-supervised representation learning.
