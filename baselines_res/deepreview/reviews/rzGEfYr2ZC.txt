## Summary:

This paper introduces SparseFW, a novel approach to pruning large language models (LLMs) that leverages the Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm. The core idea is to reformulate the mask selection problem, typically addressed through greedy heuristics, as a convex optimization problem over the convex hull of binary masks. This allows for a more nuanced consideration of weight interactions during pruning, moving beyond the limitations of local objective optimization. The authors argue that by solving this relaxed problem using the FW algorithm, they can achieve a more effective pruning mask, leading to improved performance in terms of perplexity and zero-shot accuracy. The method is designed to be memory-efficient and scalable, making it suitable for large-scale models. Empirically, the authors demonstrate that SparseFW outperforms state-of-the-art pruning methods like Wanda and RIA across various GPT architectures, including Qwen 2.5, LLaMA 3, Yi 1.5, and Gemma 2, particularly at higher sparsity levels. The paper provides theoretical justification for the convex formulation, showing that the relaxed solution, when combined with rounding, yields an approximate solution to the original combinatorial problem. The authors also highlight the practical efficiency of their method, noting that the FW algorithm is projection-free and naturally yields sparse updates. However, the method relies on a warm-start mask, typically obtained from existing methods like Wanda, and the paper acknowledges that the performance of SparseFW is sensitive to the quality of this initial mask. The experimental results are presented for both unstructured and semi-structured sparsity patterns, and the authors show that SparseFW reduces the per-layer pruning error compared to the baselines. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of LLM pruning by introducing a principled optimization-based approach that yields empirical improvements, while also acknowledging the limitations and areas for future work.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the application of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm to the LLM pruning problem is a novel and insightful contribution. By framing the mask selection problem as a convex optimization, the authors move beyond traditional greedy heuristics, offering a more principled and effective approach. This is a significant departure from existing methods that often rely on local approximations and ignore the complex interactions between weights. The theoretical justification provided for the convex formulation is another strength. The authors demonstrate that the relaxed solution obtained through the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, when combined with rounding, approximates the optimal solution to the original combinatorial problem. This theoretical foundation adds credibility to the empirical results and provides a solid basis for the proposed method. The empirical performance of SparseFW is also compelling. The method consistently outperforms existing methods like Wanda and RIA across multiple benchmarks and architectures. The improvements in perplexity and zero-shot accuracy are substantial, particularly at higher sparsity levels, showcasing the practical effectiveness of the approach. The fact that these improvements are observed across various GPT architectures further strengthens the generalizability of the method. Furthermore, the paper highlights the memory efficiency and scalability of SparseFW. The authors emphasize that the FW algorithm is projection-free and naturally yields sparse updates, which contributes to the method's memory efficiency. The precomputation of certain matrices also helps to reduce resource demands. The experimental results demonstrate the scalability of SparseFW to large models, indicating its potential for application to even larger LLMs. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide a clear explanation of their method and its theoretical justification, making it accessible to a wide audience. The experimental setup is also well-described, and the results are presented in a clear and concise manner. This makes it easy for other researchers to understand and reproduce the findings.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the proposed method is limited to layer-wise pruning. The paper explicitly states that the algorithm is applied "for each layer" and the experimental setup prunes "all linear layers with a uniform sparsity allocation across layers." This layer-wise approach ignores the interdependencies between layers, which can lead to suboptimal pruning decisions. A weight that appears unimportant when considering a single layer in isolation might be crucial for the overall network function when considering its impact on subsequent layers. This limitation is significant because it restricts the potential for achieving optimal sparsity patterns across the entire network. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the method description and experimental setup. Second, the method's performance is dependent on the quality of the warm-start mask. The paper explicitly states that SparseFW is "warm-started from Wanda or RIA." The results in Table 1 show performance differences between SparseFW (Wanda) and SparseFW (RIA), suggesting a sensitivity to the warm-start mask. This dependence raises concerns about the robustness of the method and its practical applicability when a high-quality warm-start mask is not readily available. The paper does not include a dedicated section or experiment analyzing the sensitivity to different warm-start masks beyond Wanda and RIA. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the true potential of the method and its limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the experimental setup and the observed performance variations. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method compared to existing methods. While the paper claims that SparseFW is memory-efficient and describes optimizations for computational efficiency, it does not provide a direct, quantitative comparison of the computational cost (time and memory) with the baseline methods. There is no explicit comparison of wall-clock time or detailed time complexity analysis against Wanda and RIA. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using SparseFW, particularly for large-scale models. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the absence of such comparisons in the experimental section. Fourth, the paper compares SparseFW to other methods that only consider the local objective. The paper contrasts its approach with "greedy heuristics that ignore the weight interactions in the pruning objective" and mentions that existing methods "estimate parameter importance on a small calibration set by minimizing a per-layer *local* pruning loss." The baselines used for comparison are Wanda and RIA, which are described as methods that "address the mask selection problem (*MASK SELECTION*) using a greedy pruning approach." The paper explicitly states, "We hence do not compare directly to methods that involve a reconstruction step, such as SparseGPT (Frantar & Alistarh, 2023)." This lack of comparison against methods optimizing a global objective, which often involves retraining, makes it difficult to assess the true effectiveness of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit focus on layer-wise methods and the stated reason for not comparing against methods involving reconstruction/retraining. Fifth, the paper's exploration of sparsity patterns is limited. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of SparseFW for both unstructured and semi-structured sparsity, it does not explore other sparsity patterns that might be beneficial for specific architectures or tasks. For instance, block-sparse patterns, which align with hardware acceleration capabilities, are not considered. This limits the generalizability of the findings and leaves open questions about the optimal sparsity structure for different scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit mention of the sparsity patterns explored and the absence of results for other patterns. Finally, while the paper argues that SparseFW considers weight interactions through the convex relaxation, it does not provide a specific quantitative analysis *isolating* the impact of weight interactions. The paper shows that SparseFW reduces the per-layer pruning error compared to Wanda and RIA, and also shows improved perplexity and zero-shot accuracy. However, there isn't a dedicated experiment or metric quantifying the *direct* impact of weight interactions. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the improved performance provides indirect evidence, but a direct analysis is missing.


## Suggestions:

Based on the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete suggestions for future work. First, the authors should explore methods that can optimize the pruning mask across multiple layers or the entire network, rather than being limited to layer-wise pruning. This could involve techniques like iterative layer-wise optimization with a global objective function or hierarchical pruning strategies. This would allow for a more holistic approach to pruning, potentially leading to better performance and sparsity trade-offs. For example, a method that considers the impact of pruning a weight on the subsequent layers could lead to more informed pruning decisions. Second, the authors should conduct a systematic study of how different warm-start masks affect the final performance of SparseFW. This could involve experimenting with masks obtained from different pruning methods or generated using various heuristics. The paper should also investigate the sensitivity of the method to the quality of the warm-start mask and provide guidelines for selecting an appropriate mask. For instance, the authors could analyze the convergence behavior of SparseFW with different warm-start masks and identify the characteristics of masks that lead to faster convergence and better performance. Additionally, the authors could explore strategies for generating a good warm-start mask, such as using a simple pruning method to obtain an initial mask and then refining it using SparseFW. This would make the method more robust and easier to use in practice. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of SparseFW, including a breakdown of the time complexity of each step of the algorithm and a comparison with existing methods. This analysis should include a comparison of the wall-clock time required to prune a model using SparseFW with that of other methods on the same hardware. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the memory requirements of the method and how they scale with the size of the model and the sparsity level. This would provide a more complete understanding of the practical implications of using SparseFW. Fourth, the authors should compare SparseFW against methods that consider the global objective, even if they require retraining. This is important because the pruning process inherently affects the global behavior of the network, and a method that only considers local objectives might be missing out on important global interactions. For example, methods that perform iterative pruning and retraining, or those that use a global metric to determine weight importance, could provide a more robust comparison. This would help to clarify whether the performance gains are due to the Frank-Wolfe algorithm itself or simply due to a better local approximation of the pruning problem. Fifth, the authors should explore other sparsity patterns beyond unstructured and semi-structured sparsity. This could involve adapting the Frank-Wolfe algorithm to handle different patterns, such as block sparsity, which can be more hardware-friendly and potentially offer better performance for specific tasks. This could involve modifying the Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) to accommodate block-sparse structures. Furthermore, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between different sparsity patterns in terms of performance, memory footprint, and computational cost. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed quantitative analysis of the impact of considering weight interactions. While the paper argues that SparseFW considers weight interactions through the convex relaxation, it would be beneficial to quantify this effect. For example, the authors could compute the correlation between the importance scores of weights and their neighboring weights, and show how SparseFW captures this correlation better than greedy methods. This could be done by visualizing the importance scores and their spatial distribution, and by computing metrics such as the average correlation between the importance scores of connected neurons. Furthermore, the authors could explore the impact of different types of weight interactions, such as those within the same layer or across different layers.
