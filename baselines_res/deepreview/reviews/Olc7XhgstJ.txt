## Summary:

This paper introduces Steady Thought (ST), a novel framework designed to mitigate the issue of under-thinking in Large Language Models (LLMs). The authors identify that LLMs often fail to fully develop promising reasoning trajectories, leading to suboptimal performance. The ST framework addresses this through a three-stage process: Thought Segmentation, Thought Completion, and Fine-Grained Preference Optimization. In the Thought Segmentation stage, the model's response is divided into distinct thought sequences based on an entropy threshold, aiming to identify points where the model might reconsider its reasoning. The Thought Completion stage then encourages the model to elaborate on each identified thought without switching to other trajectories, effectively promoting a more focused and in-depth exploration of each idea. Finally, the Fine-Grained Preference Optimization stage reinforces the model's preference for completing thoughts over switching, using a preference learning approach to fine-tune the model's behavior. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of ST through experiments on multiple models and datasets, showing that it reduces unnecessary thought switching, improves accuracy, and leads to more efficient reasoning. The core contribution of this work lies in its structured approach to addressing under-thinking, moving beyond simple suppression of switching to a more nuanced method of encouraging commitment to promising reasoning paths. The empirical results presented in the paper provide compelling evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed framework, highlighting its potential to enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Overall, this paper presents a significant step forward in addressing a critical challenge in the field of LLMs, offering a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to improving the quality of reasoning in these models.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its novel approach to addressing the under-thinking problem in LLMs. The proposed Steady Thought (ST) framework is a significant contribution, offering a structured method to guide models to commit to promising thoughts. The three-stage framework, consisting of Thought Segmentation, Thought Completion, and Fine-Grained Preference Optimization, is well-motivated and logically sound. The Thought Segmentation stage, which uses an entropy threshold to identify potential thought switches, provides a clear mechanism for dividing the model's response into distinct reasoning trajectories. The Thought Completion stage, which suppresses the selection of trigger words like 'wait' and 'alternatively', effectively encourages the model to elaborate on each thought without premature switching. Finally, the Fine-Grained Preference Optimization stage, which reinforces the preference for completing thoughts, provides a mechanism for fine-tuning the model's behavior. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, demonstrating the effectiveness of ST across multiple models and datasets. The authors show that ST reduces unnecessary thought switching, leading to more focused reasoning and improved performance. The quantitative comparisons with existing methods, such as NoThink, NOWAIT, and SEAL, further highlight the advantages of the proposed approach. The ablation study on different entropy thresholds also provides valuable insights into the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices. The paper is well-written and clearly explains the proposed method and the experimental setup. The authors provide a thorough analysis of the under-thinking phenomenon and formalize it as a preference optimization problem, which is a significant contribution in itself. The combination of a novel methodological approach, strong empirical results, and clear presentation makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of LLMs.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to mitigating under-thinking, several limitations warrant careful consideration. Firstly, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed ST framework, particularly concerning its performance on complex reasoning tasks. As identified by multiple reviewers, the paper does not adequately address how the method handles scenarios where the initial thought is incorrect or leads to a dead end. The core mechanism of ST, which involves suppressing thought switching, could potentially hinder the model's ability to explore alternative solutions in such cases. The paper's focus on encouraging commitment to promising thoughts, while beneficial in many cases, might lead to suboptimal performance when the initial reasoning path is flawed. This limitation is significant because real-world reasoning tasks often require the exploration of multiple solutions and the ability to recover from incorrect assumptions. The absence of a discussion on these failure cases and the lack of experiments designed to test the method's robustness in such scenarios is a notable weakness. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is supported by the method description itself, which emphasizes the suppression of switching, and the lack of any discussion or experiments addressing this issue. Secondly, the paper's reliance on an entropy threshold for thought segmentation, while effective, lacks a thorough analysis of its sensitivity and potential drawbacks. While the authors do include an ablation study on different entropy thresholds, the discussion on how this threshold is determined and whether it is sensitive to different models or datasets is limited. The paper mentions that the optimal threshold is determined through hyperparameter tuning, but it does not provide a detailed explanation of this process or offer guidance on how to select appropriate thresholds for different scenarios. Furthermore, the paper does not fully address the potential for the entropy threshold to be influenced by factors other than actual thought switches, such as variations in the model's confidence or the complexity of the reasoning step. This limitation is important because the accuracy of the thought segmentation stage directly impacts the effectiveness of the subsequent stages. If the segmentation is inaccurate, the model might be encouraged to complete flawed thoughts or miss opportunities to explore more promising alternatives. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper acknowledges the use of an entropy threshold but does not provide sufficient detail on its selection and robustness. Finally, while the paper does compare the performance of ST with existing methods like NoThink, NOWAIT, and SEAL, the comparison lacks a detailed discussion of the computational cost and implementation complexity of each method. The paper primarily focuses on quantitative performance metrics, such as accuracy and token reduction, but does not delve into the practical considerations of deploying these methods. A more comprehensive comparison should include an analysis of the computational overhead of the ST framework, particularly the fine-grained preference optimization stage, and compare it to the costs associated with other approaches. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the limitations of the ST framework, particularly concerning its performance on complex reasoning tasks and its ability to handle incorrect initial thoughts. This could involve designing experiments that specifically test the method's robustness in scenarios where the initial thought is incorrect or leads to a dead end. The authors could also explore adaptive strategies that allow for more flexibility in thought switching based on the context of the reasoning process. For instance, the model could be allowed to switch thoughts more freely in the initial stages of reasoning, where exploration is crucial, and then gradually suppress switching as the model converges towards a solution. This could be achieved by incorporating a dynamic threshold for thought switching, which is adjusted based on the model's confidence or the number of previous switches. Additionally, the authors could explore techniques that allow the model to revisit previous thoughts, enabling it to backtrack and explore alternative paths if the current thought leads to a dead end. This would make the method more robust and adaptable to various reasoning scenarios. Secondly, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the entropy threshold used for thought segmentation. This should include a more thorough explanation of how the threshold is determined, as well as an analysis of its sensitivity to different models and datasets. The authors could explore techniques that combine entropy with other metrics, such as the semantic similarity between consecutive reasoning steps or the presence of specific keywords that indicate a change in reasoning direction. For example, a significant drop in semantic similarity between two consecutive segments could indicate a thought switch, while the appearance of words like 'however,' 'alternatively,' or 'instead' could also signal a shift in the reasoning process. Furthermore, the authors could explore using a learned model to predict thought switches, which could potentially be more accurate and robust than a fixed entropy threshold. This model could be trained on a dataset of human-annotated reasoning chains, learning to identify the subtle patterns that indicate a change in thought. This would make the segmentation process more reliable and less dependent on a single hyperparameter. Finally, the authors should provide a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods for addressing under-thinking, including a discussion of the computational cost and implementation complexity of each method. This comparison should not only focus on performance metrics but also consider the practical implications of deploying each method. For example, some methods might rely on handcrafted rules or specific keywords, which could be less generalizable than the proposed ST framework. However, these methods might also be less computationally expensive and easier to implement. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their method in comparison to these alternatives, providing a more balanced view of the trade-offs involved. This would help the readers better understand the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method and its applicability to different scenarios. Furthermore, the authors should consider including a comparison with methods that use reinforcement learning to train models to reason, as these methods might offer a different approach to mitigating under-thinking.
