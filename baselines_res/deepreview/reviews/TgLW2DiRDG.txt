## Summary:

This paper delves into the geometric structure of polyhedral complexes formed by the activation regions of fully-connected ReLU neural networks. The authors present novel theoretical results, including upper and lower bounds for the average degree and diameter of the connectivity graph of these complexes. The average degree is shown to be upper bounded by $2d$, where $d$ is the input dimension, and the diameter is upper bounded by $(m+1)^	ext{	extell}$, where $m$ is the maximum layer width and $	ext{	extell}$ is the number of hidden layers. The paper also introduces an algorithm for calculating the boundaries of these polyhedra, which is based on a breadth-first search (BFS) approach. Empirical experiments are conducted to validate the theoretical findings, demonstrating that the derived bounds hold in practice. The significance of this work lies in its contribution to understanding the geometric and topological properties of ReLU networks, which can have implications for various aspects of neural network behavior, including explainability, expressivity, and robustness. However, the paper's focus on fully-connected networks and the practical limitations of the proposed algorithm raise important questions about the broader applicability and impact of the results.

## Weaknesses:

The theoretical results and the proposed algorithm are derived based on the specific structure of fully-connected layers, and the paper does not address how these findings might generalize to other types of neural networks, such as convolutional or residual networks. This is a critical issue because modern neural architectures often incorporate convolutional layers, skip connections, and other non-standard components. The lack of discussion on the applicability of the results to these architectures limits the paper's scope and impact. For instance, the definition of the connectivity graph and the method for calculating polyhedron boundaries might need significant modifications to accommodate the unique characteristics of convolutional and residual networks. The paper should explicitly acknowledge this limitation and suggest potential directions for future research to extend the analysis to a broader range of network types (e.g., Section 2, where the network structure is defined, and Section 4, where the algorithm is presented). My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper's content is heavily centered on fully-connected networks without any indication of generalizability.

Second, the proposed algorithm for calculating polyhedron boundaries has exponential time complexity, which poses a significant practical limitation. However, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the algorithm's runtime with respect to the input dimension, number of neurons, or depth of the network. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the algorithm, especially for networks with moderate to high dimensionality. The exponential complexity is a well-known issue in computational geometry, and the paper's failure to address it thoroughly is a valid concern. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the need to terminate the search early in experiments clearly indicates the algorithm's impracticality for large-scale networks.
