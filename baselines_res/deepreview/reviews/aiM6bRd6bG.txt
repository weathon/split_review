## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for prioritizing protein-protein interaction (PPI) candidates for experimental validation, a critical step in advancing our understanding of biological systems. The authors address the challenge of identifying true interactions from a vast space of possibilities by framing the problem as a ranking task, rather than a simple binary classification. Their approach leverages the interpretability of existing PPI prediction models, specifically D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy, to guide the ranking process. They achieve this by analyzing the embedding spaces of these models to identify regions that are indicative of known interactions. This interpretability-guided ranking is then refined through a re-ranking strategy that incorporates diverse biological signals, including interaction scores, structural plausibility derived from AlphaFold2 predictions, and biomedical language features extracted from protein annotations. The core idea is that by focusing on the embedding regions that are most relevant to known interactions, and then further refining these rankings with complementary evidence, they can more effectively prioritize true interactions for experimental testing. The authors evaluate their method on a large-scale dataset constructed from successive releases of the STRING database, demonstrating significant improvements over existing state-of-the-art PPI prediction models. Their results show that the proposed framework not only improves the accuracy of PPI predictions but also provides more biologically coherent rankings, which is crucial for guiding experimental design. The authors demonstrate that their approach is particularly effective at identifying true interactions within the top ranks, which is essential for experimental validation where resources are limited. The paper's contribution lies in its novel problem formulation, its use of interpretability to guide ranking, and its integration of diverse biological signals to improve the prioritization of PPI candidates. The authors' work provides a practical tool for researchers aiming to discover new protein-protein interactions, potentially accelerating the pace of biological discovery.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the authors' framing of the PPI prediction problem as a ranking task, rather than a binary classification, is a significant contribution. This shift in perspective directly addresses the practical need for prioritizing interaction candidates for experimental testing, which is a crucial step in the discovery process. By focusing on ranking, the authors are directly addressing the needs of experimentalists who require a prioritized list of interactions rather than a simple yes/no prediction. Second, the use of interpretability-guided ranking is a novel and effective approach. By analyzing the embedding spaces of D-SCRIPT and Topsy-Turvy, the authors are able to identify regions that are most indicative of known interactions. This allows them to leverage the knowledge captured by these models in a more targeted way, rather than simply using their overall prediction scores. This approach is particularly innovative because it uses the internal representations of deep learning models to guide the ranking process, which is a relatively unexplored area in the field of PPI prediction. Third, the integration of diverse biological signals in the re-ranking strategy is a strength. The authors combine interaction scores, structural plausibility, and biomedical language features to refine the initial rankings. This multi-faceted approach allows them to capture different aspects of PPIs, leading to more accurate and biologically coherent results. The use of structural plausibility, derived from AlphaFold2 predictions, is particularly noteworthy as it incorporates valuable structural information into the ranking process. Finally, the large-scale evaluation of the method on successive releases of the STRING database demonstrates the practical applicability and scalability of the approach. The authors show that their method significantly improves the ranking of PPI candidates compared to existing models, providing more accurate and biologically coherent rankings. The fact that the method is evaluated on a large-scale dataset makes the results more convincing and demonstrates the potential of the method to be used in real-world scenarios. The authors also provide a comprehensive set of metrics, including recall, precision, and average precision, which allows for a thorough assessment of the method's performance. Overall, the combination of a novel problem formulation, an innovative use of interpretability, and a multi-faceted re-ranking strategy makes this a strong contribution to the field of PPI prediction.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the method's reliance on known interactions for ranking is a significant limitation. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the framework is designed to leverage the embedding spaces of existing PPI prediction models, which are trained on known interactions. This means that the method's performance is inherently tied to the availability and quality of known interaction data. Specifically, the 'Problem Setup' section states, 'Given  $p$  and its known partners  $KP(p)$ , we construct a ranking  $R_p$  over the candidate set  $P \setminus KP(p)$ , where candidates are ordered by the model’s predicted likelihood of interacting with  $p$.' This clearly indicates that the method requires known partners to function. This reliance creates a bias towards proteins with well-characterized interaction partners, potentially hindering the discovery of interactions involving less-studied proteins. This is particularly concerning for novel or less well-characterized proteins, where the method may not be able to effectively leverage the interpretability-guided ranking. The method's performance on such proteins is likely to be significantly lower than on well-studied proteins, which could limit its applicability in exploring new areas of the interactome. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is directly supported by the method's description and the inherent nature of the approach. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed approach. While the authors mention that SpeedPPI is an accelerated pipeline, the actual computational cost of the re-ranking process, especially the structure-based methods, is not thoroughly discussed. The 'Experiments' section mentions runtimes, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each step of the pipeline. This lack of information makes it difficult for researchers to assess the feasibility of using the method on their own datasets, particularly for large-scale applications. The computational cost of the structure-based methods, such as those using AlphaFold2, can be significant, and the paper does not provide sufficient information to determine the scalability of the method. This is a critical limitation, as the computational cost can be a major barrier for researchers with limited computational resources. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper does not provide any detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the method. Finally, the evaluation of the method is primarily based on the STRING database, which may introduce biases. While the STRING database is comprehensive, it is known to contain biases due to its reliance on experimental data and text mining. The authors use a hold-out set from the STRING database, which is a common approach, but this does not fully address the potential for biases. The test set may still contain biases present in the training set, which could lead to an overestimation of the method's performance. A more robust validation would involve using an independent dataset of experimentally validated interactions that were not included in the STRING database. This would provide a more objective assessment of the method's ability to predict novel interactions. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper explicitly states that the evaluation is based on the STRING database and does not provide any evidence of validation on independent datasets.
