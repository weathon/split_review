## Summary:

This paper introduces ShadowFM, a novel approach for learning the ground states of quantum many-body systems by leveraging geometric considerations within a flow matching framework. The core idea is to model the distribution of classical shadows of a target quantum state, specifically the ground state, by incorporating geometric structures into the generative process. The authors propose two distinct methods: a Riemannian-based approach that utilizes the geometry of the Bloch sphere to guide the flow, and an anisotropic probability path-based method that respects the asymmetric structure of shadows. The Riemannian approach, termed Spherical Flow, adapts the flow matching process to the spherical geometry of single-qubit quantum states, aiming to better capture the underlying structure of the data. The anisotropic probability path-based method, named Anisotropic Dirichlet Flow, introduces an anisotropic drift term to the probability path, which is designed to respect the inherent (target, anti-target) pairing structure of shadows. The authors evaluate their methods on two paradigmatic quantum many-body models: the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) and the Heisenberg model. The empirical results demonstrate that ShadowFM outperforms various classical machine learning models and non-autoregressive baselines in predicting observables such as correlation functions and entanglement entropy. Notably, the proposed methods exhibit improved accuracy in predicting these observables, especially in extrapolating to unseen quantum states. The paper's contribution lies in its innovative application of geometric flow matching to the problem of learning quantum many-body systems, providing a new perspective on how to effectively model the distribution of classical shadows. The authors argue that their approach is the first to explicitly account for the geometry of shadows and leverage geometric generative modeling for learning quantum states, although this claim requires further scrutiny. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically supported approach that advances the field of quantum machine learning by introducing a novel geometric perspective on learning quantum many-body systems.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The core strength lies in the innovative application of geometric flow matching to the problem of learning quantum many-body systems. The authors' motivation for incorporating geometric structure into the generative modeling of classical shadows is clearly articulated and well-supported. The idea of adapting flow matching to the spherical geometry of the Bloch sphere, as implemented in the Spherical Flow method, is both novel and theoretically sound. This approach leverages the inherent geometric structure of single-qubit quantum states, which is a significant departure from traditional Euclidean-based methods. Similarly, the Anisotropic Dirichlet Flow method, with its anisotropic drift term, is a clever way to respect the asymmetric structure of shadows, further enhancing the method's ability to capture the underlying physics. The paper is also well-written and organized, making it easy to follow the technical details of the proposed methods. The mathematical formulations are clearly presented, and the algorithmic descriptions are detailed enough to allow for reproducibility. The experimental results are comprehensive, covering multiple quantum many-body systems and observables. The authors demonstrate that ShadowFM consistently outperforms various classical machine learning models and non-autoregressive baselines, which provides strong empirical support for their approach. The results on both the transverse-field Ising model and the Heisenberg model are compelling, showcasing the method's effectiveness in predicting observables and its potential for extrapolating to unseen quantum states. The inclusion of figures and tables further enhances the clarity of the presentation, making it easy to understand the performance of the proposed methods. The authors also provide a clear explanation of the experimental setup, including the specific choices of hyperparameters and the details of the training process. The paper's overall contribution is significant, as it introduces a novel geometric perspective on learning quantum many-body systems, which has the potential to advance the field of quantum machine learning. The authors have successfully demonstrated the effectiveness of their approach, and the results are promising for future applications.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A recurring theme across multiple reviews is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed methods. While the paper presents empirical results demonstrating the accuracy of ShadowFM, it does not provide a thorough comparison of the computational cost with existing approaches. Specifically, the paper lacks a detailed breakdown of the computational cost associated with the Riemannian-based (Spherical Flow) and anisotropic probability path-based (Anisotropic Dirichlet Flow) methods. This includes the number of parameters in the model, the time required for training, the time required for inference, and the memory usage. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss how these costs scale with system size, such as the number of qubits. This is a significant omission, as it makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of ShadowFM, especially for larger quantum systems. The absence of this analysis is evident throughout the paper, with no explicit discussion or experimental data on the computational cost of ShadowFM compared to baselines. This is a critical weakness, as it limits the practical impact of the work. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the conclusion briefly mentions future work regarding more complex systems, the paper does not delve into the challenges of scaling to larger systems, the impact of hyperparameter choices, or the applicability to different quantum states and observables in detail. The paper does not explicitly discuss the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, batch size, and the architecture of the neural network used for the velocity field. A sensitivity analysis that shows how the performance of ShadowFM varies with different hyperparameter settings is missing. Furthermore, the paper does not address how the geometric considerations might break down or become less effective in systems with long-range interactions or strong correlations. The absence of a detailed theoretical analysis is a weakness, as it limits the understanding of the method's behavior and its potential for further development. Finally, the paper does not explicitly clarify whether the method is applicable to full quantum state tomography or is limited to learning ground states. The focus is on learning the distribution of shadows of ground states, not full quantum state tomography.
