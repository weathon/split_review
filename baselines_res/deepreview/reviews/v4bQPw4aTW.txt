## Summary:

This paper introduces AdaBoN, a novel prompt-adaptive approach to Best-of-N (BoN) alignment for language models. The core idea is to optimize inference-time compute allocation by dynamically adjusting the number of samples generated per prompt, rather than using a fixed budget. The authors observe that different prompts may require varying levels of computational effort to achieve high-reward responses, and thus propose a two-stage algorithm. In the first stage, a small exploration budget is used to estimate the reward distribution for each prompt. This involves generating a few samples and using Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE) to approximate the reward distribution. In the second stage, the remaining budget is adaptively allocated based on these estimated reward distributions, using a greedy algorithm to maximize the expected reward. The method aims to improve the efficiency of BoN sampling by concentrating computational resources on prompts that are likely to yield high-reward outputs. The authors evaluate AdaBoN across 12 language model-reward model (LM-RM) pairs and 50 different batches of prompts, demonstrating that it outperforms uniform allocation strategies and remains competitive against larger inference budgets. They also introduce new evaluation metrics, such as Batch Win Rate (BWR) and Expected Survival Time (EST), to provide a more nuanced assessment of the method's performance. The paper provides a theoretical justification for the greedy allocation in the second stage, showing that it is optimal under certain conditions. The authors emphasize the practical benefits of their approach, highlighting its simplicity, minimal hyperparameter tuning requirements, and low latency. Overall, the paper presents a compelling method for improving the efficiency of inference-time alignment, with a focus on adapting to the varying difficulty of different prompts. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to further strengthen the paper's contributions.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper tackles a practical and relevant problem in language model alignment: the inefficient allocation of computational resources in Best-of-N sampling. The core idea of adapting the sampling budget to the difficulty of individual prompts is both intuitive and well-motivated. The proposed two-stage algorithm is simple and elegant, making it easy to understand and implement. The use of Gaussian kernel density estimation to approximate reward distributions, while not without its limitations, is a reasonable approach given the observed smoothness of the reward distributions across the tested LM-RM pairs. The paper's empirical evaluation is also a significant strength. The authors conduct a comprehensive set of experiments across 12 LM-RM pairs and 50 different batches of prompts, demonstrating the robustness and effectiveness of AdaBoN. The results consistently show that AdaBoN outperforms uniform allocation strategies and remains competitive against larger inference budgets. The introduction of new evaluation metrics, such as Batch Win Rate (BWR) and Expected Survival Time (EST), provides a more nuanced assessment of the method's performance, going beyond simple average reward comparisons. Furthermore, the paper provides a theoretical justification for the greedy allocation in the second stage, showing that it is optimal under certain conditions. This theoretical grounding adds credibility to the proposed method. Finally, the authors emphasize the practical benefits of their approach, highlighting its simplicity, minimal hyperparameter tuning requirements, and low latency. The fact that AdaBoN requires only a single hyperparameter (the exploration budget) and minimizes latency makes it a compelling solution for real-world applications. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the proposed method and experimental setup. The authors provide a thorough discussion of the limitations of their approach and potential directions for future research, demonstrating a balanced and critical perspective.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper assumes a fixed total budget for each prompt, which may not be realistic in many real-world scenarios. As the first reviewer pointed out, the budget is often determined by the requirements of the task, and a uniform budget may be impractical when tasks vary significantly in difficulty. The paper does not address how AdaBoN would perform if the budget were not fixed but instead determined by the complexity of the prompt or the desired level of performance. This is a critical limitation, as in practice, some prompts may require significantly more computation than others to achieve satisfactory results. The paper's formulation and experiments are based on a fixed per-prompt budget, and this assumption limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the novelty of the two-stage approach is somewhat limited. As the first reviewer also noted, the paradigm of exploration followed by exploitation is a well-known concept in reinforcement learning and resource allocation. While the application to the Best-of-N problem is interesting, the core algorithmic idea is not particularly novel. The paper does not provide a detailed discussion of how the proposed method differs from existing approaches in reinforcement learning and resource allocation, and what specific technical challenges were overcome in adapting these ideas to the Best-of-N problem. This lack of a detailed comparison makes it difficult to assess the unique contributions of the paper. Third, the paper lacks a strong theoretical justification for the two-stage policy compared to a pure exploitation approach. While the authors provide a theoretical analysis of the greedy algorithm in the second stage, they do not adequately explain why a two-stage approach is superior to directly allocating resources based on predicted reward. The paper should include a theoretical analysis comparing the two-stage approach to a pure exploitation strategy, demonstrating the conditions under which the two-stage approach is expected to perform better. This analysis should consider the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, and provide insights into the optimal allocation of resources in the exploration phase. The paper motivates the two-stage approach by latency concerns, but a theoretical analysis of the benefits of the exploratory phase would strengthen the paper. Fourth, the method relies on Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE) for reward distribution estimation, which may not be suitable for all reward models or distributions. As the second and third reviewers pointed out, the paper acknowledges that the reward distributions are mostly smooth, but it does not fully address scenarios with highly non-Gaussian distributions. The reliance on KDE can be problematic for complex distributions, potentially leading to inaccurate reward estimates and suboptimal allocation of the sampling budget. The paper does not explore alternative density estimation techniques, such as mixture models or empirical distributions, and does not provide a sensitivity analysis of the method's performance with respect to different bandwidth values for the KDE. Fifth, the two-stage procedure does not dynamically refine its estimates during allocation. As the second and third reviewers noted, the allocation in the second stage is based on the initial estimates from the first stage, without further refinement. This could lead to suboptimal performance, especially when the initial reward distribution estimates are inaccurate. The paper does not explore the potential benefits of dynamically updating the reward distribution estimates as more samples are generated. Sixth, the method assumes access to a batch of prompts, which may not be available in all applications. As the second reviewer pointed out, the paper does not discuss how the method could be adapted for online or single-prompt scenarios. This limits the applicability of the method in scenarios where prompts arrive sequentially. Seventh, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of the proposed method compared to simpler alternatives. While the authors mention that the method is efficient, a quantitative comparison of the runtime and memory usage with uniform BoN sampling is missing. This makes it difficult to assess the practical trade-offs between the improved performance and the increased computational cost. The paper lacks a breakdown of the time spent on each stage of the algorithm (exploration, estimation, allocation). Finally, the paper does not fully explore the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters. While the paper includes an ablation study on the exploration budget, it does not explicitly analyze the sensitivity to the KDE bandwidth parameter, relying on automatic selection using Scott's rule. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to strengthen the paper's contributions.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should investigate the performance of AdaBoN under varying budget constraints. Specifically, the authors should conduct experiments where the budget is not fixed but instead determined by the complexity of the prompt or the desired level of performance. This could involve varying the budget based on some measure of prompt difficulty or adjusting the budget to achieve a specific target performance level. The results of these experiments would provide valuable insights into the practical applicability of the proposed method and its robustness to different budget constraints. Furthermore, the paper should include a discussion of the limitations of the fixed-budget assumption and its potential impact on the conclusions. Second, to address the limited novelty, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of their method to existing approaches in reinforcement learning and resource allocation. This comparison should highlight the specific technical challenges that were overcome in adapting these ideas to the Best-of-N problem. For example, the authors could discuss how their method differs from existing bandit algorithms or reinforcement learning approaches that also involve exploration and exploitation. This discussion should also include a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method compared to these alternatives. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the specific technical contributions of their work, emphasizing the novel aspects of their approach and its potential impact on the field. Third, the paper needs a more robust theoretical justification for the two-stage approach. The authors should provide a theoretical analysis comparing the two-stage approach to a pure exploitation strategy, demonstrating the conditions under which the two-stage approach is expected to perform better. This analysis should consider the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, and provide insights into the optimal allocation of resources in the exploration phase. The analysis should also consider the impact of the exploration budget on the overall performance of the method. This theoretical analysis should be complemented by empirical results that demonstrate the practical benefits of the two-stage approach compared to a pure exploitation strategy, under various conditions. Fourth, the paper's reliance on Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE) for reward distribution estimation is a potential limitation. The authors should consider exploring alternative density estimation techniques, such as mixture models or empirical distributions, and provide a comparative analysis of their performance. Furthermore, the paper should include a sensitivity analysis of the method's performance with respect to different bandwidth values for the Gaussian kernel and provide guidance on how to select an appropriate bandwidth for different reward distributions. It would also be beneficial to investigate the impact of using a fixed bandwidth across all prompts versus adapting the bandwidth based on the characteristics of each prompt's reward distribution. Fifth, the two-stage procedure, while simple, does not dynamically refine its estimates during the allocation process. The authors should consider incorporating a mechanism for dynamically updating the reward distribution estimates as more samples are generated. For example, a Bayesian approach could be used to update the reward distribution parameters based on the observed rewards. This would allow the method to adapt to changes in the reward distribution and potentially improve the allocation of resources. Additionally, the paper should explore the trade-off between the computational cost of updating the reward distribution estimates and the potential improvement in performance. It would also be useful to investigate the impact of different update frequencies on the overall performance of the method. Sixth, the assumption that the method has access to a batch of prompts limits its applicability in scenarios where prompts arrive sequentially. The authors should explore how the method can be adapted to handle online or streaming settings. One possible approach is to use a sliding window of prompts and update the reward distribution estimates based on the prompts within the window. Another approach is to use a meta-learning technique to learn a general allocation strategy that can be applied to new prompts. The paper should also investigate the impact of the batch size on the performance of the method and provide guidance on how to choose an appropriate batch size for different applications. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the limitations of the method in scenarios where the prompts are highly diverse or have very different reward distributions. Seventh, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead of the proposed method. This should include a quantitative comparison of the runtime and memory usage with uniform BoN sampling, as well as a breakdown of the time spent on each stage of the algorithm. The authors should also explore the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the exploration budget and the bandwidth parameter for the KDE. This could involve conducting a grid search over a range of hyperparameter values and reporting the performance of the method for each combination. This would provide a more complete picture of the method's performance and help to identify the optimal hyperparameter settings for different scenarios. The paper should also include a discussion on the practical implications of the computational overhead, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Finally, the paper should include a more thorough investigation into the assumptions underlying the proposed method. Specifically, the assumption of smooth reward distributions should be rigorously justified, and the paper should explore how the method performs with more complex reward landscapes. The authors could consider using alternative reward estimation techniques that are less sensitive to the smoothness assumption, such as Gaussian process regression or other non-parametric methods. Additionally, the paper should include a discussion on the limitations of the method when applied to highly multimodal or discontinuous reward functions. It would also be beneficial to provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method, particularly in relation to the choice of exploration budget and the bandwidth parameter for the KDE. These suggestions, which are directly linked to the identified weaknesses, would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its contributions to the field.
