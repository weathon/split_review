## Summary:

This paper introduces AetherCode, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the coding and reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) in the context of competitive programming. The authors argue that existing benchmarks often overstate LLM proficiency due to limitations in problem difficulty and evaluation bias stemming from low-quality test cases. To address these shortcomings, AetherCode draws problems from premier programming competitions such as the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI) and the International Collegiate Programming Contest (ICPC), ensuring a higher level of difficulty and broader coverage. The benchmark categorizes problems by difficulty (Easy, Medium, Hard, Extreme) and algorithmic domains, providing a structured framework for evaluation. A key innovation of AetherCode lies in its rigorous test case construction methodology, which combines automated generation with expert annotation. This hybrid approach aims to mitigate evaluation bias and ensure the reliability of the benchmark. The authors report achieving 100% True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) on their collected solution set, indicating the high quality of their test cases. The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of several reasoning and non-reasoning models on AetherCode, revealing a significant performance gap between these model types and highlighting the challenges LLMs still face in complex algorithmic problem-solving. The analysis includes a breakdown of performance across different problem categories and difficulty levels, providing valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of current LLMs. The authors also analyze the reasons behind model failures, categorizing them into four types: Wrong Answer, Time Limit Exceeded, Runtime Error, and Compile Error. This detailed analysis helps to pinpoint common issues that models encounter when tackling complex programming problems. Overall, AetherCode represents a significant step forward in the evaluation of LLMs for competitive programming, offering a more challenging and reliable benchmark than existing alternatives. The paper's findings underscore the need for further research to enhance the reasoning and coding abilities of LLMs in this domain.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a more comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art models, such as o1-mini, across various problem categories and difficulty levels. This would involve not only reporting overall performance metrics but also providing detailed breakdowns of results for each category and difficulty level. Such an analysis would allow researchers to better understand the specific strengths and weaknesses of different models and identify areas where further improvements are needed. For example, if a model performs well on easy problems but struggles with hard ones, this would indicate a limitation in its ability to handle complex reasoning. Similarly, if a model excels in dynamic programming but fails in graph algorithms, this would highlight a specific area for improvement. This level of detail is crucial for the benchmark to be truly informative and useful for the research community. Second, the authors should provide a quantitative comparison of problem difficulty with existing benchmarks. This could involve analyzing the distribution of problem scores, the number of participants who solved each problem in the original competitions, or other objective metrics. The authors should also consider using a standardized difficulty metric, such as the average time taken by human participants to solve the problems, to provide a more concrete comparison. A clear and quantitative demonstration of the increased difficulty is essential for establishing the value of AetherCode as a more challenging benchmark for evaluating the reasoning abilities of LLMs. Third, the authors need to provide a clear explanation for why a large portion of the collected problems were excluded from the benchmark. The selection criteria should be clearly defined, and the reasons for excluding problems based on difficulty or other factors should be justified. If the excluded problems are indeed too easy, the authors should provide a quantitative measure of what constitutes 'too easy' in the context of AetherCode. This could involve analyzing the distribution of problem difficulties and setting a threshold for inclusion. Additionally, the authors should consider releasing the excluded problems separately, as they might be useful for other purposes, such as warm-up tasks or for evaluating models on simpler problems. The lack of transparency in the selection process raises concerns about potential biases in the benchmark. Finally, to address the issue of potential data contamination, the authors should implement a more rigorous decontamination process. This could involve analyzing the specific problems included in the benchmark and comparing them to the training datasets of the evaluated models. If a model has been trained on any of the problems included in AetherCode, those problems should be excluded from the evaluation set. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the potential impact of contamination on the results, including a discussion of how the performance of models trained on overlapping data might be skewed. This analysis should also consider the possibility of models being trained on solutions to the problems, not just the problem statements themselves. A clear protocol for identifying and mitigating data contamination is crucial for ensuring the reliability of the benchmark.
