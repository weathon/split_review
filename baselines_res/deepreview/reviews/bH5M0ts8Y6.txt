## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to in-context image editing by training a model directly on video data, a significant departure from traditional methods that rely on paired image data. The authors propose a scalable data construction pipeline that transforms videos into contextual multimodal sequences, comprising sparsely sampled frames, textual visual transition descriptions, and segmentation masks of regions of interest. This pipeline leverages the vast amount of video data available on the web, offering a practical solution for training image editing models. The core of the method involves designing three proxy tasks: next-image prediction, current segmentation prediction, and next-segmentation prediction, to model this multimodal sequence using a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) model. These proxy tasks are designed to capture the contextual dependencies inherent in multi-turn editing scenarios. The paper also introduces a new multi-turn image editing benchmark, MSE-Bench, to advance research in this area. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model exhibits strong in-context image editing capabilities and achieves state-of-the-art results on two multi-turn editing benchmarks, MagicBrush and MSE-Bench. Furthermore, the model showcases emerging abilities such as controllable editing, multi-concept composition, story generation, and multimodal chain-of-thought, which were not explicitly part of the training objectives. These emergent abilities highlight the untapped potential of video data and the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The authors emphasize that their approach learns directly from native video data, without relying on task-specific pipelines or expert models, which is a significant contribution to the field. The paper's findings suggest that video data can be a valuable resource for training image editing models, offering a scalable and effective alternative to traditional paired image data. The proposed method not only achieves strong performance on established benchmarks but also demonstrates a capacity for generalization and emergent abilities that were not explicitly trained for, indicating a promising direction for future research in image editing.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most significant is the novel approach of training an in-context image editing model directly from video data. This is a creative and original solution to the challenge of acquiring contextualized training data, and it leverages the rich, dynamic information present in videos to learn multi-turn interactions. This approach is a fresh perspective in the field of image editing, moving away from the reliance on paired image data. The proposed data construction pipeline is another notable strength. It is scalable and leverages the vast amount of video data available on the web, making it a practical solution for training image editing models. The design of the three proxy tasks—next-image prediction, current segmentation prediction, and next-segmentation prediction—is innovative and effectively captures the contextual dependencies in multi-turn editing scenarios. These tasks are well-integrated into the methodology and contribute to the model's ability to perform complex editing tasks. The introduction of the MSE-Bench benchmark is a valuable contribution to the field, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of multi-turn image editing capabilities. This benchmark, along with the evaluation on MagicBrush, provides a solid foundation for assessing the performance of the proposed model. The experimental results are extensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks. The model's ability to achieve such strong performance without explicit training on paired image data is a testament to the robustness of the approach. Furthermore, the model showcases emerging abilities such as controllable editing, multi-concept composition, story generation, and multimodal chain-of-thought, which were not explicitly part of the training objectives. These emergent abilities highlight the untapped potential of video data and the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The fact that these capabilities arise implicitly suggests that the model is learning a more general understanding of image editing, rather than simply memorizing specific editing operations. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and logically coherent methodology, with clear explanations and well-integrated components. The combination of a novel training approach, a scalable data pipeline, and strong empirical results makes this a significant contribution to the field of image editing.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. Firstly, the proxy tasks, while effective for multi-turn image editing, are primarily designed to predict the next step in a sequence. As the paper states, "Next-image prediction (NIP). NIP is our primary in-context image editing task." (Section 3.3). This focus on sequential prediction may not adequately support other applications, such as story generation or chain-of-editing, which require a more holistic understanding of the editing process. The paper mentions these applications (Figure 1 and Section 1), but the design of the proxy tasks doesn't explicitly incorporate objectives tailored for them. The emergent nature of these capabilities, as mentioned in the conclusion, supports this point. This limitation could restrict the model's versatility in handling diverse editing tasks that extend beyond the defined proxy tasks. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the description of the proxy tasks and the lack of explicit objectives for other applications. Secondly, the model's performance is heavily reliant on the quality and quantity of video data available for training. The paper emphasizes the use of video data: "In this paper, we approach in-context image editing from a different perspective and investigate the following research question: *Can a meaningful in-context image editing model be learned solely from videos?*" (Introduction). The data construction pipeline relies on sampling frames from videos and annotating transitions. The quality of these annotations and the diversity of the video content will directly impact the model's learning. The paper also shows that scaling up the training data improves performance: "Scaling up the model and training data leads to substantial performance gains..." (Introduction). Furthermore, the paper acknowledges limitations in handling certain scenarios: "It also demonstrates reasonable generalization to scenarios that are less common in natural video, such as background changes, attribute modifications, and multi-concept compositions." (Introduction). This implies that the model's performance on these less common scenarios might be lower due to the nature of the training data. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the paper's focus on video data, the results on scaling data, and the acknowledgement of limitations in handling less common scenarios. Thirdly, the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate how well the model generalizes to editing tasks that are significantly different from the training data. While the model performs well on the proposed benchmarks, the reviewer's concern about generalization to significantly different tasks is valid. The benchmarks, while diverse, are still within the scope of typical image editing tasks. The paper lacks explicit experiments demonstrating performance on tasks far outside the training data distribution. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the focus on specific benchmark tasks and the lack of experiments explicitly designed to test generalization to significantly different editing scenarios. Fourthly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method, particularly in terms of training time and resource requirements. While the paper mentions the hardware used for training: "The 3B and 7B variants are optimized on session data for 15k and 40k steps, consuming approximately 30 and 150 hours on 256 H100 GPUs, respectively." (Section 4.1), it doesn't provide details about FLOPs, memory footprint, or a comparison of computational efficiency with existing approaches. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the scalability and practicality of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the mention of training time and GPU usage but the absence of details on FLOPs, memory footprint, and comparative analysis. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more in-depth discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, particularly in scenarios where the desired editing is not well-represented in the training data. The paper describes the data construction process, which relies on annotating transitions in existing videos. This inherently limits the model's ability to perform edits not observed in the training data. The paper acknowledges limitations: "It also demonstrates reasonable generalization to scenarios that are less common in natural video..." (Introduction). However, it doesn't include a dedicated section or analysis on failure cases or scenarios where the model struggles. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the reliance on video data for training and the brief acknowledgement of limitations without a detailed analysis of failure cases.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. Firstly, to mitigate the limitations of the proxy tasks, the authors should explore incorporating more diverse training objectives that explicitly target applications like story generation and chain-of-editing. For instance, the model could be trained with a sequence-to-sequence objective, where the input is a series of edits and the output is the final edited image, which would encourage the model to learn the compositional nature of editing. Additionally, the authors could investigate using contrastive learning techniques to learn a shared embedding space for images and editing instructions, which could improve the model's ability to generalize to new editing tasks. This would involve creating pairs of similar and dissimilar editing instructions and training the model to distinguish between them, thereby improving its understanding of the nuances of different editing operations. Furthermore, the authors should consider incorporating techniques such as data augmentation to increase the diversity of the training data and improve the model's robustness to variations in input data. Secondly, to mitigate the reliance on large amounts of high-quality video data, the authors could explore techniques such as transfer learning and few-shot learning. Pre-training the model on a large dataset of images and then fine-tuning it on a smaller dataset of videos could reduce the amount of video data required for training. Additionally, the authors could investigate using meta-learning techniques to enable the model to learn how to learn from a small number of examples, which would allow it to adapt to new editing tasks with limited training data. The authors should also explore methods for data cleaning and filtering to remove low-quality video data, which could improve the model's performance. This could involve using techniques such as object detection and tracking to identify and remove videos that contain significant motion blur or occlusions. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the use of synthetic data generation techniques to augment the training data and improve the model's ability to handle complex editing scenarios. Thirdly, to improve the evaluation of the model's generalization capabilities, the authors should evaluate the model on a wider range of datasets and real-world scenarios. This should include datasets that feature more complex editing tasks, such as those involving significant changes in lighting, viewpoint, or object deformation. The robustness of the model to noisy or low-quality input data should also be investigated. Furthermore, the authors should consider evaluating the model's performance on tasks that require more abstract or conceptual edits, such as style transfer or artistic rendering. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's capabilities and limitations. The evaluation should also include a qualitative analysis of the generated images, focusing on the visual quality and fidelity of the edits, as well as the consistency of the edits with the input instructions. Fourthly, to address the lack of computational cost analysis, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the FLOPs required for each stage of their pipeline, including data processing, model training, and inference. This should include a comparison with existing methods, highlighting the trade-offs between performance and computational resources. Furthermore, the memory footprint of the model and the data processing pipeline should be discussed, as this is a critical factor for practical deployment. It would be beneficial to include a table summarizing the computational costs, including the number of GPUs used, training time, and inference time for different model sizes. This would allow for a more informed comparison with other methods and provide a clearer understanding of the practical implications of the proposed approach. Additionally, the authors should investigate the potential for optimizing the model architecture and training process to reduce computational costs, such as through model pruning or quantization techniques. Finally, to provide a more in-depth discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, the authors should include a qualitative analysis of failure cases, highlighting the types of edits that the model struggles with and the reasons for these failures. For instance, the authors should investigate how the model handles edits that require significant changes in object pose or appearance that are not commonly observed in the video data. This analysis should also consider the impact of the training data distribution on the model's performance, and the authors should discuss potential strategies for mitigating these limitations, such as through data augmentation or curriculum learning. A more thorough discussion of these limitations would provide a more balanced and realistic assessment of the proposed method.
