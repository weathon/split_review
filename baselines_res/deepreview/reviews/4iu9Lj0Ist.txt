## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel theoretical framework for addressing the problem of certified unlearning within a continual learning setting, a domain where models are trained sequentially on tasks and may require the removal of specific data influences without full retraining. The authors tackle this challenge by adapting two established certified unlearning techniques—gradient-based and Hessian-based—to function within the constraints of continual learning. Their core contribution lies in providing theoretical performance guarantees for these adapted algorithms by analyzing two key metrics: the excess risk associated with continual learning and the unlearning loss incurred when removing data influence. The excess risk quantifies the performance degradation due to the continual learning process, while the unlearning loss measures the discrepancy between the model after unlearning and the model that would have been trained without the deleted data. The authors derive an upper bound for the excess risk under their proposed l2-regularized continual learning algorithm, which is a crucial step in understanding the trade-offs between learning new tasks and forgetting old ones. They also provide theoretical guarantees for the approximation error of their unlearning algorithms, demonstrating that the unlearning loss can be controlled under certain conditions. Empirically, the authors validate their theoretical findings using the MNIST dataset, demonstrating the impact of unlearning requests on model performance and the effectiveness of their proposed algorithms. The experiments show that the proposed methods can achieve a reasonable trade-off between excess risk and unlearning loss, although the performance is limited by the inherent challenges of continual learning and unlearning. The paper's significance lies in being the first to establish a theoretical foundation for certified unlearning in continual learning, a critical area given the increasing importance of privacy and data governance in machine learning. By providing a rigorous analysis of the problem and proposing practical algorithms with theoretical guarantees, the authors make a valuable contribution to the field. However, the paper also acknowledges the limitations of their approach, such as the non-vanishing excess risk even with large sample sizes and the reliance on a specific continual learning algorithm, which highlights the need for further research in this area.

This is a crucial area of research, given the growing need for machine learning models to adapt to new tasks while respecting data privacy and governance requirements. They introduce a novel approach by adapting two popular certified unlearning techniques—the gradient-based and Hessian-based methods—to the continual learning setting. This adaptation is not trivial, as it requires careful consideration of the sequential nature of task learning and the need to remove data influence without full retraining. The paper's theoretical analysis is centered around two key metrics: the excess risk under continual learning and the unlearning loss. The authors derive an upper bound for the excess risk under their proposed l2-regularized continual learning algorithm, which is a significant step in quantifying the performance degradation due to the continual learning process. They also provide theoretical guarantees for the approximation error of their unlearning algorithms, demonstrating that the unlearning loss can be controlled under certain conditions. ## Weaknesses:

While this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field, several weaknesses limit its practical applicability and the generalizability of its findings. First, the paper's motivation, while present, could be more compelling. While the introduction mentions privacy concerns and the limitations of current unlearning methods in continual learning, it lacks concrete real-world scenarios that would necessitate unlearning in a continual learning setting. For example, the paper could have discussed scenarios involving data corruption or privacy regulations that require the removal of specific user data from a continually learning model. This lack of concrete examples makes it difficult to fully appreciate the practical significance of the proposed framework. Second, the theoretical analysis, particularly the upper bound in Eq. (8), is complex and difficult to interpret. The presence of multiple summation terms and the dependence on the differences between optimal models for different tasks makes it challenging to understand the practical implications of this bound. While the paper provides some high-level interpretation of the terms, it lacks a detailed, intuitive breakdown of how each component contributes to the overall bound and how it can be used for practical algorithm design. This complexity obscures any actionable insights that could be derived from the theoretical analysis. Third, the paper acknowledges that the excess risk in Eq. (7) does not vanish even with sufficiently large sample sizes. This is a significant limitation, as it suggests that the proposed approach cannot achieve optimal performance even with abundant data. The paper attributes this to model heterogeneity and non-i.i.d. data, but it does not delve deeply into the implications for scalability or potential mitigation strategies. This non-vanishing excess risk raises concerns about the practical applicability of the proposed method in real-world scenarios where large datasets are often available. Fourth, the paper's task incremental learning setting assumes that the model has access to the full dataset of the current task when learning. This is unrealistic in many practical scenarios, where data arrives in an online fashion and the full dataset for the current task is not available. The paper does not address this limitation or discuss how the proposed approach could be adapted to a more realistic online learning scenario. This assumption significantly reduces the practical relevance of the work. Fifth, the paper does not provide any experimental evaluation of the derived theoretical upper bounds. While the experiments validate the overall performance of the proposed algorithms, they do not directly evaluate the tightness of the derived bounds. This makes it difficult to assess the practical significance of the theoretical results and to understand how well the bounds approximate the actual performance of the algorithms. Finally, the experiments are performed on the MNIST dataset, which is not representative of the challenging datasets in continual learning. The use of MNIST limits the generalizability of the findings and makes it difficult to assess the performance of the proposed method on more complex tasks. The paper should have included experiments on more challenging datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in more realistic scenarios. In summary, while the paper makes a valuable theoretical contribution, these limitations significantly impact its practical applicability and the generalizability of its findings. The lack of concrete motivation, the complexity of the theoretical analysis, the non-vanishing excess risk, the unrealistic data assumptions, the absence of bound validation, and the use of a simple dataset all contribute to the identified weaknesses.
