## Summary:

This paper introduces EmoSign, a novel multimodal dataset designed to advance the understanding of emotion recognition in American Sign Language (ASL). The authors address a critical gap in sign language research, where emotional indicators are often intertwined with grammatical functions, making their interpretation challenging. Unlike spoken languages, where prosody and tone convey emotion, sign languages rely on a complex interplay of facial expressions, hand movements, and body language, all of which can serve both linguistic and affective purposes. The EmoSign dataset comprises 200 ASL video clips, each annotated with sentiment ratings, the presence and intensity of ten distinct emotion categories, and open-ended descriptions of specific emotion cues. These annotations were provided by three Deaf ASL signers with professional interpretation experience, ensuring cultural relevance and accuracy. The authors also establish baseline models for sentiment and emotion classification using state-of-the-art multimodal language models (MLLMs), including GPT-4o, AffectGPT, Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, and MiniGPT4. The benchmark results highlight the limitations of current models in effectively integrating visual cues for emotional reasoning, often exhibiting a bias toward positive emotions. The paper emphasizes the need for new architectures that can disentangle the grammatical and affective functions of visual cues in sign languages, ultimately aiming to inspire the development of more nuanced and accurate emotion-aware models for sign language communication. The authors acknowledge the limitations of the dataset size and discuss future directions for expanding the dataset and improving model performance. The work is significant in that it provides a much-needed resource for the field of sign language emotion recognition, a domain that has been relatively under-explored compared to spoken language emotion recognition. By focusing on the unique challenges of sign language, the paper contributes to a more inclusive and comprehensive understanding of multimodal emotion recognition.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in the introduction of EmoSign, the first multimodal dataset specifically designed for sentiment and emotion recognition in ASL videos. This is a significant contribution, as it addresses a notable gap in sign language research, where resources for emotion recognition have been limited. The dataset's annotations, provided by three experienced Deaf ASL signers, ensure high quality and cultural relevance, which is crucial for accurate interpretation of emotional cues in sign language. The inclusion of open-ended descriptions of emotion cues adds a layer of depth to the dataset, offering valuable insights into how emotions manifest in ASL. Furthermore, the paper establishes baseline models for sentiment analysis and emotion classification, which serves as a crucial starting point for future research in this area. The benchmark results, while highlighting the limitations of current models, also underscore the need for specialized architectures that can effectively integrate visual cues for emotional reasoning. The authors' emphasis on the need to disentangle grammatical and affective functions of visual cues in sign languages is a key contribution, as it points to a critical challenge in the field. The paper also provides a clear motivation for the dataset, emphasizing the unique challenges of emotion recognition in sign language, where emotional and linguistic information are embedded within the same visual modalities. This contrasts with spoken languages, where emotional and linguistic information can be separated across different channels (prosody vs. words). The authors also clearly articulate the limitations of existing ASL datasets, which primarily focus on translation tasks and lack the emotional annotations necessary for developing and evaluating affect recognition systems. The paper's focus on the unique characteristics of sign language, such as the simultaneous use of facial expressions and hand movements for both grammatical and emotional functions, is a key strength. This focus highlights the need for specialized approaches to emotion recognition in sign language, rather than simply adapting methods developed for spoken languages. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of the dataset size and discuss future directions for expanding the dataset and improving model performance, demonstrating a commitment to ongoing research in this area.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a valuable contribution, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the dataset size, consisting of only 200 utterances, is relatively small, which may limit its usefulness for training robust machine learning models. Although the paper acknowledges this limitation in the 'LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK' section, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how this dataset size might affect the performance of different types of models, particularly those that rely heavily on large amounts of training data, such as deep learning models. This lack of analysis is a significant oversight, as it leaves open the question of how well models trained on this dataset will generalize to unseen data. Furthermore, the paper does not explicitly discuss the potential for overfitting when training models on such a small dataset, nor does it propose mitigation strategies, such as data augmentation or transfer learning, in the main body of the paper. This is a critical omission, as overfitting is a common problem when training models on small datasets, and it can lead to poor generalization performance. My analysis confirms this limitation, as the paper only mentions the small size in the limitations section, without a deeper discussion of its implications. Second, the paper does not evaluate the performance of the baseline models on multi-label emotion classification tasks. The 'Emotion Classification' experiment focuses on single-label classification, where each video is assigned a single dominant emotion. However, real-world emotional expressions are often nuanced and multi-faceted, involving multiple emotions simultaneously. The absence of multi-label evaluation leaves a gap in understanding the models' ability to handle complex emotional expressions. This is a significant limitation, as it does not fully reflect the complexity of human emotion. My analysis confirms that the experimental setup and results focus on single-label classification, and the paper acknowledges the existence of multi-expression videos but doesn't evaluate performance on them in a multi-label setting. Third, the paper does not adequately address the potential biases in the dataset or the annotation process. The 'ANNOTATION CONSTRUCTION' section describes the recruitment of annotators, who were all Deaf ASL signers with professional interpretation experience. While this is a strength in terms of cultural relevance, the paper does not discuss the potential for biases arising from this specific group or the annotation process itself. Since the annotators' interpretations of emotions might not be representative of the broader Deaf community, this could introduce biases that affect the performance of models trained on the dataset, particularly if the models are deployed in diverse Deaf communities with varying cultural norms and emotional expressions. My analysis confirms that the paper describes the annotators' background but lacks a discussion on potential biases in the dataset or annotation process. Finally, while the paper does have a 'RELATED WORK' section, it does not sufficiently discuss related work in the field of sign language emotion recognition. While the paper mentions and compares to FePh, a related dataset, the depth of comparison and analysis of methodologies and findings could be expanded. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of prior datasets, their annotation schemes, and the techniques used for emotion recognition in sign language. This lack of a comprehensive literature review makes it difficult to fully understand how EmoSign compares to or builds upon previous research efforts. My analysis confirms that the paper does have a related work section, but the reviewer's point about the *sufficiency* of the discussion is reasonable. More detailed comparisons and analysis of existing methodologies and findings could strengthen the paper.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the dataset to include a larger number of utterances. This expansion should aim to balance the representation of different emotions to mitigate potential class imbalance issues. A larger dataset would allow for more robust training of machine learning models and improve their generalization capabilities. Furthermore, the authors should explore data augmentation techniques to artificially increase the size of the dataset. Techniques such as temporal jittering, random cropping, and color perturbation could be considered to create additional training samples while preserving the emotional content of the videos. The authors should also investigate the impact of dataset size on model performance by conducting experiments with varying amounts of training data, which would provide insights into the scalability of the proposed approach. Second, the authors should incorporate a multi-label annotation scheme in future iterations of the dataset. This would involve annotating each video clip with multiple emotion labels, reflecting the complexity of real-world emotional expressions. The authors should then evaluate the performance of the baseline models on this multi-label task, using appropriate metrics such as the F1 score or the area under the ROC curve. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the models' ability to handle nuanced emotional expressions. Additionally, the authors should consider exploring more advanced models that are specifically designed for multi-label classification, such as those based on attention mechanisms or graph neural networks. Third, the authors should address the potential biases in the dataset by including a more diverse group of annotators, representing different backgrounds and experiences within the Deaf community. This would help to ensure that the dataset is more representative of the broader Deaf community and reduce the risk of introducing biases. The authors should also conduct a thorough analysis of the annotation process to identify and mitigate any potential sources of bias. This could involve comparing the annotations of different annotators and analyzing the reasons for any discrepancies. Furthermore, the authors should consider using techniques such as adversarial training to reduce the impact of biases on model performance. Finally, the authors should expand the 'RELATED WORK' section to include a more detailed discussion of existing sign language datasets and emotion recognition methodologies. This section should not only list existing datasets but also provide a detailed analysis of their annotation schemes, the types of emotions they capture, and the methods used for emotion recognition. For example, the review should discuss datasets that focus on facial expressions in sign language, those that use body motion, and those that combine multiple modalities. The authors should also discuss the limitations of these existing datasets and how EmoSign addresses these limitations. Furthermore, the related work should include a discussion of the various machine learning techniques that have been applied to sign language emotion recognition, such as convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, and transformer-based models. This would provide a more comprehensive context for the reader and highlight the novelty of the proposed dataset.
