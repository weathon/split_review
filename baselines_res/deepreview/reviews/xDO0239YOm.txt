## Summary:

This paper introduces HypoGeneAgent, a novel framework for selecting optimal clustering resolutions in single-cell and perturb-seq data by leveraging large language models (LLMs) for functional annotation. The core idea is to transform the subjective task of cluster annotation into a quantitatively optimizable process. The authors propose that the optimal clustering resolution is the one that maximizes both the coherence of clusters (intra-cluster agreement) and the distinctiveness between clusters (inter-cluster separation). To achieve this, HypoGeneAgent uses an LLM to generate hypotheses about the biological processes represented within each cluster, accompanied by confidence scores. These annotations are then used to calculate intra-cluster agreement and inter-cluster distinctiveness, which are combined into a resolution score. The resolution with the highest score is considered optimal. The method is validated on a K562 CRISPRi perturb-seq dataset, and the results indicate that HypoGeneAgent selects clustering resolutions that align better with known biological pathways compared to traditional metrics like silhouette score and modularity. The authors also explore the impact of different LLMs and text embedding methods on the performance of their framework. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative application of LLMs to the problem of clustering resolution selection, offering a more automated and biologically informed approach than traditional methods. The authors demonstrate that their method can identify resolutions that are more biologically meaningful, which is a significant step towards more robust and interpretable single-cell data analysis. However, the study is limited by its evaluation on a single dataset and a lack of comparison with other state-of-the-art methods, which raises questions about the generalizability and robustness of the approach. The paper also does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost, which is a crucial factor for practical applications. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a promising approach that could have a significant impact on the field of single-cell data analysis.

## Strengths:

The authors have creatively applied a powerful tool, the LLM, to a problem that has traditionally relied on more subjective or less biologically informed methods. The transformation of cluster annotation into a quantitatively optimizable task is a novel and compelling approach. The method, HypoGeneAgent, is well-motivated, addressing a real challenge in single-cell data analysis: the subjective nature of choosing the optimal clustering resolution. The use of intra-cluster agreement and inter-cluster distinctiveness, derived from LLM-generated annotations, to define a resolution score is a clever way to incorporate biological relevance into the selection process. The authors have also demonstrated the effectiveness of their method on a real dataset, showing that the selected resolutions align better with known biological pathways compared to traditional metrics. This is a crucial validation of their approach. The parameter benchmark experiment, where different LLMs and text embedding methods were evaluated, is another strength. It shows that the authors have considered the impact of different components of their framework on the overall performance. The use of chain-of-thought and self-verification prompts to improve the LLM's performance is also a positive aspect, indicating a thoughtful approach to leveraging LLM capabilities. The paper is well-written and clearly describes the method and the experiments. The authors have also acknowledged the limitations of their approach, which is a sign of intellectual honesty. ## Weaknesses:

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several key weaknesses that significantly impact its conclusions and generalizability. First, the method's heavy reliance on the performance of the LLM is a major concern. While the authors explore different LLMs, the core of their approach depends on the LLM's ability to generate accurate and consistent annotations. As the reviewers pointed out, LLMs can introduce biases or inaccuracies, especially when dealing with complex biological data and noisy single-cell measurements. The paper does not sufficiently address the potential for the LLM to generate spurious or inconsistent annotations, and this lack of robustness is a significant limitation. The reliance on a single LLM (GPT-o3) for the main experiment further raises concerns about the generalizability of the approach across different models and datasets. This is not a speculative concern; it's a direct consequence of the method's design. Second, the evaluation of HypoGeneAgent is severely limited by its application to only a single dataset, the K562 CRISPRi perturb-seq dataset. This is a critical flaw because the K562 cell line represents a specific cell type and experimental setup. The lack of validation on diverse datasets, including those with different cell types, perturbation methods, and levels of noise, makes it impossible to assess the robustness and general applicability of the proposed method. The reviewers correctly pointed out that the method needs to be tested on a range of experimental conditions to demonstrate its broad utility. This is not a minor oversight; it's a fundamental limitation that undermines the paper's claims of general applicability. Third, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods for cluster annotation and resolution selection. While the authors compare their method to traditional metrics like silhouette score and modularity, they do not compare it against other recent approaches that also leverage machine learning or network analysis for similar tasks. This is a significant omission because it makes it difficult to assess the advantages and limitations of HypoGeneAgent relative to existing techniques. The reviewers correctly identified this gap, and it's a major weakness that limits the paper's impact. The absence of a comparison with other ML or network-based methods makes it difficult to determine if HypoGeneAgent offers a significant improvement over existing techniques. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method. This is a critical omission because the method involves computationally intensive steps like LLM calls and sentence embedding calculations. The lack of information about computational time, memory usage, and hardware requirements makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method, especially for large-scale datasets. This is not just a minor detail; it's a crucial factor that needs to be considered when evaluating the feasibility of the method. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the paper's conclusions and generalizability. The reliance on a single dataset, the lack of comparison with other state-of-the-art methods, the dependence on LLM performance, and the absence of a computational cost analysis are all substantial limitations that need to be addressed in future work.
