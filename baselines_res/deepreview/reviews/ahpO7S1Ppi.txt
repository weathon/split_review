## Summary:

This paper introduces PCTX, a novel personalized context-aware tokenizer designed to enhance generative recommendation (GR) models. The core idea behind PCTX is to move beyond static tokenization methods, which assign a fixed semantic ID to each item, and instead generate personalized semantic IDs based on a user's historical interactions. This approach aims to capture the diverse interpretations that different users may have for the same item. The authors argue that by incorporating user-specific contexts into the tokenization process, GR models can produce more personalized and accurate recommendations. The methodology involves several key steps. First, an auxiliary model, specifically DuoRec, is used to encode user context based on historical interactions. Then, k-means++ clustering is applied to group context representations into representative centroids. These centroids are then fused with item feature representations and quantized using RQ-VAE to generate semantic IDs. To further enhance data diversity, a data augmentation strategy is employed, which randomly replaces personalized semantic IDs with other IDs corresponding to the same item. The authors conduct experiments on three public datasets from the Amazon Review dataset, namely Musical Instruments, Industrial & Scientific, and Video Games, comparing PCTX against several baselines, including both conventional sequential recommendation models and generative recommendation models. The experimental results demonstrate that PCTX achieves significant improvements in performance over non-personalized tokenization baselines, with up to 8.9% improvement in NDCG@10. The paper also includes a case study that illustrates how different user contexts lead to different semantic IDs for the same item, providing some insight into the model's interpretability. Overall, the paper presents a novel approach to tokenization in GR models, addressing the limitations of static tokenization methods and demonstrating the potential for personalized tokenization to improve recommendation accuracy. However, as I will discuss in detail, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of this method.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its novel approach to tokenization within the context of generative recommendation. The idea of personalizing the tokenization process based on user context is a significant departure from traditional static methods, which treat all users as having the same interpretation of an item. This is a valuable contribution because it acknowledges that users may have different perspectives and motivations when interacting with the same item, and that these differences should be reflected in the tokenization process. The proposed method, PCTX, effectively incorporates user historical interactions into the tokenization process, which is a step towards capturing user-specific interpretations of items. The use of an auxiliary model, DuoRec, to encode user context, followed by k-means++ clustering and RQ-VAE for semantic ID generation, is a technically sound approach. The data augmentation strategy, while adding complexity, is also a valuable addition to enhance data diversity. Furthermore, the paper provides a comprehensive set of experiments across three public datasets, which demonstrates the generalizability of the proposed method to different domains. The experimental results are promising, showing significant improvements in performance over non-personalized tokenization baselines. The case study, while limited, provides some evidence for the interpretability of the model, showing how different user contexts lead to different semantic IDs for the same item. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a wider audience. The motivation for the work is clear, and the proposed method is technically sound. The experimental results are promising, and the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of generative recommendation.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, there are several weaknesses that I have identified through my analysis. First, the proposed method introduces significant complexity by incorporating personalized context into the tokenization process. This complexity arises from several sources. The use of k-means++ clustering to group context representations introduces a hyperparameter, the number of clusters, which requires careful selection. As the paper states, "Specifically, we apply k-means++ to cluster the context representations into $C_{v_i}$ centroids". The choice of $C_{v_i}$ is not trivial and can significantly impact the performance of the model. Furthermore, the merging of semantic IDs, while intended to improve generalizability, adds another layer of complexity to the tokenization process. The paper mentions, "To mitigate this issue, we set a frequency threshold $\tau$ and remove semantic IDs that appear less frequently than this threshold." This merging strategy, while necessary, introduces another hyperparameter that needs to be tuned. Finally, the data augmentation strategy, while potentially beneficial, also introduces additional computational overhead and requires careful tuning to avoid introducing noise into the training data. The paper states, "To further enhance data diversity, we introduce an augmentation strategy that randomly replaces a personalized semantic ID with another semantic ID corresponding to the same item. Every personalized semantic ID is replaced with probability $\gamma$." These three components, clustering, merging, and augmentation, each introduce hyperparameters and computational overhead, making the model more difficult to train and tune. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the method description. Second, the paper primarily focuses on the Amazon Review datasets, which, while diverse, may not fully represent the complexity and scale of real-world industrial applications, such as those in short video or social media recommendation systems. The sequential nature of interactions and the type of item metadata in these datasets might differ significantly from the Amazon Review datasets, potentially affecting the performance of the proposed method. For example, the short video domain often involves more implicit feedback and rapidly changing user preferences, which may not be well-captured by the proposed approach. The paper explicitly states, "Following prior work (Liu et al., 2025a; Zheng et al., 2025), we conduct experiments on three categories from the latest Amazon Reviews dataset (Hou et al., 2024), namely “Musical Instruments” (**Instrument**), “Industrial & Scientific” (**Scientific**), and “Video Games” (**Game**)." The lack of experiments on datasets from other domains limits the generalizability of the findings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the experimental setup. Third, the paper does not extensively discuss the scalability of PCTX, especially concerning the computational cost of the personalized tokenization process in large-scale systems. The personalized context representation and the subsequent clustering and merging steps could become computationally expensive as the number of users and items grows. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the time and memory complexity of these operations, making it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method in large-scale industrial settings. The method section describes the steps involved in personalized tokenization, including context representation derivation, clustering, and merging, but there is no formal analysis of the time and space complexity of these steps. For example, they could investigate using a learned embedding for user context that does not require explicit clustering. This could involve training a separate neural network to map user interaction histories to a dense vector representation, which could then be used to personalize the tokenization process. This would reduce the number of hyperparameters and the computational cost associated with clustering. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the model to the hyperparameters associated with the clustering and merging steps. This would help practitioners understand the trade-offs between model performance and computational cost, and provide guidance on how to tune these parameters effectively. Furthermore, the authors should consider exploring simpler data augmentation techniques that do not introduce as much computational overhead, such as random masking or swapping of tokens, to reduce the overall complexity of the proposed method. Second, to better demonstrate the generalizability of PCTX, the authors should conduct experiments on datasets from other domains, such as short video or social media platforms. This would help to assess the robustness of the method to different types of user interactions and item metadata. Specifically, the authors should consider datasets that include implicit feedback and rapidly changing user preferences, which are common in many real-world applications. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the performance of PCTX on these datasets, including a comparison to existing baselines. This would help to demonstrate the practical applicability of the method in a wider range of scenarios. Moreover, the authors should investigate the impact of different types of item metadata on the performance of PCTX, and explore methods for incorporating this information into the tokenization process. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of PCTX, including the time and memory complexity of the personalized tokenization process. This analysis should consider the scalability of the method to large-scale systems with millions of users and items. The authors should also explore techniques for optimizing the tokenization process, such as using efficient data structures and parallel computing.
