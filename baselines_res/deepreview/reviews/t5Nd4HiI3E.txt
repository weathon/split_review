## Summary:

This paper introduces Bias-Variance Optimized Preference Optimization (BVPO), a novel method designed to enhance the alignment of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) with human preferences. The core idea behind BVPO is to address the high gradient variance that arises from stochastic trace sampling during the training of LRMs. This variance, the authors argue, hinders stable training and limits the effectiveness of preference optimization. BVPO tackles this issue by combining two gradient estimators: a high-variance trace-based estimator, which is standard in preference optimization, and a low-variance empty-trace estimator, which is computed by conditioning the policy on an empty trace. The method involves a convex combination of these two estimators, weighted by a hyperparameter α, which is theoretically derived to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) of the combined gradient. The authors provide a rigorous theoretical analysis, demonstrating that this combination reduces variance and achieves tighter convergence bounds for stochastic gradient descent (SGD). They derive a closed-form solution for the optimal mixing weight α, which minimizes the MSE of the combined gradient estimator. Empirically, the authors demonstrate that BVPO significantly improves alignment performance on benchmarks such as AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard. Furthermore, they show that BVPO enhances reasoning performance on math reasoning tasks, as evaluated on benchmarks like AIME, AMC, and MATH-500. The authors' approach is grounded in the understanding that the standard trace-based gradient estimator is prone to high variance due to the stochastic nature of reasoning trace generation. By introducing the empty-trace gradient estimator, which is deterministic with respect to trace sampling, they provide a means to reduce this variance. The combination of these two estimators, weighted by the theoretically derived optimal α, allows for a more stable and effective training process. The paper's contribution lies in its identification of the gradient variance issue in LRM training, its proposal of a variance-reducing method with theoretical guarantees, and its empirical validation on both alignment and reasoning tasks. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field of LRM training, offering a practical and theoretically sound approach to improve the alignment and reasoning capabilities of these models.

The analysis should consider how these costs scale with model size, sequence length, and batch size. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical measurements of the training time and memory usage for both BVPO and DPO on different hardware configurations. This would provide a more concrete understanding of the practical overhead introduced by BVPO and help practitioners make informed decisions about its applicability. For example, providing a table that shows the training time per epoch for both methods across different model sizes and batch sizes would be very useful. Additionally, the paper should explore adaptive strategies for α, such as annealing or learning the coefficient during training, and provide empirical evidence of their effectiveness. The authors should also discuss the potential trade-offs between the variance reduction achieved by BVPO and the potential bias introduced by the empty-trace gradient, and how the choice of α affects this trade-off. This would provide a more practical and robust approach to using BVPO in real-world scenarios.
