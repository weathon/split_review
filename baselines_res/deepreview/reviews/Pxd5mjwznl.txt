## Summary:

This paper introduces Difference Back Propagation (DBP), a novel backpropagation method that calculates gradients based on the difference of pre- and post-activation neuron values rather than using derivatives of the activation function. The authors propose that this approach maintains consistency between neuron values before and after the activation function, leading to more reliable gradients. The core idea is to use the inverse of the activation function to compute the difference in the pre-activation space, which is then used to update the weights. The paper primarily focuses on the sigmoid activation function, providing a detailed explanation of how DBP is applied in this context. The authors present three experiments to validate their method. The first two experiments involve training a small neural network with a single hidden layer on a synthetic dataset generated from a scaled cosine function. These experiments aim to demonstrate the basic functionality of DBP and compare its training loss and accuracy with standard backpropagation. The third experiment uses a transformer-based classification model trained on the AG News dataset, a text classification task. The authors compare the training loss and accuracy of DBP with standard backpropagation on this task. The results presented in the paper suggest that DBP can achieve comparable or slightly better performance than standard backpropagation in these limited settings. The authors claim that DBP is more accurate and maintains consistency between neuron values before and after the activation function. However, the paper lacks a rigorous theoretical justification for this claim, and the experimental results are not sufficient to fully support the general applicability of the method. Overall, the paper presents an interesting idea for modifying the backpropagation algorithm, but it suffers from significant limitations in terms of theoretical analysis and experimental validation, which I will detail further in the following sections. The primary contribution of this work is the introduction of the DBP algorithm and its initial evaluation on a few simple tasks. However, the lack of comprehensive experiments and theoretical analysis limits the impact of the paper.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of a novel approach to backpropagation. The core idea of using the difference of pre- and post-activation values, rather than relying on the derivative of the activation function, is a creative departure from traditional methods. This approach, as the authors suggest, could potentially offer a more consistent way to update weights, especially when dealing with activation functions that are not easily differentiable or have unstable derivatives. The paper clearly articulates the proposed Difference Back Propagation (DBP) method, providing a detailed explanation of how it can be applied to the sigmoid activation function. The use of the inverse sigmoid function to compute the difference in the pre-activation space is a technically sound approach and demonstrates a good understanding of the underlying mathematical principles. The authors also briefly discuss the potential applicability of DBP to other activation functions, including ReLU and leaky ReLU, although they do not provide detailed explanations or experimental results for these cases. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, making the proposed method accessible to a broad audience. The inclusion of three experiments, although limited in scope, provides a basic demonstration of the method's functionality. The experiments on the synthetic dataset and the transformer-based classification task offer some initial evidence that DBP can achieve comparable or slightly better performance than standard backpropagation in these specific settings. The authors' attempt to address the issue of non-differentiable activation functions is also a valuable contribution, as it highlights a potential area where DBP could be beneficial. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its novel idea, clear explanation of the proposed method, and initial experimental validation, although the latter is limited in scope and impact.


## Weaknesses:

After a thorough examination of the paper, I have identified two primary weaknesses that significantly limit its impact and generalizability. First, the paper's proposed method, Difference Back Propagation (DBP), appears to have limited applicability to the most widely used activation function in deep learning: the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU). While the authors briefly mention that DBP could be applied to ReLU and its variants, they fail to provide a detailed explanation or experimental validation for this claim. The core of the DBP method relies on the inverse of the activation function, and while the inverse of the sigmoid function is well-defined and used in the paper, the situation with ReLU is more complex. The ReLU function is not invertible in the traditional sense, and while variations like leaky ReLU have gradients defined at zero, the paper does not address how DBP would handle the non-differentiable point at x=0. The authors' brief mention of defining the derivative as 1 at this point is arbitrary and lacks a strong theoretical justification. This lack of rigorous analysis and experimental evidence for ReLU significantly restricts the practical utility of DBP, as ReLU and its variants are ubiquitous in modern deep learning architectures. This limitation is a major concern, as it suggests that the proposed method may not be readily applicable to many real-world deep learning problems. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's focus on the sigmoid function and the lack of detailed analysis for ReLU are clearly evident. The second major weakness of this paper is the simplicity of the experiments used to evaluate the proposed DBP method. The first two experiments involve training a small neural network with a single hidden layer on a synthetic dataset generated from a scaled cosine function. These experiments, while demonstrating the basic functionality of DBP, are insufficient to show its effectiveness in more complex scenarios. The third experiment, which uses a transformer-based classification model on the AG News dataset, is a slightly more realistic task, but the scale of the model and dataset is still relatively small compared to typical applications of transformers. The paper lacks experiments on standard benchmark datasets commonly used in the deep learning community, such as CIFAR-10, ImageNet, or larger NLP datasets like SST-2 or IMDB. Furthermore, the paper only compares DBP with the default backpropagation method, without considering other relevant optimization techniques like Adam, RMSprop, or other variants of backpropagation. This lack of comparative analysis makes it difficult to ascertain the relative advantages and disadvantages of DBP. The limited scope of the experiments and the lack of comparison with other methods raise serious concerns about the generalizability and robustness of DBP. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as the descriptions of the experiments clearly indicate the use of small networks and datasets, and the absence of comparisons with other optimization methods is evident in the results sections. These two weaknesses, the limited applicability to ReLU and the simplicity of the experiments, significantly undermine the paper's claims and limit its overall impact.
