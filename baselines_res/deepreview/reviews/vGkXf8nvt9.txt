## Summary:

This paper introduces Forget-to-Focus (F2F), a novel two-stage protocol designed to enhance domain specialization in large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind F2F is to first apply targeted unlearning on a 'forget' set, aiming to remove irrelevant pre-training knowledge, and then fine-tune the model on a domain-specific dataset. This approach is motivated by the observation that standard fine-tuning often struggles to overcome the influence of pre-training data, leading to suboptimal performance in specialized domains. The authors explore various unlearning methods, including gradient ascent (GA), gradient ascent with gradient descent (GA+GD), gradient ascent with KL divergence (GA+KL), and natural gradient ascent (NPO), and evaluate their impact on different domains, including medical, mathematics, and coding. The empirical results presented in the paper consistently demonstrate that F2F outperforms standard fine-tuning across a range of model architectures and sizes. Furthermore, the authors provide a theoretical framework for understanding the unlearning process, although this framework is primarily based on a simplified linear model. They also analyze the changes in the model's internal representations using techniques like Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) and Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis (SVCCA), showing that F2F leads to representations that are more aligned with the target domain. The paper also highlights that F2F improves model calibration, which is particularly important for reliable predictions in sensitive domains. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of unlearning as a preparatory step for domain specialization in LLMs, offering a practical approach that is supported by both empirical evidence and theoretical analysis, albeit with some limitations. The significance of this work lies in its innovative application of unlearning for domain adaptation, moving beyond the traditional focus on privacy, and providing a method that is both effective and relatively simple to implement. The comprehensive experimental evaluation across multiple domains and model sizes further strengthens the paper's contribution, suggesting that F2F is a robust and generalizable approach for improving domain-specific performance in LLMs.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative application of machine unlearning to the problem of domain specialization in large language models. The proposed Forget-to-Focus (F2F) protocol, which involves an initial unlearning phase followed by fine-tuning, is a novel approach that moves beyond the traditional use of unlearning for privacy purposes. This creative application of unlearning is a significant contribution, offering a new perspective on how to adapt pre-trained models to specific domains. Furthermore, the paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of the F2F protocol across multiple domains, including medical, mathematics, and coding, and across various model architectures and sizes. This extensive experimentation demonstrates the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method, showing that F2F consistently outperforms standard fine-tuning baselines. The authors also explore the impact of different unlearning methods, forget set construction strategies, and model sizes, providing valuable insights into the practical application of F2F. The consistent performance improvements observed across different settings highlight the effectiveness of the approach and its potential for real-world applications. Another notable strength is the inclusion of a theoretical analysis, which, while simplified, provides a mathematical framework for understanding the unlearning process. Although this analysis is based on a linear model, it offers a starting point for understanding the underlying mechanisms of F2F. Finally, the paper's demonstration that F2F improves model calibration is a crucial finding, as it highlights the potential of unlearning to enhance the reliability of LLMs in sensitive domains. The combination of novel methodology, extensive empirical validation, and theoretical grounding makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of domain adaptation for LLMs.


## Weaknesses:

First, the theoretical analysis, while present, is limited in its scope and depth. The authors explicitly acknowledge that they use a simplified linear model for their theoretical framework, stating, 'While LLM training objective is non-convex, we use a convex linear surrogate to clarify the mechanism in a setting where the optimization is normal and interpretable.' This simplification, while understandable, fails to capture the complexities of the non-linear loss landscape of large language models. The paper does not provide a theoretical analysis of how the unlearning process affects the geometry of the loss landscape or the distribution of gradients during subsequent fine-tuning in the non-linear setting. This lack of a deeper theoretical analysis weakens the foundation of the proposed method and limits our understanding of why F2F works effectively in practice. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more rigorous theoretical analysis that goes beyond the simplified linear case. This should involve investigating how the unlearning process alters the loss landscape of the pre-trained model and how this alteration impacts the subsequent fine-tuning process. This could include analyzing the Hessian of the loss function before and after unlearning to understand how the curvature and saddle points are affected. Furthermore, the authors could explore the relationship between the forget set and the gradient distribution during fine-tuning. For example, do the gradients during fine-tuning align better with the target domain when the model has undergone the unlearning process? Such an analysis would provide a deeper understanding of why the proposed method works and could potentially lead to more robust and generalizable unlearning strategies. The authors could also consider using tools from information theory to quantify the amount of domain-specific information removed during the unlearning phase. Second, to address the lack of detail in the forget set construction, the authors should provide a more precise definition of the criteria used to determine overlap between the forget set and the target domain. For example, if the target domain is medical, what specific keywords or concepts were used to identify and exclude medical-related text from the forget set? The authors should also discuss the potential impact of different overlap thresholds on the performance of the F2F protocol. Furthermore, the paper should include an analysis of the diversity and representativeness of the forget set. Is the forget set sufficiently diverse to ensure that the model unlearns a broad range of general knowledge, or is it biased towards certain types of information? A more detailed analysis of the forget set's characteristics would help to understand the method's sensitivity to the choice of the forget set and provide guidance for constructing effective forget sets in different domains. Third, the paper should include a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the F2F protocol. The authors should explore the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the forget set, the size of the forget set, and the specific unlearning algorithm used. For example, what happens if the forget set is too small or too large? What happens if the forget set contains information that is highly correlated with the target domain? The authors should also investigate the potential for the unlearning process to degrade the model's general capabilities. While the paper shows that the model retains general capabilities, a more detailed analysis of the trade-off between domain-specific performance and general capabilities would be valuable. The authors could also explore the computational cost of the F2F protocol and compare it to standard fine-tuning methods. A more comprehensive analysis of these limitations would provide a more balanced perspective on the proposed method and help to identify areas for future research. This should include the number of gradient updates, the memory footprint, and the wall-clock time for each unlearning method. The authors should also compare the computational cost of the unlearning phase to the cost of standard fine-tuning, providing a clear understanding of the trade-offs involved. Furthermore, the authors should investigate how the size of the 'forget' set impacts the computational cost and the final performance of the model. This analysis should be presented in a way that allows practitioners to make informed decisions about the practical applicability of the F2F protocol, considering their computational constraints. Fifth, to address the sensitivity of the F2F protocol to hyperparameters, the authors should conduct a systematic sensitivity analysis. This should involve varying the learning rate and the number of unlearning steps across a range of values and reporting the performance of the model on different domains. The authors should present the results in a way that clearly shows how the performance of the F2F protocol changes with different hyperparameter settings. This analysis should also include a discussion of the optimal hyperparameter values for each domain and model size, providing practical guidance for users of the F2F protocol. The authors should also investigate the interaction between different hyperparameters and their combined effect on the performance of the model. This analysis will help to understand the robustness of the F2F protocol and its sensitivity to variations in hyperparameter settings. Finally, to address the potential negative impacts of unlearning on general capabilities, the authors should evaluate the model's performance on a set of general benchmarks after applying the F2F protocol. This should include tasks that are not related to the target domain, such as common sense reasoning, reading comprehension, and other general language understanding tasks.
