## Summary:

This paper presents a novel perspective on the GPTQ (Greenplug-to-Quantized) algorithm, a widely used post-training quantization method for large language models (LLMs). The authors demonstrate that GPTQ is mathematically equivalent to Babai's nearest plane algorithm for solving the closest vector problem (CVP) on a lattice defined by the Hessian matrix of the layer's inputs. This equivalence provides a geometric interpretation of GPTQ, which was previously understood primarily through algebraic updates. The paper further derives a layer-wise error bound for GPTQ under the assumption of no weight clipping, leveraging this theoretical insight to propose new post-training quantization methods that avoid weight clipping. These methods, including Scale-adjusted SpQR (SSQR) and Huffman-encoded post-training quantization (HPTQ), are shown to outperform the original GPTQ in terms of accuracy. Additionally, the authors develop efficient GPU inference kernels for the proposed representations, making the methods more practical for real-world deployment. The paper's core contributions are significant, as they place GPTQ on a firm theoretical footing and open the door to importing decades of progress in lattice algorithms to the design of future quantization algorithms for billion-parameter models. However, the paper's focus on linear layers and the no-clipping assumption, as well as the limited experimental evaluation, suggest areas for improvement and further research.

## Strengths:

One of the most compelling aspects of this paper is its novel connection between GPTQ and Babai's nearest plane algorithm, which provides a deeper understanding of GPTQ's inner workings. This geometric interpretation is not only theoretically interesting but also has practical implications, as it allows for the derivation of a tight error bound for GPTQ under the no-clipping assumption. The authors rigorously prove the equivalence between GPTQ and Babai's algorithm, establishing a solid theoretical foundation for their claims. The paper is well-written and clearly structured, making it accessible to readers with a background in lattice theory and quantization. The introduction of new post-training quantization methods, such as SSQR and HPTQ, is a significant practical contribution. These methods avoid weight clipping, a common issue in quantization that can lead to significant errors, and demonstrate improved accuracy compared to the original GPTQ. The development of efficient GPU inference kernels for the proposed representations further enhances the practical value of the work, bridging the gap between theoretical advancements and real-world applications. The experimental results, while limited in scope, provide a strong initial validation of the proposed methods, showing that they can achieve better performance on the WikiText-2 dataset and the Qwen3 model family. Overall, the paper's contributions are substantial and have the potential to influence future research in LLM quantization.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and areas for improvement are evident. One of the primary concerns is the limited experimental evaluation. The paper primarily tests the proposed methods on the WikiText-2 dataset and the Qwen3 model family, with some additional results on Llama models in the appendix. While the range of model sizes is commendable, the lack of diversity in datasets and tasks limits the generalizability of the findings. For instance, the paper does not explore how the proposed methods perform on datasets with varying input distributions and complexities, such as those used in different natural language processing tasks. This limitation is particularly important because the performance of quantization methods can vary significantly across different types of data. A more comprehensive experimental evaluation, including a wider range of models and datasets, would provide a more robust understanding of the practical applicability of the proposed methods and their limitations (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Figures 4 and 5, Appendix E.4). Another critical weakness is the paper's focus on linear layers. The theoretical analysis and proposed methods are primarily discussed in the context of linear layers, with no explicit extension or discussion of how they can be adapted to handle other types of layers commonly found in LLMs, such as attention layers. Attention layers pose unique challenges due to their quadratic complexity and the presence of non-linear activation functions. The absence of a discussion on these aspects limits the applicability of the work to a broader range of models and scenarios (Confidence Level: High; Section 3.1, Section 4, Section 5). The theoretical analysis relies heavily on the assumption of no weight clipping, which may not always be feasible in practice, especially for very low bitwidths. The paper does not explore the implications of this assumption on the practical performance of the proposed methods, particularly when clipping is unavoidable. It is unclear how the derived error bounds would be affected by the introduction of clipping, and whether the proposed methods would still offer a significant advantage over existing clipping-based quantization techniques in such scenarios. This limitation is crucial because weight clipping is a common practice in quantization, and understanding its impact is essential for practical applications (Confidence Level: High; Theorem 5, Section 5). The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed methods, particularly in comparison to the original GPTQ. While the authors mention that they provide efficient GPU inference kernels, they do not provide a comprehensive breakdown of the time and memory requirements of the quantization process itself, especially when applied to large models with billions of parameters. This makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the proposed methods for large-scale applications, where computational efficiency is a key consideration (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Figure 4(c)). Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with other state-of-the-art quantization techniques that also address the clipping issue. The baselines in the experiments are limited to RTN, the original GPTQ, and HRTN, without a thorough comparison against methods that use adaptive quantization ranges or more sophisticated techniques for handling outliers. This makes it challenging to contextualize the contributions of this work and highlight its advantages and disadvantages (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Appendix E.4). Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the practical implications of the theoretical error bound derived from Babai's algorithm. While the paper presents the bound and mentions a scenario where the expected error is a fraction of the worst-case, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how this bound behaves across different model architectures or how well it predicts the observed quantization error in practice. This makes it difficult to assess the tightness of the bound and its usefulness in guiding the design of quantization strategies (Confidence Level: High; Theorem 5, Section 5).


## Suggestions:

To enhance the practical impact and robustness of this work, several concrete improvements are recommended. First, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed quantization methods. This should include testing on a wider range of models, including large language models with billions of parameters, and across diverse datasets with varying characteristics. The evaluation should not only focus on accuracy metrics but also on other relevant metrics such as inference speed and memory footprint. For example, experiments could be conducted on models with different layer structures, activation functions, and training regimes to assess the robustness of the proposed quantization techniques. This would provide a more complete picture of the practical applicability of the methods and their limitations (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Appendix E.4). Second, the authors should extend the analysis to other types of layers commonly found in large language models, particularly attention layers. This would involve adapting the geometric framework to handle the unique characteristics of attention mechanisms, such as the quadratic complexity of the attention matrix. One possible approach could be to explore the use of block-wise quantization techniques, where the attention matrix is divided into smaller blocks that can be quantized independently. Additionally, the paper should address the quantization of non-linear activation functions, which are also crucial components of modern neural networks. This could involve investigating the use of techniques such as piecewise linear approximations or other non-linear quantization methods. By extending the analysis to these other layer types, the practical applicability of the proposed methods would be significantly increased (Confidence Level: High; Section 3.1, Section 4, Section 5). Third, the authors should investigate the behavior of the proposed methods when clipping is necessary. This could involve developing adaptive clipping strategies that minimize the impact on the derived error bounds. A more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between accuracy and computational efficiency when using no-clipping methods compared to traditional clipping-based approaches would be beneficial. This could include exploring different bit-widths and analyzing the resulting performance degradation under various clipping scenarios. Such an analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the practical applicability of the proposed methods in real-world scenarios (Confidence Level: High; Theorem 5, Section 5). Fourth, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed methods, particularly in comparison to the original GPTQ. This should include a step-by-step breakdown of the time and memory requirements of the quantization process, considering the cost of each operation, such as matrix decompositions, multiplications, and other relevant computations. Expressing the complexity in terms of the dimensions of the matrices involved would allow for a more precise comparison. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the practical implications of the complexity analysis, such as the expected runtime of the proposed method on different hardware platforms and for different model sizes. This would help readers better understand the trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Figure 4(c)). Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of their proposed methods with other state-of-the-art quantization techniques that also address the clipping issue. This should include a comparison with methods that use adaptive quantization ranges, those that employ more sophisticated techniques for handling outliers, and those that use different optimization strategies. The comparison should not only focus on accuracy metrics but also on other relevant metrics such as inference speed and memory footprint. By addressing these points, the paper would provide a more complete and convincing evaluation of the proposed methods (Confidence Level: High; Section 5, Appendix E.4).
