## Summary:

This paper introduces WeCAN, a novel end-to-end reinforcement learning framework designed for scheduling Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) in heterogeneous computing environments. The core contribution of WeCAN lies in its ability to rapidly generate schedules through a single-pass network inference, leveraging a weighted cross-attention mechanism to capture task-resource compatibility. This mechanism, inspired by the Transformer architecture, allows the model to learn complex relationships between tasks and available resources. Furthermore, the framework incorporates a longest directed distance graph neural network (LDDGNN) to adapt to varying task dependencies, ensuring that the scheduling process respects the inherent structure of the DAG. A key innovation is the introduction of a skip-action mechanism, which addresses the optimality gap often found in list-scheduling-based methods. This mechanism allows the model to strategically skip certain scheduling decisions, potentially leading to more optimal solutions. The authors evaluate WeCAN on two datasets: TPC-H, a benchmark derived from industrial queries, and a synthetic Computation Graphs dataset. The experimental results demonstrate that WeCAN outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of makespan and computational efficiency. Specifically, WeCAN achieves lower makespans compared to both heuristic and neural baselines, while also exhibiting rapid schedule generation. The paper also includes an analysis of the optimality gap in list-scheduling methods, providing a theoretical justification for the proposed skip-action mechanism. Overall, the paper presents a technically sound and well-motivated approach to DAG scheduling in heterogeneous environments, with a focus on both performance and efficiency. The combination of weighted cross-attention, LDDGNN, and the skip-action mechanism represents a significant step forward in the field of learning-based scheduling. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened to further validate its claims and broaden its impact.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper presents a novel combination of techniques, the technical contribution is somewhat limited by its reliance on existing architectures. The weighted cross-attention mechanism is explicitly based on the Transformer architecture, as stated in Section 3.1: "Based on the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) and skip-connection (He et al., 2016), our WeCA layer executes message passing between tasks and pools." Similarly, the LDDGNN is inspired by Graphormer and Topformer, as noted in Section 3.1: "Inspired by Graphormer (Ying et al., 2021) and Topformer (Gagrani et al., 2022), we design the network through attention masks and biases, but based on longest directed distance (LDD) $d_c$ which is defined as the signed length of the longest directed path." While the specific combination and application of these techniques to the DAG scheduling problem is novel, the paper does not fully articulate the specific technical challenges addressed by their particular architecture beyond the combination of existing components. This reliance on existing techniques, while not inherently negative, does limit the perceived novelty of the technical contribution. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper itself acknowledges the basis of its architecture in existing works. Second, the paper's focus on a single-pass setting raises concerns about its suitability for all DAG scheduling scenarios. The paper emphasizes the rapid schedule generation of WeCAN through single-pass network inference, as stated in the abstract: "WeCAN rapidly generates schedules through single-pass network inference." However, the paper does not adequately explore the trade-offs between single-pass and multi-pass approaches, particularly in complex scheduling scenarios where iterative refinement might be beneficial. As noted in the introduction, "Most ML approaches learn policies to construct solutions sequentially (Kool et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Alternative approaches include learning policies to refine existing solutions (Wu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2021; 2024) or simplify problem instances (Li et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2024)." The lack of direct comparison with multi-round methods in the experiments further supports this concern. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's emphasis on single-pass inference is clear, and the absence of multi-pass comparisons is evident. Third, the generalization ability of the WeCAN framework is not fully evaluated. While the paper uses two datasets, the TPC-H dataset primarily varies in the number of tasks, and the Computation Graphs dataset has a fixed size of 500 tasks. The experiments in Figure 2 show evaluations under different environment fluctuations, but the core task and pool characteristics are still derived from the TPC-H dataset. The paper lacks evaluation on datasets with varying DAG sizes and complexities, which is crucial for assessing the robustness of the proposed approach. My confidence in this assessment is medium, as the paper does include some generalization experiments, but the scope of these experiments is limited. Fourth, the paper lacks sufficient details on the data generation process. The paper mentions the sources of the datasets and some modifications, but it doesn't provide specifics on the distributions and parameters used to ensure diversity and representativeness. For the TPC-H dataset, the paper states: "We use the version sorted by Wang et al. (2021), and add additional random memory constraints and task types (each with a group of compatibility coefficients)." For the Computation Graphs dataset, the paper states: "a synthetic dataset generated using approaches from Jeon et al. (2023)." However, the specific parameters used to generate the data are not provided. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the quality and representativeness of the training data. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary details on the data generation process. Fifth, the practical implementation and impact of the skip-action mechanism are not thoroughly analyzed. While the paper describes the mechanism, it doesn't quantify its impact on runtime or how this scales with problem size. The paper states: "For addressing this problem, we use the network to produce skip coefficients $u_a \geq 0, u_b \geq 0$ and $u_c$ which are derived from an MLP with the average of task embeddings $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_v$ and the average of pool embeddings $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_c^c$ as input, and then calculate skip score as $u_{\pi_{skip}} = u_a(1 - \frac{k}{2n})^{u_b} + u_c$." However, the paper does not provide a separate breakdown of the time spent on calculating skip scores. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper lacks a detailed computational overhead analysis for the skip action. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the limitations of the proposed approach in dynamic environments. The method description focuses on a single-pass inference for a given static DAG and resource pool, and the experimental setup uses static instances of the datasets. The paper does not discuss how the model would adapt to changes in task dependencies or resource availability during execution. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's focus is on static scheduling, and it doesn't address the challenges of adapting to changes during execution. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be strengthened to further validate its claims and broaden its impact.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should more clearly articulate the novelty of their approach beyond the combination of existing techniques. While the application of weighted cross-attention and GNNs to DAG scheduling is interesting, the paper needs to highlight the specific technical challenges addressed by their particular architecture. For example, a more detailed analysis of how the weighted cross-attention mechanism is adapted to handle the specific constraints of heterogeneous environments would be beneficial. This could involve a deeper discussion of the attention bias and how it incorporates the compatibility coefficients. Second, the paper should explore the limitations of the single-pass approach more thoroughly. While the efficiency of single-pass inference is a clear advantage, the authors should investigate scenarios where multi-pass approaches might be necessary or more effective. This could involve a theoretical analysis of the optimality gap introduced by the single-pass setting or an empirical study comparing the performance of single-pass and multi-pass approaches on a range of challenging scheduling problems. The authors could also consider incorporating techniques such as iterative refinement or reinforcement learning to improve the performance of their method in complex scenarios. A more detailed discussion of the trade-offs between computational efficiency and solution quality would be valuable. Third, the authors need to provide more comprehensive experimental results to support their claims of generalization. This should include experiments on a wider range of datasets with varying DAG sizes, complexities, and resource constraints. The authors should also analyze the performance of their method under different levels of heterogeneity and provide insights into how the method adapts to these variations. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to evaluate the method's performance on real-world scheduling problems, which often exhibit characteristics not captured by synthetic datasets. This would help to demonstrate the practical relevance and applicability of the proposed approach. Fourth, to address the concerns regarding training data, the authors should provide a detailed description of the data generation process, including the range of parameters used to create the synthetic datasets and the rationale behind these choices. It is crucial to demonstrate that the generated data covers a wide spectrum of realistic scenarios, including variations in task dependencies, resource requirements, and pool characteristics. The authors should also consider incorporating real-world datasets, if available, to further validate the robustness of their approach. A sensitivity analysis of the model's performance with respect to different training data distributions would also be beneficial, showing how the model's performance changes when trained on data with different characteristics. This would provide a clearer understanding of the model's limitations and its ability to generalize to unseen scenarios. Fifth, regarding the skip-action mechanism, a more detailed analysis of its computational overhead is necessary. The authors should provide a breakdown of the time spent on calculating skip scores and how this time scales with the number of tasks and resource pools. It would be helpful to compare the runtime of WeCAN with and without the skip action to quantify the actual overhead. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of the skip action on the quality of the generated schedules. It is important to understand whether the skip action consistently leads to improved makespan or if there are cases where it might lead to suboptimal solutions. A more thorough analysis of the skip action's behavior under different conditions would strengthen the paper's claims. Finally, the paper should include a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the WeCAN framework, particularly in scenarios with highly dynamic environments or real-time constraints. The authors should consider adding experiments that simulate changes in task dependencies or resource availability during execution. This could involve introducing random task failures, resource additions or removals, or changes in task priorities. The paper should also discuss the potential overhead associated with retraining or adapting the model in such scenarios. Furthermore, the authors should explore the use of techniques such as online learning or transfer learning to improve the adaptability of the framework to dynamic environments. A detailed analysis of the model's sensitivity to changes in the environment would also be beneficial, including how the model's performance varies with different levels of dynamism. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and broaden its impact.
