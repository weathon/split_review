## Summary:

This paper introduces EconAgentBench, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the capabilities of Large Language Model (LLM) agents in economic decision-making tasks. The core contribution lies in the creation of three distinct, scalable environments—procurement, scheduling, and pricing—each designed to simulate real-world economic scenarios. These environments are structured to allow for varying levels of difficulty, enabling a nuanced assessment of LLM agent performance. The authors employ a multi-turn interaction paradigm, where LLM agents interact with the environment through a defined set of tools, receiving feedback and adjusting their strategies over time. The paper evaluates the performance of several state-of-the-art LLM agents, including models like GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, across these environments. The empirical findings reveal that even advanced models struggle with the harder instances, suggesting that the benchmark effectively differentiates between the capabilities of LLMs at different difficulty levels. The authors also provide some initial analysis of the results beyond just reporting benchmark scores, examining metrics such as budget utilization, best-so-far rate, and adaptability. These metrics aim to provide a deeper understanding of the agents' behavior and their ability to learn and adapt within the economic environments. The overall significance of this work lies in its potential to drive the development of more capable LLM agents for economic applications, offering a structured and scalable framework for evaluating their performance in complex decision-making tasks. The benchmark's design, with its focus on multi-turn interactions and scalable difficulty, represents a valuable contribution to the field of LLM evaluation, particularly in the context of economic reasoning and strategy.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the design of EconAgentBench itself is a significant contribution. The creation of three distinct economic environments—procurement, scheduling, and pricing—each with scalable difficulty, is both novel and relevant. This approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of LLM agents than a single-task benchmark would provide. The environments are well-motivated by real-world economic scenarios, and the use of synthetic data enables the authors to control the complexity of the tasks and generate a large number of instances for testing. The multi-turn interaction paradigm, where LLM agents interact with the environment through a defined set of tools, is also a strength, as it more closely mirrors real-world decision-making processes than single-turn benchmarks.
