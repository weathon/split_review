## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel multi-task Visuo-Tactile World Model (VT-WM) designed to enhance robotic manipulation by integrating tactile sensing with visual information. The core idea is to leverage the high-frequency, local contact feedback from tactile sensors to improve the model's understanding of object permanence and compliance with physical laws, particularly in contact-rich tasks where vision alone often falls short. The authors employ pre-trained encoders, specifically the Cosmos tokenizer for vision and Sparsh-X for tactile data from Digit 360 sensors, to extract latent representations of the environment. These latent states are then fed into a transformer-based predictor that learns to forecast future visual and tactile states conditioned on robot actions. The model is trained on a diverse set of manipulation tasks, and its performance is evaluated through a combination of contact perception metrics and zero-shot planning experiments on a real robot. The empirical findings demonstrate that the VT-WM significantly outperforms a vision-only model in terms of object permanence, causal compliance, and success rates in zero-shot planning scenarios. Specifically, the VT-WM shows a 33% gain in object permanence and a 29% improvement in compliance with physical laws, as measured by the Normalized Fréchet distance. Furthermore, the model exhibits a 35% higher success rate in zero-shot planning on real robots compared to the vision-only model. These results highlight the crucial role of tactile information in enabling more reliable and physically grounded robotic manipulation. The authors also demonstrate that the VT-WM can be effectively used for model predictive control (MPC), allowing the robot to plan action sequences based on the predicted future states. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning by showcasing the benefits of incorporating tactile sensing into world models and by providing a robust framework for learning and planning in contact-rich environments. The paper's focus on real-world experiments further underscores the practical relevance of the proposed approach, paving the way for more capable and versatile robotic systems.

## Strengths:

By incorporating tactile data, the model gains a more grounded understanding of object interactions, leading to significant improvements in object permanence and causal compliance. The use of pre-trained encoders, specifically the Cosmos tokenizer for vision and Sparsh-X for tactile data, is a smart approach that leverages existing resources and allows the model to focus on learning the dynamics of the environment. The experimental design is also commendable, with a clear progression from contact perception evaluations to zero-shot planning experiments on a real robot. The results are compelling, demonstrating a substantial performance gap between the VT-WM and the vision-only model across various metrics. The 33% gain in object permanence and the 29% improvement in compliance with physical laws are particularly noteworthy, highlighting the effectiveness of the tactile information in enhancing the model's understanding of the physical world. Finally, the paper's focus on real-world experiments adds significant value, demonstrating the potential of the VT-WM for practical robotic applications.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the VT-WM. This section should analyze scenarios where the model is likely to fail, such as when dealing with novel objects, complex object interactions, or noisy sensory data. The authors should also discuss the potential for compounding errors in long-horizon predictions and how this might affect the model's ability to plan effectively. Furthermore, they should suggest potential mitigation strategies for these limitations, such as incorporating uncertainty estimation or using more robust tactile sensing techniques. This would provide a more balanced view of the model's capabilities and guide future research in this area. 

Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the tactile encoder and its latent space. This should include an explanation of how the tactile encoder is trained, what kind of information is captured in the latent space, and how well it aligns with the requirements of the world model. The authors could perform ablation studies to evaluate the contribution of the tactile encoder to the overall performance of the VT-WM. For example, they could compare the performance of the VT-WM with and without the tactile encoder, or with different tactile encoders. Additionally, they could visualize the latent space of the tactile encoder to gain a better understanding of the information it captures. This would help to justify the use of the pre-trained tactile encoder and provide insights into the role of tactile information in the world model. 

Third, the authors should provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the world model. This should include a clear definition and explanation of the Fréchet distance and why it is a suitable metric for evaluating object permanence and causal compliance. The authors should also consider other metrics to evaluate the world model, such as the prediction error of the next state, the accuracy of object tracking, and the ability to predict contact events. The authors could also evaluate the world model on a wider range of manipulation tasks and compare its performance with other state-of-the-art world models. This would provide a more thorough assessment of the capabilities and limitations of the proposed method. 

Fourth, the authors should include a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. This should include information on the model's memory footprint, training time, and inference time, as well as how these scale with the size of the input data and the complexity of the tasks. The authors should also discuss the hardware requirements for training and deploying the model. This information is crucial for assessing the practicality of the proposed approach and for determining its suitability for real-world applications. Furthermore, the paper should discuss potential optimizations that could be used to reduce the computational cost of the model, such as model pruning or quantization. 

Fifth, to address the limited generalizability, the authors should conduct experiments with other tactile sensors, such as BioTac or TacTips, to evaluate the robustness of their method across diverse tactile sensing modalities. This would involve adapting the input processing pipeline to accommodate the different data formats and sensing characteristics of these sensors. Furthermore, the model should be evaluated on different robotic platforms, including those with varying kinematics and dynamics. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's robustness and adaptability. 

Finally, the authors should conduct a thorough comparison with other state-of-the-art visuo-tactile world models. This should include a comparison of performance metrics, such as object permanence, compliance with physical laws, and zero-shot planning success, as well as computational efficiency and robustness. The authors should also discuss the differences in the underlying architectures and training methodologies of the compared methods. This comparison should not only highlight the strengths of the proposed method but also identify its limitations and areas for improvement.
