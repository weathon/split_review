## Summary:

This paper introduces USR 2.0, an enhanced version of the Unified Speech Recognition (USR) framework, designed to improve training efficiency and robustness in speech recognition tasks. The core contribution of USR 2.0 lies in its novel pseudo-labeling approach, which leverages CTC-driven teacher forcing to accelerate training and mitigate the computational bottleneck associated with autoregressive decoding. Instead of relying solely on autoregressive decoding for generating pseudo-labels, USR 2.0 employs greedy CTC decoding to produce pseudo-labels, which are then fed into the decoder to generate attention-based pseudo-labels in a single forward pass. This approach significantly reduces the training time by removing the sequential dependency of autoregressive decoding. To address the exposure bias introduced by this CTC-driven approach, the authors propose a mixed sampling strategy that intermittently reintroduces autoregressive decoding during training, balancing the efficiency gains with the coherence of traditional attention-based models. The method is evaluated on multiple benchmarks, including LRS3, LRS2, and WildVSR, demonstrating state-of-the-art results and improved robustness to out-of-distribution data, such as noisy audio and longer sequences. The authors also demonstrate the scalability of USR 2.0 by training a large model on a substantial amount of unlabelled data, achieving impressive word error rates (WERs) on various tasks. The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation, including robustness to long utterances, noisy conditions, and unseen datasets, and provides ablation studies to analyze the contributions of different components. Overall, USR 2.0 presents a significant advancement in unified speech recognition, offering a more efficient and robust approach for training speech recognition models, particularly in scenarios with limited labeled data and diverse acoustic conditions.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The most significant strength is the introduction of CTC-driven teacher forcing, which effectively addresses the computational bottleneck of autoregressive decoding in the pseudo-labeling process. By using greedy CTC decoding to generate pseudo-labels, the authors achieve a substantial reduction in training time, making USR 2.0 much more practical for large-scale applications. This is a crucial improvement, as the computational cost of training speech recognition models can be prohibitive, especially when dealing with large datasets and complex models. The paper also demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach through comprehensive empirical evaluations, achieving state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmarks, including LRS3, LRS2, and WildVSR. The improved robustness to out-of-distribution data, such as noisy audio and longer sequences, is another notable strength. This is essential for real-world applications where data distribution can vary significantly. The authors' use of mixed sampling to mitigate the exposure bias introduced by CTC-driven teacher forcing is also a clever and effective strategy. By intermittently reintroducing autoregressive decoding during training, they balance the efficiency gains with the coherence of traditional attention-based models. Furthermore, the paper provides a thorough analysis of the robustness of USR 2.0 to out-of-distribution data, including noisy audio and long utterances, which are critical challenges in real-world ASR tasks. The ablation studies provide valuable insights into the contributions of different components, such as the CTC-driven teacher forcing and mixed sampling strategies. Finally, the authors demonstrate the scalability of USR 2.0 by training a large model on a substantial amount of unlabelled data, achieving impressive performance gains. This scalability is crucial for leveraging the vast amounts of unlabelled data available for speech recognition tasks.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the specific implementation of CTC-driven teacher forcing and mixed sampling is novel, the underlying concepts are not entirely new. The paper could benefit from a more detailed comparison with existing methods that use similar techniques, emphasizing the unique aspects of their implementation and the specific advantages it offers. The authors do not sufficiently articulate the specific novel aspects of their implementation beyond the existing literature, which makes it harder to assess the true novelty of the proposed approach. Second, the success of the method relies heavily on the quality of CTC pseudo-labels. If the CTC model is not well-trained, the subsequent attention-based pseudo-labels may be of low quality, potentially degrading the overall performance. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of how errors in CTC predictions propagate through the system and affect the final attention-based model performance. This dependence on CTC quality is a significant limitation, as it could limit the applicability of the method in scenarios where CTC performance is not optimal. Third, the mixed sampling strategy and the integration of CTC and attention branches add complexity to the training process, which may pose challenges for reproducibility and adoption. While the paper describes the methods and provides some implementation details, it could benefit from a more detailed breakdown of the implementation steps and specific hyperparameter settings. The description of the mixed sampling strategy could be more precise, detailing how the probability of using CTC-driven teacher forcing versus autoregressive decoding is determined and how it changes during training. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for training USR 2.0. While the paper mentions reduced training time, it does not provide specific details on the hardware used, the memory footprint of the model, or the training time per epoch. This information is crucial for assessing the practical applicability of the method, especially for researchers with limited computational resources. Fifth, the paper could benefit from a more thorough analysis of the limitations of CTC-driven teacher forcing, particularly in scenarios where the CTC pseudo-labels are not reliable. The reliance on CTC pseudo-labels introduces a potential vulnerability; if the CTC model struggles with certain acoustic conditions or speaker variations, the subsequent attention-based pseudo-labels will likely inherit these errors, leading to compounding issues during training. This is especially concerning in low-resource settings or when dealing with highly diverse datasets where CTC performance might be suboptimal. Finally, while the paper includes ablation studies on pseudo-label types and mixed sampling probability, the reviewer suggests further exploration of specific aspects like loss weighting and training solely on one type of pseudo-label. The paper does not explore the impact of different CTC architectures or loss functions on the overall performance of USR 2.0, which could be a potential area for improvement. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the individual contributions of the CTC and attention branches to the final performance. These limitations, while not invalidating the contributions of the paper, highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to fully understand the potential and limitations of USR 2.0.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more thorough analysis of the novelty of the proposed approach. While the individual components, such as CTC-driven teacher forcing and mixed sampling, are not entirely new, the specific combination and application within the USR framework could be highlighted more clearly. The authors should provide a detailed comparison with existing methods that use similar techniques, emphasizing the unique aspects of their implementation and the specific advantages it offers. For example, a discussion of how their approach differs from other methods that use CTC for guiding attention training, and what specific modifications they have made to adapt it to the USR framework, would be valuable. Second, to address the dependence on CTC quality, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of how errors in CTC predictions affect the overall performance of the model. This could involve experiments where the CTC model is intentionally degraded (e.g., by adding noise to the CTC logits or using a lower-quality CTC model) and observing the impact on the final attention-based model performance. Such an analysis would provide valuable insights into the robustness of the proposed method and its limitations. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques to mitigate the impact of CTC errors, such as using confidence scores to filter out low-quality CTC predictions or incorporating error correction mechanisms into the training process. This would make the method more robust and applicable to a wider range of scenarios. Third, to improve the reproducibility of the work, the authors should provide a more detailed description of the implementation process, including specific hyperparameter settings, computational resource requirements, and any other relevant details. This could include a step-by-step guide to implementing the mixed sampling strategy and CTC-driven teacher forcing, as well as a discussion of any challenges or pitfalls that they encountered during the implementation process. The authors should also consider releasing their code and trained models to the public, which would greatly facilitate the adoption of their method by other researchers. Fourth, the authors should provide more detailed information on the computational resources required for training USR 2.0. This should include the specific hardware used (e.g., GPU model, number of GPUs), the memory footprint of the model, and the training time per epoch. It would also be helpful to provide a breakdown of the computational cost of different components of the training process, such as the CTC and attention branches. This information would allow other researchers to better assess the practical applicability of the method and to reproduce the results more easily. Additionally, the authors could explore techniques for optimizing the training process, such as mixed-precision training or model parallelism, to further reduce the computational cost. Fifth, to address the limitations of CTC-driven teacher forcing, the authors should explore techniques to mitigate the impact of noisy CTC pseudo-labels. One approach could be to incorporate a confidence measure for the CTC predictions, using only high-confidence pseudo-labels for training the attention-based model. Another strategy could involve using a more robust CTC model, perhaps pre-trained on a larger and more diverse dataset, to generate the initial pseudo-labels. Furthermore, the authors could investigate methods for error correction in the CTC pseudo-labels, such as using a language model to refine the generated sequences. These techniques could help to reduce the propagation of errors from the CTC branch to the attention branch, leading to more stable and reliable training. Finally, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive ablation study. This should include varying the mixing ratio between CTC and attention-based pseudo-labels and analyzing the impact on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance. Additionally, the authors should investigate the effect of different weighting schemes for the CTC and attention losses. It would also be beneficial to analyze the performance of the model when trained solely on CTC pseudo-labels or solely on attention pseudo-labels, to understand the individual contributions of each branch. This analysis should be performed across different datasets and acoustic conditions to provide a more complete picture of the model's behavior. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different sampling strategies during training, such as curriculum learning, to see if it can further improve the model's robustness.
