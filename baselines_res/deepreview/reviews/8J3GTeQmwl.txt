## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel graphon cross-validation (CV) method designed for selecting tuning parameters and evaluation approaches within the context of graphon models. The core contribution lies in the development of a computationally efficient and theoretically sound cross-validation technique that addresses the challenges of applying traditional CV methods to network data. The proposed method leverages a random imputation strategy to create validation sets, which allows for the use of standard cross-validation principles in a setting where edge independence is not naturally present. The authors demonstrate that the proposed cross-validation score is asymptotically aligned with the estimation error, and the selected model asymptotically converges to the optimal model. This is a significant advancement, as it provides a principled way to tune parameters in graphon models, which are increasingly used to represent complex network structures. The paper's methodological approach involves a perturbation-based sampling method that splits the data into training and testing sets while preserving the network's inherent distribution. This is achieved by randomly removing edges and imputing them with values drawn from a Bernoulli distribution, ensuring that the training and validation data remain representative of the overall network. The authors provide a theoretical analysis that supports the proposed method, showing that the cross-validation score is consistent with the true estimation error. This theoretical grounding is crucial for establishing the reliability of the method. Empirically, the paper presents extensive simulations and real-world applications to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The simulations compare the proposed method against edge cross-validation (ECV) and default selection methods, showing that the proposed method achieves superior performance in terms of both computational efficiency and accuracy. The real-world applications further validate the method's practical utility, demonstrating its ability to select appropriate tuning parameters in various network settings. The paper's overall significance lies in its contribution to the field of network analysis by providing a robust and efficient method for parameter selection in graphon models. The proposed method addresses a key challenge in this area and offers a valuable tool for researchers and practitioners working with complex network data. The combination of theoretical guarantees, computational efficiency, and empirical validation makes this paper a significant contribution to the literature on graphon models and network analysis.

## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, the paper has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. One significant weakness is the limited scope of the experimental comparisons. While the paper compares the proposed method against ECV and default selection methods, it lacks a broader comparison against other established parameter selection techniques in the field. Specifically, the paper does not compare against methods based on information criteria, such as AIC or BIC, or other cross-validation techniques tailored to network data. This omission limits the assessment of the proposed method's relative performance. For instance, the "Experiments" section explicitly states that the baselines are "Edge cross-validation (ECV) method and default selection methods," and there is no mention of comparisons with other parameter selection methods. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to fully understand the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to the wider range of existing approaches. Another critical limitation is the lack of practical guidance on selecting the parameter $K$ in the $K$-fold cross-validation. The theoretical analysis suggests that $K$ should not be too large and should increase with the number of nodes $n$, but it does not offer concrete advice for practitioners on how to choose a specific value of $K$ in real-world applications. The paper mentions "K-fold cross-validation" in the "Graphon Cross-Validation with Random Imputation" section, and Theorem 1 states "As $n \rightarrow \infty$ and $K \rightarrow \infty$...", implying $K$ should increase with $n$. However, there is no specific practical guideline or formula for choosing $K$. This lack of guidance makes it challenging for practitioners to apply the method effectively. The paper also assumes edge independence, which may not hold in many real-world networks. The "Model Setup" section explicitly states: "In this article, we assume that the $a_{ij}$ s are independent and follow Bernoulli distributions with mean $p_{ij}$ respectively, i.e., $a_{ij} \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Ber}(p_{ij})$". The theoretical justification in Section 4 relies on this independence assumption. The paper does not discuss the limitations of the method when the independence assumption is violated, nor does it compare its performance to methods designed for dependent edge structures. This assumption limits the applicability of the method to networks where edges are not influenced by shared factors or underlying structures. Furthermore, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research. While the conclusion touches upon some aspects, a more thorough discussion of the limitations and future research directions would enhance the paper. The "CONCLUSIONS AND DISCUSSIONS" section is relatively brief in its discussion of limitations and future research. Finally, while the paper provides a theoretical complexity analysis and empirical comparisons of computational time, it could benefit from a more detailed breakdown of the computational cost components and a more explicit comparison with the computational costs of other existing parameter selection methods for graphon models (beyond ECV). The "Computational cost" subsection provides a theoretical complexity analysis: "O(|M| \cdot (KCestim(n) + n^2))" for CV-imputation and "O(|M| \cdot (KCestim(n) + KTmc(n)))" for ECV. However, a more granular breakdown and comparison with a wider range of existing methods would be beneficial. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's contributions, highlight areas where further research and development are needed to enhance the method's robustness and applicability.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the paper should significantly expand the experimental comparisons to include a wider range of existing parameter selection methods. Specifically, comparisons with methods based on information criteria, such as AIC and BIC, should be included. Additionally, other cross-validation techniques tailored to network data, beyond the current comparison with ECV, should be considered. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the proposed method's relative performance and help to establish its strengths and weaknesses compared to the broader landscape of existing approaches. The comparison should not only focus on the final estimation error but also on the computational cost and the sensitivity to different network sizes and structures. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method's strengths and weaknesses in various practical scenarios. Second, the paper needs to provide more practical guidance on selecting the parameter $K$ in the $K$-fold cross-validation. While the theoretical analysis suggests that $K$ should not be too large, it does not offer a clear criterion for selecting an appropriate value in practice. The paper should explore the sensitivity of the proposed method to different values of $K$ and provide recommendations based on the network size and the desired level of accuracy. For example, the authors could investigate the use of heuristic rules for selecting $K$, such as a function of the number of nodes or edges, or explore adaptive methods that dynamically adjust $K$ based on the data. Additionally, the paper should discuss the potential trade-offs between the choice of $K$ and the computational cost of the method. A more detailed analysis of the impact of $K$ on the method's performance would be valuable for practitioners. Third, the paper should address the limitations of the proposed method in the context of dependent edge structures. The current analysis assumes that the edges are independent, which may not hold in many real-world networks. The paper should discuss the potential impact of edge dependence on the performance of the proposed method and explore possible extensions to handle such dependencies. For example, the authors could investigate the use of block cross-validation or other techniques that account for the spatial or temporal dependencies in the network data. Furthermore, the paper should provide some empirical evidence of the method's performance under different types of edge dependencies, such as those arising from community structures or temporal dynamics. Addressing these limitations would significantly enhance the practical applicability of the proposed method. Fourth, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research. This discussion should go beyond the current brief mention in the conclusion and should delve into specific scenarios where the method might underperform. For example, the paper should explore the behavior of the method with estimators that have very different structural assumptions or optimization landscapes. The paper should also discuss potential avenues for future research, such as extending the method to handle dynamic graphon models or incorporating prior knowledge about the graphon structure into the cross-validation procedure. Finally, the paper should provide a more granular analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method, breaking down the cost into different components. For example, the cost of generating the perturbed graph, the cost of fitting the model on the training set, and the cost of evaluating the model on the validation set should be analyzed separately. This would allow for a more precise comparison with existing methods. The paper should also discuss the memory requirements of the proposed method, especially when dealing with large-scale networks. A detailed comparison of the computational cost and memory requirements with existing cross-validation methods for network data would be highly valuable. This should include a discussion of the practical limitations of the method in terms of scalability. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its impact on the field.
