## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to enhancing the monitorability of chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in large language models (LLMs), focusing specifically on improving faithfulness and conciseness. The authors identify a critical limitation of standard reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning: its struggle with sparse feedback, as it only receives signals upon completing the final answer, not for intermediate reasoning steps. To address this, they reformulate CoT monitorability as a constrained optimization problem and propose a prior-guided transformation pipeline. This pipeline leverages instruct models to transform existing CoT traces into more monitorable versions, ensuring they are both faithful and concise. The core idea is to use a prior model to generate improved reasoning traces, which are then used to fine-tune the base model via supervised learning. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of standard RL fine-tuning, which struggles with sparse feedback on intermediate reasoning steps. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method on several benchmark datasets, including MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, and MATH500, showing significant improvements in faithfulness (an additional 10%) and reductions in CoT length (up to 60%) while maintaining task accuracy. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel formulation of CoT monitorability as a constrained optimization problem and the introduction of a prior-guided transformation pipeline that effectively addresses the limitations of standard RL fine-tuning. The empirical results provide robust evidence supporting the effectiveness of the approach, showing substantial improvements in both faithfulness and conciseness without sacrificing accuracy. Overall, this work presents a significant step forward in improving the interpretability and reliability of CoT reasoning in LLMs, offering a promising direction for future research in this area. The method's ability to enhance monitorability while maintaining performance on benchmark tasks highlights its potential for real-world applications where trustworthy and efficient reasoning is crucial.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First and foremost, the authors introduce a novel approach to improving CoT monitorability by framing it as a constrained optimization problem. This is a significant departure from traditional methods that rely on direct RL fine-tuning, which often struggle with sparse feedback on intermediate reasoning steps. By reformulating the problem, the authors open up new avenues for addressing the challenges of CoT monitorability. The core of their method, the prior-guided transformation pipeline, is another significant strength. This pipeline leverages instruct models to transform existing CoT traces into more monitorable versions, ensuring they are both faithful and concise. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of standard RL fine-tuning, which struggles with sparse feedback on intermediate reasoning steps. The use of a prior model to generate improved reasoning traces is a clever way to provide dense feedback for training, leading to more effective learning of monitorable reasoning patterns. Furthermore, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate significant improvements in both faithfulness (an additional 10%) and conciseness (up to 60% reduction in CoT length) while maintaining high accuracy on benchmark tasks such as MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, and MATH500. These results provide robust evidence supporting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The fact that the method achieves these improvements without sacrificing accuracy is particularly noteworthy, as it highlights the practical applicability of the method. Finally, the paper is well-structured, with a clear problem formulation and detailed explanation of the proposed method. The empirical validation section provides robust evidence supporting the effectiveness of the approach. The authors clearly articulate their methodology and provide sufficient detail for others to understand and potentially replicate their work. The combination of a novel approach, a clever implementation, and strong empirical results makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of language model reasoning.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to improving CoT monitorability, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the method's reliance on an external prior model to generate monitorable traces. As stated in the paper, "At the core of our approach is a prior policy that acts as an auxiliary generator." This introduces a dependency that could affect the generalizability and robustness of the approach. The quality of the final output is heavily contingent on the strength and reliability of this prior, which may introduce biases or artifacts. Specifically, the reliance on a single prior model raises concerns about the potential for the final model to overfit to the characteristics of that specific prior, rather than learning a more generalizable reasoning process. This could limit the method's effectiveness when applied to tasks or datasets that differ significantly from those used to train the prior. The paper does not explore methods to mitigate this overfitting or investigate the sensitivity to different prior models, which is a significant limitation. My analysis confirms this dependency, as the paper explicitly states, "We use the **Qwen 2.5-7B Instruct** model as the prior policy $\pi_s$." This reliance on a specific model without further analysis of its impact is a clear weakness. Another significant weakness is the lack of a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods. While the authors demonstrate improvements over a baseline and a naive RL-trained model, the absence of comparisons against other established techniques for improving CoT monitorability makes it challenging to fully assess the relative performance and advantages of the proposed approach. For example, a comparison with methods that use different forms of supervision or reinforcement learning would provide a more complete picture of the method's strengths and weaknesses. The paper's experimental section only includes comparisons with a base model and a naive RL model, which is insufficient to establish the true novelty and impact of the work. This lack of broader comparison limits the ability to contextualize the method's performance within the existing literature. Furthermore, the paper's focus on faithfulness and conciseness as the primary dimensions of CoT trustworthiness may overlook other important aspects, such as completeness and relevance. The paper defines monitorability in terms of faithfulness and conciseness in the "Introduction" and "Problem Formulation" sections. While these are important, the emphasis on conciseness, while beneficial for reducing token length, might inadvertently lead to the omission of crucial intermediate steps in the reasoning process, thus compromising the completeness of the CoT. The paper does not adequately address how the proposed method ensures the relevance of the generated CoT steps to the final answer, which is essential for true monitorability. The evaluation metrics are primarily focused on faithfulness (verbalization of hints) and conciseness (length of the trace), with no metrics for completeness or relevance. This narrow focus on faithfulness and conciseness, without considering other crucial aspects of monitorability, is a significant limitation. Additionally, the formulation of conciseness is inconsistent between the introduction and the experiments. In the introduction, conciseness is defined as the reasoning must be short for effective monitoring. However, in the experiments, conciseness is defined as the proportion of responses with a length less than 950 tokens. The latter definition does not necessarily reflect the former. A CoT can be considered concise if it is segmented into several short reasoning chains, but if there exists a long reasoning chain within it, it cannot be regarded as concise, even if its overall length is less than 950. This discrepancy undermines the validity of the experimental results, as the optimization target is not clearly aligned with the initial definition of conciseness. The experimental definition, based on a token length threshold, is too simplistic and does not capture the nuances of conciseness as described in the introduction. Finally, the proposed method requires a large auxiliary model (e.g., Qwen 2.5-7B Instruct) to transform the original CoT traces into more monitorable forms. This introduces additional computational overhead and may limit the applicability of the method in resource-constrained environments. The paper mentions the hardware used, implying the need for significant resources. The reliance on a large auxiliary model without exploring more efficient alternatives is a practical limitation of the method. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be improved to strengthen its claims and broaden its impact.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. First, to mitigate the dependency on an external prior model, the authors should explore methods to reduce the potential for overfitting to the prior's characteristics. This could involve techniques such as adversarial training, where the model is trained to generate monitorable traces that are distinct from those produced by the prior. This would encourage the model to learn more generalizable reasoning patterns rather than simply mimicking the prior. Another approach could be to use ensemble methods that combine the outputs of multiple priors to reduce bias. This would make the method less sensitive to the specific characteristics of a single prior model. Additionally, the authors could investigate the use of a dynamically updated prior, where the prior is refined based on the performance of the base model during training. This would allow the prior to adapt to the specific needs of the base model, potentially leading to more robust and generalizable results. Furthermore, a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the final model to variations in the prior would be beneficial, including experiments with different prior models and training data. This would provide a better understanding of the method's robustness and limitations. Second, to better contextualize the performance of the proposed method, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art techniques for improving CoT monitorability. This should include methods that use different forms of supervision, such as reinforcement learning with intermediate rewards or techniques that leverage human feedback to guide the generation of monitorable CoTs. A detailed comparison should not only focus on overall performance metrics but also analyze the strengths and weaknesses of each method in different scenarios. For instance, it would be valuable to compare the proposed method's performance on tasks with varying levels of complexity and reasoning depth, as well as its robustness to adversarial examples. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. The comparison should also include a discussion of the computational cost and ease of implementation of each method, providing a more complete picture of the trade-offs involved. Third, the authors should expand their discussion of CoT trustworthiness to include aspects such as completeness and relevance, in addition to faithfulness and conciseness. This could involve developing new metrics to evaluate these dimensions and incorporating them into the training process. For example, a completeness metric could measure the extent to which the CoT covers all necessary steps in the reasoning process, while a relevance metric could assess the degree to which each step contributes to the final answer. The authors could also explore methods to explicitly encourage the generation of complete and relevant CoTs, such as using attention mechanisms to focus on the most important parts of the input or incorporating symbolic reasoning techniques to ensure logical consistency. This would lead to a more holistic approach to CoT monitorability and improve the overall trustworthiness of the reasoning models. Fourth, the paper's definition of conciseness needs to be revisited to ensure it accurately reflects the concept of brief and to-the-point reasoning. The current experimental definition, focusing on a length threshold, is too simplistic and does not capture the nuances of conciseness as described in the introduction. A more appropriate definition could involve measuring the redundancy of the reasoning steps or the information content per token. For example, the authors could explore metrics that penalize repetitive or unnecessary steps in the CoT, or they could use a measure of the information density of the generated text. This would require a more sophisticated approach than simply setting a length threshold. Furthermore, the authors should investigate whether the model is truly learning to be concise or just mimicking a length constraint. This could be done by analyzing the generated CoTs and checking for redundancy or unnecessary steps. The authors could also try to train the model with different length thresholds and see if the model can generalize to different lengths. This would provide more evidence that the model is truly learning to be concise. Finally, to address the concern about the auxiliary model's computational cost, the authors could explore alternative methods for generating monitorable traces. For instance, they could investigate techniques that use smaller models or rely on more efficient algorithms. Another approach could be to use a combination of a small model with a few examples of concise and faithful CoTs to guide the base model. This would reduce the computational overhead and make the method more practical. Additionally, the authors should consider the trade-off between the size of the auxiliary model and the quality of the generated traces. It is possible that a smaller auxiliary model could achieve comparable results with a more efficient training process. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the computational resources required for their method, including the training time and the inference time. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.
