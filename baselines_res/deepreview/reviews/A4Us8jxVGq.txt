## Summary:

This paper presents a novel theoretical framework for understanding how semantic associations emerge in attention-based language models during training. The authors propose a method to analyze the training dynamics by approximating the gradients of the weight matrices using a leading-term expansion. This approximation allows them to express the weight matrices as compositions of three basis functions: bigram mapping, interchangeability mapping, and context mapping. These basis functions capture different aspects of token associations, such as next-token prediction, token similarity, and longer-range dependencies. The theoretical findings are validated through experiments on a 3-layer transformer trained on a toy dataset and on two real-world large language models (LLMs), Pythia-1.4B and another LLM, demonstrating that the learned weights closely match the theoretical predictions, especially in the early stages of training. The paper's core contributions lie in its innovative use of gradient leading terms to derive closed-form expressions for the weights, the clear and interpretable characterization of the model's behavior through the three basis functions, and the empirical validation of these findings on both toy and real-world models. The significance of this work is in its potential to enhance mechanistic interpretability and provide a deeper understanding of representation learning in language models, which could have implications for improving model design and training efficiency.

## Strengths:

One of the most compelling aspects of this paper is its novel theoretical framework for understanding the emergence of semantic associations in transformers. The use of gradient leading terms to derive closed-form expressions for the weights is a significant technical innovation that provides a fresh perspective on the training dynamics of these models. The identification of three basis functions—bigram mapping, interchangeability mapping, and context mapping—offers a clear and interpretable characterization of the model's behavior. This decomposition is particularly valuable because it breaks down the complex learning process into manageable and understandable components, each capturing a different aspect of token associations. The paper is well-written and structured, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide clear explanations of the theoretical framework, mathematical derivations, and experimental results, supported by figures and examples that effectively illustrate the key concepts. The empirical validation on both a toy dataset and real-world LLMs strengthens the credibility of the theoretical findings, showing that the results are not limited to simplified settings. The experiments on the 3-layer transformer and the Pythia-1.4B model, in particular, demonstrate that the learned weights closely match the theoretical predictions, especially in the early stages of training. This alignment between theory and practice is a strong indicator of the robustness of the proposed framework. The findings have important implications for both mechanistic interpretability and the broader understanding of representation learning in language models, potentially guiding future research in model design and training efficiency.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and concerns are evident. First, the analysis primarily focuses on the early stages of training, which raises questions about the validity of the leading-term approximation over longer training periods and more complex tasks. The paper states that the leading-term approximation is valid during the early stages of training, where the leading term dominates parameter updates before higher-order corrections accumulate. However, the authors do not provide a rigorous justification for why this early-stage analysis is sufficient to understand the full training dynamics. For instance, the paper does not explore the point at which the approximation begins to diverge significantly from the actual weight updates. This is a critical gap, as many interesting capabilities of transformers, such as in-context learning and complex reasoning, emerge later in training. The assumption that early learned structures persist without significant alteration may not hold, particularly as the model learns more complex relationships and abstract concepts. The experimental results on Pythia-1.4B show that the cosine similarity between the theoretical and learned weights remains relatively high even after 100 epochs, but this does not fully address the concern about the long-term validity of the approximation. A more detailed analysis of the error introduced by the leading-term approximation over extended training periods, perhaps by plotting the cosine similarity between the leading-term gradient and the full gradient, would be beneficial. Additionally, the paper should investigate how the approximation's accuracy varies across different layers of the network and with different model sizes, as this could provide a more complete picture of the training dynamics and the role of the identified basis functions.

Second, the study is limited to attention-based transformers, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. The paper explicitly focuses on attention-based language models and does not explore whether similar patterns emerge in other architectures, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or convolutional neural networks (CNNs). This limitation is significant because it leaves open the question of whether the identified basis functions are specific to the transformer architecture or a more general property of neural network learning. To address this, the authors should consider extending their analysis to other neural network architectures. This would help to determine the robustness of the theoretical framework and its applicability to a wider range of practical scenarios. Furthermore, the paper does not investigate the impact of different training regimes, such as varying batch sizes, learning rates, or optimization algorithms, on the emergence of these basis functions. Such an analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors that influence the learning of semantic associations and the generalizability of the theoretical framework.

Third, while the theoretical framework is well-developed, the practical implications for improving model design or training efficiency are not fully explored. The paper does not provide concrete examples of how the identified basis functions could be used to guide the design of more efficient or effective models. For instance, the authors could explore whether initializing the attention weights with the theoretical predictions leads to faster convergence or better performance on downstream tasks. Additionally, the paper could investigate whether manipulating the basis functions during training can lead to models that are more robust to adversarial examples or that exhibit better transfer learning capabilities. The connection between the theoretical analysis and practical applications remains somewhat abstract, which limits the immediate impact of the work. The authors should consider providing more specific examples and potential applications to bridge this gap and make the work more relevant to the broader machine learning community.

Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the assumptions underlying the theoretical results, particularly the small learning rate and the bound on the number of layers. Theorem 4.1 states that the learning rate must be greater than or equal to 1/T, which is a lower bound, but the paper does not provide a rigorous justification for how small the learning rate needs to be for the leading-term approximation to hold. Similarly, the assumption on the number of layers, $L \leq \sqrt{T}/4$, is not well-explained. The paper should provide more intuition on how the number of layers affects the learning dynamics and why this specific bound is necessary for the theoretical results to hold. This could involve additional theoretical analysis or empirical experiments with different numbers of layers. The lack of such a discussion makes it difficult to assess the practical relevance of these assumptions and the conditions under which the theoretical predictions are valid.
