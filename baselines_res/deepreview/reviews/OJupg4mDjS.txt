## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to Geodesic Principal Component Analysis (GPCA) within the Wasserstein space, focusing on the analysis of probability distributions. The authors propose two distinct algorithms: one tailored for centered Gaussian distributions, leveraging the Bures-Wasserstein geometry to lift computations to a flat space of invertible matrices, and another for absolutely continuous (a.c.) probability measures, employing neural networks to parameterize geodesic components. The Gaussian case is addressed by formulating an optimization problem that seeks to minimize the sum of squared Wasserstein-2 distances between the data distributions and a geodesic, which is then solved using gradient-based methods. For a.c. measures, the authors introduce GPCAGEN, an algorithm that uses multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) to represent the geodesic components, optimizing a loss function based on the Sinkhorn divergence, a differentiable approximation of the Wasserstein-2 distance. The paper provides a theoretical foundation for GPCA, including a detailed discussion of the Wasserstein metric and its implications for geodesic paths. The authors also present experimental results on real-world datasets, including MNIST, ModelNet40, and a weather dataset, demonstrating the effectiveness of their method in capturing meaningful modes of variation in data lying on curved spaces. The paper compares the proposed GPCA method with its linearized approximation, Tangent PCA (TPCA), highlighting scenarios where GPCA offers advantages due to its ability to account for the curvature of the Wasserstein space. The core contribution of this work lies in the development of exact GPCA algorithms for both Gaussian and a.c. probability measures, moving beyond the limitations of linearized approximations and providing a more accurate representation of data variations in the Wasserstein space. The use of neural networks to parameterize geodesics in the a.c. case is a significant technical innovation, allowing for the application of GPCA to a broader range of data types. The empirical results, while demonstrating the method's potential, also reveal areas for improvement, particularly in terms of computational efficiency and robustness. Overall, this paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of statistical analysis on manifolds, offering a novel approach to GPCA that is both theoretically sound and practically relevant.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths, primarily centered around its novel approach to Geodesic Principal Component Analysis (GPCA) and its rigorous theoretical foundation. The most significant contribution is the development of exact GPCA algorithms for both Gaussian and absolutely continuous (a.c.) probability measures. This is a substantial advancement over existing methods that often rely on linearized approximations, such as Tangent PCA (TPCA). The authors' approach for Gaussian distributions, which leverages the Bures-Wasserstein geometry to lift computations to a flat space of invertible matrices, is both elegant and computationally efficient. This allows for a more accurate representation of data variations in the Wasserstein space compared to methods that approximate the space as Euclidean. The introduction of GPCAGEN, the algorithm for a.c. probability measures, is another significant strength. The use of neural networks to parameterize geodesic components is a novel and technically innovative approach, enabling the application of GPCA to a broader range of data types. This method allows for the capture of complex, non-linear modes of variation in the data, which is a significant improvement over linear methods. The theoretical analysis provided in the paper is also a major strength. The authors provide a detailed discussion of the Wasserstein metric and its implications for geodesic paths, laying a solid foundation for the proposed algorithms. The comparison between GPCA and TPCA is well-supported by mathematical derivations, highlighting the limitations of TPCA and the advantages of GPCA in capturing the intrinsic geometry of the data. The experimental results, while not without limitations, demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed methods. The authors show that GPCA can effectively capture meaningful modes of variation in real-world datasets, which is valuable for downstream tasks such as classification and clustering. The use of real-world datasets, including MNIST, ModelNet40, and a weather dataset, provides empirical evidence of the method's potential. The paper is also well-structured and clearly written, making it accessible to readers with a background in optimal transport and Riemannian geometry. The authors provide a comprehensive overview of the necessary background information, ensuring that the paper is self-contained. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach to GPCA, its rigorous theoretical foundation, and its demonstration of the method's potential through empirical results.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of a detailed mathematical analysis of the proposed algorithms, particularly regarding convergence and computational complexity. While the paper introduces algorithms for both Gaussian and a.c. probability measures, it does not provide formal proofs of convergence for the optimization problems involved. For the Gaussian case, the optimization problem is formulated in Proposition 3 (Equation 12), and for the a.c. case, the GPCAGEN algorithm is presented in Algorithm 1. However, there is no rigorous analysis to guarantee that these optimization procedures will converge to a stable solution. This is a significant limitation, as the reliability of the method in different scenarios cannot be fully assessed without such guarantees. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a thorough investigation of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithms, especially GPCAGEN. While the authors mention that the Gaussian case is computationally efficient, the complexity of the neural network-based approach for general measures is not thoroughly analyzed. Specifically, the paper lacks a discussion on how the number of samples, the dimensionality of the data, and the architecture of the neural networks affect the overall computational cost. This is a crucial aspect for practical applications, especially when dealing with large datasets. The absence of a detailed computational complexity analysis makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the method. The paper also lacks a systematic evaluation of the algorithm's robustness and sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. While the authors demonstrate the algorithm's performance on real-world datasets, there is no systematic exploration of how different hyperparameter values affect the results. For example, the paper mentions that the regularization coefficients are set to 1.0, but there is no analysis of how different values of these coefficients impact the performance of the algorithm. Additionally, the paper does not include experiments with added noise or adversarial examples to test the robustness of the method. This lack of systematic evaluation makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the method in different scenarios. Another weakness is the limited comparison with existing methods for GPCA. While the paper mentions TPCA and other approximations of GPCA, it does not provide a detailed quantitative comparison of computational efficiency and accuracy with existing GPCA methods, especially for a.c. measures. The comparison is primarily qualitative, which makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of the proposed method compared to the state-of-the-art. The paper also does not provide a thorough analysis of the limitations of the proposed method, particularly in high-dimensional settings. The authors do not explicitly discuss the challenges of parameterizing geodesics in high-dimensional Wasserstein space using neural networks, such as the potential for overfitting or the increased computational cost. Furthermore, the paper does not address the sensitivity of the method to the choice of neural network architecture and hyperparameters, and how these choices might affect the quality of the geodesic approximation. The experiments are conducted on relatively low-dimensional data, and there are no experiments explicitly testing the performance in very high-dimensional settings. Finally, the paper assumes a certain level of familiarity with optimal transport and Riemannian geometry, which might make it less accessible to a broader audience. While the authors provide a good overview of the background, some readers might struggle with the mathematical concepts and notations. The paper also focuses on the Wasserstein-2 distance, and does not discuss the potential extension to other Wasserstein distances, such as the Wasserstein-1 distance. The paper also claims that the algorithms are exact, but does not provide a detailed analysis of the sources of error or how the accuracy of the numerical approximations is ensured. These limitations, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be significantly improved to enhance its theoretical rigor, practical applicability, and accessibility.
