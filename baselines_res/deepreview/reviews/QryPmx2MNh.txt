## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to optimizing the learning process of Transformer models for arithmetic tasks by reordering the decoder input tokens. The core idea revolves around identifying a 'learning-friendly' sequence of output tokens, which is achieved through a two-stage hierarchical method. The authors begin by training a Transformer model on a dataset where the target sequences are arranged in various orders. They then identify the most effective order by observing the speed of loss reduction during the early stages of training, with the assumption that faster loss drops indicate a more learnable sequence. To manage the factorial growth of the search space, they employ a hierarchical approach that first reorders blocks of tokens and then refines the order within each block. This method is evaluated on four order-sensitive arithmetic tasks: RELU, SQUARE-19, INDEX, and PROD. The empirical results demonstrate that the proposed method can successfully identify learning-friendly orders, even from a vast number of possibilities, and notably, it recovers the reverse-digit order for multiplication tasks, which has been reported in prior studies. The authors claim that their method improves the success rate of the Transformer models from approximately 10% to near 100% for target lengths up to 13 tokens. The paper's significance lies in its exploration of the impact of output token ordering on the learning efficiency of Transformers, particularly in the context of arithmetic tasks. It presents a novel task and a practical method for optimizing the learning process, which could have implications for other sequence-to-sequence tasks. However, the paper also has limitations, primarily in the scope of its evaluation and the depth of its analysis, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to a novel problem, but further work is needed to address the identified limitations and to fully realize the potential of the proposed method.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the introduction of a novel task—reordering decoder input tokens to enhance learning efficiency—is a significant contribution. This is a creative extension of existing work on chain-of-thought prompting and output ordering, and it offers a fresh perspective on how Transformers can be optimized for complex reasoning tasks. The idea of identifying a 'learning-friendly' order by observing the speed of loss reduction during early training is both intuitive and effective. The authors have clearly articulated the problem and proposed a method that directly addresses it. The two-stage hierarchical approach for inter- and intra-block reordering is another notable strength. This method effectively manages the factorial growth of the search space, making it feasible to explore a large number of possible token orders. The authors provide a clear explanation of the method, and the experimental results demonstrate its effectiveness in identifying optimal sequences. The empirical results are compelling, showing that the method can identify learning-friendly orders from billions of possibilities. The fact that the method recovers the reverse-digit order for multiplication tasks, which has been reported in prior studies, further validates its effectiveness. The authors also provide a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the datasets, the training procedure, and the evaluation metrics. This makes it easy to understand and reproduce the results. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, which is a significant strength in itself. The authors have clearly articulated the problem, the proposed method, and the experimental results. The use of figures and tables enhances the clarity of the presentation. Finally, the paper's focus on arithmetic tasks, while also a limitation, provides a clear and well-defined experimental framework for demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method. The choice of arithmetic tasks is appropriate given the focus on order sensitivity and the potential for generalization to other sequence-to-sequence tasks.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation, and one that is consistently highlighted by multiple reviewers, is the narrow scope of the experimental evaluation. The paper focuses exclusively on arithmetic tasks, including RELU, SQUARE-19, INDEX, and PROD. While these tasks are relevant to the study of order sensitivity, they represent a very specific domain. The paper's title, abstract, introduction, and method overview all explicitly state the focus on arithmetic tasks, and the experimental section is titled 'ORDER-SENSITIVE TASKS' which are all arithmetic based. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings to other types of sequence-to-sequence tasks. The authors do not provide any evidence to suggest that their method would be effective for tasks involving more complex input structures or semantic relationships, such as text generation or code synthesis. This lack of generalizability is a significant weakness, as it restricts the potential impact of the proposed method. The paper's reliance on early loss drops as the primary criterion for identifying learning-friendly orders is another weakness. The method explicitly uses the speed of loss reduction in the early stages of training to determine the optimal order. While this is a computationally efficient approach, it may introduce a bias towards orders that yield quick initial gains but are not necessarily optimal for long-term learning. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between early loss drops and final model performance, and it is possible that the method is overlooking potentially better orders that require more time to converge. This is a significant concern, as it raises questions about the robustness of the method. Furthermore, the paper's experiments are conducted on relatively short sequences, with the detailed results primarily focusing on target lengths up to 13 tokens. While the paper mentions a target length range up to 100, the core method demonstration and the conclusion focus on sequences up to 13 tokens. This is a limitation, as it does not fully capture the method's scalability and effectiveness for longer, more complex computations. The authors do not provide any evidence to suggest that their method would be effective for significantly longer sequences, and it is possible that the optimal order may change as the sequence length increases. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed two-stage hierarchical approach. While the authors provide some practical details about the computational cost, such as the number of training steps and the time taken for the longest exploration, they do not provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of the method. This is a significant weakness, as it makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method, especially for large-scale tasks. The paper does not cite standard computational complexity analysis techniques, and it does not compare the computational cost of its method to existing approaches. Finally, the paper lacks a dedicated section or a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors briefly mention the factorial growth of the search space as a challenge, they do not explicitly discuss the potential drawbacks of their method or its limitations. This is a significant omission, as it prevents a comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses. The absence of a dedicated limitations section and the limited discussion of drawbacks are clear indicators of this weakness. In summary, the paper's limitations stem from its narrow experimental scope, its reliance on early loss drops, the short sequence lengths used in the experiments, the lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis, and the absence of a thorough discussion of limitations. These weaknesses significantly impact the generalizability and robustness of the proposed method.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First and foremost, the authors should significantly broaden the scope of their experimental evaluation. This would involve testing the proposed method on a wider range of sequence-to-sequence tasks, beyond the current focus on arithmetic. Specifically, I suggest exploring tasks that involve more complex input structures and semantic relationships, such as text generation, code synthesis, or tasks involving graph-based reasoning. This would provide valuable insights into the generalizability of the method and its potential for real-world applications. For example, the authors could investigate how the reordering of tokens affects the Transformer's ability to solve problems in propositional logic or to perform graph traversal algorithms. This would not only broaden the applicability of the method but also reveal potential limitations or areas for improvement. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the impact of different tokenization strategies on the effectiveness of the reordering method, as the choice of tokens can significantly influence the learning dynamics of Transformers. Second, the authors should explore alternative criteria for identifying learning-friendly orders, beyond the current reliance on early loss drops. This could involve using a validation set to evaluate the performance of different orders after a fixed number of training steps, or exploring more sophisticated metrics that capture the model's learning progress beyond just the loss value. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the early stopping point for loss drops, as this could significantly impact the identified optimal orders. A more detailed analysis of these aspects would provide a more robust and reliable method for identifying learning-friendly token orders. Third, the authors should conduct experiments with significantly longer sequences to assess the method's scalability and effectiveness for more complex computations. This would require careful consideration of the computational resources needed for training and the potential for optimization. Additionally, it would be valuable to investigate how the optimal token order changes as the sequence length increases, as this could reveal interesting patterns or trends that could inform the design of more efficient reordering strategies. The authors should also consider the impact of different sequence lengths on the model's ability to generalize to unseen examples. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method. This would involve a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of the two-stage hierarchical approach, as well as a comparison of the computational cost of their method to existing approaches. The authors should provide a breakdown of the computational cost associated with each step of the method, including the time required for training and inference. This would allow readers to better understand the trade-offs between performance gains and computational resources. Finally, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed method. This section should explicitly address the potential drawbacks of the method, its limitations, and the potential challenges in applying it to more complex tasks. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and would help to clarify its scope and applicability. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and enhance its impact.
