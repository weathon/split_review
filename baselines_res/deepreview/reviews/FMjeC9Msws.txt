## Summary:

This paper presents a comprehensive empirical study on scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for training large language models (LLMs), a critical area that has lacked systematic investigation compared to pre-training. The authors address this gap by conducting a large-scale experiment involving over 400,000 GPU-hours, focusing on mathematical reasoning tasks. Their core contribution is the development of a framework for analyzing and predicting RL scaling behavior, based on fitting sigmoidal curves to compute-performance data. This framework allows them to extrapolate performance from smaller-scale runs to larger compute budgets, which is crucial for cost-effective experimentation. Through extensive ablation studies, they identify key algorithmic design choices that impact both asymptotic performance and compute efficiency. These choices include the use of off-policy algorithms, specific loss functions like CISPO, precision adjustments, and data curricula. Based on these findings, the authors propose SCALERL, a best-practice RL recipe that combines these elements to achieve predictable scaling and state-of-the-art performance. SCALERL is validated through large-scale experiments, including runs up to 100,000 GPU-hours, demonstrating its effectiveness and scalability. The paper's significance lies in its systematic approach to understanding RL scaling, providing a scientific framework and practical guidance for efficiently training LLMs with RL. The authors demonstrate that RL training can scale predictably, and that careful algorithmic choices can lead to significant improvements in both performance and compute efficiency. This work provides a valuable foundation for future research in this area, moving beyond ad-hoc approaches to a more principled and predictable methodology. The paper's findings have significant implications for the development of more capable and reliable language models, and for the efficient allocation of computational resources in RL training.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should broaden their experimental validation to include a more diverse set of tasks beyond mathematical reasoning. Specifically, they should evaluate the performance of SCALERL on tasks that involve natural language understanding, such as question answering or text summarization, as well as tasks that require more complex reasoning, such as planning or code generation. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the generalizability of the proposed framework and recipe. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the performance of SCALERL on tasks with varying levels of difficulty to understand its limitations and potential areas for improvement. Such an analysis would also help to identify the types of tasks where SCALERL is most effective and where it may struggle. This would significantly strengthen the paper's claims about the broad applicability of SCALERL. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed theoretical analysis of the design choices within SCALERL. For instance, they should provide a theoretical justification for the effectiveness of PipelineRL-8, perhaps by analyzing its convergence properties or its ability to mitigate issues such as off-policy bias. Similarly, a theoretical comparison of CISPO, DAPO, and GSPO should be included, focusing on their respective advantages and disadvantages in terms of variance, bias, and stability. This could involve analyzing the gradient estimation properties of each method or examining their sensitivity to hyperparameter settings. By providing a theoretical foundation for their choices, the authors would not only strengthen the paper's scientific contribution but also provide a more principled approach for future research in this area. This would also help in understanding the limitations of the current approach and guide the development of more robust and efficient RL algorithms. Third, the authors should address the potential stability challenges associated with scaling RL training to even larger scales. This could involve a more detailed analysis of the training dynamics, including the evolution of gradient norms, reward distributions, and policy entropy over time. The authors should also discuss potential mitigation strategies for issues such as gradient variance, reward hacking, and catastrophic forgetting. For example, they could explore the use of gradient clipping, reward shaping, or regularization techniques to improve training stability. Furthermore, it would be valuable to investigate the sensitivity of SCALERL to different hyperparameter settings at large scales and provide guidelines for tuning these parameters. By addressing these stability concerns, the authors would make a stronger case for the scalability of SCALERL and provide practical guidance for practitioners aiming to train LLMs at very large scales. Fourth, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the SCALERL recipe. This analysis should include a breakdown of the cost per task, as well as a comparison of the resource utilization of different methods. For example, the authors could provide a table that shows the GPU hours, memory usage, and training time for each method on each task. This would allow practitioners to better assess the practical feasibility of the proposed approach and make informed decisions about which method to use for their specific needs. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the trade-offs between performance and computational cost, and provide guidance on how to choose the most appropriate method for a given budget. Finally, the authors should explore the sensitivity of the SCALERL recipe to hyperparameter settings. It would be beneficial to conduct a sensitivity analysis to determine which hyperparameters have the most significant impact on performance and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate hyperparameter values for different tasks and models. This analysis should also consider the potential interaction between different hyperparameters and how they affect the overall performance of the method. This would make the method more robust and easier to use in practice.
