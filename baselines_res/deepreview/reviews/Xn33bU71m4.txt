## Summary:

This paper presents a systematic evaluation of several widely used open-source Large Language Models (LLMs) on two core reverse engineering tasks: function name recovery and type inference. The authors aim to assess the practical utility of these models in binary analysis, particularly in scenarios where debug symbols are absent. The study focuses on evaluating the performance of models such as CodeLlama, Llama 2, and DeepSeek-R1, among others, without fine-tuning, and then examines the impact of fine-tuning on their performance. The core methodology involves using an automated framework to evaluate the LLMs on a dataset of decompiled code and assembly code, with a focus on x86-64 architecture. The evaluation metrics primarily consist of F1 scores, which are used to quantify the performance of the models in both name recovery and type inference tasks. The paper explores the impact of model size, training data quality, and the use of auxiliary tools like CodeWordNet on the performance of LLMs. The main empirical findings indicate that, without fine-tuning, the LLMs perform poorly on both tasks, achieving low F1 scores. While fine-tuning does lead to some improvements, the gains are inconsistent and not guaranteed. The paper also highlights the limitations of current LLMs in binary analysis, suggesting that more effective fine-tuning strategies, higher-quality training data, and alternative input representations are needed to fully unlock their potential. The authors also explore the impact of different compiler optimization levels on the performance of the LLMs. The study's overall significance lies in its systematic evaluation of LLMs for reverse engineering tasks, providing valuable insights into their current capabilities and limitations, and highlighting the need for further research in this area. The paper's contribution is primarily empirical, focusing on evaluating existing models rather than proposing new methods, and it underscores the challenges of applying LLMs to complex tasks like reverse engineering.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should ensure consistency in the presentation of the evaluated models. The introduction should accurately reflect the models used in the experiments, and the tables should be consistent with the introduction. Specifically, Table 1 should explicitly list all models evaluated, including Llama 2 and the different sizes of CodeLlama, with their corresponding parameter sizes. This will improve the clarity and accuracy of the paper. Secondly, the authors should broaden the scope of the study to include other reverse engineering tasks, such as vulnerability detection, code obfuscation analysis, and malware classification. This would provide a more complete picture of the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in this domain. For instance, evaluating the LLMs' ability to identify common vulnerability patterns or to understand obfuscated code would be valuable contributions. Thirdly, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of how different architectural features impact the performance of LLMs. This could involve examining the effects of specific instruction set architectures (ISAs), such as x86, ARM, or MIPS, and their associated complexities on the LLMs' ability to understand and reverse engineer code. Additionally, the study should investigate how different optimization levels (e.g., O0, O1, O2, O3) affect the performance of LLMs, as these optimizations can significantly alter the structure and semantics of the code. A more granular analysis of these factors would enhance the generalizability of the findings. Fourthly, to address the concerns regarding the use of decompiled code, the authors should incorporate a more robust analysis of the impact of decompilation artifacts on the evaluation results. This could involve comparing the performance of LLMs on both decompiled code and assembly code, and quantifying the differences in performance. Furthermore, the authors should consider using multiple decompilers and analyzing the variance in the results to assess the robustness of their findings. This would help to determine whether the observed performance is due to the inherent limitations of the LLMs or the inaccuracies introduced by the decompilation process. Fifthly, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for fine-tuning and deploying the LLMs. This should include information on the hardware requirements, training time, and inference time. This information is crucial for practical applications and would help to determine the feasibility of using LLMs in real-world reverse engineering scenarios. Sixthly, the authors should explore the potential of combining LLMs with other AI techniques, such as reinforcement learning or graph neural networks, to enhance their performance in reverse engineering tasks. For example, reinforcement learning could be used to train LLMs to explore the program structure more effectively, while graph neural networks could be used to model the control flow and data dependencies in the code. These hybrid approaches could potentially lead to significant improvements in the performance of LLMs in reverse engineering. Finally, the paper should address the security implications of using LLMs in reverse engineering. This should include an analysis of the potential for adversarial attacks, such as the injection of malicious code into the training data, and the generation of incorrect or misleading information. The authors should also discuss the potential for LLMs to be used for malicious purposes, such as the automated generation of malware. This analysis is crucial for ensuring the responsible development and deployment of LLMs in reverse engineering. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly enhance the impact and generalizability of their study.
