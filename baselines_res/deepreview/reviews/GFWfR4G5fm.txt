## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel test-time training approach for supervised causal structure learning (SCL), termed Test-time Aligned Causal Training with Informed Construction (TACTIC). The authors identify critical limitations of existing SCL methods, including their fragility to distribution shifts, failure in compositional generalization, and a significant performance gap between synthetic benchmarks and real-world data. To address these issues, TACTIC dynamically generates a customized training set aligned with the distribution of a given test instance. This is achieved by optimizing a joint score that combines an Alignment of Distribution (AD) metric, which measures the similarity between the generated data and the test data, and a sparsity constraint, which enforces causal minimality. The core idea is to leverage a supervised model's capacity to generate data, but to do so in a way that is tailored to the specific test instance, rather than relying on a fixed pre-training dataset. The method begins with a seed graph, either randomly generated or derived from a traditional causal discovery method like NOTEARS, and then iteratively refines this graph using a stochastic search process. In each iteration, a set of training graphs is generated, and the one that maximizes the joint score is selected. The corresponding training data is then used to fine-tune the SCL model. The authors conduct extensive experiments on synthetic, pseudo-real, and real-world datasets, demonstrating that TACTIC outperforms existing SCL and traditional causal discovery methods, particularly in scenarios involving distribution shifts. The results suggest that TACTIC offers a more robust and generalizable approach to causal structure learning by adapting to the specific characteristics of each test instance. The paper's contribution lies in its innovative approach to test-time training for SCL, which addresses the limitations of static pre-training and provides a more flexible and adaptable framework for causal discovery. The use of a joint score that balances distribution alignment and sparsity is a key aspect of the method, and the empirical results provide strong support for its effectiveness. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in the field of causal structure learning, offering a promising direction for future research.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The core idea of test-time training for supervised causal structure learning is genuinely novel and addresses a critical gap in the existing literature. The authors have clearly identified the limitations of static pre-training, particularly the fragility to distribution shifts and the failure in compositional generalization, and have proposed a method that directly tackles these issues. The TACTIC framework, with its dynamic generation of training data aligned with the test instance, represents a significant departure from traditional approaches. The use of the Alignment of Distribution (AD) metric, combined with a sparsity constraint, is well-motivated and theoretically sound. The AD metric provides a principled way to measure the similarity between the generated data and the test data, while the sparsity constraint ensures that the learned causal graphs are consistent with the principle of causal minimality. The iterative stochastic graph refinement process, which starts from a seed graph and iteratively improves it based on the joint score, is an effective way to explore the space of possible causal structures. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow. The authors have clearly explained the problem, their proposed solution, and the experimental results. The implementation details and hyperparameters are clearly described, which is crucial for reproducibility. The experimental evaluation is comprehensive, covering synthetic, pseudo-real, and real-world datasets. The results demonstrate that TACTIC outperforms existing SCL and traditional causal discovery methods, particularly in scenarios involving distribution shifts. The inclusion of ablation studies further strengthens the validity of the claims. The provision of source code is also a significant strength, allowing other researchers to build upon this work. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated approach to causal structure learning, making a valuable contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. Firstly, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the proposed TACTIC method, particularly in comparison to existing approaches. While Appendix F provides some runtime analysis, this is not discussed in the main text, and the paper does not provide a breakdown of the time complexity for each step of the TACTIC algorithm, including the graph generation, data sampling, and model training phases. This is a significant omission, as the iterative nature of TACTIC, requiring K runs of the SCL model, introduces a substantial computational cost. The paper does not discuss how the number of variables (d) and the size of the training dataset (n) impact the runtime of the algorithm, which is crucial for understanding its scalability. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical viability of the method, especially for large-scale problems. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the absence of a detailed computational complexity analysis in the main text is clearly evident. Secondly, the paper does not provide sufficient detail on the selection of the hyperparameter λ in the joint optimization score (Equation 5). The authors state that λ balances the trade-off between the AD metric and the sparsity constraint, but they do not provide a clear rationale for the specific value of λ=0.05 used in the experiments. Furthermore, there is no sensitivity analysis of λ, which makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method to different values of this hyperparameter. It is unclear whether a single value of λ is optimal across different datasets and test distributions, or if it needs to be tuned for each specific scenario. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the generalizability of the method and the potential for overfitting to the specific datasets used in the experiments. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the value of λ used but does not provide any justification or sensitivity analysis. Thirdly, the paper does not adequately address the practical challenges of obtaining a training dataset at test time. While the method generates synthetic training data, the assumption that this can be done effectively and efficiently in all real-world scenarios needs further discussion. The paper does not explore the limitations of this approach in cases where generating training data at test time is not feasible, such as when a simulator is not available or when the data generation process is computationally expensive. This reliance on a training dataset at test time is a significant limitation that restricts the applicability of the method to situations where such data can be generated or obtained. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the core mechanism of TACTIC relies on generating training data at test time, and the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the practical limitations of this approach. Fourthly, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the method's robustness to different types of noise and data quality issues. While the paper demonstrates strong performance on the datasets used, it does not include experiments that systematically vary the level of noise and missingness in the test data. The paper does not analyze how different types of noise, such as Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper noise, and systematic biases, affect the accuracy of the causal graph recovery. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the method's applicability to real-world datasets, which often contain significant noise and data quality issues. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include any experiments specifically designed to assess the robustness of TACTIC to various noise types, outliers, or missing data. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed comparison with other test-time adaptation methods, especially those that are not specific to causal learning. While the paper compares TACTIC to traditional causal discovery methods and one SCL method, it does not deeply compare TACTIC to specific non-causal methods. This limits the understanding of TACTIC's novelty and place within the larger field of test-time adaptation. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's comparison is limited to causal discovery methods, and a more thorough analysis of its relationship to general test-time adaptation techniques is missing.


## Suggestions:

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements to this paper. Firstly, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the TACTIC algorithm in the main text. This analysis should go beyond a high-level description and provide a formal breakdown of the time complexity for each step of the algorithm, including the graph generation, data sampling, and model training phases. The analysis should consider the impact of the number of variables (d), the number of training instances (n), and the number of generated graphs (K) on the overall runtime. This analysis should be compared to the complexity of the baseline methods used in the experiments, providing a clear understanding of the computational trade-offs involved in using TACTIC. Secondly, the authors should provide a more thorough discussion on the selection of the hyperparameter λ and its impact on the performance of TACTIC. This should include a sensitivity analysis of λ, with experiments using a range of values, and the results should be presented in a way that clearly shows how the performance of TACTIC changes with different values. The authors should also provide a rationale for the specific value of λ=0.05 used in the experiments, explaining why this value was chosen and how it relates to the trade-off between the AD metric and the sparsity constraint. Furthermore, the authors could consider using a cross-validation approach to select the optimal value of λ. Thirdly, the authors should address the practical challenges of obtaining a training dataset at test time. This could involve exploring alternative approaches that do not rely on this assumption, such as methods for adapting the model to new test instances without requiring additional training data. This could involve techniques such as meta-learning or transfer learning. Another approach could be to develop methods for generating synthetic training data that are representative of the test instance, without relying on a simulator. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their method in cases where generating training data at test time is not feasible and provide guidance on when their method is most likely to be effective. Fourthly, the authors should include a more thorough analysis of the method's robustness to different types of noise and data quality issues. This should include experiments that systematically vary the level of noise and missingness in the test data, and analyze how these factors affect the accuracy of the causal graph recovery. The authors should also consider the impact of different types of noise, such as Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper noise, and systematic biases. The paper should also explore the use of robust estimation techniques to mitigate the impact of noise and missing values on the performance of the method. Finally, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with other test-time adaptation methods, especially those that are not specific to causal learning. This comparison should include a discussion of the similarities and differences between TACTIC and other methods, as well as a comparison of their performance on the same datasets. The paper should also discuss the advantages and disadvantages of TACTIC compared to these other methods, and explain why TACTIC is a better choice for the specific problem of causal discovery. This would help contextualize the novelty and effectiveness of TACTIC within the broader landscape of test-time adaptation techniques. These changes would significantly strengthen the paper and make it more impactful.


## Questions:

I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. Firstly, how does the choice of the initial seed graph affect the performance of TACTIC? The paper mentions using either a random graph or a graph estimated from the test data by the NOTEARS algorithm as a starting point. Are there any strategies for selecting a good initial seed, and how does the choice of seed impact the convergence and quality of the final causal graph? Secondly, can the authors provide more details on the computational complexity of TACTIC, especially for large-scale datasets? While Appendix F provides some runtime information, a more detailed analysis of how the runtime scales with the number of variables and the size of the dataset would be beneficial. This would help to understand the practical limitations of the method and its applicability to real-world problems. Thirdly, how sensitive is TACTIC to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly λ in the joint optimization score? The paper mentions that λ balances the trade-off between the AD metric and the sparsity constraint, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the performance of TACTIC changes with different values of λ. A sensitivity analysis of λ would be valuable for understanding the robustness of the method and how to choose appropriate values for different datasets. Fourthly, the paper mentions that optimizing AD alone can lead to degenerate dense solutions. Can the authors provide more theoretical analysis or empirical evidence to support this claim? It would be helpful to understand the conditions under which optimizing AD alone leads to degenerate solutions and how the sparsity constraint helps to mitigate this issue. Finally, how does TACTIC perform when the test data is highly noisy or contains outliers? Are there any robustness measures that can be incorporated into the framework to mitigate the impact of noise and outliers on the performance of the method? These questions target key uncertainties and methodological choices in the paper, and addressing them would further strengthen the work.
