## Summary:

This paper investigates the critical role of the sparsity hyperparameter, $L_0$, in sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on large language model (LLM) activations. The authors demonstrate that an incorrectly set $L_0$—either too low or too high—can lead to the mixing of features, thereby degrading the interpretability of the learned representations. To address this issue, they introduce a novel metric, the decoder pairwise cosine similarity ($c_{dec}$), which quantifies the degree of feature mixing by measuring the cosine similarity between pairs of decoder weight vectors. The core idea is that a lower $c_{dec}$ indicates better disentanglement of features. The authors validate their findings through a series of experiments, starting with toy models where they have ground truth feature access, and then extending their analysis to real LLMs, specifically Gemma-2-2b and Llama-3.2-1b. In the toy models, they show that $c_{dec}$ can be used to identify the correct $L_0$ that avoids feature mixing. In the LLM experiments, they demonstrate that the minimum of the $c_{dec}$ curve often aligns with the $L_0$ value that yields the best performance on sparse probing tasks, suggesting that their metric can be a useful tool for selecting the appropriate sparsity level in real-world scenarios. The paper's main contribution lies in highlighting the importance of selecting the correct $L_0$ for SAEs, demonstrating the limitations of relying solely on sparsity-reconstruction tradeoffs, and proposing a practical metric to guide the selection of $L_0$. The authors also provide a theoretical justification for their metric in Appendix A.6, further strengthening their claims. Overall, this work provides valuable insights into the training of SAEs for LLM interpretability and offers a practical approach for selecting the sparsity hyperparameter.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper tackles a very relevant and important problem in the field of sparse autoencoders: the selection of the sparsity hyperparameter $L_0$. The authors clearly demonstrate that both excessively low and excessively high values of $L_0$ can lead to feature mixing, which is a critical issue for interpretability. This is a significant contribution, as it challenges the common practice of relying solely on sparsity-reconstruction tradeoff plots for evaluating SAEs. The use of toy models to illustrate the effect of $L_0$ on feature mixing is another strength. These models, with their ground truth features, allow for a clear and intuitive understanding of the problem. The authors' ability to translate these insights to real-world LLMs is commendable. The introduction of the decoder pairwise cosine similarity metric, $c_{dec}$, is a novel and practical contribution. This metric provides a way to quantify feature mixing and can be used to guide the selection of the appropriate $L_0$. The authors also provide a theoretical justification for this metric in Appendix A.6, which adds to its credibility. The experimental results, both on toy models and real LLMs, are compelling and support the authors' claims. The alignment of the $c_{dec}$ metric with the optimal $L_0$ for sparse probing performance in LLMs is particularly noteworthy. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain their methodology and findings, and the figures are well-designed and informative. The inclusion of code in Appendix A.17 also enhances the reproducibility of the work.

The authors suggest using the 'elbow' of the $c_{dec}$ curve as an indicator of the optimal $L_0$, but as seen in Appendix A.15 for Gemma-2-2b Layer 5, the $c_{dec}$ plot does not always exhibit a clear minimum. This makes it difficult to determine the correct $L_0$ in such cases, and the paper does not offer alternative strategies for selecting $L_0$ when the elbow is not apparent. This limitation significantly reduces the practical utility of the proposed metric. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly observable in the provided results. This is a significant concern, especially for larger models where convergence might require more epochs. The absence of training loss curves for both toy and real-world experiments makes it impossible to verify whether the SAEs have reached a stable state before evaluating the performance metrics. This lack of convergence evidence undermines the reliability of the results. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should develop a more robust methodology for selecting the correct $L_0$ using the $c_{dec}$ metric, especially in cases where the elbow is not apparent. This could involve exploring alternative methods for identifying the optimal $L_0$, such as using a validation set or employing more robust optimization techniques. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the cases where the $c_{dec}$ metric fails to identify the correct $L_0$ and propose potential solutions to address these issues. Second, the authors should include training loss curves for both toy and real-world experiments to demonstrate that the SAEs have reached convergence before evaluating the performance metrics. They should also explore the impact of different training epochs on the results and provide a justification for the chosen number of epochs. Third, the authors should investigate the relationship between the width of the SAE and the optimal $L_0$. This could involve training SAEs with different widths and analyzing the corresponding $c_{dec}$ curves and probing performance. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the synthetic data generation process, including the specific distributions used for sampling feature vectors, probabilities, magnitudes, and standard deviations. This would allow other researchers to reproduce the toy experiments and further investigate the observed phenomena. Fifth, the authors should explore the impact of different correlation structures on the performance of the SAEs. This could involve generating synthetic data with different correlation matrices and analyzing the resulting $c_{dec}$ curves and probing performance. Sixth, the authors should conduct a sensitivity analysis of the SAE training process with respect to key hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and weight decay. This analysis should involve varying these parameters across a reasonable range and observing their impact on the proposed decoder pairwise cosine similarity metric and the resulting feature quality. Seventh, the authors should expand their experiments to include a more diverse set of models and datasets. This should include models with different architectures, such as transformers, recurrent neural networks, and convolutional neural networks, as well as models trained on different types of data, such as text, images, and audio. The authors should also consider using datasets of varying sizes and complexities. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of the proposed decoder pairwise cosine similarity metric with existing methods for evaluating SAE performance. This should include a discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of each metric, as well as a comparison of their computational complexity. The authors should also investigate whether the proposed metric correlates with other measures of feature quality, such as feature disentanglement and interpretability. These improvements would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its practical utility.
