## Summary:

This paper introduces Tactile Affordance in Robot Synesthesia (TARS), a novel framework designed to enhance dexterous robotic manipulation by integrating visual and tactile information. The core idea behind TARS is to create a unified representation of visual and tactile data using point clouds, enabling the robot to seamlessly transition between contact and non-contact states during manipulation tasks. The framework consists of two primary modules: the Visual-Tactile Affordance (VTA) module, which predicts affordances based on the fused visual and tactile data, and the Visual-Tactile Policy (VTP) module, which utilizes these affordances to make manipulation decisions. The authors employ a teacher-student learning approach, where a teacher policy trained with privileged information guides the learning of the student policy that operates on visual and tactile inputs. The VTA module leverages a finite element method (FEM) to estimate contact forces, which are then integrated with visual information to predict affordances. The VTP module uses a PointNet encoder to process the point cloud data and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to output the manipulation actions. The authors validate their approach through simulations in Isaac Gym and real-world experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness of TARS on four distinct manipulation tasks: lifting objects, pulling a drawer, opening a door, and pick-and-place. The empirical results, presented in tables, show that TARS outperforms several baselines, including methods that use only visual or tactile information. The authors claim that TARS can infer tactile affordances from visual input alone and supplement visual data with tactile information when available, enabling smooth transitions between contact and non-contact states. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to visuotactile integration for robotic manipulation, addressing a significant gap in the field. However, as I will detail, there are several areas where the paper could be significantly improved to strengthen its claims and impact.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of the TARS framework, which offers a novel approach to integrating visual and tactile modalities for robotic manipulation. The idea of using a unified point cloud representation to combine visual and tactile data is innovative and addresses a critical challenge in the field. This approach allows for a more seamless transition between contact and non-contact states, which is a significant advantage over methods that treat these states separately. The authors' use of a teacher-student learning approach, where a policy trained with privileged information guides the learning of a policy that operates on visual and tactile inputs, is also a well-established and effective technique. The inclusion of real-world experiments is another notable strength, demonstrating the applicability of the proposed method beyond simulation. The authors have also conducted a reasonable number of experiments across four distinct manipulation tasks, providing a good initial evaluation of the framework's performance. The experimental results, while not statistically analyzed, do show that TARS outperforms several baselines, suggesting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper also touches on the use of force estimation using FEM, which is a valuable addition to the field of robotic manipulation. The authors' attempt to model the tactile sensor's behavior using FEM, even if not fully detailed, is a step towards more realistic simulation and control of tactile sensors. The paper's focus on integrating both visual and tactile information is a significant contribution, as many existing methods rely solely on visual data. The authors' claim that TARS can infer tactile affordances from visual input alone and supplement visual data with tactile information when available is a promising direction for future research. In summary, the core idea of the paper, the proposed framework, and the initial experimental results are all promising and represent a valuable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of the proposed framework, several weaknesses significantly impact the paper's overall quality and the confidence I have in its conclusions. First, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with existing state-of-the-art methods. While the authors do compare TARS to three baselines (RS, VA, and PN+MLP), these comparisons are not sufficiently detailed to fully understand the specific advantages and disadvantages of TARS. The experimental results section (4.3) presents quantitative comparisons in Tables I, II, and III, showing success rates, but the analysis focuses on the performance differences without a deep dive into the underlying reasons for these differences based on the methods' characteristics. The "Related Work" section (2) discusses relevant areas but doesn't explicitly position TARS against specific state-of-the-art methods within these areas in terms of detailed methodological differences and advantages. This lack of a thorough comparison makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and significance of TARS. Second, the paper's discussion of the limitations of the proposed method is inadequate. There is no dedicated "Limitations" section, and the "Conclusion" section (5) only briefly mentions future work related to improving the physical model of the bubble sensor but does not discuss limitations of the current TARS framework in terms of failure cases or scenarios. This lack of discussion about potential failure cases and scenarios where TARS might not perform optimally is a significant oversight. For instance, the paper does not explore scenarios where the tactile sensors might fail to provide accurate information, such as when the sensor is contaminated or when the contact is not stable. The paper also does not discuss how the framework handles occlusions in the visual input or how it deals with unexpected object properties or deformations. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the robustness of the framework and its applicability to real-world scenarios. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the TARS framework. The paper mentions real-world experiments but does not provide details on the computational resources used or the time taken for processing. The "Method" section (3) describes the components (VTA, VTP, PointNet, MLP) but does not analyze their computational complexity. The "Experiments" section (4) mentions using Isaac Gym for simulation but does not detail the computational cost of training or inference. This lack of information on computational demands makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the framework, particularly in real-time scenarios. Fourth, the paper's organization and clarity are problematic. Section 3.2, which is supposed to detail the core contribution of visual-tactile affordance, is a dense block of technical descriptions and equations related to force estimation using FEM. There are no in-text citations within this section to support the derivations or concepts presented, and the reference list at the end of the paper does not contain a source that directly corresponds to this specific derivation. This lack of citations raises concerns about the originality or proper attribution of the content. Furthermore, the connection between this detailed force estimation method and the broader concept of "visual-tactile affordance" is not clearly established within this section. The introduction mentions "visual-tactile affordance" as a key component, but Section 3.2 focuses heavily on the force estimation aspect. The paper also lacks a clear statement of the method at the beginning of Section 3, making it difficult to understand the overall structure and goals of the method. The roles of VTA and VTP are introduced, but their interaction and specific functionalities could be explained more clearly. The paper also lacks a dedicated architectural diagram illustrating the flow of information and the interaction between different modules. The description of the tactile point cloud simulation in 3.1 is unclear, particularly the phrase "decompose the three-dimensional contact information into a planar contact point and six-axis force information." Figure 3 is mentioned but not described or explained in the text. The explanation of the visual-tactile policy in 3.3 is also unclear, particularly the term "privileged information." Figure 4 is mentioned but not explained in detail. The loss function is presented with some explanation but lacks a detailed breakdown of its components. Fifth, the experimental setup is not clearly described, and the results lack detailed statistical analysis. The "Experimental Setup and Tasks Description" section (4.1) provides an overview of the simulation environment, the robotic arm, and the tactile sensor. However, specific details about the simulation parameters, reward functions for each task, and the exact training procedures are not provided in detail. The "Simulation Results" section (4.3) presents success rates in Tables I, II, and III. While these tables show the performance of different methods, they lack statistical analysis such as standard deviations, confidence intervals, or p-values to assess the significance of the observed differences. Finally, the experimental tasks are relatively simple. The paper evaluates TARS on four tasks: lifting objects, pulling a drawer, opening a door, and pick-and-place. While these tasks demonstrate the core functionality of TARS, they do not fully showcase its capabilities in handling the complexities of real-world dexterous manipulation, particularly in contact-rich scenarios. The paper does not evaluate TARS on more complex tasks, such as those included in the contact-rich manipulation benchmark, which involve intricate contact sequences and object deformations. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the impact and credibility of the paper. I have high confidence in these identified issues, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of necessary details.
