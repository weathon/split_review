## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to multi-task learning by leveraging the Gray-Wyner Network (GWN) from information theory to separate common and task-specific information. The authors propose a learnable coding scheme that encodes the common information and task-specific information into separate channels, aiming to improve the efficiency of multi-task learning. The core idea is to exploit the inherent redundancy in multi-task scenarios by identifying and encoding the shared information only once, while also encoding task-specific information separately. This approach is motivated by the potential for more efficient data transmission and processing in scenarios where multiple tasks need to be performed on the same input. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of the GWN, including bounds on the lossy common information and an optimization objective to balance the trade-off between transmit and receive rates. The transmit rate refers to the amount of information transmitted when performing both tasks on a single device, while the receive rate refers to the amount of information transmitted when performing each task on a separate device. The proposed method is evaluated on two-task scenarios across six vision benchmarks, including classification, detection, segmentation, and depth estimation. The empirical results demonstrate that the proposed approach substantially reduces redundancy and consistently outperforms independent coding, achieving a better trade-off between transmit and receive rates. The authors also conduct ablation studies on the architecture design, providing insights into the impact of different components on the overall performance. The paper's main contribution lies in the creative combination of information theory and machine learning to address the challenges of multi-task learning, offering a principled framework for separating common and task-specific information. While the paper demonstrates promising results, it also acknowledges limitations, particularly in the scalability of the approach to more than two tasks and the need for further exploration of practical applications. The authors also highlight the trade-offs between transmit and receive rates, indicating that the proposed method may not always achieve the best performance in joint task execution compared to a joint model, but offers advantages in scenarios where tasks are performed separately or with resource constraints. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of multi-task learning, offering a novel perspective and a promising approach for efficient representation learning.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative application of the Gray-Wyner Network (GWN) to the problem of multi-task learning. This is a novel and creative approach that effectively bridges the gap between information theory and machine learning. The authors have successfully translated the theoretical concepts of the GWN into a practical, learnable architecture for separating common and task-specific information. This is a significant contribution, as it provides a principled framework for handling multi-task scenarios, moving beyond ad-hoc solutions. The theoretical analysis of the GWN, including the derivation of bounds on lossy common information and the formulation of an optimization objective to balance transmit and receive rates, is another notable strength. This analysis provides a solid foundation for the proposed method and demonstrates a deep understanding of the underlying information-theoretic principles. The authors have also provided a good summary of the related work, contextualizing their approach within the existing literature on multi-task learning and coding for multiple consumers. The empirical evaluation, while not without limitations, demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method in reducing redundancy and achieving a better trade-off between transmit and receive rates. The experiments on various vision benchmarks, including classification, detection, segmentation, and depth estimation, provide evidence of the method's applicability to different tasks. The ablation studies on the architecture design further contribute to the understanding of the method's behavior and the impact of different components. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the theoretical concepts and experimental results. The authors have made an effort to provide intuitive explanations alongside the mathematical formulations, which aids in comprehension. The use of figures and diagrams also enhances the clarity of the presentation. The paper's focus on the practical implications of the proposed method, particularly in scenarios where tasks are performed separately or with resource constraints, is another positive aspect. This highlights the potential of the method for real-world applications and demonstrates a clear understanding of the challenges in multi-task learning.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. One significant limitation is the lack of a detailed explanation of the practical benefits of separating common and task-specific information. While the paper motivates the approach with the potential for improved efficiency in multi-task settings, it does not fully articulate the specific scenarios where this separation offers a significant advantage over existing methods that do not explicitly enforce this separation. The paper provides examples of object detection and semantic segmentation, but it does not delve into concrete use cases where the proposed method would be particularly beneficial. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to fully appreciate the practical implications of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the paper is not as clear as it could be, particularly in its use of mathematical notations. The excessive use of notations without sufficient intuitive explanations makes it challenging to grasp the core ideas, especially for readers not deeply familiar with information theory. The paper would benefit from more intuitive explanations and examples to illustrate the concepts. The lack of clear definitions for some of the notations further complicates the understanding of the proposed method. The paper also lacks sufficient detail on the architectures of the proposed method. While Figure 2 provides a high-level overview, the specific layers and their configurations are not fully described in the main text. The interaction between the common and task-specific channels is not fully clarified, and the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of how the common information is extracted and used by the different task-specific decoders. This lack of architectural detail makes it difficult to fully understand the proposed method and to reproduce the results. The performance of the proposed method is also a point of concern. The method does not consistently outperform the 'Joint' method in terms of raw performance, particularly in joint task execution. The 'Joint' method achieves lower error/rate for the same bitrate, as shown in Figures 3 and 5. While the paper emphasizes the trade-off between transmit and receive rates, it does not fully explain why the proposed method is still valuable despite its performance limitations in joint task execution. The paper also limits its scope to two sources and two tasks. While the authors acknowledge the potential for extension, they do not provide a detailed analysis of how the proposed method would scale to more sources and tasks. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world scenarios involve more than two tasks. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. The paper describes the architecture components but does not provide a formal analysis of the number of operations or parameters. This is a relevant concern, especially for practical applications and comparisons with other methods. The paper also does not provide a comparison with existing methods for joint compression of multiple sources. This makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed approach in the broader context of multi-source compression. The experimental evaluation is also limited in scope. The paper uses a synthetic dataset, colored MNIST, and two pairs of vision tasks on Cityscapes and COCO 2017. While these datasets are standard benchmarks, the scale of the datasets and models could be larger. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between the common and private channel rates, and how these trade-offs impact the overall performance on downstream tasks. Finally, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the proposed approach in detail. It would be beneficial to understand the potential challenges and limitations of the proposed method in real-world applications. The paper does not explore how the proposed method performs on tasks with different data distributions or task requirements, and it does not explore how the choice of the Lagrangian parameter affects the performance of the proposed method.
