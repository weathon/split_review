## Summary:

This paper introduces Nonparametric Variational Differential Privacy (NVDP), a novel approach for sharing transformer embeddings while preserving privacy. The core idea behind NVDP is to inject noise into the embeddings using a Nonparametric Variational Information Bottleneck (NVIB) layer, which is integrated within the transformer architecture. This noise injection mechanism is designed to obscure sensitive information in the embeddings, thereby providing privacy guarantees while aiming to maintain the utility of the data. The authors leverage Rényi divergence and Bayesian Differential Privacy (BDP) to quantify the privacy protection offered by their method. The NVIB layer is trained to learn a noise model that is calibrated to the downstream task, which is a key aspect of the proposed approach. The empirical evaluation of NVDP is conducted on the GLUE benchmark, a collection of natural language processing tasks. The results demonstrate a trade-off between privacy and accuracy, showing that the model can maintain high utility with strong privacy guarantees at lower noise levels. The authors compare NVDP against a Vector-Space VIB (VTDP) baseline, showing that NVDP consistently achieves better privacy-utility trade-offs, particularly in terms of BDP and Rényi Divergence (RD) metrics. The paper provides a detailed explanation of the proposed method, including the mathematical foundations of the NVIB layer and the privacy guarantees offered by RDP and BDP. The authors also discuss the use of a denoising multi-head attention (MHA) layer to further enhance the performance of the model. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in the context of transformer models. The integration of the NVIB layer within the transformer architecture and the use of nonparametric variational regularization for attention-based representations are novel aspects of this work. The empirical results on the GLUE benchmark provide evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method in achieving a balance between privacy and utility. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be improved to further strengthen its claims and impact.

## Strengths:

The paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First and foremost, the core idea of integrating a Nonparametric Variational Information Bottleneck (NVIB) layer within a transformer architecture to achieve differential privacy is a novel and valuable contribution. This approach allows for a more nuanced and effective noise injection mechanism that is better suited to the complex structure of transformer embeddings compared to traditional methods. The use of the NVIB layer, which learns a noise model calibrated to the downstream task, is a key innovation that distinguishes this work from other privacy-preserving techniques. The paper also provides a comprehensive explanation of the proposed method, including the mathematical foundations of the NVIB layer and the privacy guarantees offered by Rényi divergence and Bayesian Differential Privacy (BDP). This thoroughness in methodological description helps in understanding the underlying mechanics of the approach. The authors clearly articulate the role of the NVIB layer in introducing noise into the embeddings and how this noise helps to obscure sensitive information. Furthermore, the empirical evaluation of the proposed NVDP method on the GLUE benchmark is a significant strength. The results demonstrate that NVDP achieves a favorable privacy-utility trade-off compared to the Vector-Space VIB (VTDP) baseline. The authors show that NVDP consistently outperforms VTDP in terms of both BDP and Rényi Divergence (RD) metrics, indicating that it provides stronger privacy guarantees while maintaining comparable utility. The paper also includes a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the datasets used, the baselines compared against, and the evaluation metrics. This level of detail allows for a clear understanding of the experimental results and facilitates reproducibility. The authors also provide a clear explanation of the privacy-utility trade-off, showing that the model can maintain high utility with strong privacy guarantees at lower noise levels. This is an important finding that highlights the practical applicability of the proposed method. Finally, the paper is well-structured and clearly written, making it easy to follow the motivation behind the NVDP approach and the technical details of the method. The use of clear language and logical organization enhances the overall readability of the paper.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses need to be addressed to further solidify its claims and impact. A primary concern, consistently raised by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the NVIB layer. The paper does not provide any concrete metrics such as training time per epoch, inference latency, or memory footprint for the NVDP model compared to baseline models. This absence of computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the proposed method in real-world applications, especially when considering the computational demands of transformer models. For instance, the paper does not quantify the additional training and inference time required by NVDP compared to a standard BERT model, which is a critical factor for practitioners. This lack of analysis is a significant limitation, as it prevents a comprehensive evaluation of the trade-off between privacy, utility, and computational cost. The confidence level for this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the absence of such metrics in the 'Experiments' section. Another significant weakness is the limited scope of the experimental evaluation. The paper focuses solely on the GLUE benchmark, which primarily consists of text classification and similarity tasks. While the results on GLUE are promising, it is unclear how NVDP would perform on other types of NLP tasks, such as sequence generation, question answering, or tasks involving different data modalities. The lack of evaluation on a wider range of datasets and tasks limits the generalizability of the proposed method and its applicability to different domains. For example, the paper does not explore the performance of NVDP on tasks such as text summarization or machine translation, which involve different types of text processing and require different model architectures. This limitation is a major concern, as it restricts the applicability of the method to a narrow range of tasks. The confidence level for this weakness is high, as the 'Experiments' section explicitly states that the evaluation is limited to the GLUE benchmark. Furthermore, the paper lacks a comparison with other state-of-the-art differential privacy methods. The baselines used in the experiments are limited to a vanilla BERT model and a BERT model with basic regularization techniques (Dropout and Weight Decay). There is no comparison to other established DP methods or simpler noise addition techniques, such as directly adding Gaussian noise to the output of the transformer. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the necessity of the NVIB layer and the superiority of NVDP over other existing methods. For example, the paper does not compare NVDP to methods that use different noise mechanisms or different approaches to achieving differential privacy. This is a critical omission, as it prevents a clear understanding of the relative advantages of NVDP. The confidence level for this weakness is high, as the 'Baselines' section clearly lists the limited set of comparison methods. Additionally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the proposed method to hyperparameters. While the paper mentions the existence of hyperparameters, it does not provide a systematic study of how these parameters affect the privacy-utility trade-off. For example, the paper does not analyze how the learning rate, the noise scale, and the regularization parameters of the NVIB layer impact the final performance and privacy guarantees. This lack of analysis makes it challenging to tune the method for different datasets and tasks. For instance, the paper does not explore how the parameters of the NVIB layer, such as the prior parameters, affect the privacy-utility trade-off. This is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to optimize the method for specific applications. The confidence level for this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a dedicated section or analysis on hyperparameter sensitivity. The paper only focuses on the privacy aspect measured by Rényi divergence and BDP, but it does not analyze how the noisy embeddings affect the model's robustness to adversarial perturbations. It is unclear whether the added noise makes the model more or less susceptible to adversarial attacks, which is a critical consideration for real-world applications. For example, the paper does not evaluate the performance of NVDP under adversarial attacks, such as gradient-based attacks or perturbation-based attacks. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete and actionable improvements can be made. First and foremost, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the NVIB layer. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the training and inference time for the NVDP model compared to baseline models. Specifically, the authors should report metrics such as the time taken for one training epoch, the inference latency per sample, and the memory footprint of the model. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze how these metrics scale with the size of the input data and the complexity of the transformer architecture. For example, the authors could compare the training time of NVDP on different sizes of the GLUE datasets or with different transformer base models (e.g., BERT-base vs. BERT-large). This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the computational cost associated with the proposed method and allow practitioners to make informed decisions about its applicability in resource-constrained environments. Additionally, the authors should investigate the impact of the NVIB layer on the convergence speed of the training process. It would be useful to know if the introduction of the NVIB layer leads to a significant increase in the number of training epochs required to achieve a certain level of performance. To address the limited scope of the experimental evaluation, the authors should evaluate NVDP on a more diverse set of datasets and tasks. This could include tasks such as text summarization, machine translation, or question answering, which involve different types of text processing and require different model architectures. Furthermore, it would be valuable to explore the performance of NVDP on datasets from different domains, such as medical text, legal documents, or social media data. This would help to assess the robustness of the method to variations in data distribution and vocabulary. The authors could also consider evaluating NVDP on tasks involving different data modalities, such as image or audio data, to explore the potential of the method beyond text processing. This would require adapting the NVIB layer to handle different types of input data, but it could lead to a more general and versatile privacy-preserving method. The evaluation should also include a comparison with other privacy-preserving methods that are applicable to these tasks, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the performance of NVDP. To address the lack of comparison with other state-of-the-art DP methods, the authors should include a comparison with established DP methods and simpler noise addition techniques. This should include a comparison with methods that use different noise mechanisms or different approaches to achieving differential privacy. For example, the authors could compare NVDP to methods that add Gaussian noise directly to the output of the transformer or to other methods that use different types of noise. This would help to isolate the benefits of the NVIB layer and provide a more comprehensive assessment of the performance of NVDP. To address the lack of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, the authors should conduct a systematic study of how the hyperparameters of the NVIB layer affect the privacy-utility trade-off. This study should include a range of values for the learning rate, the noise scale, and the regularization parameters. The authors should also provide visualizations of the privacy-utility trade-off curves for different hyperparameter settings. This would allow practitioners to tune the method for their specific needs and provide a better understanding of the method's behavior. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the optimal strategy for selecting hyperparameters, such as using a validation set or cross-validation. To address the lack of robustness analysis, the authors should evaluate the performance of the proposed method under adversarial attacks. This evaluation should include a range of adversarial attack methods, such as gradient-based attacks and perturbation-based attacks. The authors should also compare the robustness of the proposed method with existing approaches. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and allow for a more informed comparison with existing approaches. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of the proposed method in terms of robustness and suggest future research directions to address these limitations. Finally, to improve the clarity and organization of the paper, the authors should provide more detailed explanations of the mathematical derivations, perhaps by including more intermediate steps and providing more intuitive explanations of the key concepts. All acronyms and technical terms should be clearly defined upon their first use. The authors should also consider reorganizing the paper to improve the flow of information, perhaps by moving some of the more technical details to an appendix. This would make the paper more accessible to a broader audience and improve its overall impact. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of the novelty of their approach. This should include a clear articulation of how the specific combination of NVIB, RDP, and BDP within the transformer architecture differs from existing methods. The authors should explicitly discuss how their approach builds upon and extends previous work, rather than simply stating that the individual components are not novel.
