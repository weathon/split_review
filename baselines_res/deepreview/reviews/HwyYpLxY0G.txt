## Summary:

This paper introduces an Aligned Scoring Rule (ASR) designed to enhance the alignment of automated grading systems with human preferences, particularly in the context of peer grading. The core idea revolves around optimizing a proper scoring rule to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between the automated score and a reference score, which is typically a human-annotated score. The authors build upon the framework of Wu and Hartline (2024), which reduces textual elicitation to numerical elicitation, and then optimize the scoring rule to better match human judgments. The method begins by using a language model to generate summary points from a collection of text reviews. These summary points are then used to create a binary representation of each review. A question-answering model is used to determine whether a review agrees with each summary point. The ASR is then applied to these binary representations to produce a final score. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through experiments on peer grading datasets, showing that the ASR achieves better alignment with human preferences, as measured by MSE, Pearson correlation, and Spearman rank correlation, compared to several baseline methods. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the ASR, proving its properness and convexity, which ensures that the optimization process is well-behaved. The authors also show that the optimization problem can be solved efficiently using gradient descent. Overall, the paper presents a novel approach to designing scoring rules for text evaluation that is both theoretically sound and empirically effective. The work addresses a critical gap in the literature by focusing on the alignment of proper scoring rules with human preferences in the context of textual information, which is particularly relevant given the increasing use of language models in various applications. The authors' approach of optimizing a proper scoring rule to align with human preferences is a significant contribution to the field of automated grading and evaluation.

Specifically, the paper assumes that the agent's posterior belief distribution is either 0, 1, or the prior (Assumption 2.2). This assumption is explicitly stated in the paper and is fundamental to the definition of the scoring rules for know-it-or-not reports. This assumption essentially limits the agent's beliefs to either complete certainty, complete uncertainty, or the prior belief, which is a very narrow view of how agents form beliefs in real-world scenarios. The paper does not provide any justification for this assumption, nor does it discuss the potential impact of this assumption on the performance of the ASR.
