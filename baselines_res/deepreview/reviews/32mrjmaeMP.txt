## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel dataless approach to enhance weight disentanglement in task arithmetic for foundation models, addressing the challenge of cross-task interference that often degrades performance when combining task-specific parameter updates. The authors frame the problem of minimizing representation drift as a curvature matrix approximation problem, leveraging the Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC) technique. This innovative approach allows for effective task vector composition and negation while mitigating interference, all without requiring access to task-specific data. The proposed method, termed Task Arithmetic with KFAC regularization (TAK), achieves state-of-the-art results in both task addition and negation across various benchmarks and model architectures, including vision and language tasks. The core idea revolves around approximating the curvature matrix using KFAC, which allows the authors to formulate a practical regularizer that promotes weight disentanglement. This regularizer is applied during fine-tuning, guiding the learning of task vectors that are less prone to interfering with each other. The authors demonstrate that their approach maintains constant complexity and storage requirements regardless of the number of tasks, making it scalable and practical for real-world applications. The paper presents a thorough analysis of the proposed method, including empirical evaluations on diverse datasets and model architectures, demonstrating its effectiveness in both linearized and non-linear fine-tuning regimes. The results show that TAK outperforms existing methods in task addition and negation, exhibiting desirable properties such as task localization and robustness to task vector rescaling. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead and memory usage, further strengthening the paper's practical relevance. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field of task arithmetic by introducing a dataless, efficient, and effective approach to weight disentanglement, addressing key limitations of existing methods and paving the way for more robust and scalable task adaptation in foundation models.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the method's reliance on linearization, while empirically effective, is a potential limitation. The paper explicitly uses a first-order Taylor approximation of the model around its pre-trained parameters, as shown in Equation 1, and acknowledges that this approach is beneficial for weight disentanglement. However, as the reviewer correctly points out, this linearization may not accurately capture the true curvature of the loss landscape, especially when the task-specific updates are large enough to move the parameters significantly away from the pre-trained point. This could lead to suboptimal task vectors and reduced performance in scenarios where non-linearities play a crucial role. While the paper does explore the non-linear regime and shows that TAK, when combined with Attention-Only Fine-Tuning, can still be effective, the theoretical foundation is primarily in the linearized setting. This reliance on linearization is a valid concern, and I have high confidence in this assessment. Second, the implementation of the KFAC aggregation, while described, may involve complex steps that could pose challenges, particularly for large models. While the paper describes the aggregation process in Equation 8 and mentions that it empirically matches the un-merged formulation, the practical details of efficiently computing and storing the KFAC approximations could be complex. The process of inverting or decomposing the Kronecker factors for each task and then merging them might introduce computational bottlenecks and require careful memory management. Although the paper provides experimental evidence that the computational overhead and memory usage are manageable, as shown in Figure 6, the inherent complexity of KFAC implementation is a valid concern, and I have medium confidence in this assessment. Third, the paper does not fully explore the potential trade-offs in terms of computational efficiency for very large models or tasks with high dimensionality. While the authors mention that the method scales linearly with the number of parameters and that aggregating all per-task KFAC factors into a single surrogate keeps the training footprint of the method at O(1) with respect to the number of tasks, the actual computational cost of calculating and applying the KFAC approximations could still be substantial for extremely large models. The memory footprint of storing the KFAC factors for multiple tasks could also become a limiting factor. Although the paper explores KFAC compression techniques to mitigate memory footprint, as shown in Figure 7b, a detailed analysis of computational cost for extremely large models is missing, and I have medium confidence in this assessment. Fourth, the paper's reliance on the KFAC approximation, while effective, may not fully capture the complexity of the curvature matrix in all scenarios. The KFAC approximation, by its nature, simplifies the curvature by assuming independence between different layers' inputs and outputs, as described in Section 3.3. This simplification could lead to suboptimal performance in scenarios where the true curvature deviates significantly from the KFAC approximation, particularly in models with intricate skip connections or attention mechanisms that create complex dependencies between layers. While the paper evaluates on models with attention mechanisms and shows good performance, the inherent limitations of the KFAC approximation are a valid concern, and I have high confidence in this assessment. Fifth, the paper primarily focuses on vision and language tasks, and it is unclear how well the proposed method would generalize to other domains or more complex tasks. The experiments are conducted on the "8 Vision" benchmark and natural language processing tasks, as described in Section 4. While these tasks are diverse, they are relatively well-defined and may not fully represent the challenges of more complex, real-world applications. For example, tasks involving multimodal data or those requiring complex reasoning and planning might expose limitations of the proposed approach. The paper lacks a discussion on the potential limitations of the method when applied to tasks with very different characteristics, and I have high confidence in this assessment. Finally, the paper does not extensively discuss the potential limitations of the proposed method in scenarios where the number of tasks is very large or the tasks are highly heterogeneous. While the paper shows that the method has constant complexity with respect to the number of tasks, the KFAC approximation might become less accurate as the number of tasks increases, especially if the tasks are very different from each other. The accumulation of Kronecker factors, while maintaining constant complexity, might lead to a loss of task-specific information, particularly if the tasks have very different curvature properties. The paper does not provide a theoretical analysis of how the approximation error scales with the number of tasks or the heterogeneity of the task set, and I have high confidence in this assessment.


## Suggestions:

Based on the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete suggestions for improving this work. First, the authors should investigate the sensitivity of the proposed method to the accuracy of the KFAC approximation. It would be beneficial to explore alternative curvature approximations, such as those based on the full Hessian or other block-diagonal approximations, and compare their performance with KFAC. This would help to understand the trade-offs between computational efficiency and accuracy. Furthermore, the authors could analyze the eigenvalue spectrum of the approximated curvature matrix and compare it to the true curvature matrix for a few representative tasks to quantify the approximation error. This analysis could provide insights into the scenarios where KFAC is most effective and where it might fall short. Second, to address the generalization concern, the authors should consider evaluating the method on a more diverse set of tasks, including those from different domains and with varying levels of complexity. This could include tasks involving multimodal data, such as image captioning or video understanding, or tasks requiring complex reasoning, such as question answering or planning. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of the method when applied to such tasks and suggest possible extensions or modifications to address these limitations. A more thorough analysis of the method's performance across different task types would significantly strengthen the paper's claims about its general applicability. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the method's behavior in scenarios with a large number of tasks or highly heterogeneous tasks. This could involve theoretical analysis of the approximation error as a function of the number of tasks and their heterogeneity, as well as empirical evaluation on datasets with varying numbers of tasks and task similarities. The authors should also investigate the impact of different task accumulation strategies on the performance of the method. This analysis would help to understand the limitations of the method in more challenging scenarios and provide guidance on its practical application. Fourth, the authors should provide more details on the practical implementation of the KFAC aggregation. While the authors mention that the method scales linearly with the number of parameters, the constant factor associated with the matrix operations could be significant for large models. A detailed breakdown of the computational cost of each step, including the calculation of the Jacobian and Hessian approximations, the Kronecker factorization, and the merging of per-task factors, would be valuable. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the memory requirements for storing the KFAC factors, especially when dealing with multiple tasks. Exploring techniques for reducing the memory footprint, such as low-rank approximations or compression methods, would be beneficial. The authors could also consider providing a reference implementation to facilitate the adoption of their method by the community. Fifth, the paper should include a more comprehensive analysis of the computational efficiency of the method, particularly for very large models and high-dimensional tasks. This should include a comparison of the training time and memory usage of the proposed method with existing task arithmetic techniques. The authors should also investigate the impact of the number of tasks on the computational cost and memory requirements. It would be useful to see how the method scales with an increasing number of tasks and whether there are any practical limitations in terms of the number of tasks that can be handled. This analysis should be performed on a range of model sizes and task complexities to provide a more complete picture of the method's applicability. Finally, the authors should investigate the limitations of the linearization assumption more thoroughly. Specifically, the paper should include experiments that quantify the performance degradation as the magnitude of task-specific updates increases, moving further away from the linear regime. This could involve measuring the curvature of the loss landscape along the task update directions and comparing it to the linear approximation. Additionally, exploring alternative curvature approximations that better capture non-linearities, such as higher-order Taylor expansions or methods based on empirical gradients, could be beneficial. This would help to understand the trade-offs between computational cost and accuracy of the curvature approximation.
