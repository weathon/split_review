## Summary:

This paper introduces RADAR, a novel neural framework designed to enhance existing neural solvers for Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) by effectively handling asymmetric distance matrices. The core contribution of RADAR lies in its ability to address the limitations of current neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) models, which often assume symmetric Euclidean distances, thereby restricting their applicability in real-world scenarios where routing is influenced by factors like one-way streets and traffic congestion. RADAR tackles the challenge of asymmetric VRPs through two primary mechanisms: an SVD-based initialization and Sinkhorn normalization within the attention mechanism. The SVD-based initialization aims to capture static asymmetries by creating compact embeddings that encode each node's role as a source and destination, preserving global directionality. Specifically, the distance matrix is decomposed using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), and the resulting components are used to initialize node embeddings. This approach seeks to address the limitations of previous attempts at encoding static asymmetry, which often lose global structure and underperform. The Sinkhorn normalization, on the other hand, addresses dynamic asymmetries by imposing joint row and column distance awareness in the attention weights. This ensures balanced bidirectional flows, allowing the attention score to capture interactions that depend not only on each node's own neighborhood but also on the neighborhood structure of its counterparts. The authors evaluate RADAR on a diverse set of 17 synthetic and 3 real-world VRP variants, demonstrating its superior performance and robust generalization compared to existing methods. The empirical results consistently show that RADAR outperforms strong baselines on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution instances, highlighting its practical significance in solving asymmetric VRPs. The paper's findings suggest that by explicitly addressing the challenges posed by asymmetric distance matrices, RADAR offers a more realistic and effective approach to solving VRPs in real-world settings. The combination of SVD-based initialization and Sinkhorn normalization provides a novel way to incorporate asymmetry-aware distance representations into neural VRP solvers, leading to improved performance and generalization capabilities. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of neural combinatorial optimization by addressing a critical gap in the handling of asymmetric VRPs.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper demonstrates improved performance, it lacks a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of the proposed method. The use of SVD and Sinkhorn normalization could introduce additional computational costs, which are not adequately addressed. The paper provides some runtime information in the results tables, but it does not offer a detailed breakdown of the computational cost of the SVD and Sinkhorn normalization steps. The "Runtime Analysis" sections offer qualitative assessments, stating that the GPU-batched implementation keeps Sinkhorn's overhead modest, but this is not a quantitative analysis. There is no explicit comparison of the computational cost of RADAR against the baseline methods in terms of FLOPs or other standard measures of computational complexity. This lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the approach, especially for large-scale instances. The absence of a breakdown of the time spent on the SVD decomposition itself, and how this time compares to the rest of the model's operations, is a significant oversight. Furthermore, the analysis should consider the memory footprint of the SVD, especially for large distance matrices, and how this might limit the scalability of the approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lacks this analysis, and it is a crucial aspect for practical applications. Second, the paper does not sufficiently explore the limitations of the proposed method, particularly in scenarios where the asymmetry is not well-captured by the SVD initialization or where Sinkhorn normalization may not be sufficient to model dynamic asymmetries. The paper describes SVD as approximating the distance matrix, but it doesn't discuss the implications of low-rank structures or highly localized asymmetries on the effectiveness of this approximation. Similarly, the description of Sinkhorn normalization focuses on enforcing balanced bidirectional flows but doesn't address its limitations in capturing complex, non-linear interactions. The experiments cover various levels of asymmetry, but there isn't a specific analysis of scenarios where RADAR's performance degrades significantly due to specific asymmetry patterns. This lack of analysis of failure modes is a significant limitation. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a dedicated discussion on these limitations. Third, the paper could benefit from a more thorough exploration of the sensitivity of the model to hyperparameters, such as the number of singular values used in the SVD initialization and the parameters of the Sinkhorn normalization. While the "Ablation Study and Discussions" section includes an analysis of the effect of the number of singular values ($k$) on generalization performance, this analysis is limited to the initialization aspect and doesn't cover the full model. The paper mentions setting the number of Sinkhorn iterations to $T=10$, but it doesn't provide a sensitivity analysis for this parameter or discuss the regularization parameter often associated with Sinkhorn. The lack of a comprehensive sensitivity analysis for these hyperparameters makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a systematic analysis of these hyperparameters. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed RADAR framework. Specifically, they should provide a breakdown of the time complexity for each step, including the SVD decomposition, Sinkhorn normalization, and the neural network operations. This analysis should consider the impact of the input distance matrix size and the number of nodes on the overall runtime. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the memory footprint of the SVD operation, especially for large distance matrices, and discuss potential strategies to mitigate memory usage. For example, they could explore the use of randomized SVD algorithms, which can provide a good approximation of the SVD with reduced computational cost and memory requirements. A comparison of the runtime and memory usage of the proposed method with existing neural VRP solvers would also be valuable to demonstrate the practical scalability of RADAR. Second, the authors should delve deeper into the limitations of their approach, particularly concerning the SVD initialization and Sinkhorn normalization. The current discussion is somewhat superficial, and a more rigorous analysis is needed. For example, the authors could explore scenarios where the distance matrix has a low-rank structure, which might not be well-represented by a truncated SVD. They could also investigate cases where the asymmetry is highly localized, such as in urban areas with many one-way streets, and analyze how well the global SVD captures these local variations. Furthermore, the authors should consider the impact of noise in the distance matrix on the SVD initialization and how this might affect the overall performance. A more detailed analysis of these failure modes would provide a more complete picture of the method's applicability and limitations. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough sensitivity analysis of the model's hyperparameters. Specifically, they should explore how the performance varies with different numbers of singular values used in the SVD initialization and the regularization parameter in the Sinkhorn normalization. For the SVD, the authors should analyze how the reconstruction quality of the distance matrix changes with different numbers of singular values, and how this affects the final VRP solution quality. This analysis should include a quantitative evaluation of the reconstruction error and the corresponding VRP performance metrics. For the Sinkhorn normalization, the authors should examine the effect of the regularization parameter on the convergence and stability of the model. This could involve varying the regularization parameter and observing its impact on the training loss and the final solution quality. The authors should provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for these hyperparameters, based on their experimental findings. Finally, the authors should refine their claims regarding the scope of their method. They should explicitly state that RADAR is designed for VRPs with asymmetric distance matrices only and acknowledge that their approach is not directly applicable to VRPs with other forms of asymmetry without significant modifications. A more precise title and framing of the problem would greatly improve the paper's clarity and avoid misleading readers about the scope of the proposed method. For example, the title could be changed to "Learning to Route with Asymmetry-Aware Distance Representations" to better reflect the specific focus of the paper. These changes would provide a more complete and accurate picture of the proposed method's capabilities and limitations, enhancing the paper's overall quality and impact.
