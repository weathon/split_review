## Summary:

This paper introduces GRAID, a novel framework designed to enhance spatial reasoning in Vision Language Models (VLMs) through the generation of high-quality, synthetic Visual Question Answering (VQA) data. The core idea behind GRAID is to leverage 2D geometric primitives derived from object detection bounding boxes to create qualitative spatial relationships, thereby avoiding the complexities and potential inaccuracies associated with single-view 3D reconstruction and generative models. The framework operates by first utilizing object detection models to identify and classify objects within an image, then applying a set of predicates to these detections to generate spatial questions and answers. These predicates, which are designed to be lightweight and efficient, are used to filter out infeasible question-answer pairs before more computationally expensive question realization processes are applied. This approach, termed SPARQ (Sieve Predicates And Realize Questions), allows for the scalable generation of large datasets. The authors apply GRAID to three large-scale driving datasets—BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo—resulting in over 8.5 million VQA pairs. A key finding is that the generated data achieves a high human-validated accuracy rate of 91.16%, significantly surpassing existing methods. The authors demonstrate that VLMs fine-tuned on GRAID-generated data exhibit substantial improvements in spatial reasoning tasks and show strong generalization capabilities across different datasets, question types, and external benchmarks. The paper also highlights the efficiency of the SPARQ component, which provides significant speedups for computationally expensive templates. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to generating high-quality spatial reasoning data for VLMs, addressing the limitations of previous methods and demonstrating the effectiveness of the generated data through rigorous empirical evaluation. The focus on 2D geometric primitives and the use of a predicate-based filtering system are key innovations that contribute to the framework's success. The results suggest that GRAID offers a practical and effective way to improve the spatial reasoning capabilities of VLMs, with potential applications in various domains requiring spatial understanding.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to generating high-quality spatial reasoning data for Vision Language Models (VLMs). The core idea of using 2D geometric primitives derived from object detection bounding boxes to create qualitative spatial relationships is both novel and effective. By focusing on 2D information, the framework avoids the complexities and potential inaccuracies associated with single-view 3D reconstruction, which is a significant advantage over existing methods like SpatialVLM and SpatialRGPT. This design choice is well-justified, as it allows for a more robust and reliable generation of spatial reasoning data. The introduction of the SPARQ component is another notable contribution. The use of lightweight predicates to filter out infeasible question-answer pairs before applying more computationally expensive question realization processes is a clever way to improve the efficiency of the data generation process. The paper provides concrete examples of how these predicates significantly reduce the computational cost, with speedups of up to 1407x reported for certain question types. This efficiency is crucial for scaling the framework to large datasets, as demonstrated by the generation of over 8.5 million VQA pairs across three datasets. The empirical results presented in the paper are also compelling. The human-validated accuracy rate of 91.16% for the generated data is significantly higher than existing methods, indicating the high quality of the GRAID-generated datasets. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate that VLMs fine-tuned on GRAID data exhibit substantial improvements in spatial reasoning tasks and show strong generalization capabilities across different datasets, question types, and external benchmarks. This generalization ability is a key strength, as it suggests that the spatial reasoning skills learned from GRAID data are not specific to the training datasets. The rigorous evaluation, including human validation and performance benchmarks, provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The paper also clearly articulates the limitations of existing methods and how GRAID addresses these limitations, further highlighting the significance of the contribution. The focus on qualitative spatial relationships, rather than attempting to generate precise 3D information, is a deliberate and well-justified design choice that contributes to the overall success of the framework. The combination of methodological innovation, empirical rigor, and clear articulation of the problem and solution makes this a strong and impactful contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to generating spatial reasoning data, there are several limitations that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by multiple reviewers, is the framework's reliance on 2D spatial relationships. As the paper explicitly states, "GRAID does nearly all of its analysis in the 2D image space," and it avoids single-view 3D reconstruction. This design choice, while beneficial for avoiding the errors associated with 3D reconstruction, inherently limits the framework's ability to capture the full complexity of spatial arrangements, particularly those involving depth variations or occlusions. The method relies on axis-aligned bounding boxes, represented by their corner coordinates, and the predicates used to generate questions are based on these 2D relationships. This means that tasks requiring a deeper understanding of 3D spatial reasoning, such as determining the relative height of objects or understanding the precise 3D orientation of an object, are beyond the scope of the current framework. For instance, the paper does not address how GRAID would handle scenarios where the same 2D projection can result from different 3D configurations. This limitation is significant because many real-world spatial reasoning tasks inherently involve 3D understanding. The paper's experimental setup and question types, as shown in Figure 2, further confirm this focus on 2D spatial reasoning, with question categories like 'Spatial Relations,' 'Counting,' and 'Ranking' all being addressable using 2D information. The absence of any 3D-specific question types or evaluation metrics underscores this limitation. The implications of this 2D focus are that models trained on GRAID data may struggle with tasks that require a more nuanced understanding of 3D space, such as viewpoint changes or precise depth estimation. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit statements about its 2D focus and the nature of the generated data. Another significant weakness, also independently validated, is the computational cost associated with generating large-scale datasets, even with the SPARQ optimization. While the paper demonstrates that SPARQ provides significant speedups, the overall process of generating millions of VQA pairs still requires substantial computational resources. The paper mentions generating "over 8.5 million high-quality VQA pairs" and provides examples of predicate and realization times, indicating a computationally intensive process. The paper acknowledges that "Checking spatial relationships between objects quickly becomes expensive as this is a quadratic operation," and that SPARQ is designed to address this. However, even with the reported speedups, the generation of such a large dataset would still necessitate considerable computational power. This could limit the accessibility of the framework for researchers with limited infrastructure. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for dataset generation, making it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the approach for different users. The lack of a direct comparison of computational cost with other data generation methods further exacerbates this issue. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is supported by the scale of the generated datasets and the acknowledgment of the computational expense of the process. Finally, the paper does not extensively discuss the limitations of GRAID in handling complex or ambiguous spatial relationships. The framework's reliance on axis-aligned bounding boxes and relatively simple predicates may not capture nuanced spatial relationships, such as objects partially occluding each other or being viewed from oblique angles. While the paper mentions checking for non-overlapping bounding boxes as a predicate, this addresses only a basic form of ambiguity. The paper does not delve into how GRAID would handle scenarios with intricate object interactions or non-standard viewpoints. The examples in Figure 1 show relatively simple spatial relationships, and the question types in Figure 2 also lean towards basic spatial reasoning. The absence of a detailed discussion on how GRAID handles occlusions, unusual viewpoints, or complex object arrangements is a notable limitation. This lack of robustness in handling complex scenarios could lead to a decrease in performance in real-world applications where such complexities are common. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified limitations, several concrete improvements can be made to the GRAID framework. First, to mitigate the limitations of relying solely on 2D spatial relationships, future work should explore incorporating depth information or pseudo-3D cues into the framework. This could involve leveraging existing depth estimation techniques, such as monocular depth estimation models, to infer depth information from the 2D images. Alternatively, multi-view geometry could be explored to infer 3D relationships from multiple 2D images, if available. The framework could be extended to generate questions that involve depth comparisons, such as "Which object is farther away?" or "Is object A in front of or behind object B?". This would significantly enhance the framework's ability to handle more complex spatial reasoning tasks and improve its applicability to real-world scenarios where 3D understanding is crucial. Furthermore, the framework could benefit from incorporating techniques to handle occlusions more effectively. This could involve using context from surrounding objects or employing predictive models to infer the presence of occluded objects. For example, if an object is partially occluded, the framework could use the visible parts and the context of surrounding objects to infer its full shape and position. This would improve the robustness of the generated data and the models trained on it. Second, to improve the handling of complex and ambiguous spatial relationships, the framework could be enhanced to generate questions that involve more intricate object interactions and non-standard viewpoints. This could involve creating templates that specifically target scenarios with occlusions, unusual viewpoints, or complex object arrangements. For example, the framework could generate questions like "Is the car partially hidden behind the tree?" or "What is the relative position of the pedestrian when viewed from the side?". Additionally, the framework could benefit from incorporating techniques to evaluate the ambiguity of generated questions and filter out those that are too vague or open to multiple interpretations. This could involve using a combination of rule-based methods and machine learning models to assess the clarity and precision of the generated questions. The framework could also explore the use of contrastive learning to better distinguish between similar but distinct spatial relationships. This would involve training models to differentiate between subtle differences in spatial arrangements, leading to a more nuanced understanding of spatial relationships. Finally, to mitigate the computational cost of generating large-scale datasets, the authors could explore techniques to further optimize the SPARQ component or investigate alternative methods for data generation. This could involve exploring parallel processing techniques or using more efficient data structures to store and process the generated data. Additionally, the authors could investigate the possibility of using transfer learning to reduce the amount of data required for training models on spatial reasoning tasks. This could involve pre-training models on a smaller dataset and then fine-tuning them on the GRAID-generated data. Furthermore, the authors could explore the possibility of generating a smaller, but more diverse, dataset that covers a wider range of spatial relationships, rather than focusing solely on the quantity of data. This would allow for a more efficient use of computational resources while still ensuring that the models are exposed to a wide range of spatial scenarios. These suggestions are all concrete and actionable, and they directly address the identified weaknesses. Implementing these improvements would significantly enhance the capabilities and applicability of the GRAID framework.
