## Summary:

This paper delves into the phenomenon of benign relearning in machine unlearning, a critical challenge where models unintentionally recover forgotten information after being fine-tuned on seemingly innocuous data. The authors challenge the prevailing notion that topical relevance is the primary driver of this relearning, arguing instead that syntactic similarity between the relearn and target datasets plays a more significant role. Through a series of experiments, they demonstrate that syntactically similar data, even in the absence of topical overlap, can trigger the recovery of forgotten content. This finding is particularly insightful as it highlights a previously underappreciated mechanism behind unlearning failures. To mitigate this issue, the authors propose a method called syntactic diversification. This approach involves paraphrasing the forget set into diverse syntactic structures before the unlearning process. The core idea is that by exposing the model to a wider range of syntactic variations during unlearning, the model becomes less susceptible to relearning based on syntactic cues. The authors demonstrate that this method effectively suppresses benign relearning, accelerates the forgetting process, and improves the trade-off between unlearning efficacy and model utility. The paper's methodology involves a combination of controlled experiments on a synthetic dataset and analysis of representation and gradient similarities. The experiments primarily focus on the TOFU dataset, a synthetic question-answering dataset designed to study unlearning. The authors use this dataset to create topically relevant and syntactically similar relearn sets, allowing them to isolate the impact of syntactic similarity. They also analyze the representation and gradient similarities between the target and relearn sets to understand the underlying mechanisms of relearning. The main empirical findings of the paper are that syntactic similarity is a more significant driver of benign relearning than topical relevance, and that syntactic diversification is an effective method for mitigating this issue. The authors show that models fine-tuned on syntactically similar data exhibit a higher degree of relearning compared to models fine-tuned on topically relevant but syntactically dissimilar data. They also demonstrate that syntactic diversification reduces the relearning rate and improves the overall unlearning performance. The overall significance of this work lies in its novel perspective on the drivers of benign relearning and its practical solution for mitigating this issue. By shifting the focus from topical relevance to syntactic similarity, the paper provides a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind unlearning failures. The proposed method of syntactic diversification offers a practical approach for enhancing the robustness of unlearning methods, with potential implications for a wide range of applications where data privacy and model compliance are important.

## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, the paper has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant weaknesses is the heavy reliance on the TOFU dataset for the primary experiments. As the reviewers correctly point out, the TOFU dataset has a specific structure, with its synthetic question-answer pairs and limited syntactic templates. This raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to other datasets with different characteristics. The paper's description of the TOFU dataset confirms its synthetic nature and structured format, and the lack of experiments on more diverse datasets in the main body supports this concern. The consistent question-answer pairs in TOFU might artificially inflate the impact of syntactic similarity, making it difficult to assess the method's effectiveness on more diverse and less structured datasets. This limitation is particularly concerning because real-world data often exhibits a much wider range of syntactic structures and semantic content. Therefore, the observed effects might be specific to the structured format of TOFU and may not generalize to more complex scenarios. I have high confidence in this limitation as it is directly supported by the paper's description of the dataset and the absence of experiments on other datasets. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed syntactic diversification method. The paper does not explicitly address how the method performs when the syntactic structures of the forget set are inherently diverse. In such cases, the paraphrasing process might not introduce sufficient diversity to prevent relearning. The paper focuses on the benefits of the method but lacks a detailed discussion of its potential shortcomings.
