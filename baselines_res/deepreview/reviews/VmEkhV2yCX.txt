## Summary:

This paper presents a systematic investigation into the impact of incorporating reasoning-focused data at different stages of large language model (LLM) training, specifically during pretraining, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The authors' central hypothesis is that front-loading reasoning data during pretraining establishes foundational reasoning capabilities that cannot be fully replicated by SFT alone, even with increased data volume. To test this, they conduct extensive experiments using a hybrid transformer architecture with Mamba-2, self-attention, and feed-forward layers, and an 8 billion parameter model. They introduce reasoning data of varying scale, diversity, and quality at different training stages and evaluate the resulting models on a range of reasoning tasks, including math, science, code, and general purpose reasoning. The core methodological approach involves pretraining models from scratch on a 6.2T token dataset, with some models receiving additional reasoning data during pretraining. These models are then SFTed on a 4.8M token reasoning dataset and further aligned using RLHF. The main empirical finding is that pretraining with diverse reasoning data significantly enhances overall performance, particularly in science and code tasks, and that this benefit is not fully recoverable through SFT alone. The authors also demonstrate that pretraining benefits most from broad diversity in reasoning patterns, while SFT is more sensitive to data quality. Furthermore, they show that high-quality pretraining data can have latent effects, activated only after SFT, and that naively scaling SFT data can be detrimental. These findings challenge the conventional separation of language modeling and reasoning, suggesting that reasoning capabilities should be integrated into the pretraining process. The overall significance of this work lies in its systematic approach to understanding the role of reasoning data across the entire training pipeline, providing valuable insights for strategically allocating data to build more capable and efficient LLMs. The authors' findings suggest that a balanced approach, prioritizing diversity in pretraining and quality in SFT, is crucial for optimizing LLM performance on reasoning tasks. This work contributes to a growing body of research that seeks to move beyond the traditional view of pretraining as solely focused on language modeling, and towards a more integrated approach that incorporates reasoning from the outset.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several key improvements are needed. First, the authors should conduct additional experiments to assess the generalizability of their findings across different LLM architectures and pretraining datasets. This could involve evaluating the impact of early reasoning exposure on models with varying sizes, attention mechanisms, and pretraining objectives. For example, experiments could be conducted using smaller models to determine if the observed benefits of front-loading reasoning data are maintained with reduced computational resources. Furthermore, the authors should explore the effects of different pretraining objectives, such as causal language modeling, to see if the optimal data allocation strategy varies depending on the pretraining task. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay between pretraining objectives, model architecture, and the effectiveness of early reasoning exposure. The authors should also consider using multiple datasets with varying characteristics to assess the robustness of their findings. This would help to determine whether the observed benefits are specific to the chosen dataset or if they generalize to other datasets with different compositions. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational trade-offs associated with front-loading reasoning data during pretraining. This analysis should include a comparison of the computational cost of pretraining with reasoning data versus a standard pretraining pipeline followed by SFT. The authors should quantify the additional training time, memory requirements, and hyperparameter tuning efforts required when reasoning data is introduced early. This analysis should also consider the potential for diminishing returns when increasing the amount of reasoning data during pretraining, as well as the possibility of overfitting to specific reasoning tasks. A cost-benefit analysis would be valuable for practitioners to determine whether the benefits of front-loading reasoning data justify the additional computational costs. Third, to strengthen the paper's claims, a critical ablation study is needed to isolate the impact of reasoning data during pretraining. This would involve training a control model on the same total volume of data as the models that include reasoning data, but with the reasoning data replaced by an equivalent volume of general pretraining data. This would allow for a direct comparison and determine whether the observed gains are due to the specific nature of the reasoning data or simply the increased data volume. Furthermore, the paper should provide a clear metric for quantifying the diversity of the reasoning data. This could involve measuring the distribution of reasoning tasks across different domains (e.g., math, code, science) or using a more sophisticated measure of semantic diversity. This would allow for a more rigorous assessment of the claim that diversity in reasoning patterns is beneficial during pretraining. Fourth, the paper should include evaluations on more challenging benchmarks that specifically test complex reasoning abilities, such as Veril-2. This would help to determine whether the observed gains generalize to tasks that require more advanced reasoning skills. Additionally, the paper should explore the impact of reasoning data on different model sizes and investigate the interplay between data adjustments and model scaling for improving reasoning capabilities. This could involve training models with different parameter counts and comparing the results. Fifth, the authors should conduct a more rigorous analysis of the model's performance on out-of-distribution reasoning tasks. This could involve evaluating the model on datasets that are significantly different from the training data, both in terms of domain and the type of reasoning required. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different regularization techniques, such as dropout or weight decay, on the model's generalization capabilities when reasoning data is introduced at different stages. This would help to determine whether the observed gains are robust or whether they are due to overfitting to the specific training data. Finally, the authors should clarify the discrepancies in their presentation of results, such as the statement about minimal improvement in science tasks, and provide a clear rationale for the experimental design in Table 6, where the total token count is kept constant while varying the ratio of reasoning data. These changes would significantly strengthen the paper's conclusions and provide more actionable insights for practitioners.
