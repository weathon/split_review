## Summary:

This paper delves into the critical issue of data contamination in the evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs), with a specific focus on the complexities introduced by multilingual contexts. The authors investigate how contamination manifests when benchmarks are translated into Arabic, a language often considered low-resource in the context of LLM training data. The core methodology involves fine-tuning several open-weight LLMs on varying proportions of Arabic-translated datasets and subsequently evaluating their performance on the original English benchmarks. To detect memorization, the authors extend the Tested Slot (TS)-Guessing method with a choice-reordering strategy, aiming to distinguish between genuine reasoning and mere recall of contaminated data. The empirical findings reveal that while translating benchmarks into Arabic can mask traditional contamination signals, the models still benefit from exposure to contaminated data, particularly those with stronger Arabic language capabilities. This suggests that translation alone is not a sufficient solution for mitigating contamination. To address this, the authors propose a Translation-Aware Contamination Detection (TACD) framework, which involves checking for contamination across multiple translated versions of benchmarks. The paper's significance lies in its identification of a critical gap in LLM evaluation practices, specifically the lack of attention to multilingual contamination, and in its proposal of a framework that attempts to address this gap. The study highlights the need for more robust, multilingual contamination-aware evaluation pipelines to ensure fair and transparent assessment of LLMs. The authors' work underscores the challenges of relying solely on translation to mitigate contamination and points towards the necessity of developing more sophisticated detection methods that are sensitive to the nuances of multilingual data. The paper's findings have important implications for the development and evaluation of LLMs in a global context, where the use of multiple languages is increasingly common.

## Strengths:

This paper makes several valuable contributions to the field of LLM evaluation. First and foremost, it addresses a critical and often overlooked issue: data contamination in multilingual settings. By focusing on the impact of translating benchmarks into Arabic, a language often treated as low-resource, the authors highlight a significant gap in current evaluation practices. This is a novel contribution, as most existing research on contamination focuses on English-centric datasets. The paper's use of the Tested Slot (TS)-Guessing method, extended with a choice-reordering strategy, is a creative approach to detecting memorization. This method allows the authors to probe whether models are genuinely reasoning or simply recalling contaminated data. The empirical findings are compelling, demonstrating that while translation can obscure traditional contamination signals, models still benefit from exposure to contaminated data, especially those with stronger Arabic capabilities. This is a crucial insight, as it challenges the assumption that translation alone can effectively mitigate contamination. The proposed Translation-Aware Contamination Detection (TACD) framework is a practical step towards addressing multilingual contamination. By suggesting the use of multiple translated versions of benchmarks, the authors offer a potential solution for more robust contamination detection. The paper is also well-organized and clearly written, making complex ideas accessible to a broad audience. The authors effectively present their methodology, results, and implications in a logical and coherent manner. The paper's focus on a real-world problem, coupled with its innovative methodology and practical framework, makes it a valuable contribution to the field. The authors have successfully identified a critical issue and proposed a potential solution, paving the way for future research in this important area. The paper's emphasis on the need for multilingual contamination-aware pipelines is particularly significant, as it underscores the importance of developing fair and transparent evaluation practices for LLMs in a global context.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper makes several important contributions, it also has some notable limitations that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant weaknesses is the study's exclusive focus on Arabic translations. As the reviewers have pointed out, this narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings to other languages and contexts. The paper explicitly states its focus on Arabic in the abstract, main idea, and experiments, and the experimental setup uses Arabic-translated datasets. This focus, while valuable for highlighting the challenges of low-resource languages, leaves open questions about how contamination dynamics might vary across different language families, resource levels, or translation methods. Specifically, the paper does not explore how the observed contamination effects might differ with languages that have different word orders, morphological structures, or levels of linguistic resources available for translation. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings and raises concerns about the robustness of the proposed framework across diverse linguistic contexts. The paper's motivation acknowledges the underexplored nature of multilingual contamination, implicitly suggesting the limitations of focusing on a single language. This is a valid concern, as the observed contamination effects might be specific to the Arabic language and not generalizable to other languages. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit focus on Arabic and the lack of exploration of other languages. Another significant weakness is that the proposed Translation-Aware Contamination Detection (TACD) framework is presented at a conceptual level without a full implementation or empirical validation. The paper introduces TACD in Section 5, describing its key components: "Cross-Translation Benchmarking," "TS-Guessing Across Variants," and "Back-Translation Consistency." However, the explanation of these components is conceptual, lacking specific algorithmic details or computational resource requirements. The paper explicitly states that TACD is a "blueprint" and not a complete implementation, acknowledging that deploying it at scale would require shared infrastructure and community resources. The experiments focus on evaluating the impact of Arabic translation on contamination using TS-Guessing, not on implementing and validating the TACD framework itself. The absence of quantitative metrics for evaluating the framework's performance further hinders its practical evaluation. Without a working prototype or detailed experimental results demonstrating the framework's effectiveness, it is difficult to assess its real-world applicability and scalability. This lack of implementation and validation limits the immediate practical utility of the TACD framework. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit statement that TACD is a "blueprint" and the conceptual description of its components. Finally, while the paper acknowledges some challenges in implementing TACD, the discussion is relatively brief and does not thoroughly explore the potential trade-offs or challenges in implementing the framework across diverse multilingual benchmarks. The paper briefly mentions some challenges in Section 5.3, stating that TACD demands extensive multilingual resources, high-quality translations, and substantial compute. It also notes that building and maintaining multi-language benchmark variants is non-trivial and that translation introduces noise. However, this discussion is not extensive, and the paper could benefit from a more in-depth exploration of the practical difficulties and limitations of the proposed framework. The paper does not delve into the computational overhead of generating and storing multiple translated versions of benchmarks, nor does it fully address the potential for translation errors or biases to affect the accuracy of contamination detection. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does acknowledge some challenges, but the discussion is not thorough enough to fully address the reviewer's concern.
