## Summary:

This paper delves into the critical problem of decision-making under uncertainty when relying on predictive models that may not be perfectly calibrated. The authors introduce a robust framework for a decision-maker who seeks to maximize their expected utility while accounting for potential miscalibration in the predictor. The core idea revolves around formulating a minimax optimization problem, where the decision-maker aims to maximize their utility in the worst-case scenario, considering all distributions consistent with the predictor's calibration guarantees. The paper's primary contribution lies in characterizing the optimal robust decision rule, demonstrating its computational tractability, and exploring its behavior under specific calibration notions, particularly decision calibration. The authors define a general notion of calibration, denoted as $\mathcal{H}$-calibration, which encompasses various forms of calibration guarantees. They show that under this general framework, the optimal robust policy can be computed efficiently using a convex program. A key finding is that when the predictor is decision calibrated, the optimal robust policy simplifies to the plug-in best response policy, a result that is both surprising and practically significant. This implies that for decision-calibrated predictors, the decision-maker can simply use the predicted values as if they were the true conditional expectations, without incurring any robustness penalty. Furthermore, the paper explores the implications of decision calibration for multiple decision-makers, demonstrating that a single predictor that is decision-calibrated for multiple decision problems can enable all corresponding decision-makers to best respond. The authors also provide empirical validation of their theoretical findings using real-world datasets, showing that the robust policy outperforms the naive best-response policy under adversarial conditions. Overall, this paper offers a valuable contribution to the field by providing a rigorous framework for decision-making under partial calibration, highlighting the importance of decision calibration, and demonstrating the practical benefits of robust decision policies.

## Weaknesses:

While I appreciate the contributions of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation, in my view, is the assumption of a linear utility function for the decision-maker. As explicitly stated in Assumption 2.1, the utility function $u(a, v)$ is assumed to be linear in its second argument $v$. This assumption, while simplifying the analysis, is a strong simplification that does not reflect the complexities of real-world decision-making. As the paper itself acknowledges, this assumption implies risk neutrality, which is often unrealistic. Decision-makers in many applications may exhibit risk aversion, preferring a certain but smaller reward over a risky but potentially larger one. This could be modeled with a concave utility function, which the current framework does not account for. The paper acknowledges this limitation, stating that 'Utilities that are nonlinear in $v$, for example, risk-averse utilities depending on outcome variance, fall outside our framework and represent an important direction for future work.' This is a critical limitation because it restricts the applicability of the results to scenarios where decision-makers have linear preferences. The implications of this assumption are substantial, as it could lead to suboptimal decisions in practice when dealing with risk-averse decision-makers. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is explicitly stated in the paper and has a significant impact on the conclusions. 

Another significant weakness is the assumption that the decision-maker has full knowledge of the set of possible conditional distributions. The robust decision rule is formulated over the set $\mathcal{Q}$, which is constructed based on the $\mathcal{H}$-calibration constraint. The decision-maker is assumed to know $\mathcal{H}$, which defines $\mathcal{Q}$. This is a strong assumption, as in practice, the decision-maker might only have access to limited information about the possible distributions, or they might have to estimate the set from data. The paper does not address how the proposed approach could be adapted to handle situations where the decision-maker has only partial or noisy information about the set of possible distributions. This limitation is important because it restricts the applicability of the results to scenarios where the decision-maker has complete knowledge of the calibration constraints. The paper's formulation of the robust decision rule relies on this assumption, as evidenced by the definition of $\mathcal{Q}$ in Equation 4. The implications of this assumption are that the results may not be robust in practice if the decision-maker's knowledge of the set of possible distributions is incomplete or inaccurate. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly related to the core formulation of the robust decision rule. 

Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed approach. While the authors mention that the optimal decision rule can be computed efficiently using a convex program, they do not provide a precise analysis of how the computational cost scales with the size of the action space and the number of possible conditional distributions. While this provides some insight into the tractability, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the computational cost. The implications of this lack of analysis are that it is difficult to determine the scalability of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is a clear omission in the paper. 

Finally, while the paper introduces a general framework for $\mathcal{H}$-calibration, the primary focus is on decision calibration. While the authors do discuss self-orthogonality and bin-wise calibration, the analysis is primarily centered around decision calibration. This focus, while providing a strong theoretical foundation, limits the immediate applicability of the results to scenarios where other forms of calibration are more relevant. The paper does not explore how the proposed approach can be extended to handle other calibration notions, such as those based on expected calibration error or other statistical measures of calibration. This is a limitation because it restricts the scope of the paper to a specific type of calibration. The implications of this limitation are that the results may not be directly applicable to scenarios where other forms of calibration are more relevant. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is evident from the paper's structure and focus.
