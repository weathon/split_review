## Summary:

This paper introduces IRIS, a novel framework for enhancing autoregressive text-to-image (T2I) models using intrinsic rewards. The core contribution lies in the counterintuitive finding that minimizing self-certainty, rather than maximizing it, leads to improved image generation quality in T2I models. This contrasts with previous work in text generation, where maximizing self-certainty has been shown to be beneficial. The authors define self-certainty as the KL divergence between the model's output distribution and a uniform distribution, and they propose to use the negative of this quantity, termed Negative Self-Certainty (NSC), as the intrinsic reward signal. The method is implemented within a reinforcement learning framework, specifically using Group-wise Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The authors demonstrate that training a Janus-Pro model with this intrinsic reward achieves competitive performance compared to methods that rely on external reward models. Empirically, the paper presents results on three benchmark datasets: GenEval, T2I-CompBench, and WISE, showing that IRIS matches or outperforms external reward-based methods. The paper's significance lies in its introduction of a purely intrinsic reward-based approach for improving T2I models, reducing the reliance on costly and subjective human preference data or domain-specific automated rewards. The authors also highlight the interesting difference in behavior between T2I and language models, where minimizing self-certainty is beneficial for the former but not the latter. This finding suggests that the optimal reward strategy may be task-dependent and that the relationship between model confidence and output quality is more complex than previously thought. The paper's approach is novel in its application of self-certainty to the T2I domain and in its use of an intrinsic reward signal alone, without external supervision. The empirical results are promising, showing that the proposed method can achieve competitive performance with external reward-based methods. However, the paper also has several limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the 'Weakness' section. Overall, I find the paper to be a valuable contribution to the field of T2I generation, offering a new perspective on intrinsic rewards and highlighting the importance of task-specific reward design.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the core idea of applying self-certainty as an intrinsic reward for autoregressive T2I models is novel and insightful. The authors' discovery that minimizing self-certainty, rather than maximizing it, leads to improved image generation is a significant contribution. This finding challenges the conventional wisdom derived from text generation tasks and opens up new avenues for research in multimodal models. The fact that this phenomenon is specific to T2I models suggests a fundamental difference in the way these models learn and generate outputs compared to language models. This is a valuable insight that could have implications beyond the specific method proposed in this paper. Second, the development of IRIS as the first framework to use only intrinsic rewards for improving T2I models is a major strength. This approach reduces the reliance on costly and subjective human preference data or domain-specific automated rewards, making the method more practical and scalable. The authors' ability to achieve competitive performance with external reward-based methods using only intrinsic rewards is a testament to the effectiveness of their approach. The empirical results presented in the paper are also compelling. The authors demonstrate that IRIS matches or outperforms external reward-based methods on several benchmarks, including GenEval, T2I-CompBench, and WISE. These results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The ablation studies further validate the importance of minimizing self-certainty, showing that maximizing it leads to significantly worse performance. The paper is also well-written and clearly explains the methodology and experimental setup. The authors provide sufficient details for the reader to understand the core concepts and reproduce the results. The use of figures and tables effectively communicates the key findings. Finally, the paper's focus on intrinsic motivation is timely and relevant. As the field of AI moves towards more complex and creative tasks, the development of methods that can learn from intrinsic signals is becoming increasingly important. This paper makes a valuable contribution in this direction, offering a promising approach for improving T2I models without relying on external supervision.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation, and one that is consistently highlighted by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a theoretical explanation for why minimizing self-certainty improves image generation. The authors suggest that it may encourage the model to explore more diverse and less certain outputs, leading to richer and more varied images, but this remains a speculative explanation. As the paper states, "For the image tokens, as we discussed in Fig. 1, overly confident models usually generate uniform and plain figures, whereas models with a moderate confidence can generate images with richer and more diverse features." (Section 3.2). However, this observation does not provide a rigorous theoretical framework for understanding the underlying mechanisms. The paper lacks a formal analysis of the relationship between the model's confidence distribution and the resulting image diversity. It is unclear why a flatter confidence distribution across tokens necessarily leads to more diverse or higher-quality images. The authors should explore potential alternative explanations, such as the possibility that minimizing self-certainty inadvertently regularizes the model, preventing it from converging to a narrow, overfitted solution space. This lack of theoretical grounding limits the understanding of the method and makes it difficult to generalize the findings to other settings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the absence of theoretical analysis in the paper. Another significant weakness is the limited scope of the experimental validation. The experiments are conducted solely on the Janus-Pro model, an autoregressive T2I model. As the paper states, "To evaluate the effectiveness of IRIS, we primarily follow the protocol in T2I-RI Jiang et al. (2025). Our experiments focus on fine-tuning Janus-Pro (Chen et al., 2025), using Generative Relative Process Optimization (GRPO) (Guo et al., 2025a)." (Section 4.1). It is unclear how well the findings generalize to other T2I models or architectures, particularly those based on diffusion processes or transformer decoders. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the results to different model sizes or training datasets. This raises concerns about the robustness of the method and whether the observed phenomenon is specific to the Janus-Pro model's particular training regime or dataset biases. The lack of experiments on diverse models limits the generalizability of the findings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the experimental setup described in the paper. Furthermore, the paper primarily relies on objective-based evaluation metrics, without including human preference studies. While the authors use automated metrics based on external reward models (HPSv2, DINO, ORM, GIT) and benchmark scores (GenEval, T2I-CompBench, WISE), these metrics may not fully capture the nuances of human perception of image quality. As the paper states, "We use the four external rewards, namely HPSv2 (Wu et al., 2023), DINO (Liu et al., 2024), ORM (Guo et al., 2025b), and GIT (Wang et al., 2022) introduced in Sec. 4.1, to evaluate the image generation in the ablation studies." (Section 4.3). It is essential to conduct human evaluations to assess whether the changes in self-certainty and objective metrics translate to perceptually better images. The absence of human evaluation is a significant limitation, as it is difficult to determine whether the improvements observed in the objective metrics correspond to actual improvements in visual quality as perceived by humans. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the lack of human evaluation in the experimental setup. Additionally, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with other intrinsic reward methods. While the authors mention that there are no prior works that use intrinsic rewards for this specific task, they should still compare IRIS to other relevant intrinsic reward methods used in similar contexts, such as those used in language models or other generative models. As the paper states, "We define Self-Certainty (SC, Zhao et al. (2025b)) and Negative Self-Certainty (NSC) at position  $t$  by $\text{SC}(o_t|q, o_{<t}) := +\text{KL}(U|\pi_\theta(o_t|q, o_{<t}))$, and $\text{NSC}(o_t|q, o_{<t}) := -\text{SC}(o_t|q, o_{<t})$" (Section 3.2). The paper does not discuss how IRIS compares to other methods that use intrinsic motivation, such as those based on information gain or novelty. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to position IRIS within the broader landscape of intrinsic reward methods and to understand its advantages and disadvantages relative to existing approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the limited comparison in the related work section. Finally, the paper does not fully address the potential limitations of using self-certainty as the sole intrinsic reward. The authors should discuss the potential for the model to exploit this reward function, leading to degenerate or repetitive outputs. While the benchmark results are positive, the paper doesn't explicitly address the possibility of the model finding ways to minimize self-certainty without actually improving the quality or diversity of the generated images. An analysis of image diversity would strengthen the paper. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as it is a potential concern that is not explicitly addressed in the paper. In summary, the lack of theoretical explanation, limited experimental validation, absence of human evaluation, lack of comparison with other intrinsic reward methods, and the potential for reward exploitation are all significant weaknesses that need to be addressed.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable recommendations. First and foremost, the authors should delve deeper into the theoretical underpinnings of why minimizing self-certainty improves image generation. This could involve exploring the mathematical properties of the self-certainty measure and its impact on the model's output distribution. A more rigorous analysis could involve examining the entropy of the model's predicted token distributions and how this entropy changes during training with the proposed intrinsic reward. It would be beneficial to explore the connection between the self-certainty reward and the model's exploration-exploitation trade-off. Specifically, the authors should investigate whether minimizing self-certainty encourages the model to explore a wider range of image features and styles, or if it simply leads to more noisy or less coherent outputs. Furthermore, the authors should consider analyzing the gradient of the self-certainty loss with respect to the model's parameters to understand how this loss function affects the learning dynamics. This analysis could provide insights into why minimizing self-certainty leads to improved image generation quality, rather than simply reducing model confidence. To strengthen the generalizability of the findings, the authors should conduct experiments on a wider range of T2I models, including those with different architectures and training procedures. This should include models based on diffusion processes, transformer decoders, and other common architectures. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the results to different model sizes and training datasets. It would be beneficial to explore whether the observed phenomenon is consistent across different model scales and datasets, or if it is specific to the Janus-Pro model and its training regime. Furthermore, the authors should consider analyzing the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the performance of the proposed method. This would help to determine the robustness of the method and its applicability to different scenarios. The authors should also investigate the computational cost of the proposed method and compare it to other reward-based methods. To address the lack of human evaluation, the authors should conduct human preference studies to evaluate the perceptual quality of the images generated with IRIS. This should involve a diverse set of human raters and a well-defined evaluation protocol. The authors should compare the images generated with IRIS to those generated with other methods, including baseline models and models trained with external rewards. The human evaluation should focus on various aspects of image quality, such as visual appeal, diversity, and alignment with the input text prompt. The authors should also consider using pairwise comparisons to assess user preferences. The results of the human evaluation should be compared to the objective metrics to determine whether there is a correlation between the two. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of the proposed method. To provide a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods, the authors should include a detailed comparison with other intrinsic reward methods used in similar contexts, such as those used in language models or other generative models. This comparison should include both quantitative and qualitative results, and should discuss the computational cost and implementation complexity of each method. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of using self-certainty as the sole intrinsic reward. They should explore the potential for the model to exploit this reward function, leading to degenerate or repetitive outputs. It would be beneficial to see an analysis of the diversity of the generated images and whether the model is simply generating images that minimize self-certainty without improving the overall quality. Finally, the authors should consider releasing their code and models to facilitate further research in this area. This would allow other researchers to reproduce their results and build upon their work. By addressing these suggestions, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more substantial contribution to the field of T2I generation.
