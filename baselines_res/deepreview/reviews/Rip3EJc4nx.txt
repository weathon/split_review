## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel pruning method for large language models (LLMs), termed High Fidelity Pruning (HFPrune), which leverages information entropy as a criterion for evaluating neuron importance. The authors argue that traditional Taylor-based pruning methods, which rely on one-hot cross-entropy loss, fail to capture the full predictive distribution of the model, leading to suboptimal pruning decisions. In contrast, HFPrune uses the information entropy of the model's output distribution to assess the importance of neurons based on their contribution to the global prediction distribution. This approach aims to minimize the change in the global prediction distribution after pruning, thereby better preserving the model's intrinsic knowledge. The method is applied specifically to the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) modules within the transformer architecture, as these modules are identified as a key area for parameter reduction. The authors demonstrate that their method is more efficient than self-distillation approaches, as it does not require a separate teacher model. The experimental results show that HFPrune outperforms existing techniques on LLaMA and Qwen series models, even after significant parameter reduction, achieving higher accuracy and improved computational efficiency. The paper includes an ablation study that validates the effectiveness of the information entropy criterion compared to cross-entropy and self-distillation criteria. Furthermore, the authors provide an analysis of the computational efficiency of the pruning process, demonstrating that HFPrune is faster and uses less memory than existing methods. The paper also presents an analysis of the acceleration benefits of the pruned models, showing improvements in prefill latency and decoding throughput. Overall, this work presents a compelling approach to LLM pruning, offering a more nuanced method for evaluating neuron importance and achieving better performance and efficiency compared to existing techniques. The core contribution lies in the use of information entropy as a pruning criterion, which allows for a more holistic assessment of neuron importance and leads to more effective pruning decisions. The focus on MLP modules and the detailed experimental analysis further strengthen the paper's claims and demonstrate the practical benefits of the proposed method.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most compelling contribution is the introduction of information entropy as a criterion for neuron importance in the context of LLM pruning. This is a novel approach that addresses the limitations of traditional one-hot cross-entropy loss, which, as the authors rightly point out, fails to capture the full predictive distribution of the model. By using information entropy, the method considers all potential predictions, allowing for a more holistic evaluation of neuron importance and leading to more effective pruning decisions. This is a significant conceptual advance that moves beyond the limitations of existing methods. Furthermore, the method is conceptually simpler and more efficient than self-distillation approaches, as it does not require a separate teacher model. This reduces the computational overhead and makes the method more practical for large-scale models. The authors also provide a thorough analysis of the computational efficiency of the pruning process, demonstrating that the proposed method is faster and uses less memory than existing methods. This is a crucial aspect for the practical application of pruning techniques, and the authors' analysis provides strong evidence for the efficiency of their approach. The experimental results are also a major strength of the paper. The authors demonstrate that their method outperforms existing techniques on LLaMA and Qwen series models, even after significant parameter reduction. This is a compelling result that shows the effectiveness of the proposed method in preserving the model's predictive capabilities. The ablation study further strengthens the paper by validating the effectiveness of the information entropy criterion compared to cross-entropy and self-distillation criteria. This provides clear evidence that the information entropy criterion is a key factor in the superior performance of the proposed method. Finally, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow and understand the proposed method and its advantages. The figures and algorithms are helpful and easy to understand, and the authors provide sufficient detail to allow for replication of their results. The inclusion of an analysis of the acceleration benefits of the pruned models, showing improvements in prefill latency and decoding throughput, further enhances the practical value of the work.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the paper's focus on pruning MLP modules within the transformer architecture is a significant limitation. While the authors justify this choice by stating that MLP modules constitute a large portion of the model's parameters, they do not explore the applicability of their method to other critical components of LLMs, such as attention layers, normalization layers, and embedding layers. The method's effectiveness on these other layer types remains unclear, and a more comprehensive analysis of how the information entropy criterion behaves across different layer types would strengthen the paper's claims of general applicability. For instance, the authors could investigate whether the information entropy of the output distribution from attention layers can be used to guide pruning, or if the method is equally effective when applied to the weights of normalization layers. The current scope limits the impact of the work, as it leaves open the question of whether the proposed method can be generalized to other parts of the model architecture. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the paper explicitly states its focus on MLP modules (Section 1, Introduction) and the method description and experimental setup are centered around MLP pruning (Section 4.3). Second, the paper's evaluation is limited to models within the LLaMA and Qwen families. While these models are widely used, it is unclear how the method would perform on models with different architectures, such as those based on the original Transformer architecture or models with different pre-training objectives. This limits the generalizability of the findings. For example, the authors could evaluate their method on models like the original BERT, GPT-2, or other open-source models. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's performance and its ability to generalize across different model architectures. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the experimental evaluation is indeed limited to the LLaMA and Qwen families (Section 5.2). Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the relationship between the pruning ratio and performance degradation. While the authors present results for a few pruning ratios (20% and 30% in Table 1, and 20%, 30%, and 40% in Table 3), they do not provide a sensitivity analysis of the pruning ratio on different layers and its impact on the overall model performance. The paper also lacks a clear guideline on how to choose the optimal pruning ratio for a given task and model. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the experimental results are limited to a few specific pruning ratios, and there is no discussion on optimal ratio selection. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison of memory usage. A reduction in parameters does not necessarily translate to reduced memory consumption during inference. For a fair comparison, latency and memory usage should be evaluated using the same framework, such as Huggingface and FP16. The paper reports latency but not memory usage, which is a significant omission. This is a high-confidence weakness, as Table 4 lacks memory usage metrics. Finally, the paper lacks a comparison of different pruning ratios in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, the performance of the proposed method is lower than that of LoRAPrune at the 30% pruning ratio. The authors should provide results for finer-grained pruning ratios, such as 25% and 35%, to better understand the trends in performance. This is a high-confidence weakness, as Table 1 only shows 20% and 30% results, and HFPrune's performance is lower than LoRAPrune at 30%.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should extend their analysis to other critical components of LLMs, such as attention layers, normalization layers, and embedding layers. This would involve adapting the information entropy criterion to these different layer types and evaluating the impact of pruning on their functionality. For instance, in attention layers, the authors could explore pruning the query, key, and value projections, or the attention weights themselves. In normalization layers, the scale and shift parameters could be considered for pruning. For embedding layers, the authors could investigate pruning the embedding matrix. A detailed analysis of how the information entropy criterion behaves across these different layer types would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's applicability and limitations. Furthermore, the authors should provide a rationale for why the information entropy criterion is expected to be effective for these different layer types, given their distinct functionalities. This would involve a deeper theoretical analysis of the method's properties and its relationship to the underlying mechanisms of these layers. Second, the authors should evaluate their method on a more diverse set of LLMs, including models with different architectures and pre-training objectives. This would involve selecting models from different families, such as those based on the original Transformer architecture, models with different pre-training datasets, and models with different sizes. For example, the authors could evaluate their method on models like the original BERT, GPT-2, or other open-source models. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's performance and its ability to generalize across different model architectures. The authors should also analyze the impact of different pre-training objectives on the effectiveness of the pruning method. This would help to identify potential limitations and areas for improvement. Furthermore, the authors should consider evaluating the method on a wider range of tasks, including those that are more complex and require different types of reasoning. Third, to provide a more detailed analysis of the relationship between the pruning ratio and performance degradation, the authors should conduct a sensitivity analysis of the pruning ratio on different layers and its impact on the overall model performance. This would involve varying the pruning ratio for different layers and analyzing the resulting changes in model performance. The authors should also provide a clear guideline on how to choose the optimal pruning ratio for a given task and model. This could involve developing a heuristic or a more principled approach for selecting the pruning ratio based on the model architecture, the task requirements, and the available computational resources. The authors should also investigate the impact of different pruning strategies, such as layer-wise pruning, where different layers are pruned at different ratios, and compare their performance with the proposed method. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the trade-offs between model size, computational efficiency, and performance. Fourth, the authors should include a detailed comparison of memory usage during inference for both the original and pruned models. This analysis should include not only the memory required for model parameters but also the memory used for intermediate activations and other overheads. The comparison should be conducted using a consistent framework, such as Huggingface, and a standard precision format, such as FP16, to ensure a fair evaluation. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different pruning ratios on memory usage and identify the optimal trade-off between model size, performance, and memory consumption. This analysis should also consider the potential for memory savings through techniques such as quantization and knowledge distillation, which could be combined with the proposed pruning method to further improve efficiency. Finally, the authors should include a more fine-grained analysis of performance across a wider range of pruning ratios, such as 25% and 35%, to better understand the trends in performance and identify the optimal pruning ratio for the proposed method. This analysis should also include a discussion of the potential reasons for the performance drop at higher pruning ratios and suggest strategies for mitigating this issue. Additionally, the authors should explore the possibility of using adaptive pruning techniques that can dynamically adjust the pruning ratio based on the specific task or input data.
