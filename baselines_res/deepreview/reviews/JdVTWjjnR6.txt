## Summary:

This paper introduces HiTNet, a novel dual-stream network for multimodal sentiment analysis (MSA) under missing data conditions, drawing inspiration from the hippocampal and thalamic functions in the human brain. The core contribution of this work lies in its unique architecture, which comprises two distinct streams: an intra-modal enhancement stream and an inter-modal regulation stream. The intra-modal stream, inspired by the hippocampus, employs a semantic memory module and a sparse activation network to reconstruct missing features within each modality. This stream leverages modality-specific information to fill in gaps caused by missing data, aiming to preserve the integrity of individual modality representations. The inter-modal stream, drawing inspiration from the thalamus, utilizes a confidence-perception module and a cross-modal completion module to integrate information across modalities. This stream dynamically weights the contribution of each modality based on its perceived confidence, suppressing redundant information and focusing on reliable cross-modal cues. The model's architecture is further enhanced by a hierarchical fusion module that combines the outputs of the two streams. The authors conducted extensive experiments on multiple datasets, including MOSI, MOSEI, and SIMS, under various missing data rates. The empirical findings demonstrate that HiTNet consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving an average accuracy improvement of 1.5% to 2.0% across different missing rates. Notably, the model maintains a high level of accuracy even under extreme missing data scenarios, such as a 90% missing rate on the MOSEI dataset. These results highlight the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed brain-inspired design for handling missing data in multimodal sentiment analysis. The paper's significance lies in its novel approach to addressing the challenge of missing data in MSA by leveraging insights from neuroscience. The integration of brain-inspired mechanisms into a deep learning framework offers a fresh perspective on multimodal representation learning. The empirical results demonstrate the practical value of this approach, showcasing the potential of HiTNet to improve the performance of MSA systems in real-world scenarios where missing data is a common issue. Furthermore, the paper contributes to the growing body of research on brain-inspired computing, demonstrating the potential of biological principles to guide the development of more robust and efficient artificial intelligence systems. The comprehensive experimental evaluation and the detailed analysis of the results provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of the proposed model and its components. Overall, this work represents a significant advancement in the field of multimodal sentiment analysis and offers a promising direction for future research in brain-inspired AI.

## Strengths:

The paper's primary strength lies in its innovative, brain-inspired approach to addressing missing data in multimodal sentiment analysis. I find the concept of drawing inspiration from the hippocampus and thalamus to design the dual-stream network to be particularly compelling. This novel architectural design, with its intra-modal enhancement and inter-modal regulation streams, offers a fresh perspective on handling missing data in multimodal learning. The use of a semantic memory module and a sparse activation network in the intra-modal stream, designed to reconstruct missing features within each modality, is a technically innovative approach. Similarly, the inter-modal stream's use of a confidence-perception module and a cross-modal completion module to dynamically integrate cross-modal information based on modality confidence is a significant contribution. The paper presents a comprehensive experimental evaluation on multiple datasets, including MOSI, MOSEI, and SIMS, under various missing data rates. The results consistently demonstrate that HiTNet outperforms state-of-the-art methods, achieving an average accuracy improvement of 1.5% to 2.0% across different missing rates. The model's ability to maintain high accuracy even under extreme missing data scenarios, such as a 90% missing rate on the MOSEI dataset, is particularly impressive. These empirical achievements highlight the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed model. The paper is generally well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the methodology and experimental results. The authors provide detailed explanations of the model architecture, training process, and evaluation metrics. The use of figures and tables effectively supports the textual content, enhancing the overall clarity of the paper. The ablation studies further validate the importance of each component in the HiTNet model, providing valuable insights into the contribution of the intra-modal enhancement stream, the inter-modal regulation stream, and the individual modules within each stream. The paper's focus on a practically relevant problem, namely missing data in multimodal sentiment analysis, adds to its significance. The proposed model's ability to handle missing data effectively has important implications for real-world applications of multimodal sentiment analysis systems.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend the following improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the HiTNet model. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the number of parameters, FLOPs, memory usage, training time, and inference time for each module, as well as the overall model. A comparison of these metrics with other state-of-the-art methods would provide a clearer understanding of the computational overhead introduced by the proposed architecture. Additionally, the authors should investigate techniques to optimize the model's efficiency, such as model pruning, quantization, or knowledge distillation, to reduce its computational footprint without significantly sacrificing performance. This would enhance the practical applicability of the model, especially in resource-constrained environments. Secondly, to improve the interpretability of the model, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the interaction between the intra-modal enhancement stream and the inter-modal regulation stream. This could involve visualizing the feature representations at different stages of the model and analyzing how the confidence scores influence the cross-modal completion process. For example, visualizing the attention weights in the cross-modal completion module could help understand which parts of the input modalities are considered most relevant for reconstruction. Additionally, the authors could provide case studies that demonstrate how the model leverages the semantic memory module to reconstruct missing data and how the sparse activation network contributes to the final prediction. Such analysis would provide valuable insights into the model's decision-making process and increase its trustworthiness. Thirdly, to broaden the applicability of the model, the authors should investigate its performance under different types of missing data scenarios, such as random missing patterns and modality-specific missing. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the model's robustness. Furthermore, the authors should explore the potential of the proposed modules to be generalized to other multimodal tasks. For example, the semantic memory module could be adapted for use in video captioning by storing and retrieving visual and textual information. The sparse activation network could be used in emotion recognition to select the most relevant features for classification. By demonstrating the versatility of the proposed modules, the authors could significantly increase the impact of their work. Fourthly, to strengthen the connection to neuroscience, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of how the specific implementation details of the model relate to the underlying biological processes. This could involve comparing the model's behavior with neuroscientific findings or providing a theoretical analysis of how the model's mechanisms mimic the functions of the hippocampus and thalamus. The authors should also acknowledge the limitations of the brain-inspired analogy and discuss the potential for alternative approaches that are not explicitly based on neuroscience. Finally, the authors should provide a more consolidated and in-depth explanation of the semantic memory module and the sparse activation network. This could involve creating a dedicated section that provides a detailed description of these modules, including the dimensionality of the memory vectors, the specific retrieval mechanism, the number of layers and activation functions used in the sparse activation network, and an analysis of how these design choices impact performance. This would improve the clarity and reproducibility of the work.

Firstly, how does the model handle different types of missing data patterns, such as random missing versus sequential missing frames? Are there specific design considerations for these scenarios, or is the model primarily optimized for the frame-level missingness simulated in the experiments? Specifically, what are the training and inference times, and how does the model's complexity impact these metrics? A breakdown of the computational cost associated with each module would be particularly helpful. Thirdly, how does the semantic memory module handle noisy or irrelevant memories, especially when the input query is corrupted by missing data? Is there a mechanism to ensure the quality of retrieved memories, and how does this mechanism contribute to the overall performance of the model? Fourthly, what is the scalability of the model with respect to the number of modalities and the length of the input sequences? Are there any limitations in terms of the model's ability to handle larger datasets or more complex multimodal inputs? Finally, how well does the HiTNet model generalize to other multimodal tasks beyond sentiment analysis? What are the potential challenges and modifications required to adapt the model to different tasks, such as emotion recognition or multimodal question answering? Addressing these questions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's capabilities and limitations.
