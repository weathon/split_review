## Summary:

In this paper, I explore the novel framework TARF (TARGET-aware Forgetting), which addresses the challenge of machine unlearning in scenarios where class labels and target concepts do not coincide. The authors introduce three new unlearning tasks—target mismatch, model mismatch, and data mismatch—and propose a three-phase approach: Target Identification, Target Separation, and Retraining Approximation. This approach uses gradient ascent and descent to selectively forget and retain information, demonstrating effectiveness across various datasets and model architectures. The paper's core contributions lie in its innovative problem setting, technically sound methodology, and comprehensive empirical validation. However, the focus on class-wise unlearning, the assumption of known label domains, and the lack of theoretical analysis and practical considerations are significant limitations that need to be addressed. Despite these limitations, the paper makes a substantial contribution to the field of machine unlearning by expanding its scope to more realistic and complex scenarios.

## Strengths:

I find several strengths in this paper that make it a valuable contribution to the field of machine unlearning. Firstly, the paper introduces a novel and practical perspective by decoupling class labels and target concepts, which is a significant departure from conventional approaches. This new framework addresses real-world complexities that were previously overlooked, making the work highly relevant and impactful. The authors effectively highlight the importance of this decoupling through concrete examples and scenarios, such as the need to unlearn specific attributes or concepts that are not explicitly labeled during training. Secondly, the proposed TARF framework is technically robust, combining annealed gradient ascent and target-aware gradient descent in a novel way. The theoretical analysis, including the exploration of forgetting dynamics and representation gravity, provides a solid foundation for the method. The authors' use of gradient-based techniques to selectively forget and retain information is innovative and well-justified. Thirdly, the empirical evaluation is comprehensive, covering a wide range of datasets and model architectures. The results clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of TARF in handling mismatched label domains, and the ablation studies provide valuable insights into the method's components. The paper's empirical findings are compelling and support the claims made by the authors. Overall, the paper's contributions are significant, and its approach to machine unlearning is both novel and technically sound.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, the paper has several verified limitations that need to be addressed. Firstly, the paper's focus on class-wise unlearning, while novel, may limit its applicability to scenarios where unlearning requests are more granular, such as unlearning specific instances or a small set of data points within a class. The method description and experimental setup confirm that the framework operates at the class level, manipulating gradients based on class labels. For instance, in the 'Target Identification' phase, the loss is calculated over the entire forgetting dataset $	extbf{	extit{D}_f}$, which is defined by class labels (Section 2, Equation 3). The evaluation metrics (UA, RA, TA) are also defined at the class level, further indicating the class-wise focus. This limitation is significant because real-world unlearning requests often involve specific data points rather than entire classes. The paper lacks a discussion on how the gradient-based approach could be adapted to selectively forget individual instances without affecting the model's performance on other instances within the same class. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the evidence is clear and directly impacts the practical applicability of the framework.

Secondly, the paper assumes access to the model output label domain and the target label domain, which may not be realistic in all practical scenarios, especially when target concepts are emergent or undefined during initial training. The entire framework is built upon the relationship between these label domains, and the 'Target Identification' phase relies on observing the accuracy drop of each class, implicitly assuming knowledge of the class labels (Section 3.3). The experimental setup also relies on having predefined class labels and target concepts to set up the mismatched scenarios. This assumption is a significant constraint, as it limits the framework's applicability to scenarios where the target concept is not well-defined or is emergent. For example, in a scenario where a model is trained to identify objects in images, and later there is a need to unlearn a specific object category that was not explicitly labeled during initial training, the target label domain would be undefined. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper's formulation and experimental design heavily rely on this assumption.

Thirdly, the paper does not provide a theoretical analysis of TARF’s convergence properties or its robustness to noisy or mislabeled data in the forgetting set, which could impact real-world applicability. The paper focuses on empirical evaluation and does not provide formal proofs or analysis of these properties. While the empirical results are compelling, a theoretical understanding of how the framework behaves under different conditions is crucial for its practical deployment. For instance, it is unclear how the gradient-based updates in TARF would behave if the forgetting set contains noisy or mislabeled data. In real-world datasets, it is common to have some level of noise or mislabeling, and the performance of TARF could be significantly impacted if it is not robust to such issues. A theoretical analysis should explore the convergence rate of the algorithm, the conditions under which it is guaranteed to converge, and how the presence of noisy data affects the convergence and the final performance of the model after unlearning. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper lacks mathematical proofs or citations related to convergence and robustness.

Fourthly, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the practical challenges and limitations of implementing the method in real-world scenarios. For instance, the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings, such as the annealing schedule for gradient ascent and the criteria for selecting hard-to-affect remaining data, is not thoroughly explored. The paper introduces hyperparameters like $k(t)$ and $	au(x, y, t)$ and provides some intuition for their design (Section 3.3), but a systematic sensitivity analysis is missing. The computational cost associated with the iterative optimization process, especially when dealing with large-scale models and datasets, also needs more detailed analysis. The paper briefly mentions computational time in the experimental setup (Appendix B.1) but does not provide a deeper analysis of the factors influencing it. Additionally, the paper should address the potential for instability during the unlearning phase, particularly when the target concept is highly entangled with other concepts in the model's representation space. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper lacks a systematic exploration of these practical challenges.

Finally, the paper primarily focuses on image classification tasks, and the applicability of TARF to other domains, such as natural language processing or time-series analysis, is not thoroughly explored. The experimental section primarily focuses on image classification tasks using ResNet-18 and similar architectures. While TinyImageNet and ImageNet are used, the core evaluation remains within the image domain. The effectiveness of gradient-based unlearning might differ in recurrent neural networks compared to convolutional neural networks, and the paper should address these potential differences. The paper also lacks a discussion of the challenges of applying TARF to tasks with more complex output spaces, such as sequence generation or multi-label classification. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the experimental scope is limited to image classification, and the paper does not provide insights into how the method might perform in other domains.


## Suggestions:

To enhance the practical applicability and robustness of the TARF framework, I have several suggestions. Firstly, the authors should explore the potential extensions of TARF to instance-wise unlearning. This would involve adapting the gradient-based approach to selectively target specific data points for unlearning. For example, the authors could investigate techniques such as influence functions or gradient-based methods that can isolate the influence of individual data points. This would require a careful analysis of how to compute and apply gradients at the instance level, ensuring that the unlearning process does not negatively impact the model's performance on other instances within the same class. Addressing this challenge would significantly broaden the impact of the proposed framework and make it more relevant to real-world scenarios where unlearning requests are often instance-specific.

Secondly, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the practical implications and real-world applications of the proposed mismatched unlearning scenarios. While the paper introduces target mismatch, model mismatch, and data mismatch, it lacks concrete examples of how these scenarios might arise in practice. For instance, in the context of target mismatch, the authors could discuss how a model trained to recognize objects might need to unlearn a specific attribute (e.g., color) that was not explicitly labeled during training. Similarly, for model mismatch, they could provide examples of how a model trained on a coarse-grained label space might need to unlearn a concept that is represented at a finer-grained level. For data mismatch, they could discuss scenarios where the data used for unlearning is not perfectly aligned with the target concept, such as when unlearning a concept based on a small or biased subset of data. Providing such concrete examples would make the paper more relatable and highlight the practical relevance of the proposed framework.

Thirdly, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the method's sensitivity to hyperparameter settings. Specifically, they should explore different annealing schedules for the gradient ascent phase and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate schedules based on the characteristics of the dataset and the target concept. The criteria for selecting hard-to-affect remaining data should also be analyzed in detail, and the authors should investigate the impact of different selection strategies on the unlearning performance. For example, they could compare the performance of TARF when using data points with the highest loss values versus those with the smallest representation distances. A detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with TARF, including the time and memory requirements for different datasets and model architectures, would also be beneficial. This analysis should include a comparison with other unlearning methods and provide insights into the scalability of TARF for large-scale applications.

Fourthly, the authors should extend their evaluation to include other domains beyond image classification. For example, they could explore the applicability of TARF to natural language processing tasks, such as text classification or sentiment analysis, and time-series analysis tasks, such as anomaly detection or forecasting. This would involve adapting the method to handle different data modalities and model architectures. For instance, in NLP, the authors could investigate how TARF performs with transformer-based models, and in time-series analysis, they could explore its effectiveness with recurrent neural networks. The authors should also discuss the challenges of applying TARF to tasks with more complex output spaces, such as sequence generation or multi-label classification. This would involve analyzing how the method's performance might vary with different loss functions and output representations. The authors should consider the potential for concept drift in these domains and discuss how TARF can be adapted to handle such scenarios.

Finally, the authors should consider the ethical implications of their work. While the paper focuses on the technical aspects of machine unlearning, it is important to acknowledge the potential for misuse of this technology. For example, TARF could be used to selectively remove information from a model, which could lead to biased or unfair outcomes. The authors should discuss these ethical considerations and propose guidelines for responsible use of their method. This could include developing methods for verifying the effectiveness of unlearning and ensuring that the model's performance is not compromised after the unlearning process. Furthermore, the authors should consider the potential for adversarial attacks on the unlearning process and propose defenses against such attacks.
