## Summary:

This paper investigates the application of neural networks for predicting the temporal evolution of species concentrations and temperature in a thermal explosion problem involving a hydrogen-oxygen-air mixture. The authors compare three distinct neural network architectures: a multilayer perceptron (MLP), a U-net, and a DeepONet, using a dataset generated from a reduced kinetic mechanism involving 11 species. The primary goal is to assess how different network architectures influence the accuracy of these surrogate models in capturing the complex dynamics of combustion processes. The MLP, a standard feedforward network, serves as a baseline, while the U-net and DeepONet represent more sophisticated architectures designed to capture spatial and functional relationships, respectively. The U-net, with its skip connections, is intended to capture multi-scale features, and the DeepONet is used to learn the mapping between the input state and the time derivative of the state. The dataset is generated by solving stiff ordinary differential equations (ODEs) numerically, providing a ground truth against which the neural network predictions are evaluated. The performance of each model is assessed using the mean squared error (MSE) on an identical test set. The results indicate that the U-net architecture outperforms both the MLP and the DeepONet, achieving the lowest MSE. This finding suggests that the U-net's ability to capture both global trends and localized transients is beneficial for modeling the complex dynamics of combustion processes. The authors emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate network architectures for achieving accurate and reliable surrogate models in reactive-flow applications. The paper concludes that the U-net's superior performance highlights its potential for use in physics-aware surrogates for combustion simulations. While the paper provides a valuable comparison of different neural network architectures for a specific application, it does not introduce novel architectural modifications or significantly advance the theoretical understanding of neural networks in this domain. The study is primarily empirical, focusing on the application of existing models to a new dataset. The paper's contribution is therefore more focused on the practical implications of network architecture selection for combustion modeling rather than on fundamental advancements in machine learning.
