## Summary:

This paper investigates the application of structure-preserving machine learning models to dynamical systems, focusing on both dissipative and conservative cases. The authors argue that incorporating geometric and physical priors into neural network architectures leads to more robust and generalizable models that require fewer parameters compared to structure-naive approaches. For the dissipative case, they employ a linear state-space model with Riemannian optimization on the symmetric positive definite (SPD) manifold, while for the conservative case, they utilize symplectic Hamiltonian neural networks (SHNNs) with a symplectic time discretization. The core idea is that by respecting the underlying geometric structure of the dynamical systems, the models can learn more efficiently and generalize better, particularly for long-term predictions and out-of-distribution initial conditions. The paper presents two case studies: a heat transfer system representing a dissipative system and a Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou (FPUT) system representing a conservative system. The authors demonstrate through these case studies that structure-aware models achieve better performance and stability compared to structure-naive models, especially when extrapolating beyond the training data. The main empirical finding is that the proposed structure-preserving methods, despite using fewer parameters, outperform larger, structure-agnostic models in terms of generalization and stability. This is demonstrated through comparisons of mean squared error (MSE) for the dissipative case and one-step MSE, rollout MSE, and energy drift for the conservative case. The overall significance of the work lies in its demonstration of the benefits of incorporating geometric and physical priors into machine learning models for dynamical systems, suggesting a pathway towards more efficient and reliable modeling of complex physical phenomena. The paper's contribution is primarily in the application of these established techniques to specific cases, highlighting the advantages of structure-preserving methods over naive approaches. While the individual techniques are not novel, the comparative study and empirical results provide a compelling argument for the use of structure-aware models in this domain. The authors effectively show that by leveraging the inherent structure of the problem, they can achieve better performance with less computational resources, which is a crucial consideration for real-world applications.

## Strengths:

The paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the authors' arguments and methodology. The use of Riemannian optimization for the dissipative system and symplectic Hamiltonian neural networks for the conservative system is well-motivated and effectively implemented. The authors clearly articulate the benefits of structure-preserving models, demonstrating their advantages in terms of generalization and stability compared to structure-naive approaches. The paper provides a clear comparison between structure-aware and structure-naive models, effectively highlighting the advantages of the former. The empirical results, particularly the performance of the structure-preserving models on out-of-distribution data, are compelling and support the authors' claims. The choice of case studies, a dissipative heat transfer system and a conservative FPUT system, is appropriate for demonstrating the proposed methods. The paper's strength lies in its clear presentation of the methodology and the empirical validation of the core idea that structure-preserving models can lead to better generalization and stability. The authors successfully demonstrate that by incorporating geometric and physical priors, they can achieve better performance with fewer parameters, which is a significant advantage in many real-world applications. The paper also effectively highlights the limitations of structure-naive models, which often struggle with long-term predictions and out-of-distribution initial conditions. The use of the implicit midpoint rule for time discretization in the SHNN is also a well-justified choice, as it preserves the symplectic structure of the system. The paper's focus on the practical implications of using structure-preserving methods, such as reduced model size and improved generalization, makes it a valuable contribution to the field. The authors also provide a clear explanation of the mathematical foundations of their approach, making it accessible to a broad audience. The paper's overall strength is in its clear and concise presentation of a well-established idea, demonstrating its practical benefits through well-designed experiments.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling case for structure-preserving models, several weaknesses limit its overall impact. A primary concern is the lack of a detailed discussion regarding the computational cost and scalability of the proposed methods, particularly the Riemannian optimization used for the dissipative system. The paper does not provide any analysis of how the computational cost of Riemannian optimization scales with the dimensionality of the system, nor does it discuss the impact of the choice of Riemannian metric on convergence and stability. This is a significant omission, as the computational cost of Riemannian optimization can be substantially higher than standard Euclidean optimization, and the choice of metric can greatly affect performance. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the sensitivity of the symplectic Hamiltonian neural network (SHNN) to the choice of symplectic integrator and network architecture. While the paper mentions using the implicit midpoint rule, it does not explore the impact of other symplectic integrators or provide a detailed analysis of how different network architectures affect the performance of the SHNN. This is a critical oversight, as the choice of integrator and architecture can significantly influence the accuracy and stability of the model. Furthermore, the paper's experimental evaluation is limited in scope. The case studies are restricted to a 2D heat transfer system and an 18-dimensional FPUT system. While these examples are relevant, they do not provide sufficient evidence to support the generalizability of the proposed methods to higher-dimensional systems or systems with more complex geometric constraints. The paper lacks examples from other engineering domains, such as fluid dynamics or structural mechanics, which would further demonstrate the versatility of the approach. The absence of experiments on systems with non-trivial topology or chaotic behavior also limits the scope of the paper's conclusions. The paper also lacks a comprehensive discussion of related works, particularly recent advances in structure-preserving neural networks. While the authors cite some relevant papers, they do not provide a detailed comparison with other methods that incorporate symplectic structures or Hamiltonian dynamics, nor do they compare their approach to other Riemannian optimization techniques applied to dynamical systems. This omission makes it difficult to assess the novelty and contribution of the paper in the context of the existing literature. The paper's contribution and novelty are also somewhat unclear. While the authors demonstrate the benefits of structure-preserving methods, the techniques they use are established, and the main contribution appears to be the application of these methods to specific cases. The paper does not clearly articulate what new insights or advancements are achieved by applying these techniques to the specific dissipative and conservative systems studied. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed methods, particularly in terms of their ability to handle non-smooth or discontinuous dynamics, which are common in many real-world systems. The paper's focus on specific examples also limits its ability to provide general guidelines for selecting appropriate structure-preserving methods for different types of dynamical systems. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from a lack of detailed analysis of computational cost, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and a limited scope of experimental validation, as well as a lack of a comprehensive discussion of related works and a clear articulation of its novel contributions. These limitations significantly impact the paper's overall impact and generalizability.

This could involve comparing the performance of different metrics on a range of dissipative systems and analyzing their impact on convergence and stability. For the symplectic Hamiltonian neural network, a more thorough analysis of the sensitivity to different symplectic integrators and network architectures is needed. This could involve comparing the performance of different integrators and architectures on a range of conservative systems, and providing recommendations for selecting the most appropriate options. The authors should also consider including a discussion of the limitations of the proposed methods in terms of their ability to handle non-smooth or discontinuous dynamics, which are common in many real-world systems. To broaden the applicability of the proposed methods, the authors should include additional case studies from different engineering domains. For example, they could consider applying their methods to problems in fluid dynamics, such as the simulation of turbulent flows, or to problems in structural mechanics, such as the analysis of vibrations in complex structures. Furthermore, the authors should consider including a discussion of the challenges involved in applying their methods to systems with high levels of uncertainty or noise, and provide guidance on how to address these challenges. This could involve exploring the use of robust optimization techniques or incorporating uncertainty quantification methods into their framework. For instance, a comparison with Hamiltonian Neural Networks (HNNs) or Symplectic Neural Networks (SNNs) would be valuable. This discussion should include a comparison of the inductive biases introduced by each method, and how these biases affect the learning process and generalization capabilities. Furthermore, the authors should clarify how their Riemannian optimization approach compares to other Riemannian optimization techniques used in the context of dynamical systems, particularly in terms of convergence properties and computational efficiency. This would help to position the work more clearly within the existing literature and highlight its unique contributions. Finally, to improve the reproducibility of the results, the authors should provide more detailed information on the practical implementation of their methods. This should include a specification of the exact form of the loss function used for training the Riemannian optimization, the specific symplectic integrator used in the symplectic Hamiltonian neural network, and the choice of hyperparameters. The authors should also provide a detailed description of the evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of their models, and explain why these metrics were chosen. Furthermore, the authors should consider making their code publicly available, which would greatly facilitate the reproducibility of their results and allow other researchers to build upon their work.
