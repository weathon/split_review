## Summary:

The paper introduces ProteinVista, a 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) designed to process full-atom protein structures for improved prediction of protein function and interactions. ProteinVista voxelizes each heavy atom in the protein structure, creating a 5-channel 3D representation that captures detailed chemical and geometric information. The model is pre-trained on over 500,000 AlphaFold-2 predicted structures using a contrastive objective that aligns structure embeddings with sequence embeddings from a large language model (ESM-2). The authors demonstrate that ProteinVista outperforms sequence-based models like ESM-2 on benchmarks requiring fine structural resolution, such as enzyme-substrate classification, transporter-substrate classification, and drug-target inhibition prediction. Additionally, the paper shows that combining ProteinVista with ESM-2 through ensembling further improves performance, suggesting that structure and sequence information are complementary. The authors provide an open-source implementation of ProteinVista, making it accessible for further research and practical applications in protein-ligand binding and other structure-sensitive tasks. Despite its strengths, the paper has several limitations, including a lack of exploration of lower-confidence AlphaFold-2 predictions, limited comparison to other 3D-based protein representation methods, and a focus on voxel grids without exploring alternative 3D representations.

## Strengths:

One of the key strengths of the paper is its novel approach to protein representation learning by using a 3D convolutional neural network (3D CNN) to directly process full-atom voxelized protein structures. This method captures fine-grained atomic details that are often missed by sequence-based models and protein graph neural networks, providing a more detailed and accurate representation of protein geometry and interactions. The model is pre-trained on a large dataset of over 500,000 protein structures from AlphaFold-2, which, while smaller than the datasets used for training large protein language models (PLMs), is still substantial and demonstrates the efficiency and effectiveness of the 3D CNN approach. The authors show that ProteinVista outperforms sequence-based models on tasks that require detailed structural information, such as enzyme-substrate classification, transporter-substrate classification, and drug-target inhibition prediction. This highlights the importance of 3D structural information for accurately predicting protein function and interactions, especially in cases where precise binding pocket geometry and atomic interactions are critical. The paper also provides an open-source Python implementation of ProteinVista, making it accessible for further research and practical applications in protein-ligand binding and other structure-sensitive tasks. This contribution is valuable for the scientific community, as it allows other researchers to build upon and extend the work presented in the paper.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and areas for improvement are evident. First, the model is trained on predicted protein structures from AlphaFold-2, not experimentally verified structures. While AlphaFold-2 predictions are highly accurate, especially for high-confidence regions, the paper does not explore the impact of using lower-confidence predictions on the model's performance. This is a critical consideration for real-world applications where experimental structures are not always available. The authors do analyze performance based on pLDDT scores, showing that ProteinVista performs best on high-confidence structures (pLDDT > 90) and comparably on low-confidence models. However, they do not systematically vary the pLDDT threshold for training data, which would provide a more realistic assessment of the model's robustness and its applicability to real-world scenarios where high-confidence structures are not always available. Furthermore, the reliance on AlphaFold-2 structures introduces a potential bias, as the model may learn to exploit specific artifacts or limitations of the prediction method rather than true biological signals. This is a valid theoretical concern, and the paper does not explicitly address it, which could limit the model's generalization capabilities (Section 4.1, Figure 4.1c and d).

Second, the paper does not explore the use of alternative pretraining objectives, such as masked voxel prediction or rotation prediction, which have been shown to be effective in other 3D vision tasks. The authors do compare two pretraining objectives: multi-task regression and contrastive alignment, but they do not investigate other objectives that could potentially improve the model's ability to capture different aspects of protein structure. The current pretraining objective may not be optimal for capturing the full range of structural information present in proteins, and a more comprehensive exploration of pretraining strategies would enhance the understanding of the model's potential (Section 2.3, Section 4.2).

Third, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other 3D-based protein representation methods, particularly those that use graph neural networks (GNNs) to model atomic interactions. While the authors compare ProteinVista against sequence-based models like ESM-2, they do not provide a detailed analysis of its performance relative to other structure-aware models that directly use atomic coordinates and bond information. This makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and effectiveness of ProteinVista compared to existing 3D-aware methods. The paper does compare against SPOT, ProSmith-ESP, and Fusion_ESP, which are structure-aware models, but a more thorough comparison with GNN-based methods would better contextualize the model's performance (Table 1, Section 3.3).

Finally, the paper does not explore the potential of using alternative 3D representations, such as point clouds or surface meshes, which could capture different aspects of protein structure and potentially improve performance. The choice of voxel grids, while intuitive, might not be the most efficient or informative representation for all types of protein structures. Exploring alternative representations could reveal whether the observed performance is specific to voxel grids or a more general property of the 3D CNN architecture. This is a valid concern, and the paper's focus on voxel grids without considering other representations limits the understanding of the model's capabilities (Section 2.1).


## Suggestions:

To address the identified limitations, I suggest several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should investigate the impact of using lower-confidence AlphaFold-2 predictions on the model's performance. This could involve systematically varying the pLDDT threshold for the training data and evaluating the model's performance on a corresponding range of pLDDT values in the test set. This analysis would provide a more realistic assessment of the model's robustness and its applicability to real-world scenarios where high-confidence structures are not always available. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques to mitigate the impact of low-confidence regions, such as incorporating uncertainty estimates from AlphaFold-2 into the model's training process or using data augmentation strategies that specifically target these regions. This would help ensure that the model is learning robust features that are not overly dependent on the accuracy of the input structures (Section 4.1, Figure 4.1c and d).

Second, the authors should explore alternative pretraining objectives that have been successful in other 3D vision tasks. For example, masked voxel prediction, where random voxels are masked during training and the model is tasked with predicting the missing values, could encourage the model to learn more robust representations of the local environment. Similarly, rotation prediction, where the model is trained to predict the rotation of a protein structure, could enhance its understanding of the protein's 3D geometry. These alternative pretraining strategies could potentially improve the model's ability to capture different aspects of protein structure and enhance its performance on downstream tasks. The authors should also consider combining these objectives with the current Rosetta score prediction to see if a multi-objective pretraining approach is beneficial. A detailed analysis of the impact of different pretraining datasets on the model's performance would also be valuable, as this could provide insights into the model's generalization capabilities (Section 2.3, Section 4.2).

Third, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison to other recent structure-aware models, particularly those that operate on atomic structures using graph neural networks (GNNs). This comparison should not be limited to sequence-based models but should also include methods that explicitly model 3D structure using atom-level graphs. The authors should select several state-of-the-art GNN-based methods for protein representation and evaluate them on the same downstream tasks as ProteinVista. This would allow for a direct comparison of the performance of different 3D-aware methods and would help to determine whether ProteinVista's voxel-based approach offers any advantages over graph-based approaches. The comparison should include not only overall performance metrics but also an analysis of the computational cost and memory requirements of each method. This would provide a more complete picture of the trade-offs between different 3D-aware representation methods (Table 1, Section 3.3).

Finally, the authors should explore the potential of using alternative 3D representations, such as point clouds or surface meshes, to capture different aspects of protein structure. While voxel grids are a simple and intuitive representation, they may not be the most efficient or informative for all types of protein structures. Point clouds, for example, could offer a more compact representation that is less sensitive to the choice of voxel size. Surface meshes, on the other hand, could capture the overall shape and topology of the protein more effectively. The authors could implement and evaluate these alternative representations using the same 3D CNN architecture as ProteinVista. This would allow them to determine whether the observed performance is specific to voxel grids or a more general property of the 3D CNN architecture. The authors should also analyze the computational cost and memory requirements of each representation to determine which is most suitable for different applications (Section 2.1).


## Questions:

1. How does the model handle proteins with low pLDDT scores from AlphaFold-2, and what is the impact of low-confidence regions on the model's performance? Specifically, is there a threshold below which the model's predictions become unreliable, and how does the model's performance degrade as pLDDT scores decrease?

2. How does ProteinVista compare to other 3D-based protein representation methods, such as those using graph neural networks (GNNs) on atomic structures? Are there specific tasks or protein types where GNN-based methods outperform ProteinVista, and what are the computational and memory trade-offs between these approaches?

3. What is the impact of different voxel resolutions on the model's performance, and is there an optimal resolution for capturing detailed structural information? How does the choice of voxel resolution affect the model's computational efficiency and memory requirements?

4. How does the model generalize to proteins with novel folds or low sequence similarity to the training data? Are there specific types of proteins or structural features where the model's performance is particularly strong or weak, and what are the implications for its use in real-world applications?

5. What is the computational cost of training and fine-tuning ProteinVista compared to sequence-based models, and how does the model scale to larger protein structures? Are there specific hardware or software optimizations that could further improve the model's efficiency, and how does the model's performance vary with different batch sizes and training epochs?
