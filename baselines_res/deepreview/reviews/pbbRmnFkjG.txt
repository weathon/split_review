## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the internal representations of DINOv2, a prominent vision transformer model, using sparse autoencoders (SAEs). The authors introduce a method to extract a dictionary of 32,000 concept atoms from DINOv2's activations, which they then analyze to understand how different downstream tasks utilize these concepts. The core of their approach involves training a robust sparse autoencoder (RA-SAE) to learn an overcomplete dictionary of concepts, which they then use to analyze the model's internal representations. The paper's methodology is grounded in the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH), which posits that neural networks represent information as sparse linear combinations of features. The authors use this framework to extract and analyze the concept dictionary. They then explore the geometry and statistics of these learned concepts, finding patterns that challenge a purely sparse, near-orthogonal view of representation. Their analysis reveals that different downstream tasks, such as classification, segmentation, and depth estimation, recruit distinct subsets of these concepts, suggesting task-specific specialization. Furthermore, they observe that the learned concepts exhibit a structure that is not fully captured by the LRH, motivating the introduction of the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis (MRH). The MRH proposes that token embeddings are formed by combining convex mixtures of archetypal landmarks, which is a departure from the linear perspective of the LRH. The authors provide both theoretical arguments and empirical evidence to support the MRH, using tools from convex geometry and algebraic topology. The paper's empirical findings include the identification of task-specific concept utilization, the analysis of concept geometry, and the demonstration of the MRH through comparisons with alternative methods. The authors also explore the implications of the MRH for interpretability and model steering. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of explainable AI by providing a deeper understanding of how vision foundation models work and what they learn. The introduction of the MRH offers a novel perspective on the geometry of representations in vision transformers, and the extensive analysis of the learned concepts provides valuable insights into the internal workings of DINOv2. The paper's findings have implications for other areas of machine learning, such as representation learning, generative modeling, and transfer learning.

## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents several compelling ideas and findings, there are some notable weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the primary concerns is the paper's reliance on the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) as a starting point for studying DINOv2 representations. While the authors acknowledge limitations of the LRH and explore deviations from it, the paper does not provide sufficient evidence or justification for assuming that DINOv2 representations initially satisfy the LRH. The paper introduces the LRH in Section 2, stating that it posits that models contain many more features than neurons, arranged as sparse, quasi-orthogonal directions. The justification for using the LRH is primarily based on its theoretical appeal and its connection to the use of sparse autoencoders. However, the paper does not present any direct empirical evidence demonstrating that DINOv2's internal representations actually conform to this hypothesis. This reliance on an unproven assumption weakens the foundation of the subsequent analysis. The paper states in Section 4 that "Taken together, these effects are hard to attribute to simple feature packing and point to additional geometric constraints. We therefore step beyond the SAE lens and examine the model-native token geometry within single images." This suggests that the LRH is used as a starting point, but the paper does not explicitly discuss the potential biases introduced by this assumption. This lack of justification for the LRH as a starting point is a significant limitation, as it raises questions about the validity of the initial analysis and the conclusions drawn from it. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide any empirical evidence to support the initial assumption of LRH. Another significant weakness is the limited empirical support for the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis (MRH). While the authors provide a formal definition of the MRH in Section 6 and propose ways to test it empirically, the evidence presented is not as strong as it could be. The paper introduces the MRH as an alternative to the LRH, suggesting that token embeddings are formed by combining convex mixtures of archetypal landmarks. The empirical evidence presented in Section 6 focuses on comparisons like "straight-line interpolation to piecewise-linear  $k$ -NN geodesics" and "Archetypal Analysis (AA) Cutler & Breiman (1994) (the  $|S| = 1$  case) to an SAE". While these comparisons are illustrative, they do not provide a rigorous quantitative evaluation of the MRH itself. The analysis relies on visual comparisons of reconstruction fidelity and block structure in the Gram matrix, which are not sufficient to definitively prove the MRH. The paper also does not provide a clear explanation of how the MRH relates to the LRH, or why it is a better fit for DINOv2 representations. While the authors state that the MRH addresses the limitations of the LRH, they do not provide a detailed comparison of the two hypotheses, highlighting the specific aspects of DINOv2's representations that are better explained by the MRH. This lack of a clear comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative merits of the two hypotheses. The paper does provide a formal definition of the MRH in Definition 1 in Section 6, which is a strength. However, the empirical evidence supporting the MRH is not as strong as it could be, and the connection to the LRH is not fully elaborated. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide a rigorous quantitative evaluation of the MRH and does not fully explain why it is a better fit than the LRH. Finally, the paper does not demonstrate how the extracted concepts can be used to improve performance on downstream tasks. While the paper shows that different tasks activate different concepts, it does not show how this information can be used to enhance task performance. The paper's focus is on understanding the model's internal representations rather than directly improving downstream task performance using the extracted concepts. The experiments in Section 3 focus on analyzing which concepts are activated for different tasks, not on using these concepts to enhance task performance. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses and further strengthen the paper, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should provide more rigorous justification for the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH). This could involve comparing the performance of the sparse autoencoder under the linear assumption with its performance under alternative, non-linear assumptions. For example, the authors could explore using a kernel-based method or a neural network with non-linear activation functions to learn the dictionary of concepts and compare the reconstruction error. If the linear assumption is indeed superior, this would provide stronger evidence for the LRH. Furthermore, the authors should explore the limitations of the LRH and discuss the potential for non-linear relationships in the model's representations. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the model's internal workings and address the concern that the linear assumption might be too restrictive. This could involve a more detailed analysis of the deviations from the LRH observed in Section 4, and a discussion of how these deviations might impact the interpretation of the results. Second, to address the issues with the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis (MRH), the authors should provide a more rigorous quantitative evaluation of the hypothesis. This could involve developing new metrics that quantify the convexity of the concept spaces or the degree to which concept combinations can be represented as Minkowski sums. For example, the authors could explore metrics that measure the distance between the observed token embeddings and the embeddings predicted by the MRH. This could involve developing a metric that quantifies the degree to which the token embeddings can be represented as Minkowski sums of convex polytopes. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to investigate the stability of the observed concept representations across different datasets or model initializations. This would provide a more robust assessment of the hypothesis and its generalizability. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of how the MRH relates to the LRH, and why it is a better fit for DINOv2 representations. This could involve showing that the MRH can explain some of the observed phenomena that the LRH cannot, or that the MRH is more consistent with the known properties of the DINOv2 model. This could involve a more detailed comparison of the two hypotheses, highlighting the specific aspects of DINOv2's representations that are better explained by the MRH. Third, the authors should explore how the extracted concepts can be leveraged to improve performance on downstream tasks. While the paper demonstrates that different tasks activate different concepts, it does not show how this information can be used to enhance task performance. For instance, one could investigate whether the identified concepts can be used as features in a downstream classifier, or whether they can be used to guide the training of task-specific models. This could involve experiments where the concept activations are used as input to a linear classifier or where the concept representations are used to initialize the weights of a task-specific network. Furthermore, it would be interesting to explore whether the concept representations can be used to improve the robustness of downstream models to adversarial attacks or domain shifts. This would demonstrate the practical utility of the extracted concepts and their potential for real-world applications. Finally, the authors should provide a clearer and more formal explanation of the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis (MRH). While the authors provide a formal definition, it lacks the necessary mathematical rigor and clarity. A more formal definition, including the necessary mathematical notation and assumptions, would greatly enhance the understanding of the hypothesis. Additionally, the paper should include a figure that illustrates the hypothesis and provides a visual representation of the concept spaces and their relationships. This would make the hypothesis more accessible to a wider audience and facilitate a deeper understanding of the proposed framework. The figure should clearly show how the concept representations are related to each other and how they combine to form more complex representations. These suggestions are aimed at addressing the identified weaknesses and enhancing the overall impact and significance of the paper.
