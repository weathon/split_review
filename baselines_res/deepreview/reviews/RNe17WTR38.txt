## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel self-evolution framework for language models, aiming to improve their reasoning capabilities without relying on external supervision. The core idea revolves around a generator-verifier (GV) game, where a single base model acts as both the generator, producing candidate solutions, and the verifier, evaluating the quality of these solutions. This process generates preference data, which is then used to fine-tune the model, iteratively enhancing its performance. The authors propose two main variants of this framework: SimpleGV, which employs thresholded majority voting to extract reliable preference signals from noisy self-verifications, and RevisionGV, which incorporates multi-turn feedback for iterative improvement. The SimpleGV method uses the base model to generate multiple responses to a given prompt, then uses the same model to verify each response multiple times. Based on a threshold, the responses are categorized as positive, negative, or discarded. The RevisionGV method, on the other hand, uses the verifier to provide feedback to the generator, allowing it to revise its responses iteratively. The approach is validated on both synthetic logical reasoning tasks, specifically the Knights and Knaves problem, and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including GSM8K, MATH500, MATHHard, and TabMWP. The empirical results demonstrate consistent performance improvements across different model sizes and data scales. Furthermore, the paper explores iterative training and curriculum learning, showing that models trained on simpler instances can effectively generalize to more complex tasks. The authors also analyze the computational cost of their approach, showing that while the method is more efficient than online reinforcement learning, the computational cost grows linearly with the number of generations and verifier passes. Overall, this work presents a promising approach to self-improvement in language models, addressing the challenge of external supervision by leveraging the model's own capabilities for both generation and evaluation. The findings suggest that self-evolution can be a viable path for enhancing reasoning abilities in language models, with potential implications for various real-world applications.
