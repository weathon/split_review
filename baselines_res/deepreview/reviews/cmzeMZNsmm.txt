## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the impact of prompt optimization on the performance of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) compared to traditional Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically within the context of event extraction. The authors' core contribution lies in demonstrating that, despite their advanced reasoning capabilities, LRMs still significantly benefit from prompt optimization, often surpassing LLMs in both task performance and optimization effectiveness. The study employs a systematic approach, utilizing Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to refine prompts, and evaluates the performance of various models, including DeepSeek-R1, OpenAI's o1, and GPT-4/4o, as both task models and prompt optimizers. The empirical findings consistently show that LRMs, when optimized with carefully crafted prompts, achieve superior results in event extraction, a complex structured prediction task. Furthermore, the authors explore the generalizability of these findings by conducting experiments on two additional tasks: Geometric Shapes, a symbolic reasoning benchmark, and NCBI Disease NER, a biomedical named entity recognition task. The results from these tasks suggest that the benefits of prompt optimization for LRMs extend beyond event extraction, indicating a broader applicability of their approach. The paper also highlights the effectiveness of LRMs as prompt optimizers, showing that they can generate higher-quality prompts that lead to better task performance. This finding underscores the potential of LRMs not only as task solvers but also as tools for improving the efficiency and effectiveness of other models. The authors' methodology involves a detailed analysis of model performance as both task models and prompt optimizers, providing valuable insights into the nuances of prompt optimization for LRMs. The experimental setup is clearly defined, and the results are presented in a comprehensive manner, allowing for a thorough understanding of the findings. The paper's overall significance lies in its contribution to the growing body of research on prompt engineering for large language models, particularly in the context of reasoning-intensive tasks. By demonstrating the substantial benefits of prompt optimization for LRMs, the authors challenge the notion that these models can rely solely on their inherent reasoning capabilities and highlight the importance of careful prompt design for achieving optimal performance. The study also opens up new avenues for research into the use of LRMs as prompt optimizers and the development of more effective prompt optimization strategies for complex reasoning tasks. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field of natural language processing and offers practical guidance for researchers and practitioners working with large language models.

## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents valuable insights into prompt optimization for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the study's primary focus on event extraction, while providing a detailed analysis, limits the generalizability of the findings to other complex reasoning tasks. Although the authors mention that their findings extend to two additional tasks, Geometric Shapes and NCBI Disease NER, the depth of analysis for these tasks is significantly less comprehensive than for event extraction. Specifically, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the specific challenges and nuances of these additional tasks. For instance, the Geometric Shapes task likely relies more on spatial reasoning, while NCBI Disease NER is a named entity recognition task with different requirements than event extraction. This limited exploration of the differences between these tasks weakens the claim of broad generalizability. The paper dedicates a significant portion to the event extraction task, including detailed methodology, experimental setup, and results, whereas the analysis for the other two tasks is much shorter and less detailed. This imbalance in analysis makes it difficult to assess whether the observed benefits of prompt optimization for LRMs would hold in other complex reasoning domains with different characteristics. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is clearly evident from the disproportionate amount of space and detail dedicated to event extraction compared to the other two tasks, and the lack of in-depth discussion on the specific challenges of Geometric Shapes and NCBI Disease NER. Second, the paper does not explore the impact of different prompt optimization strategies. The study focuses on a specific approach, Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), but does not compare this approach to other established methods, such as Bayesian optimization or evolutionary algorithms. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the effectiveness of the chosen optimization strategy and limits the practical implications of the findings. While the paper mentions the computational limitations and the use of quantization to reduce inference cost, there is no detailed discussion or comparison of the computational cost of the MCTS framework itself with other prompt optimization methods. This is a crucial factor in real-world applications, and the absence of such a comparison limits the paper's practical relevance. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper's exclusive focus on MCTS for prompt optimization in the experimental section and the absence of comparative experiments with other methods is clearly evident. The limited discussion on the computational cost of MCTS further supports this concern. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the types of errors made by LRMs before and after prompt optimization. While the paper reports overall performance improvements, it lacks a fine-grained analysis of the error patterns. For example, it would be beneficial to know whether prompt optimization primarily reduces errors related to specific event types or argument roles, or whether it mainly improves the model's ability to handle complex event structures. The paper includes an "Error Analysis" section and Figure 5c, which presents a high-level categorization of errors, but it does not provide a quantitative breakdown of errors by specific event types or argument roles, nor does it include a confusion matrix. This lack of detailed error analysis makes it difficult to understand the specific mechanisms by which prompt optimization improves LRM performance and to identify areas where further improvements are needed. It would be beneficial to understand the measures taken to prevent overfitting and ensure that the optimized prompts generalize well to unseen data. The paper mentions the use of quantization to reduce inference cost, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the computational resources required for the optimization process. This information is crucial for understanding the practical feasibility of the proposed approach and for comparing it with other optimization methods. Specifically, it would be helpful to know the hardware used, the time taken for optimization, and the memory requirements. The paper does not discuss the potential for bias in the optimized prompts, which is a critical consideration, particularly in tasks involving sensitive data.
