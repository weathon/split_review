## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel two-stage algorithm for detecting change points in dynamic multilayer random dot product graphs (D-MRDPGs), a class of models that capture the complex temporal and multi-layered nature of network data. The core contribution lies in extending the framework of random dot product graphs to a dynamic multilayer setting, where the network's structure evolves over time across multiple layers. The proposed methodology combines seeded binary segmentation, a technique for identifying potential change point locations, with low-rank tensor estimation, which refines these locations and provides statistical inference. The first stage of the algorithm employs a CUSUM-based approach to identify candidate change points, while the second stage uses a low-rank tensor estimation method to refine these estimates and construct confidence intervals. The authors provide theoretical guarantees for the consistency of the proposed algorithm, demonstrating its ability to accurately estimate both the number and locations of change points. Furthermore, they derive the limiting distributions of the refined estimators, which allows for statistical inference. The paper also includes extensive numerical experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets to demonstrate the practical utility and performance of the proposed method. The real-world data analysis focuses on a worldwide agricultural trade network, showcasing the method's applicability to real-world problems. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of dynamic network analysis by providing a theoretically sound and practically useful method for change point detection in complex multilayer network data. The combination of seeded binary segmentation and low-rank tensor estimation, along with the theoretical guarantees and empirical validation, makes this a valuable contribution to the literature. However, as I will discuss in detail, several assumptions and limitations need to be addressed to further enhance the robustness and applicability of the proposed method.

The model, as defined in Definitions 1 and 2, explicitly states that the latent positions of the nodes are fixed over time. This assumption, while simplifying the model, may not hold in many real-world scenarios where the underlying network structure is evolving. For instance, in social networks, user interests and relationships change dynamically, which would likely be reflected in changes in their latent positions. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Appendix C, suggesting extensions to time-varying latent positions, but does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of this assumption on the performance of the method. Specifically, it is unclear how the estimation error in the latent positions propagates to the change point estimation, and under what conditions this error can be controlled. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the robustness of the method in real-world applications where the latent positions are not fixed.
