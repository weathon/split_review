## Summary:

This paper introduces IterRef, a novel test-time scaling method for discrete diffusion models that leverages reward-guided noising-denoising transitions to iteratively refine intermediate states. The authors formalize this process within a Multiple-Try Metropolis (MTM) framework, providing a theoretical guarantee for convergence to the reward-aligned distribution. The method is evaluated on diverse discrete diffusion models across text and image domains, demonstrating consistent improvements in reward-guided generation quality, particularly under low compute budgets. The core contribution of IterRef lies in its iterative refinement approach, which contrasts with single-pass methods by repeatedly applying a noising-denoising process guided by a reward function. This iterative process is designed to progressively steer the generated samples towards higher reward regions of the state space. The authors present a theoretical analysis, proving that the proposed method converges to the desired target distribution under certain conditions. Empirically, IterRef is shown to outperform several baselines, including Best-of-N (BoN), Search-over-Path (SoP), Stochastic Variational Diffusion Distillation (SVDD), and Feedback Knowledge (FK) steering, across various tasks and model architectures. The experiments span language generation tasks such as toxicity reduction, sentiment control, and perplexity improvement, as well as image generation tasks using CLIPScore as the reward function. The results indicate that IterRef achieves better reward alignment with fewer function evaluations (NFEs) compared to the baselines, suggesting improved sampling efficiency. The authors emphasize the method's simplicity and effectiveness, highlighting its potential for enhancing the performance of discrete diffusion models in practical applications. The paper also includes an analysis of the computational cost of IterRef, demonstrating that it is more efficient than previous methods in terms of the number of function evaluations required to achieve a certain level of reward. The authors also explore the impact of key hyperparameters, such as the number of iterations and the number of particles, on the performance of the method. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and thoroughly evaluated approach to test-time scaling for discrete diffusion models, with a strong theoretical foundation and promising empirical results. The iterative refinement strategy, combined with the MTM framework, offers a novel way to improve the quality of generated samples, particularly in scenarios where computational resources are limited. The paper's findings suggest that IterRef could be a valuable tool for enhancing the performance of discrete diffusion models in a variety of applications.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors provide a theoretical analysis of IterRef, the convergence proof, as presented in Proposition 1, lacks sufficient clarity regarding the practical implications of the method. The proof starts with the assumption that the method begins with a sample from a distribution that is "not reward-aligned," but it does not fully clarify what this means in practice. The proof then demonstrates that applying the MTM kernel preserves the target distribution, which, while mathematically sound, does not explicitly show the convergence *to* the target distribution from an arbitrary starting point over multiple iterations. This could lead to the interpretation that the method only works if it starts within the target distribution or converges instantaneously, which is counterintuitive. This lack of clarity in the theoretical analysis makes it difficult to fully understand why the method works effectively in practice. Second, the paper lacks a detailed intuitive explanation of why the method works. While the authors provide a mathematical formulation of the method, they do not offer sufficient intuitive explanations or visualizations of the noising-denoising process and its impact on the intermediate states. A visualization of the trajectory of these states in the latent space would be particularly helpful. The balance between exploration and exploitation in the MTM framework is not explicitly discussed in intuitive terms, and the paper does not provide a clear explanation of how the method avoids getting stuck in local optima or ensures that the samples are diverse and representative of the target distribution. This lack of intuitive understanding makes it challenging to fully grasp the method's behavior and limitations. Third, the empirical evaluation, while thorough, lacks specific details on the experimental setup, particularly regarding the hyperparameter settings for each task and model. While the authors mention that the hyperparameters for baselines are favorably configured by following the original papers, the specific hyperparameter values used for IterRef and the baselines are not provided in the main text. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and to fully understand the experimental setup. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed qualitative analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method in different scenarios. The results are primarily presented quantitatively, which limits the reader's ability to assess the practical implications of the method. Fourth, the paper's comparison to other methods is limited. While the authors compare IterRef to BoN, SoP, SVDD, and FK Steering, they do not include comparisons to other methods specifically designed for discrete diffusion, such as energy-based models and DTS. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to fully assess the relative performance of IterRef. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of IterRef compared to other baselines. While the authors mention that IterRef is more efficient, they do not provide a quantitative comparison of runtime and memory usage with baselines in the main experimental results. This is a critical aspect of any practical algorithm, and a thorough analysis is necessary to understand the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors briefly mention the computational bottleneck, they do not discuss other potential limitations, such as the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, the potential for mode collapse, or the challenges with very high-dimensional data. The paper also does not explicitly discuss potential failure scenarios, such as when the reward function is poorly defined or when the diffusion model is not well-suited for the task. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the specific implementation details of IterRef, particularly regarding the choice of proposal distribution and the number of particles used in the MTM algorithm. The proposal distribution is defined in Equation 2, but a more detailed justification for this specific choice could be provided. The number of particles is mentioned as a hyperparameter, and its impact is explored empirically, but a detailed discussion of the choice and sensitivity is lacking. These implementation details are crucial for the reproducibility and practical application of the method. In summary, while the paper presents a novel and promising approach, these weaknesses limit the overall impact and generalizability of the findings. I have high confidence in these limitations, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of specific details in the methods, experiments, and discussions.
