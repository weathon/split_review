## Summary:

This paper delves into the intricate trade-off between watermark strength and sampling efficiency in large language models (LLMs), a critical challenge in the practical deployment of watermarked models. The authors introduce a novel quantitative measure of watermark strength, defined as the expected Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the watermarked and original token distributions. This measure, they argue, provides a more nuanced understanding of watermark strength compared to previous binary definitions. The paper then formalizes the trade-off between watermark strength and sampling efficiency as a constrained optimization problem, deriving Pareto-optimal curves for two prominent watermarking schemes: OpenAI's Gumbel-max and Google's SynthID. These curves illustrate the inherent tension between achieving a strong watermark and maintaining high sampling efficiency. To address this trade-off, the authors propose a principled mechanism that injects pseudorandomness into the draft-token acceptance process during speculative sampling. This mechanism, they demonstrate, achieves maximal watermark strength while preserving the benefits of speculative sampling. The core idea is to use a pseudorandom number generator to determine whether a draft token is accepted, rather than relying solely on the acceptance probability derived from the target model. This approach ensures that the watermark signal is preserved even when speculative sampling is employed. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of their proposed mechanism, proving that it achieves maximal watermark strength while maintaining sampling efficiency. They also present empirical results that demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach in improving watermark detectability without sacrificing sampling efficiency. The experiments are conducted using the Gumbel-max and SynthID watermarking schemes, and the results show that the proposed mechanism improves detectability under the same efficiency. The paper's findings offer a deeper understanding of the relationship between watermark strength and sampling efficiency, and provide a practical approach to improve the trade-off, paving the way for more efficient deployment of watermarking under speculative sampling. The authors' work is significant because it provides a unified framework for understanding and addressing the trade-off between watermark strength and sampling efficiency, and offers a practical solution that can be applied to existing watermarking schemes. By introducing a quantitative measure of watermark strength and formalizing the trade-off as a constrained optimization problem, the paper provides a solid theoretical foundation for future research in this area. The proposed mechanism, which injects pseudorandomness into draft-token acceptance, is a novel and effective approach that can be used to improve the performance of existing watermarking schemes. The empirical results presented in the paper provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach, and demonstrate its potential for practical applications. Overall, this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of watermarking for large language models, and provides a solid foundation for future research in this area.

## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the scope of the watermarking schemes analyzed is limited, primarily focusing on OpenAI's Gumbel-max and Google's SynthID. While these are prominent schemes, the paper lacks extensive exploration of how the proposed approach might generalize to other watermarking techniques or more complex scenarios. Specifically, the analysis does not consider watermarks that might not achieve maximal strength, and how the trade-off characterization would be affected. The empirical validation is also limited to these two specific schemes, making it difficult to assess the broader applicability of the proposed method. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the robustness of the proposed approach in diverse settings. The paper's analysis and experiments are heavily focused on these two schemes, and there is no discussion or analysis of how the proposed method would perform with other watermarking techniques. This is a significant limitation, as it is unclear whether the proposed approach would be effective with other types of watermarks. Second, the paper's quantitative measure of watermark strength is specifically defined for unbiased watermarks, and the paper does not thoroughly discuss the implications or potential limitations when dealing with biased watermarks or scenarios where the assumption of unbiasedness might not hold. The paper defines watermark strength based on the KL divergence between the watermarked distribution and the original distribution, and explicitly mentions that the scheme is unbiased if the expected watermarked distribution equals the original distribution. However, the paper does not address how the proposed KL divergence measure would behave if the watermark introduces a systematic shift in the token distribution, potentially leading to suboptimal detection performance. This is a critical limitation, as real-world watermarking schemes may not always be perfectly unbiased, and the paper does not provide any guidance on how to handle such scenarios. The theoretical analysis and experimental results are based on the assumption of unbiased watermarks, and the paper does not explore the implications of this assumption or how it might affect the applicability of the results in real-world scenarios.
