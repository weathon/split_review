## Summary:

This paper tackles the critical issue of plasticity loss in deep reinforcement learning (RL), a phenomenon where agents' learning performance degrades over time in non-stationary environments. The authors propose a novel theoretical framework that identifies two primary mechanisms contributing to this problem: the rank collapse of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix and the decay of gradient magnitude. Through a detailed analysis, they demonstrate how these mechanisms hinder the agent's ability to adapt to changing data distributions and targets. To address the gradient decay issue, the authors introduce Sample Weight Decay (SWD), a lightweight method that adjusts the sampling probability of data in the replay buffer based on its age. The core idea behind SWD is to maintain a consistent gradient magnitude by prioritizing newer samples, which are more relevant to the current policy and environment. The method is designed to be easily integrated into existing RL algorithms, and the authors demonstrate its effectiveness through extensive experiments across various benchmarks, including MuJoCo, Atari Learning Environment (ALE), and DeepMind Control (DMC) tasks. The empirical results consistently show that SWD improves learning performance and stability compared to baseline methods, such as uniform sampling and Prioritized Experience Replay (PER). The paper's key contribution lies in its theoretical analysis of plasticity loss, which provides a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms, and in the introduction of SWD as a practical solution to mitigate the effects of gradient decay. The authors' work is significant because it addresses a fundamental challenge in deep RL, offering both theoretical insights and a practical method that can be readily adopted by the research community. By focusing on the gradient decay aspect of plasticity loss, the paper provides a valuable contribution to the field, although it leaves room for further investigation into the role of NTK rank collapse. The paper's findings suggest that maintaining a consistent gradient magnitude is crucial for sustaining learning in non-stationary environments, and the proposed SWD method offers a promising approach to achieve this goal. The authors' work is a valuable step towards developing more robust and adaptable deep RL agents.
