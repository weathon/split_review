## Summary:

This paper presents a significant contribution to the field of large language model (LLM) research by investigating the trade-off between model accuracy and inference efficiency, a critical consideration for practical deployment. The authors introduce a conditional extension to the Chinchilla scaling laws, incorporating architectural parameters such as hidden size, mlp-to-attention ratio, and grouped-query attention (GQA). This novel approach allows for the prediction of optimal model architectures that balance accuracy and inference throughput. The core methodological approach involves extensive experimentation, with over 200 models trained across a range of 80M to 3B parameters and 8B to 100B training tokens. The authors meticulously fit the proposed conditional scaling law using these experimental results. Their findings demonstrate that the conditional scaling law reliably predicts optimal architectural choices, leading to models that achieve up to 42% higher inference throughput and 2.1% better accuracy compared to baseline models like LLaMA-3.2, under the same training budget. The paper's main empirical findings highlight the importance of considering architectural factors when scaling LLMs, showing that simply increasing model size is not the most efficient approach. Instead, the proposed framework provides a principled way to select architectures that maximize performance while minimizing computational costs. The authors also introduce a two-step conditional framework for optimal architecture search, which involves first training small models to fit the conditional scaling law and then solving an optimization problem to find the best architecture. This framework is a practical approach to identifying efficient and accurate models. Overall, the paper's significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between theoretical scaling laws and practical model design, offering a valuable tool for researchers and practitioners alike. By providing a method to predict optimal architectures, this work has the potential to significantly impact the development of more efficient and effective LLMs, making them more accessible for real-world applications. The paper's focus on both accuracy and inference efficiency addresses a crucial gap in the literature, where these two aspects are often considered separately. The authors' comprehensive experimental approach and the resulting conditional scaling law provide a strong foundation for future research in this area.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand their analysis of inference efficiency to include a more comprehensive set of techniques. Specifically, they should investigate the impact of methods like FlashAttention and 1-bit LLMs on the performance of the proposed architectures. This would involve conducting experiments to evaluate how these techniques interact with the hidden size, mlp-to-attention ratio, and GQA, and how they affect both throughput and memory consumption. A detailed analysis of the memory footprint of the proposed architectures, with and without these techniques, would also be valuable. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the trade-offs between accuracy and efficiency when using these methods, as some techniques may lead to a reduction in accuracy. Second, the authors should extend their analysis to include a broader range of model architectures beyond decoder-only transformers. This could involve exploring how the proposed conditional scaling law can be adapted to encoder-decoder models or state space models, which have different architectural parameters and design considerations. For instance, the authors could investigate how the mlp-to-attention ratio in transformers relates to analogous parameters in state space models, such as the dimensionality of the state vectors or the number of layers. Such an analysis would significantly enhance the generalizability of the findings and demonstrate the broader applicability of the proposed framework. Third, the authors should provide more detailed guidance on the practical implementation of the proposed architecture search framework. This could include developing heuristics or approximations that allow for efficient exploration of the architectural search space. For example, the authors could investigate the use of surrogate models to predict the performance of different architectures, thereby reducing the need for extensive training runs. Additionally, the authors could explore the use of early stopping techniques to identify promising architectures without having to train them to convergence. The paper should also include a discussion of the computational cost associated with different architectural choices, providing researchers with a better understanding of the trade-offs involved. Fourth, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of the computational resources required to train and evaluate the models. This should include specific details on the hardware used, such as the type of GPUs, the amount of memory, and the number of nodes. The authors should also report the training time for each model size, the energy consumption, and the cost of training. This information is crucial for reproducibility and for assessing the practical feasibility of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the trade-offs between model size, training time, and performance, which would help practitioners make informed decisions about model design and training. Fifth, the authors should investigate the impact of different training data distributions on the proposed scaling laws. This could involve training models on datasets with varying characteristics, such as different genres of text, different languages, or different task-specific datasets. By analyzing how the optimal architectural choices change under different data distributions, the authors can provide a more robust and reliable framework for model design. Finally, the authors should include a discussion of the limitations of the proposed scaling laws, such as the range of model sizes and training data for which they are valid. This discussion should also consider the impact of other factors, such as the choice of optimizer and learning rate schedule, on the validity of the scaling laws. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly enhance the practical value and generalizability of their work.
