## Summary:

This paper introduces Augmented Intermediate Representations (AIR), a novel defense mechanism designed to enhance the robustness of large language models (LLMs) against indirect prompt injection attacks. The core idea behind AIR is to address a critical limitation in existing defense methods, which typically inject instruction hierarchy (IH) signals only at the input level. The authors argue that these input-level IH signals degrade as they propagate through the layers of the transformer network, leading to a loss of the intended instruction prioritization. To counter this, AIR injects IH signals recurrently at every layer of the LLM, using layer-specific trainable embeddings to encode privilege information. This approach aims to ensure that the IH signal is preserved throughout the model's processing, thereby improving the model's ability to follow the intended instructions even when faced with adversarial prompts. The authors evaluate their method across multiple models, including Llama-3.1-8B, and various training setups, demonstrating a significant reduction in attack success rates for gradient-based prompt injection attacks. Their experiments include both static and dynamic attacks, and they assess the method's utility by measuring its impact on the model's performance on downstream tasks. The results indicate that AIR not only improves robustness but also maintains model utility, adding minimal overhead to the model. The paper's primary contribution lies in its identification of the IH signal degradation problem and the proposal of a simple yet effective solution that addresses this issue. The authors provide empirical evidence to support their claims, showing that AIR outperforms existing defense mechanisms in terms of robustness while maintaining comparable utility. The significance of this work lies in its potential to improve the security of LLMs, which are increasingly being deployed in various real-world applications. By addressing a fundamental vulnerability in existing defense mechanisms, the paper offers a valuable contribution to the field of AI security and provides a practical approach to mitigating the risks associated with prompt injection attacks. The paper's findings suggest that recurrent injection of IH signals at every layer is a promising direction for enhancing the robustness of LLMs against adversarial attacks, and the proposed method, AIR, offers a practical and effective solution to this critical challenge.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall impact and significance. First and foremost, the identification of the critical limitation in existing prompt injection defense mechanisms is a significant contribution. The authors correctly point out that injecting instruction hierarchy (IH) signals solely at the input level is insufficient, as these signals degrade through the layers of the transformer network. This insight is crucial for understanding why existing defenses often fail and provides a clear motivation for the proposed approach. The proposed method, Augmented Intermediate Representations (AIR), is another strength of the paper. The idea of injecting IH signals recurrently at every layer, using layer-specific trainable embeddings, is both simple and effective. This approach addresses the identified limitation directly and provides a practical solution to the problem of IH signal degradation. The fact that AIR adds minimal overhead to the model is also a significant advantage, making it more feasible for real-world deployment. Furthermore, the comprehensive experimental evaluation is a major strength of the paper. The authors conduct experiments across multiple models, including Llama-3.1-8B, and various training setups, demonstrating the effectiveness of their approach. The inclusion of both static and dynamic attacks, as well as the evaluation of utility on downstream tasks, provides a thorough assessment of the method's performance. The results consistently show that AIR significantly reduces attack success rates while maintaining model utility, providing strong empirical support for the proposed approach. The paper's clear and concise writing style also contributes to its overall strength. The authors present their ideas in a logical and easy-to-understand manner, making the paper accessible to a wide audience. The use of figures and tables to illustrate the key concepts and results further enhances the paper's clarity. Finally, the paper's focus on a critical security issue in LLMs, namely prompt injection attacks, adds to its significance. As LLMs become increasingly prevalent, addressing their vulnerabilities is of paramount importance, and this paper makes a valuable contribution in this regard.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant limitations is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by AIR. While the authors mention that the overhead is minimal, they do not provide a comprehensive quantitative analysis of the increased parameter count, FLOPs, and memory consumption during both training and inference. For example, the paper states that for Llama3.1-8B, with 3 privilege levels, they require an additional 0.4M parameters, which is a 0.005% increase. However, this is the only specific data point provided, and there is no breakdown of the parameter increase per layer, nor is there any analysis of the impact on training time, FLOPs, or memory usage. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using AIR, especially for large-scale models. The absence of such data also makes it challenging to compare the overhead of AIR with other existing defense mechanisms. This is a critical omission, as the computational cost is a key factor in determining the feasibility of deploying a defense mechanism in real-world applications. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of why AIR works better than existing methods. While the authors hypothesize that input-level IH signals degrade through layers, they do not provide sufficient empirical evidence or theoretical analysis to support this claim. Figure 3, which shows the cosine similarity of intermediate representations across layers, provides some evidence of this degradation, but the paper does not delve into the mechanisms through which this degradation occurs. Specifically, there is no analysis of attention patterns or gradient flow associated with IH signals. Visualizing the attention weights associated with IH tokens across different layers could reveal how the signal is diluted or lost, and analyzing the gradient magnitudes of IH-related parameters could show how the model learns to ignore or prioritize these signals. Such an analysis would provide a more mechanistic understanding of the proposed approach and strengthen the paper's claims. Furthermore, the paper's evaluation of utility is limited in scope. While the authors claim that AIR does not significantly degrade model utility, their analysis is primarily focused on instruction-following tasks using the AlpacaFarm dataset and AlpacaEval 2.0. There is no evaluation of the method's performance on a diverse set of downstream tasks, such as question answering, text summarization, or code generation. This lack of comprehensive evaluation makes it difficult to assess the potential impact of AIR on model accuracy and generation quality across different types of tasks. It is possible that the method introduces task-specific biases or performance drops, which are not captured by the current evaluation. The paper also does not explore the potential for adversaries to circumvent the defense by reducing the length of the attack prompt. The experimental setup for gradient-based attacks uses a fixed length of 100 tokens, and there is no systematic evaluation of how the defense performs against attacks with varying lengths, particularly shorter ones. It is important to understand how the effectiveness of the defense scales with the length of the adversarial input, as shorter prompts might be more effective in bypassing the defense. Additionally, the paper does not include an ablation study on the placement of the augmented representations. The method description indicates that AIR injects IH signals at every layer, but there is no investigation into the impact of injecting at different layer positions. It is possible that the method is more effective when applied to specific layers, and this should be investigated. Finally, the paper's focus on white-box access to the model limits its practical applicability. The method requires modifications to the decoder block and fine-tuning of the models, which are not feasible for black-box models accessed via APIs. The paper does not explore whether the core idea of enforcing instruction hierarchy can be translated to a black-box setting through prompt engineering, which would significantly broaden the applicability of the work. The paper also does not evaluate against attacks based on adversarial examples or semantic manipulations, such as synonym substitution or paraphrasing. The evaluation includes static and gradient-based attacks, but it lacks evaluation against attacks that do not rely on gradient information. This is a significant omission, as such attacks could potentially bypass the instruction hierarchy defense.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by AIR. This should include a layer-by-layer breakdown of the increased parameter count, FLOPs, and memory consumption during both training and inference. The authors should also report the training time overhead for different model sizes and numbers of privilege levels. Furthermore, the inference latency should be measured and reported, as this is a critical factor for practical applications. It would be beneficial to compare the overhead of AIR with other existing defense mechanisms to provide a clear understanding of the trade-offs. The authors should also investigate the scalability of their method with respect to model size and the number of privilege levels, and discuss any potential limitations. Second, to strengthen the paper's claims about the mechanism of AIR, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of why AIR is more effective than existing methods. This could involve visualizing the attention patterns of the model with and without AIR, focusing on how the IH signal is processed across different layers. Specifically, the authors could examine the attention weights associated with IH tokens and analyze how these weights change as the input propagates through the network. This would provide empirical evidence to support the claim that input-level IH signals degrade through layers. Furthermore, the authors could analyze the gradient flow of IH-related parameters to understand how the model learns to prioritize or ignore these signals. This analysis should include a comparison of the gradient magnitudes with and without AIR, which could reveal how the proposed method helps preserve the IH signal. Such an analysis would provide a more mechanistic understanding of the proposed approach and strengthen the paper's claims. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between robustness and utility. This could involve evaluating the model's performance on a diverse set of downstream tasks, including question answering, text summarization, and code generation. The authors should report the performance of the model with and without AIR on these tasks, and analyze any task-specific performance drops or biases introduced by the proposed method. This analysis should also include an evaluation of the model's generalization capabilities, especially when fine-tuned on downstream tasks. For example, the authors could fine-tune the model with and without AIR on a few downstream tasks and compare the performance. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the utility trade-offs and help identify potential limitations of the proposed method. The authors should also explore the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the trade-off between robustness and utility, which could provide valuable insights for practitioners. Fourth, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the impact of varying the length of the attack prompt on the effectiveness of the proposed method. This should include experiments with both shorter and longer attack prompts, and a detailed analysis of how the effectiveness of the defense scales with the length of the adversarial input. The authors should also explore the potential for adversaries to circumvent the defense by reducing the length of the attack prompt, and discuss how their method could be adapted to mitigate these threats. It would be beneficial to provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between attack prompt length and the success rate of the attack, and to identify any critical lengths where the defense is more or less effective. The authors should also investigate the impact of different types of attack prompts, such as those that use different wording or phrasing, and discuss how their method performs against these variations. Fifth, the authors should explore the impact of placing the augmented representations at different positions within the network. This could involve injecting the IH signal at specific layers or using a weighted combination of IH signals from different layers. This would provide a deeper understanding of the method's mechanics and could lead to a more efficient or effective defense mechanism. Finally, to broaden the applicability of their work, the authors should investigate the feasibility of applying their method to black-box models through prompt engineering. This could involve exploring different prompt designs that aim to mimic the effect of injecting IH signals into the model. The authors should also analyze the limitations of such an approach and discuss the trade-offs between white-box and black-box implementations. It would be beneficial to provide a detailed analysis of the challenges involved in adapting the method to a black-box setting, and to suggest potential future research directions in this area. The authors should also consider the potential for adversarial attacks in the black-box setting and discuss how their method could be adapted to mitigate these threats.
