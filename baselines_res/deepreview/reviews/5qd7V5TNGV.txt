## Summary:

This paper introduces CP4D, a novel framework for generating photorealistic 4D scenes that adhere to complex physical dynamics. The core contribution lies in its ability to synthesize explorable and interactive 4D content by integrating static 3D backgrounds with dynamic, physically grounded foreground objects. The methodology is structured around a three-stage pipeline. First, the framework generates 3D representations of both the background and foreground objects using pre-trained models. This involves leveraging text-to-image models, image editing models, segmentation models, and image-to-3D reconstruction models. Second, it simulates the motion of the foreground objects using a hybrid approach that combines physical simulators with video diffusion models. This stage infers physical attributes of the objects using vision-language models (VLMs) and then refines these attributes using score distillation sampling (SDS) from a video diffusion model. Finally, the framework composes the dynamic foreground objects into the static background using monocular depth estimation and depth-aware heuristics. The framework is evaluated through both quantitative metrics and qualitative comparisons, demonstrating improvements over existing methods in terms of visual fidelity, physical plausibility, and semantic alignment. The authors present results on various scenes, including examples with swaying clothes and falling objects, showcasing the method's ability to generate dynamic and physically realistic scenes. The overall significance of this work lies in its ability to bridge the gap between static 3D scene generation and dynamic 4D content creation, offering a promising approach for generating realistic and interactive virtual environments. However, as I have verified, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of this framework.

This approach allows for the independent generation of these components, which are then integrated using a physically grounded motion synthesis strategy. This is a significant departure from existing methods that often struggle to produce physically consistent and visually plausible results. The use of a hybrid motion synthesis strategy that combines physical simulators with video diffusion models is another notable strength. This approach leverages the strengths of both techniques, using physical simulators to provide a physically plausible basis for motion and video diffusion models to refine the motion and ensure visual realism. This is a significant improvement over purely data-driven or physics-based approaches, which often suffer from limitations in either physical accuracy or visual fidelity. The automated composition mechanism, which uses monocular depth estimation and depth-aware heuristics, is also a practical and effective solution for seamlessly integrating dynamic objects into static backgrounds. This mechanism enhances the realism of the generated scenes by ensuring that the foreground objects are properly placed within the 3D environment. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to ensure a fairer comparison, the authors should conduct additional experiments where all methods are evaluated using the same set of pre-trained models. This would involve providing the baselines with access to the same VLMs and video diffusion models used by CP4D, or at least using a common set of pre-trained models for all methods. This would help isolate the contribution of the proposed method's architecture from the benefits of using multiple pre-trained models. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the individual contributions of each component in their pipeline, such as the 3D representation generation, physical simulator, and video diffusion model-based refinement. This would help to better understand the strengths and weaknesses of each module and how they interact to achieve the final results. Second, to address the limited complexity of the generated scenes, the authors should provide more examples that include soft body collisions, fluid dynamics, or multi-object interactions. For instance, they could demonstrate the simulation of a cloth tearing, a liquid spilling, or multiple objects interacting with each other and the environment. These examples would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's capabilities and its potential for real-world applications. Additionally, the authors should discuss the limitations of their method in terms of the types of physical dynamics that it can handle. For example, can the method handle phenomena such as fragmentation, melting, or phase transitions? Addressing these limitations would provide a more realistic assessment of the method's capabilities and its potential for future development. Third, to address the concerns about the accuracy of VLMs in estimating physical parameters, the authors should conduct experiments to investigate the accuracy of the VLM in estimating physical parameters for objects with varying attributes, such as color and material. This could involve creating a dataset of objects with different colors and materials and evaluating the VLM's ability to estimate their physical parameters. The authors should also discuss the implications of any inaccuracies in the estimated parameters and how these inaccuracies might affect the quality of the generated scenes. Fourth, to ensure a fairer comparison of the composition techniques, the authors should conduct additional experiments where the foreground video is consistent across all methods. This would involve using the same foreground video and only replacing the composition with the background. This would allow for a more direct comparison of the composition techniques and help isolate the contribution of the proposed method's composition mechanism. Fifth, to address the lack of a detailed discussion on limitations, the authors should include a dedicated section that explores the failure modes of their framework.
