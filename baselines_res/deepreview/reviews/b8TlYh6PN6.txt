## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to causal discovery in linear non-Gaussian models with latent variables and cycles, a challenging problem that has traditionally been addressed with strong structural assumptions. The authors' core contribution lies in providing a graphical characterization of distributional equivalence for such models, a fundamental step towards developing structural-assumption-free methods. They introduce the concept of 'edge rank constraints' as a key tool for this characterization, which allows for a more nuanced understanding of when two causal graphs can be considered equivalent based on observed data. The paper then leverages this characterization to develop a constraint-based algorithm, named glvLiNG, which aims to recover the underlying causal model up to distributional equivalence. The algorithm operates in three main steps: first, it uses OICA to estimate a mixing matrix from the observed data; second, it constructs a directed graph that realizes the rank patterns in the estimated mixing matrix; and finally, it traverses the equivalence class using a procedure based on the established characterization. The authors present a procedure to traverse the entire equivalence class of models, which is a significant theoretical contribution. The empirical evaluation includes simulation experiments comparing glvLiNG with existing methods and a real-world application to stock return data. The simulation experiments explore the algorithm's performance under varying numbers of observed and latent variables, graph density, and sample size. The real-world application demonstrates the algorithm's ability to recover a graph with meaningful structure, suggesting that major banks act as central causal sources. While the paper's primary focus is on the theoretical characterization of distributional equivalence, the glvLiNG algorithm serves as a proof of concept, demonstrating the feasibility of recovering causal structures without relying on strong structural assumptions. The authors acknowledge that the algorithm is not without limitations, particularly in its dependence on the exact number of latent variables and the computational cost of traversing the equivalence class. Overall, this paper makes a significant theoretical contribution to the field of causal discovery by providing a novel characterization of distributional equivalence in a challenging setting and by developing a structural-assumption-free method for recovering causal models from data.

This makes it difficult to assess the practical effectiveness of the proposed method in complex, real-world scenarios. The dataset size (14 variables) is also relatively small, which limits the ability to demonstrate the scalability of the algorithm. Another significant weakness is the paper's reliance on the assumption that the exact number of latent variables is known. The glvLiNG algorithm, as described, implicitly assumes that the number of latent variables is correctly estimated by OICA. However, in real-world scenarios, this is often not the case, and mis-specifying the number of latent variables can have a significant impact on the algorithm's performance.
