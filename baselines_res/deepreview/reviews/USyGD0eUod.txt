## Summary:

This paper presents a critical investigation into the effectiveness of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) for extracting meaningful features from transformer models, specifically focusing on whether SAEs can distinguish between trained and randomly initialized transformers. The authors employ a rigorous experimental setup, utilizing the Pythia suite of transformer models with varying parameter sizes, and explore different randomization schemes, including re-randomizing weights while preserving embeddings, randomizing both weights and embeddings, and using a control with random Gaussian embeddings. The core methodology involves training per-layer SAEs on the residual stream activation vectors of these models and evaluating their performance using a variety of metrics, including auto-interpretability scores, reconstruction errors, cosine similarity, and a novel metric termed 'token distribution entropy.' The auto-interpretability scores are derived from a language model's ability to predict whether a token activates a given SAE latent, while reconstruction error measures how well the SAE can reconstruct the original activations. The token distribution entropy is introduced as a measure of the complexity of the SAE latents, quantifying the number of distinct token IDs that activate a given latent. The central finding of this work is that SAEs trained on randomly initialized transformers often achieve auto-interpretability scores and reconstruction metrics that are surprisingly similar to those obtained from trained models. This suggests that high auto-interpretability scores alone do not guarantee the recovery of computationally relevant features, and that standard SAE metrics may be insufficient for assessing the mechanistic interpretability of SAEs. The authors also observe that the latents of SAEs trained on trained transformers tend to be more complex and abstract, particularly in later layers, as indicated by higher token distribution entropy. The paper concludes by advocating for the use of routine randomized baselines and targeted measures of feature 'abstractness' to improve the robustness of interpretability evaluations. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of mechanistic interpretability by highlighting the limitations of current evaluation metrics and emphasizing the need for more rigorous methods to validate the meaningfulness of extracted features. The findings challenge the assumption that high auto-interpretability scores inherently indicate the discovery of complex, learned computations, and encourage the development of more robust evaluation techniques.

## Strengths:

This paper demonstrates several notable strengths that contribute to its overall impact and significance. First and foremost, the paper addresses a critical gap in the field of mechanistic interpretability by directly challenging the reliability of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) in distinguishing between learned and random features in transformer models. This is a significant contribution because it questions the validity of widely used interpretability metrics and encourages the development of more robust evaluation methods. The authors' approach is grounded in a clear understanding of the current limitations of SAE-based interpretability and offers a valuable perspective on the challenges of validating the meaningfulness of extracted features. The methodology employed is another key strength of this work. The authors conduct a comprehensive experimental analysis using multiple model sizes from the Pythia suite, and they explore various randomization schemes, including re-randomizing weights while preserving embeddings, randomizing both weights and embeddings, and using a control with random Gaussian embeddings. This rigorous approach allows for a thorough investigation of the behavior of SAEs under different conditions and strengthens the validity of the findings. The use of controlled experiments with different initialization states (trained, re-randomized, step-0, control) provides a robust framework for evaluating the performance of SAEs. Furthermore, the selection of evaluation metrics is well-considered, covering auto-interpretability scores, reconstruction errors, cosine similarity, and the novel token distribution entropy. These metrics provide a multi-faceted view of SAE performance and allow for a comprehensive analysis of the extracted features. The inclusion of the token distribution entropy is particularly noteworthy, as it introduces a new perspective on the complexity of SAE latents and provides a potential avenue for quantifying feature 'abstractness.' The paper is also well-organized and clearly written, making it accessible to a broad audience. The introduction effectively frames the problem, and the related work section provides a thorough overview of relevant literature on sparse dictionary learning and mechanistic interpretability. The results are presented in a clear and logical manner, supported by detailed figures and tables that enhance understanding. The authors' findings have substantial implications for the field of mechanistic interpretability, particularly in the context of transformer models. By demonstrating that high auto-interpretability scores do not necessarily correlate with the extraction of meaningful features, the paper encourages researchers to reconsider the validity of current evaluation metrics. The recommendation to incorporate routine randomized baselines and targeted measures of feature abstractness offers a practical path forward, potentially leading to more reliable and meaningful interpretability techniques in future research. Finally, the inclusion of qualitative examples in the appendix, while not a central part of the main paper, provides additional context and supports the quantitative findings. These examples offer a glimpse into the specific features extracted by SAEs in trained versus randomized models, further reinforcing the paper's core message.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several limitations that warrant careful consideration. A primary weakness lies in the limited exploration of alternative metrics for assessing the quality of SAE-derived features. While the authors rightly identify the shortcomings of current metrics like auto-interpretability scores and reconstruction errors, they do not propose or explore a wide range of concrete alternatives. The paper introduces token distribution entropy as a measure of latent complexity, but this is only a preliminary step. The paper lacks a detailed discussion on how to quantify the 'abstractness' of a feature, moving beyond simple reconstruction fidelity. For example, metrics that measure the degree to which a feature is entangled with other features or the extent to which it is sensitive to specific input patterns are not explored. This limitation is significant because it leaves the reader with a clear understanding of the problem but without a clear path forward for developing more robust evaluation methods. The paper's experimental focus on the Pythia suite of transformer models also presents a limitation. While Pythia models are a common benchmark, the exclusive use of this model family restricts the generalizability of the findings. The paper does not explore models with different architectures, attention mechanisms, activation functions, or pre-training objectives. This is a significant limitation because it is unclear whether the observed phenomena are specific to the Pythia architecture or are more broadly applicable to transformer models in general. For instance, it is not known if the limitations of SAEs are consistent across different model families or if they are sensitive to the size of the model. This narrow focus limits the scope of the conclusions and raises questions about the broader applicability of the findings. Another notable weakness is the lack of a detailed qualitative analysis of the features extracted by SAEs in trained versus randomized models within the main body of the paper. While the authors mention providing qualitative examples in the appendix, the main paper relies heavily on quantitative metrics. This is a significant limitation because it prevents a deeper understanding of the nature of the features being extracted and the limitations of current evaluation methods. For example, visualizing the activation patterns of specific SAE latents for different input tokens and comparing these patterns across trained and randomized models could reveal whether the features are capturing semantically meaningful information or are simply artifacts of the model architecture. The absence of such qualitative analysis in the main paper makes it difficult to fully grasp the practical implications of the observed limitations of SAEs. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of why SAEs are able to produce similar scores for trained and randomized transformers. While the authors suggest that random networks can preserve superposition, they do not delve deeply into the specific properties of the SAEs or the transformer architectures that lead to this behavior. The paper does not explore whether the SAEs are simply learning to reconstruct the input activations regardless of their origin, or if they are capturing some underlying structure that is shared between trained and randomized models. This lack of a clear explanation is a significant limitation because it leaves the reader with a phenomenon but without a clear understanding of its underlying mechanisms. The paper also lacks a clear recommendation for how to validate interpretability techniques. While the authors suggest that more rigorous methods are needed, they do not offer concrete steps or methodologies for achieving this. The paper does not provide a detailed, actionable framework for how to validate interpretability techniques beyond the suggestions of developing better metrics and using null models. This is a significant limitation because it leaves the reader with a problem but without a clear path forward for addressing it. Finally, the discussion section could be significantly expanded to tie together the various findings and explore their implications for the field of mechanistic interpretability. The authors do not discuss the limitations of using SAEs to interpret random networks or propose alternative approaches that might be more effective. For example, they do not explore the use of other interpretability techniques, such as attention analysis or probing, to gain a deeper understanding of the internal representations of random networks. The authors also do not discuss the implications of their findings for the broader field of machine learning. This lack of a more in-depth discussion is a significant limitation because it prevents the reader from fully appreciating the broader significance of the findings.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made to this work. First, the paper should significantly expand its exploration of alternative metrics for assessing the quality of SAE-derived features. This should involve not only proposing new metrics but also providing a detailed analysis of their properties and their ability to capture the complexity and abstractness of learned features. For instance, the authors could explore metrics that measure the effective dimensionality of the latent space, which would provide insights into the richness and diversity of the extracted features. Another approach could involve quantifying the degree of polysemy of each latent, i.e., the number of distinct concepts that a single latent seems to represent. This could be achieved by analyzing the activation patterns of each latent across a diverse set of inputs and identifying clusters of inputs that elicit similar activations. Furthermore, the authors could explore metrics that measure the sensitivity of each latent to specific input patterns, which would help in determining whether the latents are capturing general or specific features. These metrics should be designed to move beyond simple reconstruction fidelity and provide a more nuanced understanding of the nature of the learned features. The paper should also include a detailed discussion on how to quantify the 'abstractness' of a feature, moving beyond simple reconstruction fidelity. This could involve metrics that measure the degree to which a feature is entangled with other features or the extent to which it is sensitive to specific input patterns, rather than just overall activation patterns. Second, the authors should extend their experiments to include models from different families and architectures. This could involve exploring models with different attention mechanisms, such as sparse attention or linear attention, as well as models with different activation functions, such as SwiGLU or GELU. Additionally, the authors should consider including models trained on different datasets or with different pre-training objectives, as this could reveal whether the observed phenomena are specific to the Pythia architecture or are more broadly applicable to transformer models in general. This would also help in understanding if the observed limitations of SAEs are consistent across different model families. Furthermore, the authors could investigate whether the observed phenomena are sensitive to the size of the model, by including models with different numbers of parameters. Third, the paper should include a more detailed qualitative analysis of the features extracted by SAEs in trained versus randomized models within the main body of the paper. This could involve visualizing the activation patterns of specific latents for different input tokens and comparing these patterns across trained and randomized models. The authors could also provide examples of the explanations generated for specific latents and analyze whether these explanations are semantically meaningful or are simply artifacts of the model architecture. This would help in understanding the practical implications of the observed limitations of SAEs and provide a more nuanced understanding of the nature of the learned features. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of techniques such as feature visualization to gain a better understanding of the features being extracted by the SAEs. Fourth, the paper should delve deeper into the mechanisms by which random networks exhibit interpretability. This should include a more detailed analysis of the weight matrices, perhaps using techniques from random matrix theory, to identify specific properties that might lead to the preservation of structure. The authors should also investigate whether the observed interpretability is robust to different random initializations by showing the variance of the interpretability scores across multiple random seeds. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different activation functions on the observed interpretability, as this could reveal whether the phenomenon is specific to the chosen activation function or a more general property of random networks. Fifth, the paper should provide a more detailed discussion of the implications of these findings for the use of SAEs in interpretability research. This should include a clear recommendation for how to validate interpretability techniques, offering concrete steps or methodologies for achieving this. The authors should propose a set of criteria or guidelines that researchers can use to assess the validity of their methods. This could involve developing a suite of benchmark tasks or datasets that are specifically designed to test the ability of interpretability techniques to capture meaningful computations. Another approach would be to develop quantitative metrics that measure the degree to which an interpretability technique captures the underlying structure of the model. Finally, the discussion section should be significantly expanded to tie together the various findings and explore their implications for the field of mechanistic interpretability. The authors should discuss the limitations of using SAEs to interpret random networks and propose alternative approaches that might be more effective. For example, they could explore the use of other interpretability techniques, such as attention analysis or probing, to gain a deeper understanding of the internal representations of random networks. The authors should also discuss the implications of their findings for the broader field of machine learning. If random networks can exhibit a degree of interpretability, what does this say about the nature of intelligence and the role of training in achieving it? These broader questions should be addressed to provide a more complete picture of the significance of the findings.

A deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind this phenomenon is crucial for developing more robust interpretability techniques. What specific mathematical formulations or computational methods can be used to quantify these aspects of feature complexity? Are there specific architectural features that make transformers more susceptible to this phenomenon, or is it a more general property of neural networks? A more detailed analysis of the weight matrices, perhaps using techniques from random matrix theory, could provide valuable insights into this question. The paper highlights the limitations of current evaluation metrics, but it does not provide a detailed, actionable framework for how to validate interpretability techniques. What specific types of analyses or experiments should researchers conduct to ensure that their interpretability techniques are actually capturing meaningful computations, rather than just artifacts of the model or training process? Fifth, what are the implications of these findings for the broader field of machine learning?
