## Summary:

This paper introduces SmartSwitch, a novel inference framework designed to mitigate the issue of 'underthinking' in large language models (LLMs) when tackling complex reasoning tasks. The authors define underthinking as the premature abandonment of potentially valuable reasoning paths, leading to suboptimal solutions. SmartSwitch operates by monitoring the model's reasoning process, detecting instances of underthinking, and intervening to encourage deeper exploration of promising ideas. The framework is composed of two primary modules: a Perception module and an Intervention module. The Perception module identifies points where the model switches thoughts, using predefined linguistic cues, and evaluates the potential of the preceding thought using a process reward model (PRM). If a high-potential thought is deemed to have been prematurely abandoned, the Intervention module activates. This module interrupts the current inference, backtracks to the promising thought, and inserts a 'deepen prompt' to guide the model to explore that path further. The authors conducted extensive experiments on a range of challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH-500, and GaoKao2023en. These experiments demonstrate that SmartSwitch significantly enhances the performance of various LLMs across different model sizes, improving both their reasoning effectiveness and token efficiency. The core contribution of this work lies in the identification and characterization of the underthinking phenomenon, the proposal of a practical and plug-and-play framework to address it, and the empirical validation of its effectiveness on challenging mathematical reasoning tasks. The authors' approach is particularly notable for its ability to improve LLM performance without requiring model retraining, making it broadly applicable and accessible. The paper's findings suggest that by strategically intervening in the reasoning process, it is possible to encourage LLMs to explore their full potential and arrive at more accurate solutions. The SmartSwitch framework offers a promising avenue for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, and its plug-and-play nature makes it a valuable tool for researchers and practitioners alike. The authors' work highlights the importance of carefully monitoring and guiding the reasoning process of LLMs to overcome their inherent limitations and achieve optimal performance.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the authors have done an excellent job in identifying and defining the underthinking problem in LLMs, which is a significant contribution to the field. They provide both qualitative and quantitative analyses to demonstrate the prevalence and impact of underthinking on LLM performance. This clear articulation of the problem is crucial for understanding the need for a solution like SmartSwitch. Second, the proposed SmartSwitch framework is novel and well-designed. The combination of the Perception and Intervention modules provides a practical and effective approach to addressing underthinking. The framework's plug-and-play nature, requiring no additional training, makes it broadly applicable and accessible. The use of linguistic cues and a PRM to detect underthinking is an interesting approach, and the authors provide a comprehensive list of these cues in the appendix. Third, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors conducted comprehensive experiments on a range of challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating significant performance improvements across various LLMs and model sizes. The results consistently show that SmartSwitch not only enhances accuracy but also improves inference efficiency by reducing response length and underthinking frequency. The ablation study on the PRM further strengthens the validity of their approach. The fact that SmartSwitch can improve the performance of various LLMs without requiring model retraining is a significant advantage. The paper is also well-structured and clearly written, making the problem of underthinking and the proposed SmartSwitch framework easy to understand. The authors effectively use both qualitative and quantitative analyses to illustrate the issue and demonstrate the framework's effectiveness. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, well-designed, and well-validated approach to addressing a critical problem in LLM reasoning.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the paper's evaluation is primarily focused on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. While these benchmarks are challenging, they represent a specific type of problem-solving. The authors do not provide any experiments or analysis on other types of reasoning tasks, such as natural language inference, commonsense reasoning, or multi-step planning. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the SmartSwitch framework. As the reviewers pointed out, the definition of a 'thought' and the criteria for 'underthinking' might need to be significantly different for tasks outside of mathematical reasoning. The paper lacks a discussion of the potential challenges and adaptations required to apply SmartSwitch to these other domains. This is a significant limitation, as the effectiveness of SmartSwitch in other reasoning domains remains unclear. Second, the SmartSwitch framework relies heavily on predefined linguistic cues and a PRM. The Perception module detects thought switches based on these linguistic cues, and the PRM evaluates the potential of the preceding thought. The paper does not sufficiently explore the sensitivity of the framework to the choice of linguistic cues and the calibration of the PRM. While the ablation study on the PRM (Table 4) shows performance variations with different PRMs, it does not fully address the impact of varying PRM accuracy and calibration errors. The authors do not experiment with PRMs of varying degrees of accuracy or calibration errors, which is a critical omission. If the PRM is not well-calibrated or capable of accurately assessing the potential of different reasoning paths, it could lead to inappropriate interventions, either by failing to intervene when necessary or by intervening too frequently, which could disrupt the model's reasoning process. The paper also does not explore the sensitivity of the framework to the choice of linguistic cues. The current selection of linguistic cues, while effective, may not be universally applicable across all types of reasoning tasks or LLMs. A more systematic approach to selecting these cues, perhaps based on linguistic analysis or empirical studies, would strengthen the robustness of the framework. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the SmartSwitch framework. While the authors claim improved inference efficiency, the paper does not provide a breakdown of the additional time and resources required by the perception and intervention modules. The paper reports overall inference time reduction (Table 3), but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the time spent on thought segmentation, PRM scoring, intervention management, and backtracking. It is unclear how the time spent on PRM scoring and intervention management compares to the time saved by reducing underthinking. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the potential memory overhead of storing and managing multiple reasoning paths, which could be a concern for resource-constrained environments. This lack of a granular computational cost analysis makes it difficult to fully assess the practical applicability of the framework. Finally, the paper does not explicitly discuss how the framework distinguishes between underthinking and legitimate exploration of different approaches. The Perception module detects thought switches based on linguistic cues and evaluates the potential of the preceding thought using a PRM (Section 4.2). The Intervention module activates only if the PRM score exceeds a threshold, indicating a high-potential abandoned thought (Section 4.2). However, the paper does not explicitly discuss how the framework distinguishes between underthinking and legitimate exploration of different approaches. This is a valid concern, as the framework might interrupt the model even when it is simply exploring different valid approaches to the problem. This lack of discussion on the potential for false positives in underthinking detection is a notable weakness. All of these weaknesses are supported by the paper's content and are significant enough to warrant further investigation.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough evaluation of the SmartSwitch framework on a wider range of complex reasoning tasks beyond mathematical problem-solving. This should include tasks involving natural language understanding, commonsense reasoning, or multi-step planning. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the framework's ability to generalize beyond mathematical problem-solving. For example, the authors could explore datasets like the HANS dataset for natural language inference, which tests the model's ability to handle syntactic and semantic nuances, or the PlanBench dataset for evaluating multi-step planning capabilities. This would help to determine if the underthinking problem is as prevalent in these other domains and if the proposed SmartSwitch framework can effectively address it. Second, the authors should conduct a more thorough sensitivity analysis of the SmartSwitch framework to the choice of linguistic cues and the calibration of the PRM. This should include experiments with PRMs of varying quality and calibration, perhaps by introducing controlled errors or biases into the PRM's scoring mechanism. This would help quantify the robustness of SmartSwitch to PRM inaccuracies. Furthermore, the authors should explore methods to mitigate the impact of a poorly calibrated PRM, such as incorporating uncertainty estimates into the intervention decision process. For example, the framework could be modified to only intervene when the PRM score is significantly high, or to use an ensemble of PRMs to reduce the risk of relying on a single, potentially flawed model. The authors should also explore alternative methods for detecting underthinking that do not rely on predefined cues or external models, such as using the LLM's own internal state to identify when it is not fully exploring a reasoning path. Third, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the computational overhead introduced by the SmartSwitch framework. This should include the time spent on thought segmentation, PRM scoring, intervention management, and backtracking. The analysis should also compare these costs to the time saved by reducing underthinking, providing a clear picture of the overall efficiency gains. Additionally, the authors should investigate the memory overhead of storing and managing multiple reasoning paths, especially for longer reasoning processes. This analysis should consider the impact of different thought segmentation strategies and the number of interventions performed. It would also be beneficial to explore techniques to minimize the memory footprint of the framework, such as using more efficient data structures or discarding less promising reasoning paths. Finally, the authors should explicitly discuss how the framework distinguishes between underthinking and legitimate exploration of different approaches. This could involve incorporating additional criteria or heuristics into the Perception module to better identify instances of underthinking. The authors could also explore the use of a more sophisticated PRM that is capable of distinguishing between a model that is underthinking and a model that is simply exploring different valid approaches. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper and make the SmartSwitch framework more robust and applicable to a wider range of reasoning tasks.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are important for further understanding the SmartSwitch framework. First, how sensitive is the SmartSwitch framework to the quality and calibration of the Process Reward Model (PRM)? Have the authors experimented with different PRMs, and if so, how does the choice of PRM affect the framework's performance? Specifically, what happens when the PRM is poorly calibrated or when it fails to accurately assess the potential of different reasoning paths? Second, can the authors provide a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the SmartSwitch framework? Specifically, how do the costs of thought segmentation, PRM scoring, and intervention management compare to the time saved by reducing underthinking? What is the memory overhead of storing and managing multiple reasoning paths, and how does this scale with longer reasoning processes? Third, have the authors considered the potential for the SmartSwitch framework to be applied to other types of complex reasoning tasks beyond mathematical problem-solving? What challenges might arise in adapting the framework to other domains, and how could these be addressed? How would the definition of a 'thought' and the criteria for 'underthinking' need to be modified for tasks involving natural language understanding, commonsense reasoning, or multi-step planning? Finally, how does the SmartSwitch framework handle cases where the model is not underthinking but is instead exploring different approaches to the problem? What mechanisms are in place to prevent the framework from interrupting the model when it is simply exploring different valid approaches? How can the framework be improved to better distinguish between underthinking and legitimate exploration?
