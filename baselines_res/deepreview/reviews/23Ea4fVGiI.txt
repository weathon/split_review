## Summary:

This paper introduces the Task-Method-Knowledge (TMK) framework as a novel approach to enhance the reasoning and planning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly in the context of the Blocksworld domain within the PlanBench benchmark. The core idea revolves around structuring prompts using the TMK framework, which decomposes problems into tasks, methods, and knowledge components, aiming to provide a more explicit representation of causal, teleological, and hierarchical reasoning structures. The authors hypothesize that this structured prompting approach can steer LLMs towards more formal, code-like reasoning pathways, moving away from purely linguistic approximations. The methodology involves converting domain knowledge from plain text into the TMK format, which is then used to prompt various LLMs, including GPT-4, GPT-3.5, and Llama-2. The primary empirical finding is that TMK-structured prompts significantly improve the performance of these LLMs on Blocksworld planning tasks, suggesting that the framework can indeed enhance the model's ability to engage in more systematic and logical reasoning. The authors also suggest that TMK acts as a cognitive scaffold, helping the model to decompose complex tasks into more manageable sub-problems. While the paper presents a promising approach to improving LLM reasoning, it also acknowledges several limitations, including the restricted scope of the experimental evaluation, which is solely focused on the Blocksworld domain. The paper concludes by emphasizing the potential of the TMK framework as a general prompting strategy for LLMs and suggests future research directions, including expanding the evaluation to other planning domains and exploring the underlying mechanisms through which TMK improves performance. Overall, this work contributes a novel prompting technique that shows promise for enhancing LLM reasoning, but also highlights the need for further investigation to fully understand its capabilities and limitations.

The idea of structuring prompts to explicitly represent tasks, methods, and knowledge is a unique way to guide LLMs towards more systematic reasoning. This approach moves beyond simple instruction-based prompting and attempts to leverage the inherent reasoning capabilities of LLMs by providing a structured framework for problem-solving. The paper clearly articulates the motivation behind the TMK framework, drawing connections to cognitive architectures and educational science, which provides a solid theoretical foundation for the proposed method. Furthermore, the paper is well-organized and clearly explains the TMK framework and its application to LLMs. The authors provide a detailed description of the framework's components, the process of converting domain knowledge into the TMK format, and the experimental setup. This clarity makes it easy to understand the core concepts and the methodology used in the study. The empirical results, which demonstrate a significant improvement in LLM performance on Blocksworld planning tasks when using TMK-structured prompts, are compelling. The results presented in this paper are promising and could influence future research directions in this area. ## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation, and one that the authors themselves acknowledge, is the restricted experimental scope. The paper's evaluation is exclusively focused on the Blocksworld domain within the PlanBench benchmark. While Blocksworld is a well-established benchmark for planning, it is a relatively constrained domain with simple action spaces and hierarchical structures. As the reviewer correctly pointed out, the paper explicitly states its focus on Blocksworld in the Introduction: "To test this hypothesis, we will use OpenAI models available at time of writing against the PlanBench benchmark (Valmeekam et al., 2023b). The benchmark evaluates language models on a set of planning problems, which maintains a publicly available leader board (Valmeekam, 2023) of Blocksworld problems and their variations that we will use as a comparison for our hypothesis." This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings. The paper does not provide evidence that the TMK framework would be equally effective in more complex or diverse planning domains, such as those involving spatial reasoning or multi-agent interactions. The conclusion section also acknowledges this limitation: "Our research only investigates improving language model reasoning and planning within the domain of Blocksworld and its variant found in the PlanBench benchmark. Future work should expand into Logistics problems (within PlanBench) or other extensible domains and other families of models." The lack of evaluation on other domains makes it difficult to assess the broader applicability of the TMK framework. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is explicitly stated and demonstrated throughout the paper. Another significant weakness is the potential complexity of prompt design when using the TMK framework. The paper describes the TMK framework as having three components: Task, Method, and Knowledge, and it mentions the hierarchical nature of the framework. The paper states: "For the purpose of this paper, the authors converted the domain knowledge from plain text to TMK by breaking down each possible step within Blocksworld into methods." While the paper provides an example of the TMK structure in Figure 1, it does not provide a clear methodology or tools to streamline the process of creating these prompts, especially for more complex tasks. The paper mentions the iterative nature of TMK design: "It is important to note that the process of designing and building a TMK is iterative and can be decomposed deeper or abstracted further, as evident in Murdock (2001)." However, this does not address the practical challenges of prompt design for intricate planning problems. The lack of a systematic approach or tools for creating TMK prompts could make it cumbersome and time-consuming for practitioners to apply this framework to real-world scenarios. This limitation is evident in the paper's methodology, which relies on manual conversion of domain knowledge into the TMK format. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as the paper lacks any discussion of tools or methodologies to simplify the prompt design process. Finally, the paper does not thoroughly analyze why the TMK framework improves model performance. While the authors suggest that TMK acts as a symbolic steering mechanism, there is a lack of detailed analysis of the internal model representations or attention patterns to support this claim. The paper proposes the hypothesis that TMK acts as a "symbolic steering mechanism" in the Introduction: "We introduce TMK (Task, Method, Knowledge) (Murdock & Goel, 2008), a knowledge-based self-explanation framework used successfully to explain procedural learning to students (Sushri et al., 2024), into a language model prompt to determine if TMK can improve a language model’s procedural planning ability. The paper seeks to extend on previous work from other fields by focusing on TMK language, which was developed as part of research into cognitive architectures in intelligent agent systems to allow systems to reason about their own processing and make any necessary adjustments (Murdock, 2001; Murdock & Goel, 2008). However, the paper does not provide empirical evidence to support this claim through techniques such as probing the model's internal representations or visualizing attention patterns. The experiments focus solely on measuring the accuracy of the generated plans, without delving into the underlying mechanisms of improvement. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the experimental evaluation to include more diverse and complex planning domains beyond Blocksworld. Specifically, they should consider domains that involve spatial reasoning, such as the logistics domain in PlanBench, or multi-agent scenarios. This would involve not only demonstrating that the framework can achieve high accuracy in these domains but also analyzing the types of errors that occur and how they differ from those observed in Blocksworld. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the scalability of the TMK framework with respect to the complexity of the planning task, including the length of the planning horizon and the number of objects involved. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the framework's limitations and potential for real-world applications. The inclusion of such experiments would significantly enhance the paper's impact and credibility. Second, to address the practical challenges of prompt design, the authors should develop a more systematic approach for creating TMK prompts, especially for hierarchical tasks. This could involve providing a set of guidelines or a template that practitioners can use to decompose complex problems into Method, Task, and Knowledge components. Furthermore, the authors could explore the possibility of automating the TMK prompt generation process using a secondary model or a rule-based system. This would significantly reduce the burden on users and make the TMK framework more accessible. The paper should also include an analysis of the time and effort required to create TMK prompts for different types of planning tasks, comparing it to other prompting techniques. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the practicality of the TMK framework. Finally, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the underlying mechanisms through which the TMK framework improves model performance. This could involve techniques such as probing the model's internal representations to understand how the TMK structure affects its reasoning process. For example, the authors could analyze the attention patterns of the model when using TMK prompts versus standard prompts to see if there is a shift in focus towards more symbolic or code-related tokens. Additionally, the authors could perform ablation studies to determine the relative importance of the different components of the TMK framework (Task, Method, Knowledge) and how they interact to improve performance. Such analysis would provide a more solid foundation for the claims made in the paper and offer valuable insights into the nature of reasoning in language models. These suggestions are all aimed at addressing the specific weaknesses I have identified, and I believe that implementing them would significantly strengthen the paper and its contribution to the field.
