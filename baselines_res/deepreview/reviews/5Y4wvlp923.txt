## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to address semantic imbalance in time-series representation learning, a problem where dominant components, such as trends, can overshadow minor but equally important components, like seasonality. The authors propose the Semantic Disentanglement Error (SDE) metric to quantify the recoverability of individual semantic components within a learned representation. This metric is then integrated into an adaptive weighting strategy that dynamically adjusts the emphasis on different components during the contrastive learning process. The core idea is to ensure that the learned representations capture all relevant semantic information, rather than being dominated by a single component. The authors build upon the CoST framework, a well-established method for time-series representation learning, and demonstrate that their proposed method, CoST + APW (Asymmetric Perceptual Weighting), can be integrated without requiring architectural changes. The experimental results, primarily focused on forecasting tasks, show that CoST + APW achieves improved forecasting accuracy and representational robustness, particularly under conditions of semantic skew, where the imbalance between components is more pronounced. The paper's main contribution lies in the introduction of the SDE metric and its application to guide the learning process, ensuring a more balanced representation of time-series data. The authors also demonstrate the practical applicability of their approach by showing its effectiveness on benchmark datasets. While the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of time-series representation learning, it also has several limitations that need to be addressed. The evaluation is primarily focused on forecasting, and the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed method. Additionally, the interpretation of the SDE metric and its relationship to the overall evaluation metrics could be clearer. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field and provides a solid foundation for future research.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper tackles a relevant and important problem in time-series representation learning: the issue of semantic imbalance. The authors correctly identify that dominant components can overshadow minor ones, leading to biased representations. This is a critical issue that has practical implications for the robustness and accuracy of time-series models in real-world applications. Second, the introduction of the Semantic Disentanglement Error (SDE) metric is a novel and valuable contribution. This metric provides a way to quantify the recoverability of semantic components, which is crucial for ensuring balanced representations. The SDE metric is a well-defined measure that can be used to assess the quality of learned representations. Third, the proposed method is designed to be plug-and-play, meaning it can be easily integrated into existing frameworks without requiring architectural changes. This makes the approach highly practical and accessible to researchers and practitioners. The authors demonstrate this by integrating their method with the CoST framework. Fourth, the experimental results show consistent gains in forecasting accuracy and representational robustness. The results are particularly compelling under conditions of semantic skew, where the proposed method shows significant improvements. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed approach in addressing the identified problem. Finally, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the authors' arguments and understand the proposed method. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and results. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated approach to address semantic imbalance in time-series representation learning.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that need to be addressed. First, the paper claims that the proposed method can be plugged into existing frameworks like CoST without architectural changes. However, the experiments only integrate it with CoST. This lack of experimentation with other frameworks limits the assessment of the method's generality. Specifically, the paper lacks experiments demonstrating the method's effectiveness when applied to other contrastive learning frameworks for time series, such as those based on different augmentation strategies or contrastive objectives. This absence of such experiments makes it difficult to assess the true versatility of the proposed approach. Second, the paper introduces the Semantic Separability Error (SDE) to evaluate whether a representation model preserves component-level semantics. However, there is no clear explanation of how to interpret the SDE values. While the paper states that smaller SDE values indicate better recoverability, it lacks a detailed analysis of what different SDE values signify and how they relate to the quality of the learned representations. For instance, the paper does not discuss what range of SDE values indicates good separability, or how sensitive the metric is to small changes in the representation space. Furthermore, it is unclear if a lower SDE value always corresponds to a better representation, or if there are cases where a higher SDE value might be more desirable. This lack of interpretability limits the practical use of the SDE metric. Third, the paper introduces the Semantic Disentanglement Error (SDE) metric but does not explain how it relates to the overall evaluation metrics (MSE and MAE) used in the experiments. The paper presents SDE values alongside MSE and MAE but doesn't provide a detailed analysis of how changes in SDE values correlate with changes in MSE or MAE. Without this analysis, it is difficult to understand whether minimizing SDE directly contributes to improved forecasting performance, or if it is simply an independent measure of semantic disentanglement. This lack of correlation analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical impact of the SDE metric on the forecasting task. Fourth, the paper primarily focuses on forecasting accuracy as the evaluation metric. While forecasting is an important application of time-series representation learning, the authors could strengthen their evaluation by including a wider range of downstream tasks, such as anomaly detection or classification. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's generalizability and robustness. The current evaluation is limited to forecasting, which may not fully capture the quality of the learned representations for other tasks that rely on different aspects of the time-series data. Fifth, the paper could benefit from more detailed ablation studies to isolate the impact of SDE and the adaptive weighting strategy from other components of the proposed method. It is unclear how much each component contributes to the overall performance gain. While the paper includes an experiment on "Direct SDE Regularization", a more granular ablation of the asymmetric perceptual weighting components is missing. Sixth, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed method. The additional computational cost introduced by the SDE calculation and adaptive weighting needs to be quantified and discussed. The paper describes the added components but lacks a quantitative analysis of the computational complexity and scalability. Finally, the paper could be improved by providing more insights into how the proposed method affects the learned embeddings and by visualizing the differences between representations learned with and without the proposed approach. It is not clear how the embeddings are structured and how the method affects their separability. The paper presents quantitative results but lacks qualitative analysis or visualizations of the learned embeddings. These weaknesses, taken together, limit the overall impact and generalizability of the proposed method.

By demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed approach across diverse frameworks, the authors can provide stronger evidence for its versatility and robustness. Second, to improve the interpretability of the SDE metric, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of its behavior and relationship to the quality of the learned representations. This could include a discussion of the range of SDE values that indicate good separability, as well as an analysis of the sensitivity of the metric to small changes in the representation space. The authors should also investigate whether a lower SDE value always corresponds to a better representation, or if there are cases where a higher SDE value might be more desirable. This analysis could involve visualizing the learned representations and examining how different SDE values correspond to different levels of semantic disentanglement. Additionally, the authors should provide a clear explanation of how the SDE metric is calculated and what each component of the metric represents. Third, to clarify the relationship between SDE and the evaluation metrics, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of how changes in SDE values correlate with changes in MSE and MAE. This analysis could involve plotting the SDE values against the MSE and MAE values for different experiments, and examining the trends in these plots. The authors should also investigate whether minimizing SDE directly contributes to improved forecasting performance, or if it is simply an independent measure of semantic disentanglement. This analysis could involve conducting experiments where the SDE term is weighted differently in the loss function, and examining how these different weights affect the forecasting performance. Fourth, to strengthen the evaluation, the authors should include experiments on a diverse set of downstream tasks beyond forecasting. Fifth, to better understand the contribution of each component, the authors should conduct more detailed ablation studies. Specifically, they should isolate the impact of the SDE metric and the adaptive weighting strategy. This could involve experiments where the SDE metric is removed, or where a fixed weighting strategy is used instead of the adaptive one. The ablation studies should also explore the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameter settings, such as the weighting parameters in the adaptive weighting strategy. Sixth, the paper should include a more thorough analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed method. This should include a detailed breakdown of the computational cost associated with the SDE calculation and adaptive weighting, and how these costs scale with the size of the time-series data. The authors should also discuss the memory requirements of the proposed method, and how they compare to existing approaches. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical results on the training time and memory usage of the proposed method on different datasets, including large-scale time-series datasets. Finally, the paper should provide more insights into how the proposed method affects the learned embeddings and by visualizing the differences between representations learned with and without the proposed approach. This could include visualizations of the learned embeddings to illustrate the differences between representations learned with and without the proposed approach. This would help to provide a more intuitive understanding of how the proposed method affects the learned representations and how it improves the disentanglement of semantic components.
