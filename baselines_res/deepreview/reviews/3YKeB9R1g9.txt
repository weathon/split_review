## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the training dynamics of large language models (LLMs), focusing on the phenomenon of training loss curve (TLC) collapse. The authors demonstrate that, under specific conditions, TLCs across different model sizes can be made to collapse onto a single curve when normalized by the final loss. This collapse is achieved by carefully controlling three key factors: the AdamW timescale (τ), the tokens-per-parameter (TPP) ratio, and the learning rate (LR) schedule. The paper introduces the Celerity family of models, trained with optimal τ scaling and demonstrating this TLC collapse, showcasing the practical benefits of their approach. The authors further leverage the predictability of collapsed TLCs to propose a method for early stopping in large-scale hyperparameter tuning, which could significantly reduce computational costs. The core methodological approach involves a detailed empirical study of how these three factors influence the shape of the training loss curves. They show that fixing the AdamW timescale and TPP ratio, while using a consistent learning rate schedule, leads to the observed collapse. The authors also provide a theoretical explanation for the observed phenomena, particularly focusing on the role of the AdamW timescale in the bias-variance trade-off and the influence of the TPP ratio on the pace of learning. The main empirical findings include the demonstration of TLC collapse across different model sizes, the development of a simple functional form for normalized TLCs, and the successful application of this understanding to early stopping in hyperparameter tuning. The paper's significance lies in its novel perspective on LLM training, providing valuable insights into the underlying dynamics and offering practical tools for more efficient training and hyperparameter optimization. By identifying the key factors that govern TLC shape, the authors have opened up new avenues for understanding and controlling the training process of large language models. The Celerity models serve as a concrete example of how these insights can be used to train more compute-efficient models, achieving competitive performance with fewer resources. The proposed early stopping method further enhances the practical value of this work, offering a way to reduce the computational burden of hyperparameter tuning. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field by providing a deeper understanding of LLM training dynamics and offering practical tools for more efficient training and hyperparameter optimization.
