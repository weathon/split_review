## Summary:

This paper introduces RedSage, an open-source large language model (LLM) specifically designed for cybersecurity applications. The authors address the need for privacy-preserving, domain-specific LLMs in cybersecurity by developing a comprehensive pipeline that includes data collection, pretraining, augmentation, and evaluation. RedSage is trained on a curated dataset of 11.8 billion tokens of cybersecurity-focused text and further refined through an agentic augmentation process that simulates expert workflows. The model is evaluated on a newly introduced benchmark, RedSage-Bench, which covers a broad range of cybersecurity knowledge, skills, and tool usage. The results demonstrate that RedSage outperforms existing cybersecurity LLMs and general-purpose models on both cybersecurity-specific and general benchmarks, highlighting its effectiveness and versatility. The authors also open-source their datasets, benchmarks, and model, contributing valuable resources to the field of cybersecurity LLMs. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field by providing a comprehensive framework for developing a cybersecurity-specific LLM and demonstrating its superior performance on a range of tasks.

## Strengths:

The authors have clearly outlined the various stages of development, from data collection and pretraining to augmentation and evaluation, which provides a solid foundation for their work. The use of agentic augmentation to simulate expert workflows is a particularly innovative approach that enhances the model's practical utility. This method allows the model to learn from simulated expert interactions, which is a valuable way to incorporate real-world cybersecurity practices into the training process. Furthermore, the introduction of RedSage-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark with multiple-choice and open-ended questions, provides a robust framework for evaluating cybersecurity LLMs. The model's strong performance on this benchmark, as well as on existing ones, underscores its effectiveness. The authors' decision to release RedSage, along with its training data and evaluation benchmark, as open-source is a commendable contribution to the community. This will undoubtedly facilitate further research and development in the field of cybersecurity LLMs. The paper also does a good job of presenting the model's performance across a variety of tasks, demonstrating its versatility and potential for real-world applications. The inclusion of both cybersecurity-specific and general benchmarks allows for a more comprehensive assessment of the model's capabilities. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly presents the methodology and results, making it easy to follow and understand the contributions of the work.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, to enhance the description of the RedSage-Conv dataset, the authors should provide a more comprehensive explanation of the data augmentation process in the main text. This should include a detailed explanation of the augmentation techniques used, such as back-translation, paraphrasing, or synthetic data generation. The authors should also specify the criteria used to select data for augmentation, such as the complexity of the original data, the diversity of the augmented data, and the relevance to specific cybersecurity tasks. Furthermore, the authors should describe the methods used to ensure the quality of the augmented data, such as manual review, automated quality checks, or human evaluation. Providing examples of the augmented data, along with the corresponding original data, would also be beneficial for readers to understand the nature of the augmentation process. This would allow for a more thorough assessment of the reliability and validity of the augmented dataset. Secondly, to enhance the evaluation of the model, the authors should include experiments that simulate real-world cybersecurity scenarios. This could involve tasks such as incident response, vulnerability analysis, or threat hunting. For example, the authors could create a simulated environment where the model is tasked with identifying and mitigating a security breach, or analyzing a complex security log to identify potential threats. These experiments should be designed to assess the model's ability to integrate multiple pieces of information, apply its knowledge in a practical context, and adapt to new and unseen situations. The authors should also consider including metrics that measure the model's performance in these real-world scenarios, such as the time taken to complete a task, the accuracy of the model's decisions, and the effectiveness of the model's recommendations. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's practical utility and its ability to generalize to real-world cybersecurity challenges. Thirdly, to address the potential biases in the training data, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the data's composition, including the distribution of different cybersecurity topics, attack types, and vendor-specific information. This analysis should identify specific areas where the data might be skewed or underrepresented. For example, if the data contains a disproportionate amount of information about certain types of attacks or specific vendors, the model might develop a bias towards those areas, leading to suboptimal performance in other areas. The authors should also explore techniques for mitigating these biases, such as data augmentation or re-weighting of training samples. Furthermore, the evaluation should include specific tests designed to measure the model's susceptibility to these biases, such as by evaluating its performance on scenarios that are not well-represented in the training data. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's robustness and fairness. Finally, the authors should include a discussion on how RedSage handles adversarial inputs and attempts to extract sensitive information. This should include a description of any adversarial testing that was conducted and the methods used to mitigate such attacks. The authors should also discuss safeguards to prevent the model from being misused or providing information that could be used for malicious purposes. This could include techniques such as input filtering, output sanitization, or the use of safety prompts. By addressing these limitations, the authors can provide a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the capabilities and limitations of RedSage.
