## Summary:

This paper introduces DIST, a novel corrective sampling method designed to enhance the stability and performance of diffusion models in the context of 3D molecular generation. The authors begin by identifying a key challenge in applying diffusion models to molecular data: the dense and concentrated (DC) nature of molecular data distributions. They argue that this DC structure leads to error propagation and distributional discrepancies during the reverse inference process of diffusion models, causing the generation process to drift away from valid molecular configurations. To address this, DIST proposes a two-pronged approach: first, it diffuses the intermediate distribution to realign inference trajectories, and second, it steers these trajectories towards valid molecular peaks. The method involves dividing the space into radius-based batches, performing pilot reverse inferences on subsets of these batches, and then selectively applying a corrected reverse process based on the outcomes of these pilots. The core idea is to filter out regions of the distribution that are inconsistent with valid molecular structures, thereby improving the quality and stability of the generated molecules. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of DIST by integrating it with several existing diffusion-based molecular generation models, including EDM, GeoLDM, and RADM. Their empirical results, primarily on the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs datasets, show that DIST consistently improves the performance of these models across various metrics, such as atom stability, molecule stability, validity, and uniqueness. Furthermore, the authors claim that DIST significantly reduces the computational cost by requiring fewer timesteps for inference. The paper provides a theoretical foundation for the DC-structure and the proposed corrective method, which adds to the rigor of the work. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to address a critical challenge in molecular generation, with the potential to improve the reliability and efficiency of diffusion models in this domain. The authors also provide ablation studies in the appendix, which further support the effectiveness of the proposed method. The method is presented as a plug-in module, making it easily adaptable to various diffusion-based molecular generation methods. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the method, which is a valuable contribution to the field.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the authors provide a compelling and well-articulated analysis of the challenges associated with applying diffusion models to molecular generation, specifically highlighting the dense and concentrated (DC) nature of molecular data distributions. This identification of the DC-structure as a key obstacle is a significant contribution, as it provides a clear rationale for the need for corrective methods. The theoretical foundation provided for the DC-structure and the proposed corrective method is another strength. The authors formally define the DC-structure and provide error bounds for their selective reverse correction, which adds a layer of rigor to their approach. This theoretical grounding helps to establish the validity of their method and provides a framework for understanding its effectiveness. The proposed DIST method itself is a significant strength. It is designed as a plug-in module, making it highly versatile and easily integrable into a variety of existing diffusion-based molecular generation models. This modularity is a valuable feature, as it allows practitioners to readily incorporate DIST into their existing workflows. The empirical results presented in the paper are also compelling. The authors demonstrate that DIST consistently improves the performance of multiple diffusion models across various metrics, including atom stability, molecule stability, validity, and uniqueness. These improvements are observed on both the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs datasets, which are widely used benchmarks in the field. The fact that DIST enhances the performance of different models suggests that it is not specific to a particular architecture and is therefore more broadly applicable. Furthermore, the authors provide evidence that DIST significantly reduces the computational cost by requiring fewer timesteps for inference. This reduction in computational cost is a valuable contribution, as it makes the method more practical for large-scale applications. The ablation studies in the appendix further support the effectiveness of the proposed method. The authors explore the impact of different hyperparameters, such as the batch size, threshold, and perturbation intensity, which provides a more complete understanding of the method's behavior. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly explains the motivation, methodology, and results. The authors effectively communicate their ideas and make the paper accessible to a broad audience.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the method's reliance on predefined batches and thresholds is a significant limitation. As the authors describe, the method divides the space into radius-$r$ batches and uses a fixed threshold $	au$ to select batches for the corrected reverse process. This approach requires manual selection of these parameters, which may not be optimal for all datasets or molecular structures. The paper does not explore adaptive or learned thresholds, which could enhance the method's robustness. This lack of adaptability is a concern, as the optimal batch size and threshold may vary depending on the specific characteristics of the data distribution. The absence of a mechanism to automatically adjust these parameters limits the method's flexibility and generalizability. Second, while the paper provides a high-level analysis of the computational cost, it lacks a detailed breakdown of the time complexity and memory requirements. The authors mention that DIST requires fewer timesteps, but they do not provide a granular analysis of the computational cost of each step in the algorithm, such as the pilot sampling, batch creation, and selective reverse process. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the method's feasibility for large-scale applications. A more detailed analysis of the time spent on pilot runs versus the main sampling process is needed, as well as an analysis of the memory requirements for storing the molecular data, intermediate results, and model parameters. This is particularly important given that the method involves running a full reverse inference on a pilot subset to obtain scores, which could introduce additional computational overhead. Third, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach. The authors focus on the strengths of the method in the context of the DC-structure, but they do not discuss scenarios where the DC-structure assumption might not hold or how the method would perform on different types of molecular data, such as proteins. It is also unclear how the method behaves when the initial molecular structure is significantly different from the target distribution or if the diffusion process gets stuck in local minima. A more thorough analysis of these potential failure cases would provide a more complete understanding of the method's applicability and robustness. Fourth, the experimental evaluation is limited in several ways. The authors only provide results for EDM, GeoLDM, and RADM, which are all diffusion-based models. While they compare against ENF and G-SchNet, the core claim of the method is its ability to improve diffusion models, and thus a wider range of diffusion models should be tested. The authors should include results for other diffusion models, such as 3D-schNet and Geodiff, to demonstrate the general applicability of the proposed method. Furthermore, the evaluation is limited to the QM9 and GEOM-Drugs datasets. While these are standard benchmarks, it would be beneficial to evaluate the method on more diverse datasets, such as OGB-MolHP and GDB-9, to demonstrate its robustness across different molecular datasets. The paper also lacks results for no batch inference (i.e., |B|=1), which would help to understand the method's behavior in a more standard setting. The authors only provide results for a fixed radius $r=0.1$, perturbation intensity of 0.05, subset size of 10, intermediate timestep of 300, and threshold of -0.5. A more thorough analysis of the impact of these parameters on performance is needed. While the appendix includes some ablation studies, the main paper lacks a comprehensive sensitivity analysis for these key hyperparameters. Finally, the evaluation metrics are limited. The authors only provide results for atom stability, molecule stability, validity, and validity x uniqueness. It would be beneficial to include metrics such as the Earth Mover's Distance (EMD), the root mean square deviation (RMSD), precision and recall, the expected calibration error (ECE), maximum calibration error (MCE), and the negative log-likelihood (NLL). These additional metrics would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's performance. In summary, the lack of adaptability in hyperparameters, the limited computational cost analysis, the absence of a thorough discussion of limitations, the limited scope of the experimental evaluation, and the limited set of evaluation metrics are all significant weaknesses that need to be addressed.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should explore adaptive methods for determining the batch size, threshold, and perturbation intensity. Instead of relying on predefined values, they could investigate using a clustering algorithm to identify regions of high density in the molecular data distribution and then use these clusters to define the batches. Similarly, the threshold could be determined based on the statistical properties of the data, such as the mean or median score within each batch. This would make the method more flexible and less sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters. The authors should also investigate the impact of different batch sizes on the performance of the method, as it is possible that smaller batches may lead to better performance in some cases, while larger batches may be more appropriate for others. A thorough analysis of this parameter is crucial for the practical application of the method. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method. This should include a breakdown of the time spent on each step of the algorithm, including the pilot sampling, batch creation, and selective reverse process. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the memory requirements of the method, including the memory needed to store the molecular data, the intermediate results of the diffusion process, and the parameters of the model. This analysis should be performed for different batch sizes and different molecular structures. Furthermore, the authors should compare the computational cost of their method to that of other state-of-the-art methods for molecular generation. This would help practitioners better assess the feasibility of using the method for large-scale applications. It would also be beneficial to provide an estimate of the wall-clock time required to generate a single molecule using the proposed method, as this would provide a more concrete measure of the computational cost. Third, the authors should include a more thorough discussion of the limitations of their approach. They should discuss the cases where the DC-structure is not as pronounced, and how this may affect the performance of the method. They should also discuss the applicability of the method to different types of molecular data, such as proteins or other large molecules. It is important to understand the limitations of the method in order to determine when it is appropriate to use it. The authors should also consider the potential for the method to introduce biases into the generated molecular structures. For example, if the batches are not defined appropriately, the method may favor certain types of molecules over others. A thorough discussion of these limitations is essential for the responsible application of the method. Fourth, the authors should expand the scope of their experimental evaluation. They should include results for more diffusion models, such as 3D-schNet and Geodiff, to demonstrate the general applicability of the proposed method. Furthermore, they should consider evaluating their method on more datasets, such as OGB-MolHP and GDB-9, to demonstrate its robustness across different molecular datasets. The authors should also include results for no batch inference (i.e., |B|=1) to understand the method's behavior in a more standard setting. They should also provide results for a wider range of radii, perturbation intensities, subset sizes, intermediate timesteps, and thresholds. A more thorough sensitivity analysis for these key hyperparameters is needed. Finally, the authors should use a wider range of evaluation metrics to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's performance. They should include metrics such as the Earth Mover's Distance (EMD), the root mean square deviation (RMSD), precision and recall, the expected calibration error (ECE), maximum calibration error (MCE), and the negative log-likelihood (NLL). These additional metrics would provide a more complete picture of the method's performance, including its ability to generate diverse and realistic molecules, as well as its calibration and uncertainty estimation capabilities. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper and provide a more compelling case for the effectiveness of their proposed method.
