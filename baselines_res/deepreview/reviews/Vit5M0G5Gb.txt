## Summary:

This paper introduces a theoretical framework to understand the phenomenon of simplicity bias in neural networks, characterized by a tendency to learn increasingly complex solutions over time. The authors propose that this behavior can be explained by saddle-to-saddle dynamics, where the network transitions between fixed points of increasing complexity. They demonstrate that these transitions occur on invariant manifolds, effectively reducing the network's dimensionality and leading to low-rank or sparse weight configurations. The core of their analysis focuses on two-layer networks with homogeneous polynomial activation functions, where they provide rigorous mathematical proofs for the existence of fixed points and invariant manifolds. They further show that the learning dynamics can be understood as a sequence of transitions between these fixed points, driven by gradient descent. The paper also explores the interplay between data distribution and weight initialization, showing that data-induced dynamics lead to low-rank weights, while initialization-induced dynamics result in sparse weights. The authors support their theoretical findings with numerical experiments on various architectures, including fully-connected, convolutional, and attention-based networks, although the detailed analysis is primarily focused on two-layer networks. The paper's main contribution lies in providing a unifying theoretical framework for understanding simplicity bias across different architectures, and in identifying the underlying mechanisms of saddle-to-saddle dynamics. This framework offers a novel perspective on how neural networks learn, suggesting that they progressively recruit new effective units to increase the complexity of the learned solution. The authors also provide insights into how data distribution and initialization affect the learning process, which can inform the design of more effective neural network architectures and training procedures. Overall, this work provides a significant step towards a deeper understanding of the learning dynamics of neural networks, and opens up new avenues for future research in this area.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The most significant strength is the introduction of a novel theoretical framework that unifies the understanding of simplicity bias across diverse neural network architectures. The authors' use of saddle-to-saddle dynamics to explain the progressive learning of complex solutions is both elegant and insightful. The rigorous mathematical analysis, particularly the proofs for the existence of fixed points and invariant manifolds, provides a solid foundation for their claims. I appreciate the way they connect the theoretical framework to concrete architectural choices, showing how the simplicity bias manifests in different network types. The analysis of invariant manifolds and their connection to effectively narrower networks is a key contribution, offering a new perspective on how neural networks learn. The paper also does a commendable job of disentangling the effects of data distribution and weight initialization on the learning dynamics. The distinction between data-induced low-rank weights and initialization-induced sparse weights is a valuable contribution, providing new insights into the behavior of neural networks. The empirical evidence presented in the paper, while primarily focused on two-layer networks, supports the theoretical findings and demonstrates the practical relevance of the proposed framework. The experiments in Figure 2C, showing how different initializations affect the learning dynamics, are particularly compelling. The paper is also well-written and clearly explains complex concepts, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors have successfully integrated a wide range of ideas, from dynamical systems theory to neural network training, into a coherent and compelling narrative. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its theoretical rigor, its novel perspective on neural network learning, and its clear and insightful presentation.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation is the scope of the theoretical analysis, which is primarily focused on two-layer networks. While the authors acknowledge this limitation and propose a conjecture for deep networks, the lack of a rigorous analysis for deeper architectures significantly restricts the generalizability of their findings. The paper explicitly states in the 'Saddle-to-Saddle Dynamics' section that their analysis focuses on two-layer networks where the activation function is a homogeneous polynomial in the weights. This limitation is further emphasized in the 'Discussion' section, where the authors state that the analysis of dynamics in Section 5 only applies to two-layer networks, while the fixed points and invariant manifolds analysis extends to general deep networks. This is a critical weakness because it leaves open the question of whether the same saddle-to-saddle dynamics and simplicity bias hold for deeper networks, which are more commonly used in practice. The paper does not address how the interplay of multiple layers might affect the saddle-to-saddle dynamics, or whether the observed low-rank and sparse weight configurations would still emerge in deeper networks. This is a significant gap, as the dynamics of deeper networks can be substantially different from those of two-layer networks. Furthermore, the paper does not explicitly analyze the impact of skip connections or batch normalization, which are common components of modern deep networks. These architectural elements can introduce complex interactions between layers that are not captured by the current analysis. The absence of such analysis limits the applicability of the theoretical framework to real-world architectures. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion on the limitations of the saddle-to-saddle dynamics, specifically when this behavior might not be observed, and what factors could lead to different learning trajectories. While the 'Discussion' section does mention that tanh networks with small initialization and networks with large random initialization might not exhibit saddle-to-saddle dynamics, this discussion is not comprehensive enough. The paper does not explore the impact of different optimization algorithms, learning rates, or data distributions on the observed dynamics. This lack of a thorough discussion on the limitations of the framework reduces its overall robustness and applicability. Another weakness is the limited exploration of non-polynomial activation functions. The detailed analysis focuses on polynomial activation functions, and while the paper discusses the case of ReLU networks, it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how the results would generalize to other non-polynomial activations commonly used in practice. This is a concern because the behavior of neural networks can be highly sensitive to the choice of activation function. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion on the practical implications of the findings. While the theoretical framework is robust, it would be valuable to explore how the insights gained from the analysis can be applied to improve the design and training of neural networks in real-world scenarios. For example, the paper could discuss how the understanding of saddle-to-saddle dynamics could inform the choice of initialization schemes, learning rates, or regularization techniques. Furthermore, the paper could explore how the identified low-rank and sparse weight configurations could be leveraged for model compression or pruning. The absence of such a discussion limits the practical impact of the paper. Finally, while the paper provides some experimental details, I found that more specific information about the optimization algorithms, learning rate schedules, hyperparameter tuning processes, data preprocessing steps, and the exact architectures used in the experiments would be beneficial for reproducibility. The lack of detail makes it difficult to replicate the results and verify the claims made in the paper. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the absence of certain analyses.
