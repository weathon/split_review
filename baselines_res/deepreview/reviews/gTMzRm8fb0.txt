## Summary:

This paper introduces GoalRank, a novel generator-only ranking framework designed to challenge the conventional Generator-Evaluator (G-E) paradigm prevalent in recommender systems. The core argument of the paper is that a single-stage generator, when sufficiently large, can achieve superior ranking quality compared to multi-stage G-E models, which often suffer from diminishing returns as the number of candidate lists increases. The authors present a rigorous theoretical analysis, demonstrating that a generator-only model can achieve a strictly smaller approximation error to the optimal ranking policy than any finite G-E model, and that this error decreases as the model size increases. This theoretical foundation is supported by a proposed group-relative optimization principle, which leverages a reward model trained on real user feedback to guide the generator. The paper's methodology involves training a generator to directly produce a ranked list of recommendations, bypassing the need for a separate evaluator. The training process is guided by a reward model that estimates the expected user feedback for a given recommendation list, and the generator is optimized to maximize this reward. The authors conduct extensive offline experiments on public benchmarks and large-scale online A/B tests to validate the effectiveness of GoalRank. The offline experiments include comparisons with state-of-the-art methods, demonstrating that GoalRank consistently outperforms strong baselines. The online A/B tests, conducted on a real-world recommender system, further confirm the practical effectiveness of GoalRank, showing significant improvements in key business metrics. The paper's main contribution lies in its theoretical proof of the superiority of generator-only models, the introduction of a practical training framework, and the empirical validation of its effectiveness. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for rethinking the traditional G-E paradigm in recommender systems and offers a promising new direction for research in this area. The work is significant because it challenges a widely accepted approach and provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated alternative, which could lead to more efficient and effective recommender systems.
