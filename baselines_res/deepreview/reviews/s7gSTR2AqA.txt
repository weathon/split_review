## Summary:

This paper investigates the capacity of large language models (LLMs) to develop human-like semantic categories, specifically focusing on color naming. The authors explore this question through the lens of the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle, which balances the complexity and accuracy of communication. The core idea is that human languages tend to optimize for efficient communication by compressing meanings into words while preserving as much information as possible. The authors replicate classic human studies on color naming with a range of LLMs, assessing their performance in terms of both alignment with human color categories and their adherence to the IB principle. The study employs two main experimental approaches: an English color-naming task, where LLMs are prompted to name colors, and an iterated learning experiment, where LLMs are used to simulate the cultural evolution of color categories. The iterated learning experiment involves iteratively refining color-naming systems based on the model's own predictions, mimicking how human languages evolve over time. The paper's main empirical findings indicate that larger, instruction-tuned models, particularly Gemini 2.0, demonstrate the best alignment with human color naming systems and achieve higher IB-efficiency. Furthermore, the iterated learning experiments show that LLMs can evolve initially random color categories towards more efficient and structured systems, mirroring human cultural evolution. The authors argue that these findings suggest that LLMs may possess an inductive bias towards IB-efficiency, which could explain their ability to develop human-like semantic categories without explicit training. Overall, the paper bridges cognitive science and machine learning by applying the IB framework to analyze LLM behavior, providing insights into the potential mechanisms underlying semantic category formation in both artificial and human intelligence. The study's significance lies in its exploration of how LLMs, despite being trained on text data, can exhibit behaviors consistent with human cognitive principles, suggesting a possible convergence towards optimal communication strategies.

## Strengths:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the capacity of LLMs to develop human-like semantic categories, and I find several aspects of the work to be particularly strong. First, the paper's use of the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle as a theoretical framework is a significant strength. This framework, which originates from cognitive science, provides a principled way to evaluate the efficiency of semantic systems by balancing complexity and accuracy. By applying this framework to LLMs, the authors bridge the gap between cognitive science and machine learning, offering a novel perspective on how these models learn and represent information. Second, the experimental design is thorough and well-executed. The authors conduct two distinct but complementary experiments: an English color-naming task and an iterated learning experiment. The color-naming task allows for a direct comparison between LLM performance and human color categories, while the iterated learning experiment provides insights into the dynamic evolution of these categories. The inclusion of a wide range of models, including both open-source and proprietary models, adds to the robustness of the findings. The authors also consider both text-based and multimodal models, which allows for a more comprehensive analysis of the factors influencing performance. The use of the World Color Survey (WCS) data as a benchmark for human color naming is also a strength, as it provides a large and diverse dataset for comparison. Furthermore, the paper's finding that larger, instruction-tuned models, particularly Gemini 2.0, achieve the best alignment with human color categories and IB-efficiency is noteworthy. This suggests that these models have developed more sophisticated internal representations that allow them to capture the underlying structure of color space. Finally, the paper's exploration of iterated learning is a valuable contribution. The fact that LLMs can evolve initially random color categories towards more efficient and structured systems, mirroring human cultural evolution, is a fascinating result. This suggests that the tendency towards IB-efficiency may be an emergent property of the learning process, rather than being explicitly encoded in the training data. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel application of the IB framework, its thorough experimental design, and its compelling findings that suggest a convergence between LLM behavior and human cognitive principles.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents several strengths, I have identified some weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's main contribution is more empirical than theoretical. While the authors apply the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle to LLMs, they do not introduce a novel theoretical framework. Instead, they use an existing framework to analyze LLM behavior. The paper's primary contribution lies in demonstrating that LLMs can achieve IB-efficiency, which, while interesting, is not entirely surprising given the known capabilities of these models to learn complex patterns and optimize for information compression. The paper itself acknowledges that larger, instruction-tuned models perform better on the color-naming task, which aligns with general expectations about LLM capabilities. This raises the question of whether the paper provides novel insights beyond what we already know about LLMs. The paper's results show a correlation between model size and IB-efficiency, and it is known that larger language models learn more complex and structured representations. Therefore, the observed correlation could be explained by these more complex and structured representations in large models, rather than an inherent inductive bias towards IB-efficiency. This is a critical point because the paper claims that its findings suggest that LLMs might have an inductive bias toward IB-efficiency. However, the results could also be explained by the increased representational capacity of larger models due to their training. The paper does not provide sufficient evidence to rule out this alternative explanation. Second, the study relies heavily on color categorization, which, while a classic testbed in cognitive science, may not fully capture the complexities of semantic category formation in LLMs. It is unclear if the findings will generalize to other domains, such as abstract concepts or social knowledge. The paper does not explore the potential limitations of using a perceptually grounded domain like color, where the underlying structure is relatively well-defined, compared to more abstract or culturally influenced domains. The authors acknowledge that color is a unique domain with readily available human data, but this also means that the findings may not be directly applicable to other types of semantic categories. The paper includes a preliminary experiment in a different domain, but this is not a full-scale analysis comparable to the color-naming task. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the paper does not deeply explore why certain models, such as Gemini 2.0, outperform others in achieving human-like IB-efficiency. The analysis lacks a detailed investigation into the architectural differences or training procedures that might explain the observed performance variations. For example, the paper does not analyze the impact of different pre-training datasets, fine-tuning methods, or model sizes on the emergence of IB-efficient color categories. While the paper mentions instruction tuning as a factor, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how this impacts the results. The paper also does not explore the role of model architecture, such as attention mechanisms or the number of layers, in the emergence of IB-efficient category systems. This lack of a detailed analysis of the factors driving performance differences between models is a significant limitation, as it prevents a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the observed results. The paper's claims about inductive bias and the emergence of human-like semantic categories are weakened by the lack of a mechanistic explanation for the observed performance differences.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to strengthen the paper's theoretical contribution, the authors should explore the mechanisms driving the observed IB-efficiency in more detail. While the correlation between model size and IB-efficiency is interesting, it is not sufficient to claim an inductive bias. The authors should investigate whether the observed efficiency is a result of the specific training process or an emergent property of the model architecture. This could involve comparing models trained on different datasets or with different training objectives, but with similar architectural properties. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the internal representations of the models to understand how they encode color information and how this encoding relates to the observed IB-efficiency. This could involve techniques such as probing or representational similarity analysis to identify the specific features that contribute to the efficient color naming systems. Second, to address the limitation of relying heavily on color categorization, the authors should expand their analysis to include other domains that are more representative of the complexities of human semantic knowledge. For example, they could investigate the emergence of categories in domains such as emotion, social roles, or even abstract concepts like justice or freedom. This would help to determine whether the observed IB-efficiency is a general property of LLMs or specific to perceptually grounded domains. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different types of training data on the emergence of semantic categories. For instance, they could compare models trained on datasets with varying levels of semantic structure or models trained on data from different cultures to see how these factors influence the resulting category systems. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the factors that contribute to the emergence of human-like semantic categories in LLMs. Third, to address the lack of a detailed analysis of the factors driving performance differences between models, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the architectural and training differences between the models they tested. This could involve examining the impact of model size, attention mechanisms, and pre-training objectives on the ability of LLMs to achieve IB-efficiency. For example, they could investigate whether models with specific architectural features, such as larger attention heads or more layers, are better at learning efficient category systems. They could also analyze the impact of different pre-training datasets, such as those with more or less semantic structure, on the resulting category systems. This would provide a more mechanistic understanding of the factors that contribute to the observed performance differences between models. Furthermore, the authors should explore the role of instruction tuning in the emergence of IB-efficient category systems. It would be beneficial to compare the performance of instruction-tuned models with that of models trained directly on the color categorization task to determine whether instruction tuning is a necessary or sufficient condition for the emergence of human-like category systems. Finally, the authors should consider incorporating a more fine-grained analysis of the category systems that emerge in LLMs. This could involve examining the boundaries between categories, the degree of overlap between categories, and the hierarchical structure of the category systems. For example, they could analyze whether the category systems that emerge in LLMs are more or less structured than those observed in human languages. They could also investigate whether the category systems that emerge in LLMs are more or less sensitive to perceptual cues than those observed in human languages. This would provide a more detailed understanding of the nature of the semantic categories that emerge in LLMs and how they compare to those observed in human languages.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are crucial for further understanding the paper's findings. First, how do the authors ensure that the LLMs' color naming systems are genuinely optimizing for IB-efficiency rather than simply mimicking patterns in their training data? Could the observed alignment with human color categories be a result of overfitting to human language data rather than an independent drive for IB-efficiency? This is a critical question because the paper's claims about inductive bias rely on the assumption that the models are not simply reproducing patterns they have seen during training. Second, given the wide range of complexities and alignments observed across different LLMs, what factors do the authors believe contribute to the superior performance of models like Gemini 2.0? Is it primarily due to model size, instruction tuning, or other architectural differences? The paper acknowledges the correlation between model size and performance, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the specific factors that drive these differences. A more mechanistic explanation is needed to fully understand why some models perform better than others. Third, the study focuses on color categorization, but how do the authors envision their findings generalizing to other types of semantic categories that are less perceptually grounded, such as abstract concepts or social categories? The paper acknowledges the limitations of focusing solely on color, but it does not provide a clear roadmap for how the findings could be extended to other domains. A more detailed discussion of the potential challenges and opportunities in generalizing the findings is needed. Finally, how do the authors plan to extend this research to explore the dynamics of category evolution in LLMs over longer iterations or in response to different types of linguistic input? The paper's iterated learning experiment provides initial evidence for the emergence of IB-efficient category systems, but it is unclear how these systems would evolve over longer periods or under different conditions. A more detailed exploration of the dynamics of category evolution is needed to fully understand the potential of LLMs to develop human-like semantic categories.
