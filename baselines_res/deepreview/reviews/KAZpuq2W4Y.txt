## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel multi-instance learning (MIL) framework, termed Higher-Order Multi-Instance Learning (HOMIL), designed for the classification of whole-slide images (WSIs) in computational pathology. The core contribution of HOMIL lies in its integration of both first-order (mean) and second-order (covariance) statistical moments to capture the complex variability and inter-feature relationships among patches within a WSI. This approach moves beyond the traditional reliance on mean-based representations, aiming to provide a more nuanced understanding of the spatial and feature-wise heterogeneity inherent in WSIs. To address the computational challenges associated with processing high-resolution WSIs, the authors employ an adaptive clustering strategy using the DBSCAN algorithm. This clustering approach groups similar patches into variable-sized clusters, allowing for a more efficient processing of abundant normal tissues while preserving the fine-grained details of rare pathological regions. The method then aggregates patch features within each cluster using both first and second-order statistical moments, which are subsequently used for WSI-level classification. The authors evaluate HOMIL on two real-world WSI datasets, CAMELYON16 and TCGA-NSCLC, demonstrating significant improvements in accuracy, AUC, and F1 score compared to several state-of-the-art MIL baselines. The empirical results are further supported by a thorough ablation study and sensitivity analysis, which validate the effectiveness of both the clustering module and the second-order moment module. The paper's findings suggest that incorporating higher-order statistical moments and adaptive clustering can lead to more accurate and efficient WSI classification, with potential implications for various clinical applications. The authors also provide a detailed description of their method, including mathematical formulations and a logical flow of ideas, which enhances the clarity and reproducibility of their work. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and empirically supported contribution to the field of computational pathology, offering a promising approach for WSI analysis.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the integration of higher-order statistical moments, specifically the covariance matrix, into the MIL framework is a novel and valuable contribution. By moving beyond simple mean-based representations, the authors capture richer information about the patch features, which is particularly well-suited for the heterogeneous nature of WSIs. This approach allows the model to consider not only the average intensity of patches but also the variability and relationships between different feature dimensions, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying tissue structure. Second, the use of adaptive clustering via DBSCAN is a clever way to address the computational challenges of processing high-resolution WSIs. By grouping similar patches into clusters, the method reduces the number of instances that need to be processed, leading to significant improvements in computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. This adaptive approach is particularly beneficial because it allows for variable-resolution processing, emphasizing rare pathological regions while reducing the computational load in abundant normal tissues. The authors' decision to use DBSCAN is well-motivated by its ability to identify clusters of varying densities and sizes, which is crucial for capturing the diverse patch distributions in WSIs. Third, the empirical evaluation is thorough and convincing. The authors compare their method against a wide range of baselines, including both traditional and more recent MIL approaches. The results on two real-world datasets demonstrate the superiority of HOMIL across multiple metrics, including accuracy, AUC, and F1 score. The inclusion of both a CAMELYON16 and a TCGA-NSCLC dataset provides a robust evaluation of the method's generalizability. Furthermore, the ablation study and sensitivity analysis further strengthen the paper by showing the importance of each component of the proposed framework. The ablation study clearly demonstrates the contribution of both the clustering module and the second-order moment module, while the sensitivity analysis provides evidence of the method's robustness to variations in the compression rate. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly structured. The authors provide a comprehensive introduction to the problem of WSI classification and the limitations of existing MIL methods. The proposed method is explained in detail, with clear mathematical formulations and a logical flow of ideas. This clarity enhances the readability and reproducibility of the work, making it accessible to a wider audience.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. While the authors mention that HOMIL is more efficient than some baselines and provide empirical runtime comparisons in Tables 1 and 2, they do not offer a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of each step in the HOMIL framework. Specifically, the paper should include a breakdown of the computational cost associated with DBSCAN clustering, covariance matrix calculation, and higher-order moment aggregation. This analysis should consider the impact of the number of patches, the number of clusters, and the dimensionality of the feature space on the overall computational cost. Furthermore, a comparison of the computational complexity with the baseline methods should be provided, going beyond just reporting the runtime in tables. The absence of this analysis makes it difficult to fully assess the scalability and efficiency of the method, particularly for very large WSIs or datasets. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lacks any formal complexity analysis, relying solely on empirical runtime data. Second, the method's performance is highly dependent on the choice of hyperparameters, particularly those related to DBSCAN clustering (e.g., epsilon and minPts). While the sensitivity analysis in the Appendix shows that the method is robust to some extent with respect to the compression rate, a more systematic approach to hyperparameter tuning, such as grid search or Bayesian optimization, would strengthen the paper. The paper should also discuss the potential impact of suboptimal hyperparameter choices on the performance of the method and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for different datasets. It is also unclear how the hyperparameters are selected for each dataset and whether the same procedure is used for both datasets. The paper mentions that epsilon is set to the 65th percentile of nearest neighbor distances and minPts is set to 4, but it does not justify these choices or discuss their potential impact. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper provides specific hyperparameter values without a detailed justification or sensitivity analysis of these parameters themselves. Third, the paper does not adequately address the potential limitations of using second-order moments. While the authors argue that higher-order moments capture more information, they do not discuss the potential drawbacks, such as increased sensitivity to noise and outliers. A more nuanced discussion of the trade-offs between first-order and second-order moments, and when each might be more appropriate, would be valuable. For example, the paper should discuss how the method handles cases where the patch features are not normally distributed, and how the covariance matrix might be affected by outliers or noisy patches. Furthermore, the paper should explore alternative methods for capturing higher-order statistics, such as kernel methods or tensor-based approaches, and discuss their potential advantages and disadvantages compared to the proposed method. The paper does not cite any literature discussing the limitations of covariance matrices, which is a significant oversight. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any discussion of the limitations of covariance and does not explore alternative methods. Fourth, the motivation for introducing covariance in multi-instance learning (MIL) is not entirely clear. While the authors provide a general explanation of the limitations of first-order moments and the potential benefits of second-order moments, they do not provide a strong theoretical or empirical rationale for why capturing second-order statistics is necessary for improving WSI classification. The explanation of how covariance enhances the model's ability to capture complex patterns is insufficient. The paper states that covariance captures “patch variability and inter-feature relationships,” but it does not provide a deep explanation of *why* this is the case in the context of WSIs or what specific information is being captured. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does provide some motivation but lacks a deep theoretical backing. Finally, the calculation of the covariance matrix, as defined in Equation (4), is not clearly explained, especially considering that the elements of $g_k$ are not independent. The paper lacks a detailed explanation of how the covariance matrix is computed and what its specific properties represent in the context of WSI patch features. The lack of independence among $g_k$ elements raises questions about the validity of using this covariance calculation. The paper presents the formula for the covariance matrix but does not explicitly address the assumption of independence between cluster features or discuss the implications of this assumption. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper provides a brief explanation of the covariance calculation without addressing the independence assumption.

## Questions:

I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how does the choice of feature representation impact the performance of the method? For example, would using features extracted from a different pre-trained model or a model trained specifically for WSI analysis lead to different results? This question is important because the performance of the method might be sensitive to the quality and relevance of the input features. Second, the paper focuses on binary classification tasks. How well does the proposed method generalize to multi-class classification problems? Are there any modifications needed to adapt the method to such scenarios? This question is crucial for assessing the broader applicability of the method to more complex clinical problems. Third, the authors mention that DBSCAN adaptively adjusts the granularity of clustering. Can the authors provide more insights into how this affects the model's performance? For example, how does the number of clusters impact the accuracy and computational efficiency of the method? This question is important for understanding the trade-offs between accuracy and efficiency and for optimizing the clustering parameters. Fourth, what specific information do covariance matrices capture in WSI patches that mean-based methods miss? The paper does not provide a clear rationale for why covariance is a suitable measure for capturing important information in this context. This question seeks a deeper understanding of the interpretability of the second-order moment representation. Finally, how does the model handle patches with similar mean values but different distributions? Since the model relies heavily on mean values, it is unclear how it differentiates between patches that have the same average intensity but different spatial arrangements or textures. While the paper incorporates covariance, this question seeks further clarification on how effectively this addresses the issue.
