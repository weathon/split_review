## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for constructing equivariant machine learning models that process tensor data while respecting specific symmetries. The authors focus on equivariance with respect to the orthogonal group O(d), the indefinite orthogonal group O(s, k-s), and the symplectic group Sp(d), which are relevant in various scientific domains such as physics, materials science, and time series analysis. The core contribution of the paper is a theoretical framework that provides explicit parameterizations for polynomial and analytic functions that are equivariant with respect to these groups. The authors demonstrate the practical utility of their framework through three distinct applications: learning the relationship between stress and strain tensors in materials science, representing time series data using path signatures, and sparse vector estimation in theoretical computer science. The proposed equivariant models outperform non-equivariant baseline models and prior static methods in these applications, highlighting the benefits of incorporating symmetry constraints into machine learning models. However, the paper is dense and notation-heavy, which can make it difficult to follow, and the empirical validation is limited in scale, particularly in terms of the output tensor rank, input size, and input dimension. Additionally, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with existing equivariant models and a thorough analysis of computational efficiency and scalability, which are crucial for practical applicability.

## Strengths:

The paper makes several significant contributions to the field of equivariant machine learning. Firstly, it provides a complete characterization of polynomial functions from multiple tensor inputs to tensor outputs that are equivariant with respect to the diagonal action of classical Lie groups, including the orthogonal group, the symplectic group, and the Lorentz group. This theoretical framework is a substantial advancement, as it generalizes prior work and leverages tensor invariant theory to create models suitable for machine learning applications. The authors' approach is original in its explicit parameterization of equivariant functions using tensor invariants, which offers a new perspective on building symmetry-preserving models. The paper is technically sound, with rigorous proofs for the main theorems and corollaries, establishing a solid foundation for the proposed framework. The experimental results are comprehensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of the equivariant models across three diverse applications. In materials science, the models learn the relationship between second-order stress and strain tensors, outperforming non-equivariant baselines. In time series analysis, the models estimate path signatures from sampled points, showing improved performance over non-equivariant methods. In sparse vector estimation, the learned models can operate in settings where theoretical guarantees have yet to be developed, further validating the practical benefits of the framework. The paper is well-written and organized, with clear definitions and explanations of key concepts, making it accessible to readers with a background in machine learning and physics. The use of examples and illustrations helps to clarify the theoretical results and their practical implications. Overall, the paper addresses an important problem in machine learning—how to incorporate symmetry constraints into models that process tensor data—and provides a robust solution with demonstrated practical benefits.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its significant contributions, the paper has several limitations that need to be addressed. One of the most prominent issues is the dense and notation-heavy nature of the paper, which can make it difficult to follow. For example, equation (11) is particularly complex, with a mix of subscripts and superscripts, summation over multiple variables, and a somewhat unusual notation for the Kronecker delta. While the authors claim this notation is standard in physics, it is not common in machine learning, making the paper less accessible to the broader ML community. The lack of clear definitions for each symbol in the equation further exacerbates the issue, and a more step-by-step approach to introducing the notation would greatly enhance readability. The paper also claims that the proposed method is more computationally efficient than previous methods, such as e3nn, but does not provide empirical evidence to support this claim. A direct comparison of computational efficiency, including runtime and memory usage, with existing methods would help validate this claim and provide a more concrete understanding of the practical benefits of the approach. The claim of universality is another area that needs more rigorous justification. The authors state that their construction is universal in the Stone-Weierstrass sense, but this requires the ability to approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy. The paper does not provide a clear argument that the specific parameterization used achieves this, and a more detailed explanation of how the learnable MLPs ensure this universality is needed. The numerical examples are limited in scale, which makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method in more complex scenarios. For instance, in the first example, the output is always a rank-2 tensor, and it would be interesting to see how the method performs when the output is a higher-order tensor. Similarly, in the second example, the input is a set of 4 vectors, and it would be valuable to see how the method scales to larger input sizes. The third example has a relatively small input dimension (d=5), and testing the method with higher input dimensions would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of its practical applicability. The limited scale of the experiments also makes it challenging to fully assess the scalability and practical applicability of the method to more complex scenarios involving higher-order tensors, larger input sizes, and higher input dimensions. The paper primarily compares the proposed models against non-equivariant baselines, which makes it difficult to evaluate the relative strengths and weaknesses of the framework compared to existing equivariant approaches. A more thorough comparison with other equivariant models, such as those based on group convolutions or tensor field networks, would help contextualize the contribution of this work within the broader landscape of equivariant machine learning. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the framework, particularly in terms of the number of parameters and the time required for forward and backward passes. While the authors acknowledge that directly evaluating the equivariant functions defined in Corollary 1 can be computationally expensive, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed parameterization or discuss potential strategies for mitigating this issue. This is especially important when dealing with high-order tensors and large input sizes. The paper's focus on specific symmetry groups (orthogonal, indefinite orthogonal, and symplectic) is a strength, but the lack of discussion on generalizing the framework to other symmetry groups relevant in different scientific domains limits its broader applicability. For example, it would be valuable to explore the challenges and possibilities of extending the framework to other Lie groups or discrete symmetry groups, such as permutations or non-continuous transformations. Finally, the paper provides a high-level overview of the framework but lacks detailed implementation guidelines for practitioners. While the authors mention that the coefficients in Corollary 1 can be learned using MLPs, they do not provide specific details on how to implement this in practice, including the choice of neural network architectures, optimization strategies, and hyperparameter tuning. Concrete examples of how to implement the proposed method in popular deep learning frameworks would make the framework more accessible to a wider audience and facilitate its adoption in practical applications.


## Suggestions:

Specifically, in equation (11), they should avoid using multiple subscripts and superscripts on the same symbol and provide clear definitions for each symbol used. A step-by-step introduction of the notation, perhaps with a running example, would greatly enhance readability. The authors should also include more explanatory text to guide the reader through the more complex equations, breaking down the equation into smaller parts and explaining the purpose of each term. A notation table at the beginning of the paper for quick reference would be beneficial. To address the lack of empirical evidence for computational efficiency, the authors should include a direct comparison of their method with existing methods, such as e3nn, in terms of runtime and memory usage. This comparison should be performed on a range of problem sizes to demonstrate the scalability of their method. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of how the learnable MLPs ensure the universality of their construction. They should clarify how the MLPs are used to approximate arbitrary continuous functions of the pairwise inner products and provide a theoretical justification for this approximation. A concrete example of how the MLPs are trained and used in practice would also be beneficial. To strengthen the empirical validation, the authors should include experiments with higher-order tensors and larger input sizes. For example, they could explore the performance of their method when the output is a rank-3 or rank-4 tensor. They should also investigate how the method scales with the number of input vectors and test their method on datasets with higher input dimensions. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's practical applicability. The authors should also consider comparing their method with other equivariant methods, not just MLP baselines, to provide a more complete picture of its performance. To address the limited comparison with existing equivariant models, the authors should include a more comprehensive experimental evaluation that directly compares their approach with other state-of-the-art equivariant methods. This should include methods that also leverage tensor representations and symmetry, such as those based on group convolutions or tensor field networks. The comparison should not only focus on performance metrics but also on computational efficiency and scalability. For example, the authors could compare their method with a tensor field network or other similar architectures on a benchmark dataset. This would provide a more complete picture of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed framework and help to contextualize its contribution within the broader landscape of equivariant machine learning. To improve the practical implementation details, the authors should provide more specific guidelines on how to implement their framework in popular deep learning frameworks. This should include detailed examples of how to construct the equivariant layers using the proposed parameterization, as well as specific choices of neural network architectures for the coefficients in Corollary 1. The authors should also discuss optimization strategies and hyperparameter tuning, providing practical advice for practitioners who want to use their framework. For example, the authors could provide a code snippet demonstrating how to implement a simple equivariant layer using PyTorch or TensorFlow. This would make the framework more accessible to a wider audience and facilitate its adoption in practical applications. To address the lack of detailed analysis of computational complexity, the authors should provide a rigorous analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed parameterization, particularly in terms of the number of parameters and the time required for forward and backward passes. This analysis should consider the impact of tensor order and dimensionality on the computational cost. The authors should also discuss potential strategies for mitigating the computational burden, such as using low-degree polynomials or other approximation techniques, and provide a practical demonstration of these strategies. Finally, the authors should discuss the potential limitations of their approach in terms of computational cost and memory requirements, especially when dealing with high-order tensors and large input sizes. This would help to identify the scenarios where the proposed method is most likely to be effective and where it might face challenges.
