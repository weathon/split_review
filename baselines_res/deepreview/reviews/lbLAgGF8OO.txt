## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel model-free decision-estimation coefficient (DEC), termed Dig-DEC, for decision-making with structured observations (DMSO). The core contribution of Dig-DEC is its ability to drive exploration purely through information gain, removing the need for optimism, which is a common approach in existing DEC frameworks. By applying Dig-DEC to hybrid Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with stochastic transitions and adversarial rewards, the paper establishes the first model-free regret bounds for such settings under linear reward and general transition structures. Additionally, the paper refines the online function estimation procedure, leading to improved regret bounds in several canonical settings, including bilinear classes and Bellman-complete MDPs. The significance of this work lies in its theoretical advancements, particularly in resolving the gap between upper and lower regret bounds that scales with the size of the model class, and in its potential to influence future research in model-free reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms. However, the paper's abstract presentation and lack of empirical validation pose challenges for readers and practitioners seeking to understand and apply the proposed methods.

## Strengths:

The paper makes several valuable contributions to the field of decision-making with structured observations (DMSO). Firstly, the introduction of Dig-DEC is a significant advancement, as it removes the reliance on optimism, a principle that has been central to previous DEC frameworks. This innovation not only aligns more closely with the general idea of DEC but also enables the handling of adversarial environments without explicit reward estimators. The authors demonstrate that Dig-DEC can achieve tighter regret bounds in certain settings, which is a notable improvement over existing methods. Secondly, the paper establishes the first sublinear regret bounds for model-free learning in hybrid MDPs with bandit feedback, a problem that has been open in the literature. This is a crucial step forward, as it broadens the applicability of DEC to more complex and realistic environments. Thirdly, the refinement of the online function estimation procedure is another technical innovation that leads to sharper regret bounds in several canonical settings, such as bilinear classes and Bellman-complete MDPs. The techniques used to achieve these improvements, particularly in the case of squared estimation error, could be of independent interest to the research community. Overall, the paper's theoretical contributions are robust and well-supported, providing a strong foundation for future research in model-free RL.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its significant theoretical contributions, the paper has several weaknesses that impact its accessibility and practical relevance. The primary issue is the abstract and dense presentation, which makes it challenging for readers, especially those unfamiliar with the field, to follow the main ideas and the proof strategies. For instance, the introduction of new concepts and notations, such as Dig-DEC and the specific divergence measure $D$, is abrupt and lacks intuitive explanations or concrete examples. This is evident in Sections 2.1 and 4, where the definitions are presented formally without sufficient context or motivation. The connection between Dig-DEC and the removal of optimism is not clearly established, and the benefits of this approach are not immediately apparent. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the assumptions required for the theoretical results, and how these assumptions relate to existing work. This would help readers understand the scope and limitations of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the paper focuses on specific settings, such as linear Q/V* MDPs and hybrid MDPs with linear rewards, which limits the generalizability of the results. ## Suggestions:

To enhance the clarity and accessibility of the paper, several concrete improvements can be made. Firstly, the introduction of new concepts and notations, such as Dig-DEC and the divergence measure $D$, should be accompanied by more intuitive explanations and concrete examples. For instance, when introducing Dig-DEC, the authors could provide a simple illustrative example, such as a tabular MDP, to demonstrate how it differs from the standard DEC and how it enables the removal of optimism. This would help readers grasp the practical implications of the theoretical results. Secondly, the paper should include a step-by-step explanation of the key ideas behind the proof, highlighting the role of each component in the overall framework. For example, when introducing the divergence measure $D$, the authors should explain why this specific measure is chosen and how it relates to the information gain. The connection between Dig-DEC and the removal of optimism should be made more explicit, perhaps by contrasting it with existing optimistic approaches and explaining the advantages of the proposed method in terms of regret bounds and computational efficiency. Thirdly, the paper could benefit from more illustrative examples and diagrams to help readers visualize the concepts and the flow of the algorithm. A diagram illustrating the interaction between the agent and the environment in a hybrid MDP setting would be particularly useful. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of the mathematical derivations, including the motivation behind each step and the assumptions being made. This would make the paper more accessible to a broader audience. Fourthly, the paper should include a section that summarizes the key results and their implications, making it easier for readers to understand the main contributions of the paper. The authors should also consider adding a section that discusses the limitations of the proposed approach and potential directions for future research. This would help to put the work in context and highlight the open questions in the field. Finally, the paper should include empirical validation to support the theoretical findings. The authors could conduct experiments on a variety of benchmark problems, including both synthetic and real-world datasets, to compare the performance of the proposed algorithm with existing methods. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed approach and help readers understand its strengths and weaknesses. The experiments should explore the sensitivity of the algorithm to different parameter settings and problem characteristics, and the results should be presented in a clear and concise manner, with appropriate statistical analysis to support the claims made in the paper.
