## Summary:

This paper introduces the Language Confusion Gate (LCG), a novel mechanism designed to mitigate language confusion in large language models (LLMs) without requiring model retraining or restricting legitimate code-switching. Language confusion, the unintended mixing of languages during text generation, can degrade the quality of output, particularly in multilingual contexts. The LCG operates as a lightweight, plug-in module that filters tokens dynamically during decoding. It employs a norm-adjusted self-distillation approach to predict appropriate language families and applies masking only when necessary. The core idea is to train a small two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) to predict the language family of the next token, using the LLM's own top-k predictions as pseudo-targets. During inference, the LCG masks tokens that do not belong to the predicted language family. The authors demonstrate that LCG significantly reduces language confusion across various models, including Qwen3, GPT-OSS, and Llama3.1, without degrading task performance. The method is based on key observations about language confusion, including its infrequency, the presence of correct-language tokens among top predictions, and the bias towards high-resource languages due to token embedding norm imbalances. The authors also open-source specialized training and evaluation datasets, contributing valuable resources for further research in this area. The evaluation includes a variety of tasks and models, demonstrating the effectiveness of LCG in reducing language confusion while preserving or slightly improving task performance metrics like BLEU scores and accuracy. The paper also explores the impact of LCG on code-switching, showing that while the method reduces the frequency of code-switching, it largely preserves the model's code-switching ability. The authors acknowledge the limitations of the current approach, particularly its script-level granularity, which may not be sufficient for nuanced language mixing scenarios. Overall, this paper presents a practical and effective solution to a significant problem in multilingual LLMs, offering a valuable contribution to the field.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in the introduction of the Language Confusion Gate (LCG), a novel and practical mechanism for addressing language confusion in large language models. The LCG is designed as a lightweight, plug-in module, which means it can be easily integrated with existing LLMs without requiring model retraining or altering the base model's parameters. This is a significant advantage, as it allows for broad applicability across various models and avoids the computational costs associated with retraining. The method is grounded in key observations about language confusion, such as its infrequency, the presence of correct-language tokens among top predictions, and the bias towards high-resource languages due to token embedding norm imbalances. These insights inform the design of the LCG and contribute to its effectiveness. The use of norm-adjusted self-distillation for training the LCG is another notable strength. This approach leverages the LLM's own predictions as pseudo-targets, which is an efficient way to train the gate without requiring additional labeled data. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate that LCG significantly reduces language confusion across multiple models and tasks, often by an order of magnitude, while preserving or slightly improving task performance metrics like BLEU scores and accuracy. This is a crucial finding, as it shows that the LCG effectively addresses language confusion without compromising the quality of generated text. Furthermore, the authors have open-sourced specialized training and evaluation datasets, which is a valuable contribution to the research community. These datasets will facilitate further research in this area and enable other researchers to build upon the work presented in this paper. Finally, the paper's evaluation includes a variety of models and tasks, which demonstrates the robustness and generalizability of the LCG. The fact that the LCG performs well across different models and tasks increases its practical relevance and impact.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling solution to language confusion, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper initially lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of the LCG. While the authors claim the LCG is lightweight, the paper does not provide concrete metrics for inference speed and memory usage in the main body. Although the Discussion and Conclusion section mentions a minimal increase in inference time (0.4%) for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct 2507, this is a specific case and doesn't provide a comprehensive analysis of how the LCG's overhead scales with different model sizes or sequence lengths. The paper also does not provide a detailed memory usage analysis, which is a crucial factor for practical deployment, especially in resource-constrained environments. This lack of a thorough computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the LCG in various scenarios. Second, the paper does not explore alternative training methods for the LCG. The current approach relies solely on norm-adjusted self-distillation, which, while effective, might not be optimal. The paper does not discuss or investigate the potential benefits of other training methods, such as adversarial training or reinforcement learning. Adversarial training could enhance the robustness of the LCG by training it to handle more challenging language confusion scenarios, while reinforcement learning could be used to optimize the LCG's performance on specific tasks. The absence of such exploration limits the potential for further improvement of the LCG. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the failure cases of the LCG. While the authors acknowledge that the LCG reduces the frequency of legitimate code-switching and that the current approach is limited by its script-level granularity, the paper lacks a dedicated section or detailed analysis of specific scenarios where the LCG fails. For instance, the paper does not provide examples of language confusion scenarios that the LCG is unable to handle effectively, such as code-switching or the use of mixed-language text. This lack of a detailed failure analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the limitations of the LCG and identify areas for future improvement. Fourth, the current approach is limited by its script-level granularity. The LCG categorizes tokens into broad language families such as “Latin” or “Low-Res”, which may not be sufficient for nuanced language mixing scenarios. As the authors themselves acknowledge, this approach cannot resolve more nuanced confusion between languages that share the same script (e.g., Spanish vs. English) or between two different low-resource languages. This limitation could lead to over-filtering, potentially removing valid tokens in code-switching contexts. For example, in a sentence mixing English and Spanish, both using the Latin script, the LCG might incorrectly mask valid Spanish words when the context is predominantly English, and vice-versa. This limitation significantly impacts the LCG's ability to handle complex multilingual scenarios effectively. Finally, the evaluation of the LCG's impact on code-switching is not comprehensive. The paper primarily focuses on the frequency of code-switching, measured by the presence of Latin characters, and does not adequately assess the naturalness, fluency, and grammatical correctness of the generated code-switched text. The paper does not explore how the LCG handles complex code-switching scenarios where multiple languages are interleaved within a single sentence or paragraph, potentially leading to unnatural or grammatically incorrect outputs. This lack of a nuanced evaluation of code-switching limits the understanding of the LCG's real-world applicability in multilingual contexts.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the paper should include a more thorough analysis of the computational costs associated with the LCG. This analysis should go beyond simply stating that the LCG is lightweight and should provide concrete metrics, such as the percentage increase in inference time per token and the additional memory required for the LCG's parameters and computations. Furthermore, the analysis should consider how these costs scale with the size of the base language model and the length of the generated text. For example, does the overhead of the LCG become more significant for larger models or longer sequences? Providing such a detailed analysis would allow practitioners to make informed decisions about whether to deploy the LCG in resource-constrained environments. Second, the paper should explore alternative training methods for the LCG. While the current approach uses norm-adjusted self-distillation, it would be beneficial to investigate the effectiveness of adversarial training and reinforcement learning. Adversarial training could enhance the robustness of the LCG by training it to handle more challenging language confusion scenarios. For example, the LCG could be trained to distinguish between subtle differences in language usage that might be missed by the current approach. Reinforcement learning could be used to optimize the LCG's performance on specific tasks, such as translation or text summarization. This could be achieved by defining a reward function that encourages the LCG to reduce language confusion while maintaining or improving task performance. The paper should discuss the potential benefits and challenges of these alternative training methods and provide a rationale for the chosen approach. Third, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of the failure cases of the LCG. This analysis should provide specific examples of language confusion scenarios that the LCG is unable to handle effectively. For instance, does the LCG struggle with code-switching or the use of mixed-language text? Does it have difficulty with low-resource languages or specific language families? Understanding these failure cases would help to identify areas for future improvement and would provide a more complete picture of the LCG's capabilities. This analysis should also consider the potential impact of the training data on the LCG's performance. For example, if the training data does not include examples of certain types of language confusion, the LCG may not be able to handle them effectively. Fourth, to address the limitations of script-level granularity, future work should explore more fine-grained language identification techniques. This could involve training a language-specific gate or using a multi-label classification approach that predicts the probability of each language being used at a given token position. Such an approach would allow for more nuanced interventions, reducing the risk of over-filtering and preserving the naturalness of code-switched text. For example, instead of simply identifying a token as belonging to the 'Latin' script, the model could predict the specific language (e.g., English, Spanish, French) and apply masking only if the predicted language is inconsistent with the context. This would require a more sophisticated training dataset that includes examples of code-switching and mixed-language usage, which could be a valuable contribution in itself. Finally, the evaluation of the LCG's impact on code-switching should be significantly enhanced. The current evaluation focuses on the frequency of code-switching, but it does not adequately assess the quality and naturalness of the generated code-switched text. Future evaluations should include human evaluations of the fluency and naturalness of code-switched outputs, as well as metrics that capture the grammatical correctness of the generated text. This could involve using native speakers to rate the quality of the code-switched text on a Likert scale, or employing automated metrics that are sensitive to grammatical correctness. Additionally, the evaluation should include more complex code-switching scenarios, such as sentences with multiple language switches and paragraphs with interleaved languages, to fully assess the LCG's ability to handle real-world code-switching usage.

The paper provides some inference speed data for one specific model, but a more comprehensive analysis of the computational costs would be valuable for practical deployment.
