## Summary:

This paper introduces UniMoD, a task-aware token pruning method designed to enhance the computational efficiency of unified multimodal transformers. These models, which handle both generative and understanding tasks within a shared parameter space, often suffer from high computational costs due to redundant tokens and heavy attention mechanisms. UniMoD addresses this issue by employing separate routers for each task, allowing for selective token pruning based on task-specific importance. The method is evaluated on two representative unified transformers, Show-o and Emu3, demonstrating significant reductions in training FLOPs (15% for Show-o and 40% for Emu3) while maintaining or improving performance on various benchmarks. The paper's core contributions include a thorough empirical analysis of attention weights, layer importance, and task interactions, which motivates the design of UniMoD. The authors also provide a detailed methodology for task-aware pruning and demonstrate its effectiveness through extensive experiments. However, the paper's focus on only two models and the lack of a detailed analysis of the router's performance and limitations raise questions about the generalizability and robustness of the proposed method.

## Strengths:

I find several strengths in this paper that make it a valuable contribution to the field of multimodal transformers. Firstly, the introduction of UniMoD, a task-aware token pruning method, is a novel and significant advancement. By recognizing that token redundancy varies across different tasks and layers, the authors have developed a method that can selectively prune tokens, thereby improving computational efficiency without sacrificing performance. This task-specific approach is a clear improvement over generic pruning methods, which often fail to account for the unique characteristics of different tasks. Secondly, the empirical analysis conducted in the paper is thorough and insightful. The authors examine attention weights, layer importance, and task interactions, providing a detailed understanding of the behavior of unified transformers. These findings are crucial for designing effective pruning strategies and contribute to the broader understanding of how these models operate. The paper is also well-structured and clearly written, making complex concepts accessible to readers. The use of figures and tables to illustrate key points, such as the variations in attention weights and ARank values across layers and tasks, enhances the paper's readability and impact. Lastly, the method's effectiveness is demonstrated through significant reductions in training FLOPs, which is particularly valuable for training large-scale multimodal models. The authors show that UniMoD can achieve up to 40% reduction in FLOPs for Emu3 while maintaining or improving performance on various benchmarks, which is a substantial achievement.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and concerns are evident. One of the most significant issues is the lack of a detailed analysis of the task router's performance and its impact on overall model accuracy. The paper introduces the task-specific routers but does not provide quantitative metrics on their ability to identify and prune redundant tokens. For instance, in Section 4.1, the authors describe the task-aware MoD layer and the layer switch module but do not detail the router architecture or its training process. This omission makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the approach, especially in scenarios where the router might fail to accurately identify redundant tokens, potentially leading to performance degradation. The potential for cascading errors due to incorrect pruning is a valid concern that is not addressed with specific metrics or experiments. Another limitation is the requirement for finetuning from scratch on the target task. The experimental setup, as described in Section 5.1, involves finetuning the models on 8 H100 GPUs, which may limit the applicability of UniMoD in scenarios where finetuning resources are constrained. This requirement could be a significant drawback in practical applications where pre-trained models are often adapted to new tasks with limited computational resources. The paper does not explore the possibility of adapting UniMoD to existing pre-trained models without full finetuning, which would greatly enhance its practical utility. Furthermore, the paper primarily focuses on two specific unified transformer architectures, Show-o and Emu3. While these models are representative of unified multimodal transformers, the limited experimental scope raises questions about the generalizability of the findings. The paper does not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the proposed method would be equally effective on other architectures, particularly those with different layer depths, attention mechanisms, or pre-training datasets. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified limitations and strengthen the paper, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the task router's performance, including quantitative metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score for token pruning. This analysis should be conducted across different layers and tasks to understand the router's behavior and identify potential failure cases. Additionally, the authors should investigate the impact of different pruning thresholds on the overall performance of the model and visualize the attention maps of the router to provide valuable insights into its decision-making process. This would help to understand which tokens are being pruned and why, and whether the pruning is consistent with the importance of tokens as determined by the attention mechanism. Secondly, the authors should explore methods to adapt UniMoD to existing pre-trained models without requiring full finetuning. This could involve techniques such as adapter layers or prompt tuning, which have been shown to be effective in adapting pre-trained models to new tasks with limited computational resources. The paper should also investigate the possibility of using UniMoD as a regularization technique during finetuning, which could potentially improve the model's generalization performance. Thirdly, the authors should evaluate UniMoD on a wider range of unified transformer architectures and tasks to demonstrate its general applicability. This should include models with varying layer depths, attention mechanisms, and pre-training datasets. It is also important to consider models that use different tokenization strategies or have different ratios of generative to understanding components, as these factors could significantly impact the effectiveness of task-aware pruning. The authors should also explore the method's performance on more complex multimodal tasks, such as video understanding or audio processing, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its capabilities. Finally, the authors should provide a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art token pruning methods, including magnitude-based pruning and gradient-based pruning. This comparison should include not only performance metrics but also computational cost and memory usage. The authors should analyze the trade-offs between different pruning methods and discuss the specific scenarios where UniMoD offers the most significant advantages. This would help to contextualize the performance of UniMoD and highlight its unique contributions.
