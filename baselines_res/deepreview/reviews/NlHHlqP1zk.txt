## Summary:

This paper introduces the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework, a novel approach for evaluating the sufficiency of concept annotations in Explainable AI (XAI) without human supervision. The core idea revolves around simulating two modes of annotation: a 'fast' mode, which relies on opaque visual labeling without explicit conceptual reasoning, and a 'slow' mode, which employs a multi-step, conceptual coarse-to-fine annotation strategy. The framework progressively annotates concept-class test cases along a continuum from fast to slow, allowing for a systematic assessment of annotation sufficiency at each step. To quantify this sufficiency, the authors propose the Class Representation Index (CRI), a metric designed to measure how well the annotated concepts represent the target classes against semantically similar alternatives. The CRI is calculated by comparing the model's predictions based on the annotated concepts to the ground truth labels. The authors conduct extensive experiments on diverse datasets, including fine-grained datasets like CUB-Bird, Cars-196, and Flowers-102, as well as more general datasets like CIFAR-100 and Caltech-101. They utilize various large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) for annotation. The main empirical finding is that current automated annotation methods, particularly in fine-grained tasks, often lack sufficient semantic coverage for accurate concept-class mapping. The slow mode, which is intended to provide more detailed and accurate annotations, frequently underperforms compared to the fast mode on specialized datasets. Furthermore, the fusion approach, which combines both fast and slow modes, often performs similarly to the fast mode alone, suggesting that high performance in downstream tasks may not correlate with adequate conceptual supervision. The paper challenges the assumption that utility in downstream tasks is a reliable proxy for annotation sufficiency, highlighting the need for more robust annotation strategies. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of XAI by providing a framework for evaluating annotation quality and identifying limitations in current automated annotation methods.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The introduction of the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework is a novel and interesting approach to evaluating concept annotations. The idea of simulating fast and slow annotation modes to assess the sufficiency of conceptual reasoning is a creative way to tackle the problem of annotation quality in XAI. The framework's ability to operate without human supervision is a significant advantage, as it allows for scalable and objective evaluation. The Class Representation Index (CRI) is another notable contribution. It provides a quantitative metric for measuring how well concepts represent target classes, which is crucial for assessing the quality of annotations. The CRI moves beyond simple accuracy metrics by focusing on the semantic representation of the classes. The experiments are extensive and cover a wide range of datasets and models. The use of both fine-grained and general datasets allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the framework's performance. The inclusion of various LLMs and VLMs provides a robust assessment of the annotation quality across different models. The paper's findings are insightful and challenge the assumption that utility in downstream tasks is a reliable proxy for annotation sufficiency. The experiments demonstrate that high performance in downstream tasks does not necessarily guarantee adequate conceptual supervision, highlighting the need for more robust annotation strategies. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and findings. The paper's focus on the limitations of current automated annotation methods is important for the field of XAI, as it highlights the need for more robust and semantically meaningful annotations. The paper's overall contribution is significant, as it provides a framework for evaluating annotation quality and identifying deficiencies in current annotation strategies.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a novel framework and insightful findings, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further attention. Firstly, while the paper does provide a motivation for the FSE framework, the explanation of the connection between the 'fast' and 'slow' modes and their relevance to assessing annotation quality could be more explicit. The paper states that the fast mode represents a baseline and the slow mode aims to capture more nuanced, concept-based reasoning, but a more detailed explanation of how this contrast directly relates to assessing annotation quality is needed. The paper could benefit from a more concrete example of how the FSE framework would be applied in practice, including a step-by-step walkthrough of the annotation process and how the CRI metric is used to assess the sufficiency of the annotations. This would help clarify the practical implications of the framework and its potential benefits. Secondly, while the mathematical formulation of the Class Representation Index (CRI) is provided, the explanation of the indicator function and the averaging over 't' instances could be more detailed. The paper states that CRI measures the proportion of correctly predicted labels compared to ground truth, but a more thorough explanation of the underlying assumptions and limitations of this metric is needed. The paper should also discuss the sensitivity of the CRI to different types of annotations, and how this sensitivity might affect the interpretation of the results. For example, the paper could investigate how the CRI metric behaves when the concept sets are incomplete or when the concepts are not perfectly aligned with the class labels. Thirdly, the paper lacks specific details on the hyperparameter settings used for the LLMs and VLMs. While the paper mentions running experiments with different seeds and reporting standard deviations, the absence of specific hyperparameter settings makes it difficult to reproduce the experiments. This lack of detail also makes it challenging to assess the validity of the claims made in the paper. The paper should include a detailed description of the specific prompts used for the LLMs, the hyperparameter settings, and the statistical significance of the results. Fourthly, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the FSE framework. Understanding the computational requirements is crucial for assessing the framework's scalability and practicality, especially when applied to large datasets or complex models. The analysis should include a breakdown of the time and space complexity for each step of the framework, such as the fast and slow annotation processes, and the calculation of the CRI. This should also consider the impact of different model sizes and input data dimensions on the overall computational cost. Fifthly, the paper does not adequately discuss the potential biases that may be present in the LLMs and VLMs used for annotation. Since these models are trained on large datasets, they may inherit biases that could affect the quality and fairness of the annotations. The paper should explore how these biases might influence the results of the FSE framework, particularly in sensitive domains or when dealing with underrepresented groups. It should also discuss methods for mitigating these biases, such as using debiasing techniques or incorporating fairness constraints into the annotation process. Sixthly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the FSE framework to different hyperparameters or model configurations. Understanding how the framework's performance varies with different settings is crucial for its practical application. The paper should include a sensitivity analysis that explores the impact of parameters such as the number of concepts used for annotation, the temperature parameter for sampling, and the specific architectures of the LLMs and VLMs. This analysis should also consider the interaction between different hyperparameters and their combined effect on the framework's performance. Finally, while the paper motivates the FSE framework by highlighting the shortcomings of existing methods, it lacks a direct quantitative comparison with other evaluation metrics. The paper should discuss how the FSE framework and CRI metric compare to other approaches in terms of their ability to capture the semantic sufficiency of annotations. For example, the authors could compare their approach to methods that use human annotations as a gold standard or to methods that rely on automatic evaluation metrics based on concept similarity. A detailed comparison would help to establish the novelty and significance of the proposed approach. These weaknesses, while not invalidating the paper's contributions, highlight areas where further work is needed to strengthen the framework and its evaluation.
