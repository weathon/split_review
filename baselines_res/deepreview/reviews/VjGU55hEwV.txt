## Summary:

This paper introduces RLIE, a novel framework that integrates the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) with probabilistic methods for rule learning and combination. The core idea is to leverage LLMs to generate rules expressed in natural language, which are then evaluated and combined using logistic regression. The framework operates in four stages: rule generation, logistic regression, iterative refinement, and evaluation. In the rule generation phase, the LLM is prompted to produce rules based on the input data. These rules are then evaluated by the LLM itself, which provides a ternary output indicating whether a rule is satisfied, not satisfied, or inapplicable for a given data point. The logistic regression stage learns weights for the generated rules, effectively combining them into a cohesive model. The iterative refinement stage uses the errors from the logistic regression model to prompt the LLM to generate new, more effective rules, thereby iteratively improving the overall model. Finally, the evaluation stage assesses the performance of the learned rule set. The authors evaluate RLIE on six real-world binary classification tasks from the HypoBench benchmark, comparing its performance against several baselines. The results demonstrate that RLIE achieves competitive performance, with the direct application of rules and their corresponding weights yielding superior results compared to methods that inject rules into the LLM. The paper highlights the potential of combining LLMs with probabilistic methods for rule learning, offering a more robust and flexible approach than existing methods that often overlook the combination effects of rules. The authors also emphasize the semantic clarity of the learned rule sets, which facilitates knowledge discovery and human-AI consensus. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically supported framework that advances the field of rule learning by effectively integrating the strengths of LLMs and probabilistic modeling.

## Strengths:

I find several strengths in this paper, primarily in its novel approach and empirical validation. The core contribution of RLIE lies in its effective integration of LLMs with probabilistic methods for rule learning. This is a significant step forward, as it addresses the limitations of existing methods that often treat rules as independent entities or focus on the iterative optimization of single rules. The use of logistic regression to combine rules, each evaluated by an LLM, allows for a more nuanced and robust approach to rule-based classification. The iterative refinement process, where the LLM is prompted to generate new rules based on the errors of the logistic regression model, is another notable strength. This process allows the framework to learn from its mistakes and iteratively improve its performance. The empirical evaluation of RLIE on six real-world binary classification tasks from the HypoBench benchmark is also a significant strength. The results demonstrate that RLIE achieves competitive performance compared to several baselines, and the direct application of rules with their corresponding weights outperforms methods that inject rules into the LLM. This finding highlights the importance of the probabilistic combination of rules, which is a key aspect of the RLIE framework. Furthermore, the authors emphasize the semantic clarity of the learned rule sets, which is a valuable contribution. The fact that the rules are expressed in natural language makes them more interpretable and accessible, facilitating knowledge discovery and human-AI consensus. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which enhances its overall impact. The authors clearly articulate the motivation for their work, the details of their method, and the implications of their findings. Finally, the inclusion of a detailed case study in the appendix, which provides examples of the generated rules, further strengthens the paper's claims about the interpretability and effectiveness of the RLIE framework.

While the paper mentions that the LLM provides a ternary output (-1, 0, +1) indicating whether a rule is satisfied, not satisfied, or inapplicable, it does not provide the specific prompts used to elicit this behavior. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the rule evaluation process. The paper states that a '0' signifies the rule is not applicable, but it does not explain how the LLM determines this, nor does it discuss how the abstain option is handled during training and evaluation. This is a critical detail, as the abstain option is crucial for explicitly modeling rule coverage and enabling sparse, robust combinations. The absence of this information in the main body of the paper, with only a reference to Appendix E, makes it harder to understand the exact mechanism and reliability of the LLM's judgment. Furthermore, the paper should clarify how the abstain option (0) is handled during training and evaluation. For instance, is the LLM's confidence in its abstain decision considered? How does the framework deal with situations where the LLM frequently abstains on certain rules? A more detailed analysis of the LLM's behavior in these cases would significantly improve the understanding of the method's robustness and reliability. The authors should also discuss the potential biases introduced by the LLM's judgment and how these biases might affect the overall performance of the framework. This discussion should include an analysis of how the LLM's performance varies across different types of rules and datasets.
