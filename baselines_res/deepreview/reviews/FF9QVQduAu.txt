## Summary:

This paper introduces CrowdFM, a novel foundation model for crowdsourced label aggregation, addressing the challenge of inferring accurate ground truth from noisy, crowdsourced data. The authors argue that traditional dataset-specific parameter estimation methods are not scalable and fail to transfer knowledge between datasets. They also point out that recent universal aggregation models do not fully capture the complexities of human-annotated crowdsourcing, leading to suboptimal real-world performance. To overcome these limitations, CrowdFM is designed as a bipartite graph neural network (GNN) pre-trained on a large, domain-randomized synthetic dataset. This pre-training process allows the model to learn diverse behavioral patterns and universal principles of collective intelligence. The model employs a size-invariant initialization and attention-based message passing to effectively capture relationships between workers and tasks. The authors claim that their single, fixed model consistently matches or surpasses bespoke, per-dataset methods in both accuracy and efficiency across 22 real-world benchmarks. Furthermore, the representations learned by CrowdFM are said to readily support diverse downstream applications, such as worker assessment and task assignment. The core idea is to leverage a foundation model that can generalize across different crowdsourcing scenarios without requiring dataset-specific training. The authors emphasize the retraining-free nature of CrowdFM, which contrasts with existing methods that often require extensive parameter tuning for each new dataset. The paper presents extensive experimental results on 22 real-world datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed model. The results show that CrowdFM achieves competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art methods, often with lower computational cost. The authors also provide an analysis of the model's performance on datasets with different characteristics, showing its robustness and generalization capabilities. The paper makes a significant contribution by proposing a foundation model for crowdsourced label aggregation, which has the potential to significantly improve the efficiency and accuracy of crowdsourced data labeling. The use of a synthetic pre-training dataset is a key aspect of the approach, allowing the model to learn generalizable patterns that can be applied to a variety of real-world scenarios. The paper's findings suggest that foundation models can be a promising direction for addressing the challenges of crowdsourced label aggregation.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall impact and significance. First and foremost, the introduction of CrowdFM as a foundation model for crowdsourced label aggregation is a novel and compelling contribution. The concept of a foundation model in this domain is relatively new, and the authors provide a clear and well-motivated rationale for its development. They effectively highlight the limitations of existing approaches, particularly the lack of scalability and generalization of dataset-specific methods, and the failure of recent universal aggregation models to fully capture the complexities of human-annotated crowdsourcing. The proposed model, a bipartite graph neural network pre-trained on a large, domain-randomized synthetic dataset, is a technically innovative approach that addresses these limitations. The use of a size-invariant initialization and attention-based message passing allows the model to effectively learn universal principles of collective intelligence. The authors' emphasis on the retraining-free nature of CrowdFM is another significant strength, as it contrasts with existing methods that often require extensive parameter tuning for each new dataset. The extensive experimental evaluation on 22 real-world benchmarks is a major strength of the paper. This comprehensive evaluation demonstrates the robustness and generalizability of the proposed model across diverse datasets and scenarios. The results show that CrowdFM consistently matches or surpasses bespoke, per-dataset methods in both accuracy and efficiency, providing strong empirical support for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the paper is well-organized and easy to follow. The authors provide a clear problem formulation, detailed methodology, and comprehensive experimental results. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and findings. The authors also make their code publicly available, which enhances the reproducibility of their results and facilitates further research in this area. The paper's focus on a practical problem with a novel and effective solution makes it a valuable contribution to the field of crowdsourced label aggregation. The combination of a technically sound approach, extensive empirical validation, and clear presentation makes this paper a significant contribution.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to crowdsourced label aggregation, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. One significant limitation is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed model. While the paper describes the GNN structure and attention-based encoder, it does not provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of these components. The paper mentions a 'size-invariant initialization' which hints at scalability, but lacks a theoretical breakdown of the computational cost in terms of the number of workers, tasks, and the graph structure. This absence of a formal complexity analysis makes it difficult to theoretically understand the scalability of CrowdFM, which is a crucial aspect given the paper's emphasis on handling large-scale datasets. The paper does provide empirical runtime results in Table 1, but this is insufficient to fully assess the model's computational efficiency. This lack of theoretical analysis is a significant oversight, as it limits our understanding of the model's practical applicability and scalability. Another notable weakness is the absence of a dedicated 'Limitations' section in the main body of the paper. While the authors touch upon the model's performance on diverse datasets and acknowledge domain shifts, a more thorough discussion of the model's limitations is needed. Specifically, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the model's sensitivity to the quality and diversity of the synthetic pre-training data. The performance of CrowdFM is likely contingent on the synthetic data accurately reflecting the complexities of real-world crowdsourcing scenarios, and this dependency should be explored more thoroughly. The paper does include an ablation study in Section 4.4 that removes the synthetic data generator, showing a performance drop, and Appendix G provides some experiments on varying synthetic dataset configurations. However, a more in-depth analysis of how different parameters of the synthetic data generation process affect the model's performance is needed. Furthermore, the paper does not explicitly discuss the computational cost associated with pre-training the model, which could be a barrier to adoption for some researchers. This lack of discussion on the limitations of the model and its pre-training process is a significant oversight that needs to be addressed. Finally, while the paper provides a quantitative comparison of CrowdFM with several state-of-the-art methods in Table 1, it lacks a detailed qualitative discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of each method. The paper does discuss the performance of CrowdFM relative to these baselines, highlighting strengths and weaknesses in terms of accuracy and runtime. However, a more in-depth analysis of the model's performance, including a breakdown of its performance on different types of tasks and datasets, and a comparison of its computational cost and scalability with existing methods, is needed. The paper should also clarify how the proposed model addresses the limitations of existing methods, such as the need for dataset-specific training or the inability to handle complex annotation patterns. While the paper does implicitly define a foundation model as one that generalizes across datasets without retraining, a more explicit definition of the expected properties of a foundation model in this context would be beneficial. The paper's lack of a detailed discussion of these limitations and comparisons weakens the overall impact of the work. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the absence of specific analyses and discussions within the paper's main body.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more rigorous analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed CrowdFM model. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the time and space complexity of the model, considering the number of workers, tasks, and the graph structure. The authors should provide a formal analysis of the number of operations required for each layer of the GNN and the attention mechanism, using Big O notation to express the complexity. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to provide empirical results on the training and inference time of the model on datasets of varying sizes to validate the theoretical analysis. This would help readers understand the practical scalability of the model and its suitability for different application scenarios. Second, the authors should include a dedicated 'Limitations' section in the main body of the paper. This section should provide a thorough discussion of the model's limitations, including its sensitivity to the quality and diversity of the synthetic pre-training data. The authors should conduct experiments to analyze how different parameters of the synthetic data generation process affect the model's performance on real-world datasets. This analysis should include a discussion of the potential for bias in the synthetic data and how this might affect the model's performance on real-world datasets. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the computational cost associated with pre-training the model, including the time and resources required, and explore techniques for reducing the computational cost, such as using more efficient optimization algorithms or reducing the size of the pre-training dataset. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of the proposed model with other state-of-the-art methods. This comparison should not only focus on overall performance but also consider other factors such as computational cost, robustness to noise, and interpretability. The authors should provide a clear explanation of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, highlighting the specific scenarios where each method performs best. For example, the authors could compare the proposed model with methods that use different aggregation techniques, such as majority voting or Bayesian methods. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the landscape of label aggregation methods and help readers choose the most appropriate method for their specific needs. Additionally, the authors should provide a more explicit definition of the expected properties of a foundation model in the context of crowdsourced label aggregation. This definition should articulate the expected capabilities, such as the ability to generalize across diverse datasets, handle varying levels of noise, and adapt to different task complexities without requiring extensive retraining. The authors should then explicitly demonstrate how their proposed model fulfills these criteria, providing concrete examples and evidence. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the reasons for the observed performance improvements. This should include an ablation study to determine the contribution of different components of the model, such as the pre-training strategy, the graph neural network architecture, and the loss function. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of the evaluation metrics used and how these limitations might affect the interpretation of the results. For example, if the datasets are imbalanced, the authors should consider using metrics such as F1-score or area under the ROC curve in addition to accuracy. These suggestions are aimed at providing a more complete and balanced view of the proposed model and its applicability.


## Questions:

Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how does the model handle noisy or unreliable annotations from workers? While the paper demonstrates strong performance on real-world datasets, it does not explicitly analyze the model's robustness to varying levels of noise in the annotations. It would be beneficial to understand how the model's performance degrades as the noise level increases and what mechanisms are in place to mitigate the impact of unreliable annotations. Second, how does the model perform on datasets with very different characteristics than those used in the experiments? The paper evaluates the model on 22 real-world datasets, but it is unclear how the model would perform on datasets with significantly different characteristics, such as those with a much larger number of workers, tasks, or categories, or those with different annotation protocols. It would be helpful to see an analysis of the model's performance on such datasets to better understand its generalization capabilities. Third, how does the model compare to other state-of-the-art methods in terms of computational cost and scalability? While the paper provides some runtime results, a more detailed comparison of the computational cost of the proposed model with existing methods is needed. This comparison should include an analysis of the time and memory requirements of each method, as well as their scalability to larger datasets. Fourth, what is the impact of the synthetic data generation process on the model's performance? The paper uses a domain-randomized synthetic dataset for pre-training, but it is unclear how the specific parameters of this process affect the model's performance on real-world datasets. It would be helpful to see a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the model to different aspects of the synthetic data generation process. Finally, what are the potential failure modes of the model? It is important to understand the conditions under which the model might not perform well. This could include scenarios with very unreliable workers, tasks that require specialized knowledge, or datasets with complex annotation patterns. Understanding these limitations is crucial for the practical application of the model. These questions are aimed at clarifying key uncertainties and limitations of the proposed model, and they are essential for a comprehensive understanding of its capabilities and applicability.
