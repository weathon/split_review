## Summary:

This paper tackles the increasingly pertinent issue of desk rejection policies at academic conferences, specifically those arising from per-author submission limits. The authors propose a novel optimization-based approach to minimize unnecessary desk rejections while adhering to these limits, framing the problem as a mathematical optimization problem. Their method seeks to maximize the number of papers accepted for review, subject to the constraint that no author exceeds their submission quota. The core of their approach involves developing an efficient algorithm to solve this optimization problem, which they then evaluate using real-world data from the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR). The authors model the desk-rejection process as a mathematical optimization problem and develop an efficient algorithm that significantly reduces needless rejections compared to current policies. The method is validated using 11 years of real-world ICLR submission data. The paper's main contribution lies in its formulation of the desk-rejection problem as an optimization problem and the development of an efficient algorithm to solve it. The authors demonstrate that their method significantly reduces the number of desk-rejected papers compared to existing policies, which typically reject papers based on author ID order. The paper is well-written and clearly explains the problem and the proposed solution. The method is evaluated on real-world data from ICLR, showing that it can significantly reduce unnecessary desk rejections. The algorithm is computationally efficient, making it practical for large-scale conferences. The authors also provide a thorough theoretical analysis of their method, including proofs of correctness and time complexity. However, the paper's evaluation is primarily focused on reducing desk rejections, without a comprehensive analysis of the impact on review quality, reviewer workload, or overall conference outcomes. The paper also relies on the accuracy of the authorship matrix, which may not always be complete or accurate in practice. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss potential ethical implications or biases that could arise from using an optimization-based desk-rejection system. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field by introducing a new optimization framework for desk-rejection policies and demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing unnecessary desk rejections.

## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to minimizing desk rejections, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison to other potential optimization approaches or desk-rejection policies. While the authors compare their method to existing, simplistic desk-rejection policies (Algorithm 1 and 2), they do not explore or compare against simpler heuristic methods, such as a greedy algorithm that iteratively accepts papers until the submission limit is reached for each author. This absence of comparison makes it difficult to assess whether the proposed optimization approach provides a significant advantage over simpler, potentially less computationally intensive methods. Furthermore, the paper does not explore alternative optimization objectives, such as maximizing the diversity of accepted papers or prioritizing papers based on estimated quality. The paper's focus on maximizing the number of accepted papers, while seemingly straightforward, may not align with the broader goals of a conference, which might include ensuring a diverse set of topics or prioritizing high-quality submissions. The lack of exploration of these alternative objectives is a notable limitation. Second, the paper does not explore the impact of its method on the overall quality of accepted papers or the review process. The paper focuses solely on reducing desk rejections, but it does not address whether this leads to a higher workload for reviewers or a decrease in the overall quality of accepted papers. It is possible that by accepting more papers, the review process becomes more burdensome, potentially leading to less thorough reviews and a lower quality conference. A critical analysis of the trade-offs between reducing desk rejections and maintaining review quality is missing. The paper's objective function (Definition 4.1) is solely to maximize the number of desk-accepted papers, with no explicit consideration of paper quality or review workload. The experiments also focus on the number of desk-rejected papers as the primary metric, with no experiments or metrics related to review quality or reviewer workload. This lack of evaluation of the downstream effects on the review process is a significant weakness. Third, the method relies on historical data and may not generalize well to conferences with different submission patterns or author networks. The paper's evaluation is limited to ICLR data, and it doesn't provide evidence or discussion on the algorithm's robustness to different submission patterns or author networks. The algorithm's performance is inherently tied to the structure of the input authorship matrix, and it is unclear how the algorithm would perform if the number of submissions per author or the size of author groups changed significantly. A robustness analysis under different scenarios is needed. The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of the proposed method to changes in the distribution of author submissions or the structure of author collaborations. Fourth, the paper lacks empirical evaluation of the proposed method's impact on review quality or overall conference outcomes. The paper's evaluation is limited to the number of desk rejections and does not empirically assess the impact on review quality or broader conference outcomes. The authors should have considered conducting simulations or pilot studies using historical conference data to evaluate how their method affects the distribution of reviews per paper, the overall quality of accepted papers, and the diversity of accepted authors. Finally, the method's effectiveness may depend on the accuracy of the authorship matrix A, which could be incomplete or inaccurate in practice. The paper assumes that this matrix is complete and accurate, but in practice, this may not always be the case. Authorship information can be incomplete or incorrect due to various reasons, such as errors in data entry, missing affiliations, or intentional misrepresentation. The authors should have discussed the potential impact of such inaccuracies on the performance of their method. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss potential ethical implications or biases that could arise from using an optimization-based desk-rejection system. While the authors claim that their method is neutral, the optimization process itself could inadvertently amplify existing biases in the submission data. For example, if certain groups of authors are systematically underrepresented in the submission data, the optimization algorithm might learn to deprioritize their submissions. The authors should have conducted a thorough analysis of potential biases and discussed how they could be mitigated. These limitations collectively highlight the need for a more comprehensive evaluation and analysis of the proposed method.

This could be done by incorporating additional constraints or modifying the objective function. For example, a diversity metric could be added to the objective function to encourage the acceptance of papers from different research areas or with different methodologies. The authors could also explore using a proxy for paper quality, such as the number of citations of the authors' previous work, to prioritize the acceptance of higher-quality papers. Second, the paper needs to address the potential impact of the proposed method on the review process. The authors should investigate whether reducing desk rejections leads to a higher workload for reviewers or a decrease in the overall quality of accepted papers. This could be done by simulating the review process under different desk-rejection policies and analyzing the distribution of reviews per paper and the quality of the reviews. The authors could also survey reviewers to gather feedback on the workload and the quality of the review process under different desk-rejection policies. A critical analysis of the trade-offs between reducing desk rejections and maintaining review quality is essential for the practical adoption of the proposed method. The authors should also consider the potential for bias in the review process if certain types of papers or authors are more likely to be desk-rejected. Third, the paper should include a robustness analysis to assess the sensitivity of the proposed method to changes in the distribution of author submissions or the structure of author collaborations. The authors could simulate different scenarios, such as an increase in the number of submissions per author or a change in the size of author groups, and evaluate the performance of the proposed method under these scenarios. This would help to determine whether the method is generalizable to conferences with different submission patterns or author networks. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the proposed method and identify areas for future research. For example, the authors could explore the use of machine learning techniques to predict the quality of submissions and incorporate this information into the optimization process. They could explore methods to mitigate the impact of incomplete data, such as using probabilistic models or incorporating uncertainty into the optimization framework. Furthermore, they should discuss the potential for manipulation of the system by authors who are not listed in the authorship matrix, as this could lead to unfair advantages. Finally, the paper needs to address the potential ethical implications and biases that could arise from using an optimization-based desk-rejection system. The authors should conduct a thorough analysis of potential biases and discuss how they could be mitigated. This could involve techniques such as fairness-aware optimization or post-processing adjustments to ensure that the system is equitable for all authors. Furthermore, they should consider the potential for gaming the system, where authors might strategically modify their submissions to increase their chances of acceptance. A discussion of these ethical considerations is crucial for the responsible deployment of the proposed method. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their work and increase its practical impact.
