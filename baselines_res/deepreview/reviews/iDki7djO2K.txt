## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel theoretical framework for understanding and quantifying forgetting in machine learning, defining it as a lack of self-consistency in a learner's predictive distribution over future experiences. The authors propose a measure called the "propensity to forget," which quantifies the extent to which a learner's predictive distribution changes after updates. This measure is grounded in a rigorous mathematical formulation, using concepts from measure theory to define the learner's state and predictive distributions. The core idea is that forgetting occurs when a learner's predictions about future experiences become inconsistent with its previous predictions, even when those experiences are consistent with its own prior expectations. The authors validate their framework through a series of experiments across various learning paradigms, including regression, classification, generative modeling, continual learning, and reinforcement learning. These experiments demonstrate that forgetting, as measured by their proposed metric, is a pervasive phenomenon that impacts learning efficiency. They also show that optimal learning often involves a balance between adaptation and retention, with the propensity to forget varying across different learning scenarios. The paper's main contribution lies in providing a unified, algorithm- and task-agnostic definition of forgetting, which allows for a more general understanding of this phenomenon across different learning settings. The authors argue that existing measures of forgetting, particularly in continual learning, often conflate forgetting with backward transfer, making it difficult to distinguish between constructive and destructive adaptation. By focusing on the self-consistency of predictive distributions, their framework aims to provide a more fine-grained and accurate measure of forgetting. The empirical findings support the validity of the proposed measure, showing that it captures the intuitive notion of forgetting as a deviation from self-consistency. The paper's theoretical framework and empirical validation provide a valuable contribution to the field by offering a new perspective on forgetting and a general measure that can be applied across various learning paradigms. However, the paper also has limitations, particularly in its lack of practical applications and its reliance on relatively simple models and datasets. Despite these limitations, the paper's theoretical framework and empirical validation provide a valuable contribution to the field by offering a new perspective on forgetting and a general measure that can be applied across various learning paradigms.

## Strengths:

This paper presents a significant contribution to the field by introducing a novel, algorithm- and task-agnostic framework for understanding and quantifying forgetting in machine learning. The core strength lies in the originality of defining forgetting as a lack of self-consistency in a learner's predictive distribution over future experiences. This perspective is a departure from traditional views that often focus on the loss of specific information or the degradation of performance on a particular task. By framing forgetting as a violation of self-consistency, the authors provide a more general and nuanced understanding of this phenomenon. The theoretical foundation is well-developed, with clear definitions and a rigorous mathematical formulation. The concept of "propensity to forget," derived from this framework, is a valuable contribution that allows for quantitative analysis of forgetting. The use of measure theory, while potentially complex for some readers, provides a solid foundation for the proposed measure. The paper's empirical validation is another significant strength. The authors conduct a comprehensive set of experiments across multiple learning paradigms, including regression, classification, generative modeling, continual learning, and reinforcement learning. These experiments demonstrate the pervasiveness of forgetting and the validity of the proposed measure. The experiments are well-designed and provide strong support for the theoretical framework, showing that the propensity to forget is consistently non-zero across various settings. The paper is also well-written and clearly structured, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide intuitive explanations and visualizations that aid in understanding the complex concepts. The paper's focus on a general definition of forgetting, applicable across different learning paradigms, is a valuable contribution. This approach addresses the limitations of existing measures, particularly in continual learning, which often conflate forgetting with backward transfer. The authors' framework provides a more fine-grained and accurate measure of forgetting, allowing for a clearer distinction between constructive and destructive adaptation. The empirical results, showing the trade-off between forgetting and training efficiency, are also noteworthy. These findings suggest that optimal learning often involves a balance between adapting to new information and retaining previously acquired knowledge. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel theoretical framework, rigorous mathematical formulation, comprehensive empirical validation, and clear presentation.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a novel and valuable framework for understanding forgetting, several weaknesses limit its immediate practical applicability and the strength of its conclusions. One significant limitation is the lack of practical applications or case studies demonstrating how the proposed framework can be used to develop methods for mitigating forgetting in real-world scenarios. The paper focuses primarily on defining and measuring forgetting, with experiments validating the measure's ability to capture forgetting dynamics. However, there is no explicit discussion or experimentation on using the framework to develop or evaluate mitigation techniques. This limits the immediate impact of the work on practitioners looking for solutions to forgetting. The paper's experiments, while comprehensive, are primarily conducted on synthetic datasets and relatively simple models. The regression, classification, and generative modeling tasks use shallow neural networks trained on synthetic datasets of 1000 points. The class-incremental learning task uses a single-layer neural network on a two-moons dataset. The reinforcement learning task uses a DQN agent on the CartPole environment. While these experiments demonstrate the pervasiveness of forgetting, it is unclear how the findings would generalize to more complex, real-world datasets and architectures, particularly in domains like natural language processing or computer vision. This reliance on simple datasets and models raises questions about the generalizability of the findings. Another crucial weakness is the absence of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of calculating the "propensity to forget." The measure involves computing divergences between predictive distributions, which can be computationally expensive for large models and datasets. The paper does not provide any theoretical bounds or empirical measurements of the computational cost, which is a crucial aspect for practical applications. The framework assumes that the learner's predictive distribution accurately reflects its state. However, in practice, many learning algorithms use approximations or heuristics that may not perfectly align with this assumption. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the "Scope and boundary of validity" section, stating that the formalism applies when the learner's predictive distribution accurately represents its state. However, the paper does not adequately address how these approximations might affect the validity of the "propensity to forget" measure. This is a significant concern, as many common learning algorithms use techniques like variational inference or dropout, which could impact the accuracy of the predictive distribution. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the relationship between the proposed framework and existing methods for mitigating forgetting, such as regularization techniques or replay buffers. A discussion of how the "propensity to forget" measure can be used to guide the development or evaluation of these methods would strengthen the paper's practical relevance. The paper introduces a novel measure without a detailed comparison to existing ones. While the "Related Work" section discusses existing metrics, it does not provide a detailed comparative analysis of their strengths and weaknesses relative to the proposed measure. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed measure. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed theory and measure of forgetting. While the "Scope and boundary of validity" section acknowledges some limitations, such as the assumption that the predictive distribution accurately reflects the learner's state, it does not explicitly discuss limitations related to non-stationary data distributions or poorly calibrated predictive distributions. This lack of a comprehensive discussion of limitations leaves open questions about the robustness and applicability of the framework in various scenarios. The presentation of the proposed theory is unnecessarily complex. The use of measure theory and information about the learner’s state that is not directly observed makes the theory difficult to understand. The core concept of self-consistency could be expressed more simply, without the need for such formalism. The paper would benefit from a more intuitive explanation of the theoretical framework, focusing on the practical implications of the proposed measure of forgetting. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear demonstration of how the proposed measure correlates with or explains observable phenomena in learning systems. The experiments measure the proposed measure in a variety of settings, but do not show what is interesting about the resulting values. The paper lacks a clear demonstration of how the proposed measure correlates with or explains observable phenomena in learning systems.
