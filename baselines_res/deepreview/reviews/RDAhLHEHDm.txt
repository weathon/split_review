## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel paradigm for integrating biomolecular data into Scientific Large Language Models (Sci-LLMs), addressing what the authors term the 'tokenization dilemma.' Instead of treating sequences as either a specialized language or a separate modality, the authors propose a context-driven approach that leverages high-level structured context derived from established bioinformatics tools. This approach aims to provide Sci-LLMs with expert knowledge, allowing them to reason more effectively about biological sequences. The core idea is to transform raw biomolecular sequences into a textual context using tools like InterProScan, BLASTp, and ProTrek, which extract information about sequence homology, conserved domains, and functional terms. The authors argue that Sci-LLMs are better suited for reasoning over structured knowledge than for interpreting raw sequences directly. They demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through a series of experiments on protein sequences, comparing the performance of Sci-LLMs when provided with sequence-only input, context-only input, and a combination of both. The results consistently show that the context-only approach outperforms the other input modes, suggesting that Sci-LLMs excel at reasoning over structured knowledge. This finding reframes Sci-LLMs as powerful reasoning engines over expert knowledge, rather than as sequence decoders. The paper's key contribution is the introduction of a new paradigm for integrating biomolecular data into Sci-LLMs, which has significant implications for the development of these models and their applications in biological research. By shifting the focus from sequence interpretation to knowledge synthesis, the authors open up new avenues for leveraging Sci-LLMs in various scientific domains. The paper also includes a quantitative analysis of real-world efficiency, demonstrating that the context-driven approach is not only more accurate but also computationally efficient compared to traditional sequence-based methods. This work lays the foundation for a new class of hybrid scientific AI agents focused on high-level knowledge synthesis, and it challenges the conventional wisdom of how biomolecular data should be processed by large language models. The authors' approach of using existing bioinformatics tools to generate context is both practical and effective, providing a clear path for researchers to improve the performance of Sci-LLMs in biological applications. The paper's findings suggest that the future of Sci-LLMs may lie more in their ability to reason over expert knowledge than in their ability to directly interpret raw biological sequences.

## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a compelling approach to integrating biomolecular data into Sci-LLMs, several limitations warrant careful consideration. One significant weakness is the method's reliance on existing bioinformatics tools for generating high-level structured context. As the authors themselves acknowledge, this dependence could limit the approach's ability to discover novel biomolecular patterns. Since the context is constrained by the capabilities and biases of these pre-existing tools, the model may struggle to identify truly novel insights. For instance, if a new type of RNA structure is present but not recognized by the tools, the context will not include this information, thus limiting the model's ability to identify it. This is a valid concern, as the paper explicitly states that it uses tools like InterProScan, BLASTp, and ProTrek to generate the context (Section 1, Introduction and Method). The experimental focus on established protein biology tasks further supports this concern, as these tasks are well-covered by existing bioinformatics tools. This limitation is significant because it suggests that the context-driven approach may not be suitable for exploring truly novel biological phenomena. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's methodology and experimental design. Another key weakness is the limited generalizability of the proposed approach to other types of biomolecular data. The paper primarily focuses on protein sequences, and it is unclear how well the method would generalize to other types of biomolecules, such as DNA or RNA sequences. The context generation pipeline is specifically designed for protein sequences, using tools like InterProScan and BLASTp, which are specific to protein analysis. All experiments described in the paper use protein sequences, and the 'Wet-lab Validation on Novel Sequences' section also uses protein sequences (Rhodopsin and PETase). The paper lacks any discussion or experiments on applying the method to DNA or RNA. This is a significant limitation because it restricts the applicability of the method to a specific type of biomolecular data. The lack of experiments on other biomolecular data raises concerns about the method's generalizability and its potential for broader applications in biological research. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's method description and experimental design. Finally, the paper does not extensively discuss the potential limitations of relying on established bioinformatics tools for generating context. The accuracy and completeness of the context are directly dependent on the performance of these tools, which may introduce biases or errors. The paper mentions the use of 'established bioinformatics tools' but doesn't delve into their limitations. While the paper shows strong performance, it doesn't explicitly address how errors or biases in the context generation might affect the results. This is a valid concern, as the quality of the context directly impacts the performance of the Sci-LLMs. If a particular bioinformatics tool has a low sensitivity for a specific type of protein domain, the context generated for proteins containing that domain might be incomplete or inaccurate. This could lead to suboptimal performance of the Sci-LLMs. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is a logical consequence of the proposed method and is not adequately addressed in the paper.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, to mitigate the limitation of relying solely on existing bioinformatics tools, the authors should explore methods for integrating novel pattern discovery directly into the context generation process. This could involve training smaller, specialized models to identify patterns not captured by existing tools and incorporating their outputs into the context. For example, a model could be trained to identify novel motifs or structural features in sequences, and these features could be included in the context provided to the Sci-LLM. This would allow the approach to move beyond the constraints of existing tools and potentially uncover new biological insights. This could involve using techniques like unsupervised learning or deep learning to identify patterns in the data that are not already known. The authors could also explore methods for incorporating uncertainty into the context, so that the Sci-LLM is aware of the limitations of the context. This would allow the model to be more cautious when making predictions based on uncertain context. Second, to address the limited generalizability of the approach, the authors should conduct experiments on other types of biomolecular data, such as DNA and RNA sequences. This would involve adapting their context generation pipeline to incorporate relevant bioinformatics tools for these modalities. For example, they could use tools for identifying regulatory elements in DNA or RNA secondary structure prediction tools. The evaluation should also include tasks relevant to these modalities, such as predicting gene expression levels from DNA sequences or RNA-protein binding sites. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and limitations of the context-driven approach. The authors should also discuss the challenges of adapting their approach to different data types and propose potential solutions. This could involve developing different context generation pipelines for different types of biomolecular data. The authors should also explore methods for transferring knowledge between different types of biomolecular data, so that the model can leverage knowledge learned from one type of data to improve its performance on another type of data. Third, to address the potential for biases or errors introduced by the bioinformatics tools, the authors should acknowledge these limitations and explore methods for mitigating them. This could involve using multiple tools and comparing their outputs, or incorporating error correction mechanisms. The authors should also discuss the robustness of their approach to variations in the quality of the context. This could involve conducting experiments with different versions of the bioinformatics tools, or with different parameters. The authors should also explore methods for quantifying the uncertainty in the context, so that the Sci-LLM is aware of the limitations of the context. This would allow the model to be more cautious when making predictions based on uncertain context. Finally, the authors should include experiments that involve more complex scientific tasks. Instead of focusing solely on question-answering, they should explore tasks that require multi-step reasoning or the integration of information from multiple sources. For example, they could evaluate the performance of their method on tasks such as predicting protein-protein interactions, designing novel molecules, or generating hypotheses based on experimental data. These tasks would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the reasoning capabilities of Sci-LLMs and better demonstrate the potential of the proposed context-driven approach. The authors should also consider using benchmark datasets that are specifically designed for these complex tasks to ensure a fair comparison with existing methods. This would provide a more robust evaluation of the proposed method and address the current limitation of focusing solely on question-answering.


## Questions:

Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, given the reliance of the context-driven approach on existing bioinformatics tools, how does the method perform when encountering novel biomolecular sequences that lack established context? This is a critical question because it directly addresses the limitations of the approach in discovering new biological insights. The paper's methodology relies on tools that are trained on existing knowledge, and it is unclear how the method would perform when faced with sequences that are significantly different from those in the training data. Second, for what kinds of biological questions is the context-driven approach most suitable, and when might it not be a good idea to use this approach? The paper's experiments focus on question-answering tasks related to protein function, pathway involvement, and subcellular localization. However, it is unclear whether the approach would be equally effective for other types of biological questions, such as those involving complex interactions between multiple biomolecules or those requiring a deep understanding of biophysical mechanisms. A more detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of the approach for different types of biological questions would be beneficial. Third, how does the computational cost of generating high-level structured context compare to traditional sequence-based approaches, and what are the trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost? While the paper includes a section on real-world efficiency, a more detailed analysis of the computational resources required for context generation would be valuable. This should include not only the time taken but also the memory footprint and the specific hardware used. A breakdown of the time spent on each step of the pipeline (e.g., running InterProScan, BLASTp, ProTrek) would be beneficial. Furthermore, it would be useful to compare the computational cost of their approach to that of traditional sequence-based methods, considering both training and inference time. Finally, what are the potential limitations of relying on established bioinformatics tools for generating context, and how do the authors address the accuracy and completeness of the context provided to the Sci-LLMs? The paper does not extensively discuss the potential for biases or errors introduced by these tools. A more detailed discussion of these limitations and how they might affect the performance of the approach would be valuable. These questions highlight key uncertainties and areas for further investigation, and they are essential for a comprehensive understanding of the proposed method and its potential applications.
