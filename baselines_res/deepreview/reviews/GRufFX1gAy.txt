## Summary:

In this paper, I introduce InnoGym, a benchmark and framework designed to evaluate the innovation potential of AI agents. InnoGym addresses the limitations of existing benchmarks, which primarily focus on correctness, by introducing two key metrics: performance gain (G) and novelty (N). Performance gain measures the improvement of a solution relative to the best-known baseline, while novelty captures the methodological differences from prior approaches. The framework is built on a task quadruple (P, S, V, D), where P is the problem instance, S is the solution space, V is the performance measure, and D is the measure of dissimilarity between solutions. The paper presents extensive experiments on InnoGym with several existing agent frameworks, revealing key limitations in robustness and highlighting the gap between novelty and effective performance. The benchmark includes 18 carefully curated tasks from real-world engineering and scientific domains, and a unified execution environment, iGym, is provided for reproducible and long-horizon evaluations. The experiments show that while some agents produce novel approaches, their lack of robustness limits performance gains, underscoring the need for further research in this area.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and concerns need to be addressed. One of the main weaknesses lies in the reliance on the GPT-5 model for evaluating novelty. The authors use GPT-5 to rate the methodological dissimilarity of solutions along six rubric dimensions, each scored on a 0~4 scale. This approach raises concerns about the objectivity and accuracy of the novelty scores, as the reliance on a single LLM could introduce biases and artifacts. For instance, a solution that is slightly different from existing solutions might be considered novel, even if it does not offer significant improvement or new insights. The paper does not discuss the potential biases in the training data of GPT-5, which could affect the fairness of the novelty scores. This is a critical issue, as the novelty metric is a cornerstone of the InnoGym framework. The authors should explore using an ensemble of LLMs, potentially including models with different architectures and training data, to generate novelty scores. This would provide a more robust measure of novelty and allow for the calculation of inter-quantile ranges, giving a better sense of the uncertainty in the novelty assessment. Additionally, the prompts used to elicit novelty judgments from these models should be carefully analyzed and validated to ensure they are not introducing biases or artifacts. A sensitivity analysis of how different prompts affect the resulting novelty scores would be beneficial. The authors should also consider incorporating human evaluation as a benchmark for the LLM-based novelty scores, at least on a subset of the data, to establish the validity of the approach. The confidence level in this issue is high, as the reliance on a single LLM for a critical metric is a significant methodological concern. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the reliance on a single LLM, specifically GPT-5, for evaluating novelty should be mitigated by exploring the use of an ensemble of LLMs. This ensemble could include models with different architectures and training data, which would provide a more robust measure of novelty and allow for the calculation of inter-quantile ranges. This would give a better sense of the uncertainty in the novelty assessment and enhance the objectivity of the metric. The prompts used to elicit novelty judgments from these models should be carefully analyzed and validated to ensure they are not introducing biases or artifacts. A sensitivity analysis of how different prompts affect the resulting novelty scores would be beneficial. Additionally, incorporating human evaluation as a benchmark for the LLM-based novelty scores, at least on a subset of the data, would help establish the validity of the approach. Second, the paper should provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for implementing InnoGym. This analysis should include not only the hardware requirements (e.g., GPU memory, CPU cores) but also the time complexity of the different components of the framework. The authors should explore strategies for optimizing the computational efficiency of InnoGym, such as parallelization or distributed computing. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the limitations of the framework in terms of scalability and provide recommendations for how to adapt it to more complex tasks or a larger number of agents. This could involve exploring techniques for hierarchical task decomposition or using more efficient search algorithms. The authors should also consider providing a cloud-based implementation of InnoGym to make it more accessible to researchers with limited computational resources. Third, the paper should include a more in-depth analysis of the cases where agents achieve high novelty but low performance gain. This analysis should focus on understanding the reasons behind these results, such as whether the novel solutions are simply incorrect or if they represent valid but suboptimal approaches. The authors should investigate whether there are specific types of tasks or agent architectures that are more prone to generating novel but low-performing solutions. This analysis could provide valuable insights into the limitations of current AI agents and guide the development of more innovative and effective AI systems. Additionally, the authors should explore methods for encouraging agents to generate solutions that are both novel and high-performing, such as using reward shaping or curriculum learning techniques. Finally, the paper should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed framework and benchmark. This section should address the potential biases introduced by the selection of tasks, the limitations of the evaluation metrics, and the potential for the framework to be gamed by agents that are specifically designed to exploit its weaknesses. For example, the authors should acknowledge that the current set of tasks might not be representative of all types of real-world problems, and that the evaluation metrics might not capture all aspects of innovation. They should also discuss how the framework might be vulnerable to agents that are designed to produce solutions that are superficially different but do not offer any real improvement. This discussion is crucial for a balanced assessment of the work and for guiding future research in this area.
