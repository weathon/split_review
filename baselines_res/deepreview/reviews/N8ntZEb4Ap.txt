## Summary:

This paper introduces AutoNFS, a novel neural network-based approach for automatic feature selection. The core idea revolves around an end-to-end differentiable framework that combines a masking network, employing Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling, with a task-specific network. The masking network learns a feature selection mask, while the task network evaluates the relevance of the selected features for a given downstream task. The entire model is trained jointly using a differentiable loss function that incorporates both the task performance and a penalty term that encourages sparsity in the feature selection mask. This penalty term automatically determines the minimal subset of features necessary for the task, eliminating the need for manual hyperparameter tuning for feature selection. The authors claim that AutoNFS achieves a nearly constant computational overhead regardless of the input dimensionality, making it highly scalable for high-dimensional data. The empirical evaluation of AutoNFS includes experiments on classification and regression benchmarks with corrupted features, as well as real-world metagenomic datasets. The results demonstrate that AutoNFS consistently outperforms existing feature selection methods in terms of predictive performance while selecting fewer features. The paper also includes an analysis of the computational complexity of AutoNFS, showing that it scales near-constant with the number of input features. The authors emphasize the interpretability and efficiency of their method, particularly in the context of high-dimensional biological data. Overall, the paper presents a practical and effective approach to feature selection, leveraging the power of neural networks and differentiable programming. However, as I will discuss in detail, there are some limitations in the novelty of the approach, the scope of the experimental evaluation, and the depth of the interpretability analysis that need to be addressed to further strengthen the contribution.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The proposed AutoNFS method is, first and foremost, simple and easy to understand. The combination of a masking network with Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling and a task-specific network is a straightforward yet effective approach to feature selection. The use of Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling allows for a differentiable feature selection process, which is a significant advantage over traditional non-differentiable methods. The end-to-end training of the entire model, including the feature selection mask, is another strength, as it allows for the automatic determination of the optimal number of features for a given task. This eliminates the need for manual hyperparameter tuning, which is a common pain point in many feature selection methods. The paper also provides a solid theoretical analysis of the method, explaining the different components and their interactions clearly. The authors' claim of a nearly constant computational overhead is also a significant strength, as it makes AutoNFS highly scalable for high-dimensional data. The empirical evaluation of AutoNFS is also quite extensive, including comparisons with state-of-the-art feature selection methods on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The results consistently demonstrate that AutoNFS outperforms existing methods in terms of predictive performance while selecting fewer features. The inclusion of metagenomic datasets is particularly valuable, as it demonstrates the applicability of AutoNFS to real-world biological data. The computational complexity analysis, while not as detailed as it could be, does provide evidence for the near-constant scaling behavior of AutoNFS. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a wide audience. The clear presentation of the method, the experimental setup, and the results contributes to the overall quality of the paper.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of the paper, I have identified several weaknesses that need to be addressed. First, while the proposed method is functional and effective, its novelty is limited. The core mechanism of selecting features based on a learned mask and a sparsity-inducing penalty is not fundamentally new. The paper itself acknowledges the use of Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling and masking networks, which have been used in prior work. The novelty lies in the specific combination and the automatic determination of the number of features, but this is not a substantial leap forward in terms of algorithmic innovation. The paper does not introduce any novel theoretical insights or algorithmic modifications to this established framework. This lack of fundamental novelty is a significant limitation, as it reduces the overall impact of the paper. Second, the experimental evaluation, while extensive, is not as comprehensive as it could be. While the paper includes experiments on both benchmark datasets and metagenomic datasets, the number of features in these datasets, while higher than the initial benchmarks, does not reach the 'thousands' scale suggested by one of the reviewers. The benchmark datasets also have a limited number of features. The computational complexity estimation uses synthetic data. This raises concerns about the scalability and performance of AutoNFS on more complex, real-world data with thousands of features. The absence of experiments on larger datasets makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the sensitivity of the method to the hyperparameter $\lambda$, which controls the trade-off between feature selection and task performance. The paper uses a fixed value of $\lambda = 1$ for all experiments and does not explore the impact of varying this parameter. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to understand the robustness and reliability of the method. The paper also does not provide a detailed breakdown of the computational cost of each component of AutoNFS, including the Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling and the task-specific network training. While the paper claims a 'nearly constant computational overhead,' this claim lacks rigorous justification. The paper estimates the complexity exponent and compares it with other methods, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the computational cost of individual components. This makes it difficult to fully assess the computational efficiency of AutoNFS. Finally, the paper does not explore the interpretability of the selected features in detail, which is a crucial aspect of feature selection, especially in high-dimensional biological data. The paper focuses on the predictive performance of the selected features but does not delve into the biological relevance or interpretability of these features. This is a significant oversight, especially given the application to metagenomic data, where understanding the selected features is as important as achieving good predictive performance. The lack of analysis on the selected features' biological meaning limits the practical impact of the method in this domain. The experimental sections, particularly for metagenomic data, focus on predictive performance metrics without delving into the biological interpretation of the selected features. These limitations, taken together, significantly impact the overall contribution of the paper.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to enhance the novelty of the method, the authors should explore more sophisticated masking mechanisms or introduce a novel regularization term that goes beyond the standard L1 penalty. For example, they could investigate adaptive masking strategies that dynamically adjust the mask based on the feature importance during training, or explore the use of information-theoretic measures to guide feature selection. Furthermore, the authors could consider incorporating domain-specific knowledge into the feature selection process, which could lead to more meaningful and interpretable feature subsets. This could involve using prior knowledge about feature relationships or constraints to guide the learning process. The current approach is a direct application of existing techniques, and more innovative methods are needed to make a significant contribution to the field. Second, to strengthen the experimental evaluation, the authors should significantly expand their experiments to include larger, real-world datasets. These datasets should encompass a variety of domains and characteristics, such as high dimensionality, varying sample sizes, and different types of noise. For example, datasets from UCI machine learning repository, or those available through OpenML, would provide a more robust evaluation of the method's performance. Furthermore, the authors should consider using datasets with known feature relevance, allowing for a more detailed analysis of the method's ability to identify important features. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses in practical scenarios. Third, the authors should conduct a thorough sensitivity analysis of the $\lambda$ parameter. This analysis should include a range of $\lambda$ values, and the results should be presented in a way that clearly shows the impact of this parameter on both feature selection and task performance. Specifically, the authors should investigate how the number of selected features and the downstream task accuracy change as $\lambda$ varies. This analysis should also explore the trade-off between sparsity and accuracy, providing guidance on how to choose an appropriate $\lambda$ value for different applications. Furthermore, the authors should consider visualizing the feature selection process for different $\lambda$ values to gain a deeper understanding of how this parameter influences the method's behavior. Fourth, to strengthen the computational analysis, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time complexity of each step in the AutoNFS algorithm, including the Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling, the forward and backward passes of the neural network, and the optimization process. This analysis should be compared to the time complexity of other feature selection methods, such as recursive feature elimination or filter-based methods. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical evidence of the computational efficiency of AutoNFS by reporting the actual running time on different datasets and comparing it to other methods. This should include a comparison of the training time and inference time, as well as the memory usage. The analysis should also consider the impact of the input dimensionality on the computational cost of AutoNFS, and whether the 'nearly constant overhead' claim holds true in practice for very high-dimensional data. Finally, to address the interpretability issue, the authors should include a detailed analysis of the selected features in the metagenomic datasets. This analysis should go beyond simply reporting the predictive performance and should explore the biological relevance of the selected features. For example, the authors could investigate whether the selected features are known to be associated with the outcome of interest, or whether they are involved in specific biological pathways. This analysis could involve using external databases or tools to annotate the selected features and assess their biological significance. Furthermore, the authors could explore visualization techniques to illustrate the relationships between the selected features and the outcome of interest. This would help to make the results more interpretable and would increase the practical impact of the method in the field of metagenomics.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are crucial for further understanding the proposed method. First, could the authors provide a more detailed comparison of AutoNFS with other recent neural feature selection methods, particularly in terms of computational efficiency and scalability? This would help to better position AutoNFS within the existing literature and highlight its unique advantages and limitations. Second, how does the performance of AutoNFS vary with different types of datasets, especially those with varying levels of noise and dimensionality? This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's robustness and generalizability. Third, could the authors elaborate on the interpretability of the selected features, especially in the context of metagenomic data, and provide examples of how the selected features can be interpreted in a biological context? This would help to demonstrate the practical impact of the method in the field of metagenomics. Fourth, what is the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the temperature parameter in the Gumbel-Sigmoid sampling? This is a crucial hyperparameter that controls the sharpness of the sampling, and it is important to understand how it affects the performance of the method. Fifth, what is the impact of the architecture of the task-specific network on the performance of AutoNFS? The paper does not provide much detail on the architecture of the task network, and it would be useful to understand how different architectures affect the results. Finally, how does the method perform when the number of features is much larger than the number of samples? This is a common scenario in many real-world datasets, and it is important to understand how AutoNFS behaves in such cases. These questions target the core methodological choices and assumptions of the paper and seek clarification on critical aspects of the proposed approach.
