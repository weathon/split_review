## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to enhance the faithfulness of large language model (LLM) responses in Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) settings. The core idea revolves around encouraging LLMs to directly copy lexical units from the provided context, thereby reducing the likelihood of generating unfaithful or hallucinated content. The authors observe an inverse correlation between the degree of copying and the occurrence of faithlessness and hallucinations, which forms the basis of their method. To achieve this, they propose a two-stage framework. The first stage involves three prompting methods—CP-Link, CP-Order, and CP-Refine—designed to generate responses with varying degrees of copying from the retrieved context. CP-Link directly embeds sentences from the context into the response, CP-Order reorders these sentences, and CP-Refine iteratively refines the response to improve its faithfulness. The second stage leverages preference learning to train a model, termed CopyPasteLLM, to prioritize responses with higher copying degrees. This is achieved by constructing preference data using an automated pipeline that ranks responses based on their faithfulness and copying degree. The authors demonstrate that CopyPasteLLM outperforms state-of-the-art models on FaithEval and ConFiQA, particularly in counterfactual scenarios where the context contradicts the model's internal knowledge. They also provide interpretability results, showing that CopyPasteLLM relies more on contextual knowledge compared to the base model. The paper's findings suggest that promoting direct copying from the context can be an effective strategy for improving the faithfulness of LLM-generated responses in RAG systems. However, the paper also acknowledges that this approach may not be suitable for all tasks, especially those requiring creative generation or summarization. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field by highlighting the importance of copying in ensuring faithfulness and by providing a practical method for achieving this goal.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper addresses a critical and practical problem in the field of natural language processing: ensuring the faithfulness of LLM-generated responses in RAG systems. This is a significant challenge, as LLMs often exhibit a tendency to generate responses that are not grounded in the provided context, leading to hallucinations and inaccuracies. The authors' focus on this issue is both timely and relevant. Second, the proposed method is remarkably simple and straightforward, yet it demonstrates significant improvements in faithfulness. The core idea of encouraging direct copying from the context is intuitive and easy to implement. The three prompting methods—CP-Link, CP-Order, and CP-Refine—are also simple but effective in generating responses with varying degrees of copying. The fact that such a simple approach can yield substantial improvements is a testament to the authors' insight. Third, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate that CopyPasteLLM outperforms state-of-the-art models on FaithEval and ConFiQA, particularly in counterfactual scenarios. These results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The inclusion of interpretability results, which show that CopyPasteLLM relies more on contextual knowledge, further strengthens the paper's findings. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors clearly explain their methodology, experimental setup, and results. The use of figures and tables enhances the clarity of the presentation. The detailed analysis of the relationship between copying degree and hallucination density is also a valuable contribution to the field. The authors have successfully demonstrated a practical approach to improve faithfulness in RAG systems, and the simplicity of the method makes it highly accessible.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper's focus on a single retrieved document limits its applicability in real-world scenarios. The authors' method, as described, operates on the assumption that there is only one context document provided to the LLM. This is a significant limitation, as most RAG systems retrieve multiple documents, often containing conflicting information. The paper does not discuss how the proposed approach would handle such cases. For example, how would the model decide which document to copy from when faced with contradictory information across multiple sources? Would it attempt to synthesize information from multiple documents, and if so, how would it ensure faithfulness to each source? This lack of discussion about multi-document scenarios is a major weakness, as it significantly reduces the practical relevance of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's problem formulation and method descriptions consistently refer to a single context 'C'. The paper acknowledges that high copying can sometimes sacrifice fluency, but it does not delve into the broader implications of this limitation. High copying is not always desirable, especially when the retrieved context contains irrelevant or redundant information. The paper lacks a discussion of scenarios where abstractive summarization or creative generation would be more appropriate than verbatim copying. For instance, in tasks requiring reasoning or synthesis of information from multiple sources, a model that simply copies may not perform well. The authors also do not consider the potential for copying to propagate errors or biases present in the source material. This lack of a balanced view of the proposed method and its applicability is a significant weakness. ## Suggestions:

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several improvements to this paper. First, the authors should extend their approach to handle multiple retrieved documents. This could involve exploring techniques for identifying and resolving conflicts between documents, such as using attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of different sources or incorporating a conflict detection module into their framework. They could also investigate methods for synthesizing information from multiple documents while ensuring faithfulness to each source. This would significantly enhance the practical relevance of the proposed method. Second, the authors should provide a more balanced discussion of the limitations of the high-copying approach. They should explore scenarios where abstractive summarization or creative generation would be more appropriate than verbatim copying. They could also investigate techniques for mitigating the potential for copying to propagate errors or biases present in the source material. This could involve incorporating a mechanism to filter out irrelevant or redundant information or to paraphrase copied fragments while maintaining their meaning. A more nuanced analysis of these trade-offs would strengthen the paper's contribution. Third, the authors should validate the quality of the preference data using human annotations. This would involve having human annotators evaluate the generated responses and assess whether the preference data accurately reflects the relative quality of different responses. Such an evaluation would provide more confidence in the robustness of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the authors should consider the potential for bias in the automated pipeline and discuss how they have addressed this issue. Fourth, the authors should evaluate their method on a wider range of datasets and models. This would include datasets with different characteristics (e.g., different domains, different question types, different lengths of context) and a variety of model architectures and sizes. This would help to establish the method's applicability in diverse scenarios and with different model capabilities. Fifth, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method, including the training time, inference time, and memory usage. This analysis should include a comparison with existing baselines to understand the trade-offs between performance and computational resources. This information is crucial for practitioners to assess the feasibility of deploying the method in real-world applications. Finally, the authors should conduct a systematic study to evaluate the impact of key hyperparameters on the performance of the proposed method. This analysis should include a range of values for each hyperparameter and report the corresponding performance metrics. This would help to identify the optimal hyperparameter settings and understand the robustness of the method to variations in these parameters. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly enhance the impact and practical applicability of their work.


## Questions:

I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how does the proposed method perform on tasks that require creative generation or summarization, where direct copying from the context may not be the most appropriate approach? The paper's evaluation focuses on question answering tasks, and it would be valuable to understand the method's limitations in other types of tasks. Second, how does the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art methods in terms of computational cost? The paper does not provide any details on the training time, inference time, or memory usage of CopyPasteLLM or the baselines, and this information is crucial for assessing the practical feasibility of the method. Third, how does the proposed method handle noisy or incomplete context? The paper's method relies on direct copying from the context, and it is unclear how it would perform if the context contains irrelevant or contradictory information. Fourth, in the CP-Refine method, the authors use the LLM itself as a copy scout to evaluate the copying degree of the generated response. However, it is unclear whether the LLM is capable of accurately assessing the copying degree. Could the authors provide more details on how the LLM evaluates the copying degree and whether it aligns with human judgment? It would be helpful to see examples of how the LLM evaluates different responses and compare its evaluations to human annotations. Fifth, the authors use FaithEval to evaluate the performance of CopyPasteLLM in counterfactual scenarios. However, it would be helpful to understand why this dataset is particularly suitable for evaluating the model's ability to trust contextual knowledge over its internal knowledge. Could the authors elaborate on the characteristics of FaithEval that make it a good benchmark for this purpose? Additionally, it would be interesting to see how CopyPasteLLM performs on other counterfactual datasets or in scenarios where the context contradicts the model's internal knowledge in more subtle ways. Finally, the authors mention that CopyPasteLLM recalibrates the reliance on internal parametric knowledge during generation. Could the authors provide more details on how this recalibration occurs? What specific mechanisms or parameters are adjusted during the training process to achieve this recalibration? It would be helpful to see a more detailed analysis of the model's internal representations and how they change after training with the proposed method.
