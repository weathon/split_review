## Summary:

This paper introduces Constraint-aware Reward (Re)Labeling (CARL), a novel and minimalist approach designed to address the challenges of offline safe reinforcement learning (OSRL). The core idea behind CARL is to iteratively refine a policy by alternating between two key steps: updating the cost evaluation function using an off-policy evaluation (OPE) procedure and updating the policy using relabeled rewards. The reward relabeling mechanism is the heart of CARL; it penalizes state-action pairs deemed unsafe based on the estimated cost-to-go function by assigning a large negative reward. This approach effectively transforms the constrained optimization problem into an unconstrained one, simplifying the learning process. A significant advantage of CARL is its ability to be integrated with existing batch-update offline RL algorithms without introducing additional hyperparameters, making it both versatile and easy to implement. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of CARL on the DSRL benchmark tasks, showing that it reliably enforces safety constraints, even under strict cost budgets, while achieving high rewards. Notably, CARL demonstrates the capacity to learn safe policies even when trained on datasets containing unsafe trajectories, a significant achievement in the context of offline safe RL. The paper presents a clear and concise explanation of the proposed method, making it accessible to a broad audience. The experimental results are compelling, showcasing the potential of CARL to advance the field of offline safe reinforcement learning. The authors also provide a thorough analysis of the proposed method, including ablation studies and comparisons with other state-of-the-art methods, further solidifying the contributions of this work. Overall, CARL presents a promising and practical solution to the challenges of offline safe reinforcement learning, offering a simple yet effective approach that can be readily adopted by researchers and practitioners alike. The paper's focus on a minimalist design, coupled with strong empirical results, makes it a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the theoretical underpinnings of the proposed method, specifically Theorem 1, lack sufficient rigor. The proof of Theorem 1 does not explicitly state the conditions under which the maximum value function, $V_{max}$, is finite, nor does it address the existence of an optimal policy for the constrained problem (2). The proof assumes the existence of a solution to Problem (2) without further justification, which is a significant oversight. For $V_{max}$ to be finite, the reward function must be bounded, and the discount factor must be less than 1. Furthermore, the proof does not address the potential for the cost constraint to render the problem infeasible, which could impact the existence of a solution. This lack of rigor in the theoretical foundation weakens the overall contribution of the paper. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the proof explicitly omits these crucial details. Second, the paper does not provide a convergence guarantee for the proposed CARL algorithm. The iterative nature of CARL, involving off-policy evaluation and policy optimization, raises concerns about convergence. The authors acknowledge this limitation, stating that "theoretical convergence guarantees are unclear." While they provide an intuitive explanation for stability with small M and K, a formal analysis is missing. This lack of convergence analysis is a significant weakness, as it leaves open the possibility of oscillations or divergence during the iterative process. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the lack of theoretical convergence guarantees. Third, the paper lacks a sensitivity analysis of CARL to the hyperparameters of the underlying offline RL algorithm and the off-policy evaluation method. While the authors claim that CARL doesn't introduce any additional hyperparameters, it is still important to analyze the sensitivity of the algorithm to the hyperparameters of the underlying OPE and OPO methods. The authors state that they have not found values that consistently outperform CARL across benchmarks, but this is an empirical observation rather than a systematic sensitivity analysis. This omission makes it difficult to assess the robustness of CARL and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for these hyperparameters. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include a systematic analysis of the sensitivity to hyperparameters. Fourth, the paper's reward relabeling strategy, while effective, is conceptually similar to existing penalty-based methods in safe RL. The core mechanism of penalizing unsafe actions based on a cost threshold is not entirely novel. While the authors frame their method as constraint-aware reward relabeling, the underlying idea of penalizing unsafe actions is a common technique. The paper could benefit from a more explicit comparison to similar offline methods, highlighting the specific differences and advantages of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the core mechanism is similar to existing methods, but the specific application and formulation are novel. Fifth, the paper claims that CARL can enforce state-action-wise safety constraints, but this claim is not fully supported by the method's reliance on the estimated Q-function. The accuracy of the Q-function is crucial for identifying unsafe actions, and if the Q-function is inaccurate, especially at the beginning of training, the method may incorrectly label safe actions as unsafe or vice-versa. This could lead to suboptimal policies or even unsafe behavior. The paper does not adequately address the potential for inaccuracies in the Q-function estimates to impact the safety guarantees. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the method relies on an approximation that can be inaccurate. Finally, while the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of CARL in continuous action spaces, it relies on underlying off-policy algorithms that handle continuous actions. The method itself does not provide a general solution for continuous action spaces within the Q-learning framework. The paper could clarify that CARL is not a direct implementation of Q-learning in continuous spaces but a framework built on top of such algorithms. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the method relies on existing solutions for continuous action spaces. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach, these weaknesses highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to strengthen the theoretical foundations and practical applicability of CARL.
