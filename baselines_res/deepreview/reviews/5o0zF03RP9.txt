## Summary:

This paper introduces IncentRL, a novel reinforcement learning framework that aims to integrate external rewards with internal incentives through a KL-based preference prediction alignment mechanism. The core idea is to augment the standard reinforcement learning objective with a term that penalizes the KL divergence between the agent's predicted outcome distribution and a preferred outcome distribution. This preferred outcome distribution can be either hand-crafted or learned from demonstrations, providing a way to guide the agent towards desired behaviors. The authors propose treating the weight of this KL divergence term, denoted as $\beta$, as a Bayesian random variable, which is updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning. This Bayesian adaptation is a key aspect of the framework, aiming to make it more robust and adaptable to various tasks. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the method, focusing on the behavior of the agent for small and large values of $\beta$. Empirically, the authors evaluate IncentRL on a set of environments, including a simple 2-state MDP, the MountainCar environment, and the MiniGrid Doorkey 8x8 environment. The results suggest that IncentRL can improve sample efficiency and final performance compared to standard RL baselines and fixed-regularization methods. The authors also claim that their approach is inspired by the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error signals, although the connection to these concepts is not fully elaborated. Overall, the paper presents an interesting approach to integrating internal incentives into reinforcement learning, but it also has several limitations that need to be addressed. The paper's main contribution lies in the proposed framework for integrating internal incentives through KL-based preference prediction alignment and the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight. However, the theoretical analysis is limited, the empirical evaluation is not comprehensive, and the motivation for some design choices is not fully justified. The paper also lacks a thorough comparison with existing intrinsic motivation methods and does not fully explore the implications of the theoretical connections to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error signals. Despite these limitations, the paper provides a valuable contribution to the field of reinforcement learning and opens up new avenues for future research.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The core idea of integrating external rewards with internal incentives through a KL-based preference prediction alignment is both intuitive and potentially powerful. The use of a preferred outcome distribution, which can be hand-crafted or learned, provides a flexible way to guide the agent's behavior. The introduction of Bayesian adaptation for the incentive weight $\beta$ is a novel approach that aims to remove the need for manual tuning, making the framework more adaptable. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, which makes the core ideas accessible to a wide audience. The theoretical analysis, while limited in scope, provides some insights into the behavior of the agent for small and large values of $\beta$. The empirical results, although not comprehensive, demonstrate that IncentRL can improve sample efficiency and final performance in the tested environments. The authors' attempt to connect their approach to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error signals is also interesting, although this connection could be further elaborated. The paper's focus on intrinsic motivation and exploration is timely and relevant to the field of reinforcement learning. The use of KL divergence as a measure of discrepancy between the predicted and preferred outcome distributions is a reasonable choice, and the authors provide a clear explanation of their approach. The paper's overall contribution lies in the novel combination of these elements, which provides a unique perspective on how to integrate internal incentives into reinforcement learning. The authors have identified a relevant problem and proposed a novel solution that warrants further investigation. The potential of the method to improve sample efficiency and final performance is promising, and the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight is a significant technical innovation. The paper's clear presentation and well-structured arguments make it a valuable contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of the paper, I have identified several significant weaknesses that need to be addressed. First, the motivation for learning a policy when a preferred outcome distribution can be specified is unclear. As Reviewer 1 pointed out, if the goal is to achieve outcomes that align with $q(o|s)$, and this distribution is known, then the agent could directly sample actions from it, rendering the learning process unnecessary. The paper does not adequately articulate why a policy is needed instead of direct sampling, especially given that the KL divergence is minimized to make the predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s)$ match $q(o|s)$. This lack of justification undermines the core motivation of the proposed approach. Second, the paper's justification for using the KL divergence as an exploration signal is weak. While the KL divergence can encourage the agent to explore outcomes that are different from its current predictions, it is not clear why this is a better exploration strategy than using the uncertainty of the agent, which is a common approach in intrinsic motivation methods. As Reviewer 1 noted, the paper lacks a detailed comparison of the proposed method with uncertainty-based exploration techniques, and it does not provide empirical evidence to support its claim that the KL divergence is a more effective exploration signal. This lack of justification raises concerns about the novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach. Third, the Bayesian adaptation of the weighting of the KL divergence term is not well-justified. As Reviewer 1 correctly pointed out, the theoretical analysis only shows that small and large values of $\beta$ lead to similar optimal policies, but it does not explain why the Bayesian adaptation is effective. The experimental results are also not convincing, as they only show the performance of the proposed method with a fixed $\beta$ value and do not demonstrate the effectiveness of the Bayesian adaptation. The paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the Bayesian adaptation, including a discussion of the convergence properties and the stability of the learned parameters. This lack of theoretical and empirical support undermines the credibility of the proposed adaptation mechanism. Fourth, the connection to the Free Energy Principle (FEP) is tenuous. While the paper mentions inspiration from the FEP, the specific application of KL divergence is different. As Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2 noted, in FEP, KL divergence is used to minimize the difference between the empirical distribution and the predicted distribution, whereas in IncentRL, it is used to minimize the difference between the predicted outcome distribution and a preferred outcome distribution. The paper acknowledges this difference, but it does not fully justify the connection to the FEP. Fifth, the proposed method seems to be very similar to intrinsic motivation approaches that use the error in the prediction of the next state as an intrinsic reward to encourage exploration. As Reviewer 2 pointed out, the use of KL divergence between predicted and preferred outcomes appears functionally equivalent to using a prediction error in the state space itself, especially when the 'outcome' is directly derived from the state. The paper does not adequately distinguish its approach from existing methods that use prediction errors for exploration, such as those based on learned world models or state reconstruction errors. Sixth, the connection to active inference is weak. As Reviewer 2 noted, the method does not seem to capture the iterative process of belief and preference updating that is central to active inference. The use of a fixed preference or a preference learned offline does not align with the core principles of active inference where preferences are dynamically adjusted based on the agent's interactions with the environment. Seventh, the empirical validation is limited to a few simple environments. As Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 4 pointed out, the experiments do not include a wide range of tasks with varying complexities and characteristics. The lack of evaluation on more challenging benchmarks makes it difficult to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method. Eighth, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with existing intrinsic motivation methods. As Reviewer 3 and Reviewer 4 noted, the paper does not provide a thorough comparison with methods that explicitly model curiosity or empowerment, which are closely related to the concept of internal incentives. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to evaluate the relative performance and advantages of the proposed method. Ninth, the paper does not fully explore the practical implications of the theoretical connections to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error. As Reviewer 3 noted, the paper does not delve into the specific biological implications of the Bayesian adaptation or the limitations of the analogy. Finally, the paper lacks detailed ablation studies to understand the impact of different components of the framework. As Reviewer 3 noted, it would be valuable to see how the performance of IncentRL changes when the KL-based preference prediction alignment is removed or when the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight is replaced with a simpler heuristic. These weaknesses, which are supported by the evidence from the paper and the reviewers' comments, significantly undermine the paper's claims and limit its overall impact.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should clearly articulate the scenarios where direct sampling from the preferred outcome distribution is not feasible or practical, and why a learned policy is essential in those cases. This explanation should include a discussion of the limitations of directly using $q(o|s)$ for action selection, such as computational complexity or the need for a differentiable policy for gradient-based optimization. Second, the authors should provide a detailed comparison of the proposed method with uncertainty-based exploration techniques, highlighting the specific advantages of using the KL divergence. This comparison should include a discussion of the exploration properties of both approaches, such as the ability to explore novel states and avoid redundant exploration. The authors should also provide empirical evidence to support their claim that the KL divergence is a more effective exploration signal than uncertainty-based methods, especially in complex environments. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed theoretical analysis of the Bayesian adaptation, including a discussion of the convergence properties and the stability of the learned parameters. They should also provide more comprehensive experimental results that demonstrate the effectiveness of the Bayesian adaptation across a range of different environments and tasks, and compare the performance of the Bayesian adaptation with other methods for adapting the weighting of intrinsic rewards. Fourth, the authors should clarify the connection to the Free Energy Principle by explaining how the KL divergence term in their objective function relates to the prediction errors in the predictive coding framework. They should also acknowledge the differences between their approach and the standard FEP formulation and discuss the limitations of the analogy. Fifth, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of their method with existing intrinsic motivation techniques, particularly those that use prediction errors for exploration. A rigorous analysis of the differences in the objective functions and the resulting learning dynamics is needed. For example, the authors could compare their approach to methods that use a variational autoencoder to learn a latent representation of the state space and then use the reconstruction error as an intrinsic reward. Sixth, to strengthen the connection to active inference, the authors should explore how the agent's preferences can be dynamically updated based on its interactions with the environment. This could involve incorporating a mechanism for the agent to learn from its experiences and adjust its preferences accordingly. Seventh, the authors should evaluate their method in more complex environments to demonstrate its scalability and robustness. This could include environments with high-dimensional state spaces, complex reward structures, and stochastic transitions. For example, the authors could test their method in a robotic manipulation task or a navigation task in a complex environment. These experiments should include a comparison with other state-of-the-art exploration methods to show the relative performance of the proposed method. Eighth, the authors should include a more comprehensive analysis of how IncentRL relates to and differs from methods that use intrinsic rewards, such as curiosity-driven exploration or empowerment-based approaches. This should include a discussion of the specific mechanisms used by these methods, their strengths and weaknesses, and how IncentRL's approach to integrating external rewards with internal incentives provides a unique advantage. Ninth, the authors should delve deeper into the implications of the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error for the design and interpretation of IncentRL. This should include a more detailed explanation of how the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight relates to the biological mechanisms of dopamine signaling. Finally, the authors should conduct more detailed ablation studies to isolate the contributions of each component. This should include experiments where the KL-based preference prediction alignment is removed or where the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight is replaced with a simpler heuristic. These changes would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are crucial for understanding the proposed method. First, how does the performance of IncentRL compare to other methods that use intrinsic rewards or preference-based learning, particularly those that use prediction errors for exploration? A direct comparison with these methods would help to clarify the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed approach. Second, what are the practical implications of the theoretical connections to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error? A more detailed explanation of how the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight relates to the biological mechanisms of dopamine signaling would be valuable. Third, how sensitive is the performance of IncentRL to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly the prior distribution of the incentive weight? A thorough investigation of the sensitivity of the method to various hyperparameter settings would be valuable for practitioners who want to apply IncentRL to new problems. Fourth, can the authors provide more details on how the Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight works in practice? How does it affect the exploration-exploitation trade-off? A more detailed explanation of the Bayesian adaptation mechanism would be helpful. Fifth, how does the proposed method perform in more complex environments with high-dimensional state spaces and complex reward structures? The current evaluations are limited to relatively simple environments, and it is unclear how the method would perform in more challenging scenarios. Sixth, how do the authors propose to learn the agent's preferences? The paper mentions that the preferred outcome distribution can be hand-crafted or learned from demonstrations, but it does not provide details on how this learning process would work. Seventh, how does the KL-based incentive shaping compare to other intrinsic motivation methods in terms of performance and sample efficiency? A direct comparison with other methods would help to clarify the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed approach. Eighth, can the authors provide more insights into the convergence properties of the proposed method in more complex environments? The current theoretical analysis is limited to a toy example, and it is unclear how the results generalize to more complex scenarios. Ninth, how does the proposed method relate to the predictive coding framework, which is a key component of the Free Energy Principle? A more detailed explanation of how the KL divergence term in their objective function relates to the prediction errors in the predictive coding framework is needed. Finally, what are the limitations of the analogy between IncentRL and biological systems? Acknowledging the limitations of the analogy would provide a more nuanced understanding of the theoretical underpinnings of IncentRL and its potential for future research. These questions target core methodological choices and seek clarification of critical assumptions, which are essential for a thorough understanding of the proposed framework.
