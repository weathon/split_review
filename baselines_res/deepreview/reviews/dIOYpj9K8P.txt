## Summary:

This paper introduces the Massive Genre-Audience (MGA) reformulation method, a novel approach to augmenting training corpora for large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind MGA is to generate diverse variations of existing text by adaptively creating genre-audience pairs for each source document and then reformulating the text according to these pairs. This method aims to address the limitations of traditional data augmentation techniques, such as simple repetition or upsampling, which can lead to diminishing returns and model collapse. The authors propose a two-stage framework: first, an adaptive genre-audience pair generation stage, and second, a controlled reformulation stage using fine-tuned tool language models (SLMs). The reformulated data is then filtered using a heuristic cleaning process to ensure quality. The paper presents MGACorpus, a large-scale corpus of 770 billion tokens, created using the MGA method. The authors demonstrate that models trained on MGACorpus outperform models trained on the original data across various downstream tasks, particularly in data-constrained scenarios. They also analyze the impact of synthesis principles, such as diversity and consistency, on generation quality and model performance. The paper's key findings highlight the effectiveness of MGA in improving LLM performance, especially when scaling up training data, and reveal nuances in evaluating model capabilities using standard loss metrics. The authors emphasize that MGA provides a reliable pathway to overcome the data repetition bottleneck and enable more efficient scaling of LLMs. The work contributes a valuable resource to the NLP community and offers a systematic framework for data augmentation that can be applied to other domains. The paper also delves into the trade-offs between diversity and consistency, showing that a balanced approach yields the best results. The authors also explore how the MGA method impacts the learning process, suggesting that it helps models prioritize generalizable patterns over memorizing specific sequence dependencies. Overall, this work presents a significant advancement in data augmentation techniques for LLMs, offering both a practical method and a valuable dataset for future research.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of the MGA framework, a novel and well-motivated approach to data augmentation for large language models. The core idea of adaptively generating genre-audience pairs to create diverse variations of existing text is both intuitive and effective. This method moves beyond simple repetition or upsampling, addressing a critical limitation in current LLM training practices. The creation of MGACorpus, a 770 billion token dataset, is a significant contribution in itself, providing the research community with a valuable resource for exploring data scaling properties and pretraining techniques. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, demonstrating that models trained on MGA-augmented data outperform those trained on the original data across various downstream tasks. This performance improvement is particularly pronounced in data-constrained scenarios, highlighting the practical utility of the MGA method. The authors also provide a thorough analysis of the MGA method, examining the impact of synthesis principles such as diversity and consistency on generation quality and model performance. The exploration of the trade-offs between these principles, and the identification of a balanced approach as optimal, is a valuable contribution to the field. Furthermore, the paper's investigation into how MGA impacts the learning process, suggesting that it helps models prioritize generalizable patterns, offers valuable insights into the mechanisms behind its effectiveness. The authors also provide a detailed description of their methodology, including the two-stage framework, the fine-tuning of tool SLMs, and the heuristic cleaning process. This level of detail enhances the reproducibility of the work and allows other researchers to build upon their findings. The paper is well-written and organized, with clear figures and tables that effectively communicate the results. The authors also discuss the limitations of their work and suggest directions for future research, demonstrating a thorough understanding of the topic. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in data augmentation techniques for LLMs, offering both a practical method and a valuable dataset for future research.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to data augmentation, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the quality of the reformulated corpus is a concern. Although the authors claim that the tool SLMs are fine-tuned with a scoring function to maintain quality, the reliance on the LLM's own scoring function for this purpose introduces a potential bias. As the paper states, "The teacher LLM first produces an initial set of synthetic reformulations... To circumvent this, we leverage the teacher LLM as a quality judge, using its scoring function... to filter for a high-quality subset." (Section 3.2). This means that the evaluation might favor outputs that align with the model's own generation style, rather than strictly adhering to factual accuracy and logical consistency. While the paper mentions human-in-the-loop cross-checking, the extent and methodology of this cross-checking are not detailed, making it difficult to assess the reliability of the quality control process. The paper also lacks explicit metrics for factual consistency and overall coherence of the reformulated corpus beyond the LLM's scoring. This reliance on the LLM's self-evaluation, without a more robust external evaluation, raises concerns about the potential for subtle factual errors or inconsistencies to propagate through the augmented data. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the use of the LLM's scoring function for quality filtering, and the lack of external evaluation metrics is evident. Second, the paper's description of the keyword coverage metric used in the final heuristic cleaning process is insufficient. The paper mentions that "documents with extremely low keyword coverage with the source document" are removed (Section 3.2), but it fails to define how this coverage is calculated. This lack of a clear definition makes it difficult to assess the effectiveness of this filtering step. The reviewer correctly points out the potential issue of varying text lengths, and the paper does not address how this is accounted for in the coverage calculation. Without a precise metric, it is impossible to determine whether the filtering process effectively removes low-quality documents or if it inadvertently discards valuable information. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as the paper explicitly mentions keyword coverage as a filtering criterion but provides no definition or calculation method. Third, the paper does not compare the proposed method with other established data augmentation techniques, such as WRAP. While the paper compares MGA with Nemotron-CC, a seed-based synthetic data method, the absence of comparisons with other rephrasing or general data augmentation techniques makes it difficult to determine the relative effectiveness of the proposed method. As the paper states, "The paper compares MGA-Expansion with 'repeat 50b high quality (hq) data 10 epochs' and 'upsample hq data 5 times with 450b hq data' in Figure 3. The paper compares MGA with Nemotron-CC in Section 4.3.1." (Section 4.3.1). The lack of comparison with WRAP, a relevant rephrasing method, leaves a gap in the evaluation, making it unclear whether the observed improvements are due to the specific genre-audience reformulation approach or simply due to the increased diversity introduced by any data augmentation method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include a direct comparison with WRAP or other general data augmentation techniques in the main experiments. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for the MGA method and the creation of MGACorpus. The paper mentions using a "stable training/inference platform" (Section 5), but it does not provide specific details on hardware specifications, training time, or energy consumption. This lack of information makes it difficult for other researchers to reproduce the results or apply the MGA method to their own datasets. The paper also does not discuss the scalability of the method, including how the computational costs scale with the size of the input corpus and the number of genre-audience pairs generated. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks specific details on hardware, training time, and energy consumption for the MGA method and MGACorpus creation. Fifth, the paper focuses on the application of the MGA method to English text and does not provide any evidence or discussion about its generalizability to other languages, particularly low-resource languages. The paper's method description and experiments are all centered on English datasets, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of the method's applicability to a broader range of languages. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper only presents results on English text and lacks discussion or experiments on other languages. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear definition of the term "genre-audience pairs," a key concept in the MGA framework. While the paper provides examples of genres and audiences, it lacks a formal, concise definition. The paper states, "Genre dictates the structural and stylistic format of the content... Audience defines the intended reader’s profile..." (Section 3.2). However, the mechanism for determining how these pairs are adaptively generated and related to the source text is not fully explained. The paper also does not provide explicit metrics for measuring the balance between stylistic diversity and factual fidelity, relying instead on qualitative descriptions and the performance of the models trained on the reformulated data as indirect evidence of the principle's effectiveness. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, to mitigate the concerns about the quality of the reformulated corpus, the authors should incorporate a more robust evaluation process that goes beyond the model's own scoring function. This could involve human evaluation of a subset of the reformulated data to assess factual consistency, logical coherence, and overall quality. The authors should provide clear guidelines for human evaluators and report inter-annotator agreement to ensure the reliability of this evaluation. Furthermore, the use of an external model, trained on a different dataset, could provide a more objective evaluation of the reformulated text. This would help to mitigate the potential bias introduced by using the same model for both generation and evaluation. The authors should also provide examples of both high-quality and low-quality reformulations to better illustrate the capabilities and limitations of the proposed method. Second, to clarify the keyword coverage metric, the authors should provide a precise definition of how this coverage is calculated, including the specific method for identifying keywords and measuring their presence in the reformulated text. It is important to address the potential issue of varying text lengths, and how this is accounted for in the coverage calculation. For example, is the coverage calculated as a percentage of keywords present, or is it normalized by the length of the document? Providing a clear and unambiguous definition of this metric is crucial for understanding the effectiveness of the filtering process. Additionally, it would be helpful to explore alternative metrics for assessing the semantic similarity between the source and reformulated documents, such as embedding-based similarity measures, which might be more robust to variations in wording and length. Third, to better contextualize the performance of the proposed method, it is essential to compare it against other data augmentation baselines, such as WRAP. This would help to determine whether the observed improvements are specific to the genre-audience reformulation approach or are simply due to the introduction of more diverse training data. The comparison should include a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, as well as a discussion of the computational cost and complexity of each approach. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method's contribution and its potential advantages over existing techniques. Fourth, the paper should include a more detailed breakdown of the computational costs associated with the MGA method. Specifically, the authors should provide information on the hardware used (e.g., number and type of GPUs, CPU specifications, memory), the training time for each stage of the process (e.g., genre-audience pair generation, text reformulation), and the energy consumption. This would allow researchers to better assess the feasibility of using the MGA method in their own settings. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to include a discussion of the scalability of the method, including how the computational costs scale with the size of the input corpus and the number of genre-audience pairs generated. This analysis should also include a discussion of the software infrastructure used, such as the specific libraries and frameworks, to ensure reproducibility. Fifth, to address the concern about the generalizability of the MGA method to other languages, the authors should conduct experiments on a diverse set of languages, including low-resource languages. This would involve creating datasets for these languages using the MGA method and evaluating the performance of models trained on these datasets. The evaluation should include a comparison of the performance of models trained on MGA-augmented data with models trained on the original data, as well as a comparison with models trained on data augmented using other methods. The authors should also analyze the impact of different genre-audience pairs on the performance of models trained on different languages. This analysis should include a discussion of the challenges of applying the MGA method to low-resource languages, such as the lack of labeled data and the difficulty of generating high-quality genre-audience pairs. Finally, the paper should provide a formal definition of genre-audience pairs, clarifying the specific criteria used to define both genres and audiences. For example, the paper could specify that genres are defined based on the communicative purpose of the text (e.g., to inform, to persuade, to entertain), and that audiences are defined based on their level of expertise and background knowledge. The paper should also explain how these pairs are generated from the source text, and whether this process is manual or automated. Furthermore, the paper should provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between the source text and the generated genre-audience pairs, including examples of how different pairs lead to different reformulations. To address the lack of clarity regarding the "Limited Consistency" principle, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of how this principle is implemented and controlled. Specifically, the paper should define the metrics used to measure both stylistic diversity and factual fidelity. For example, the paper could use metrics such as perplexity or BLEU score to measure stylistic diversity, and metrics such as semantic similarity or logical consistency checks to measure factual fidelity. The paper should also explain how these metrics are used to determine the optimal level of consistency, and how this level varies across different types of data and tasks. Furthermore, the paper should provide a detailed analysis of the trade-offs involved in balancing stylistic diversity and factual fidelity, and how the MGA framework addresses these trade-offs.
