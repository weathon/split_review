## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel method for analyzing gated activation functions in large language models (LLMs), focusing on the read-write (RW) functionalities of individual neurons. The authors propose a technique to investigate these functionalities by computing the cosine similarities between input and output weight vectors of neurons, revealing a specific class of neurons termed 'weakening neurons.' These neurons, despite their scarcity, exhibit a significant influence on model behavior, particularly when gate values are negative. The study identifies that weakening neurons are present across various models and layers, with a higher concentration in later layers, while conditional strengthening neurons are more prevalent in earlier-middle layers. The authors conduct ablation studies to demonstrate the impact of these weakening neurons on model performance, using metrics such as attribute rate and entropy. They also introduce a method of conditional ablation to identify which activations of a given neuron are responsible for a certain behavior. The core contribution of this work lies in its novel approach to understanding neuron behavior through the lens of read-write functionality and the discovery of the significant role played by weakening neurons. The paper's methodology involves analyzing the cosine similarities between input and output weight vectors of neurons, which allows for the classification of neurons into different RW categories. The empirical findings are based on experiments conducted across multiple LLMs, providing a comprehensive analysis of neuron behavior. The authors' findings challenge previous assumptions about the role of negative gate values, highlighting their importance in model functionality. Overall, this paper offers a fresh perspective on mechanistic interpretability in LLMs, providing valuable insights into the inner workings of transformers and the behavior of individual neurons. The study's focus on gated activation functions and the discovery of weakening neurons contribute to a deeper understanding of how these complex models operate. The authors' approach of using cosine similarity to analyze neuron behavior provides a new way to interpret the roles of individual neurons in the overall model functionality. The paper's findings have implications for both model training and deployment, potentially leading to more efficient and interpretable LLMs.

## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents several compelling findings, there are some notable weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One significant limitation is the paper's reliance on cosine similarity as the primary metric for analyzing neuron behavior. While cosine similarity effectively measures the alignment of weight vectors, it does not directly quantify the magnitude of these vectors or the impact of different input activations on the neuron's output. As a result, the analysis may overlook neurons with large weight vectors that could have a significant impact on the model's behavior, even if their cosine similarity is not exceptionally high. Furthermore, the method does not account for the dynamic nature of neuron activations during inference, which could be influenced by the specific input sequence and the model's internal state. This limitation is evident in the methodology section (Section 4), which heavily relies on mathematical notation and lacks intuitive explanations, making it difficult to follow for readers not deeply familiar with the concepts. The paper's claim that weakening neurons have an 'outsized influence' is not fully substantiated by the ablation studies. While the authors demonstrate that ablating weakening neurons has a significant effect on metrics like attribute rate and entropy, they do not provide a direct, quantitative comparison to the impact of ablating other neuron types. The results for other neuron classes are mentioned as being 'indistinguishable from the 'clean' line' (Section 6.1), but this is a qualitative statement without direct quantitative comparison in the main text. This lack of direct comparison makes it difficult to assess the unique importance of weakening neurons compared to other neuron types. The paper also lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the conclusion (Section 9) mentions future research directions, it does not explicitly address the limitations of the current approach, such as the reliance on cosine similarity, the potential for misclassification of neurons, or the scope of the taxonomy. The paper also does not explore the potential for adversarial attacks that could exploit the identified weakening neurons to manipulate model behavior. The paper's focus on a specific set of LLMs, while providing consistent results, limits the generalizability of the findings. Testing on a broader range of models, including those with different architectures or trained on different datasets, would strengthen the universality of the findings. The paper also lacks an in-depth exploration of why weakening neurons exhibit their behavior. While the case study in Section 8 provides some qualitative insights, it does not delve deeply into the underlying mechanisms that cause this behavior across the class of weakening neurons. This lack of mechanistic understanding limits the impact of the findings. The paper's reliance on cosine similarity and conditional ablation might not capture the full complexity of neuron interactions. Exploring additional interpretability methods, such as activation patching or feature visualization, could provide a more comprehensive view. Finally, the implications of weakening neurons for practical applications, such as model training or deployment, are not fully discussed. The paper does not delve into specific practical implications for model training or deployment, which could increase the paper’s impact. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's findings, highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to fully understand the role of weakening neurons in LLMs. The confidence level for each of these identified weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the analysis performed.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the authors should expand their analysis beyond cosine similarity to capture the full complexity of neuron interactions. This could involve analyzing the magnitude of the weight vectors associated with each neuron, as well as the distribution of input activations that lead to significant neuron outputs. This could include examining the variance and skewness of the input activations, as well as the correlation between different input features. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of techniques such as activation patching or ablation studies to directly assess the impact of individual neurons on model behavior. This would provide a more direct measure of a neuron's influence, rather than relying solely on the indirect measure of cosine similarity. The authors should also consider analyzing the temporal dynamics of neuron activations during inference, as this could reveal important patterns that are not captured by static weight vector analysis. This could involve tracking the activation patterns of neurons across different layers and time steps, and identifying any recurrent or sequential patterns that might be indicative of specific functional roles. To better establish the significance of weakening neurons, the authors should conduct more targeted experiments that directly link the ablation of these neurons to specific performance degradation on well-established benchmark tasks. Instead of relying on attribute rate and entropy, which are somewhat abstract metrics, the authors should consider evaluating the impact of weakening neuron ablation on tasks such as question answering, text summarization, or code generation. This would provide a more concrete demonstration of the practical significance of these neurons. Furthermore, the authors should explore the specific conditions under which weakening neurons have the largest impact. For example, are they more important in certain types of inputs or for specific model architectures? This would help to clarify the unique role of weakening neurons and their relevance to model behavior. The paper should include a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This should include a critical evaluation of the method's sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, such as the threshold for defining weakening neurons, and how these choices might affect the results. The authors should also discuss the potential for adversarial attacks that could exploit the identified weakening neurons to manipulate model behavior. Furthermore, they should explore how the method might be extended to analyze other types of neurons or models, or how it could be used to improve model interpretability or performance. For example, the authors could investigate whether the identified weakening neurons are specific to certain types of tasks or datasets, or whether they are a general feature of large language models. They could also explore whether the weakening neurons play a role in specific model behaviors, such as hallucination or bias. Finally, the authors should consider how their method could be used to develop more robust and reliable models, by identifying and mitigating the impact of potentially problematic neurons. To strengthen the generalizability of the findings, the authors should consider expanding their analysis to include a more diverse set of LLMs. This should include models with different architectures, such as those employing different attention mechanisms or normalization techniques, as well as models trained on different datasets or for different tasks. This would provide a more robust assessment of the universality of weakening neurons. The authors should also explore the evolutionary patterns of weakening neurons during model training. This could involve tracking the activation patterns and synaptic weights of these neurons across different training epochs. This would provide insights into how these neurons emerge and evolve during the training process. Finally, the paper should explore alternative interpretability methods to complement the cosine similarity and conditional ablation techniques. For example, techniques such as activation patching or feature visualization could provide a more direct understanding of the functional role of weakening neurons. Activation patching involves directly modifying the output of a neuron and observing the effect on the model's behavior. This could help determine whether the observed 'weakening' effect is indeed caused by the identified neurons. Feature visualization, on the other hand, involves generating input patterns that maximally activate a given neuron. This could provide insights into the specific features that these neurons are sensitive to. The authors should also discuss the potential implications of their findings for practical applications, such as model optimization and interpretability. For example, could the identification of weakening neurons be used to improve the efficiency or robustness of LLMs?

How might different metrics, such as those that consider the magnitude of weight vectors or the dynamic nature of activations, affect the analysis of neuron read-write functionality? Second, how sensitive are the findings to the choice of models used in the study? Would the results generalize to other types of models or architectures, particularly those with different attention mechanisms or normalization techniques? Third, the paper mentions that weakening neurons can have a significant impact on model behavior even with negative gate values. Could the authors provide more context on why this is surprising or counterintuitive? What specific mechanisms allow these neurons to exert such influence despite negative gate values? Fourth, could the authors provide more details on the specific criteria used to classify neurons into different read-write categories? Were these thresholds consistent across models, and how were they determined? Fifth, how might the findings about weakening neurons be leveraged to improve model training or deployment in practical applications? Are there specific strategies that could be used to mitigate the impact of potentially problematic weakening neurons or to enhance the positive effects of strengthening neurons? Sixth, have the authors considered testing the behavior of weakening neurons in tasks beyond language modeling, such as image recognition or other AI domains? This could help determine whether the observed patterns are specific to language models or are a more general property of transformer networks. Finally, could the authors elaborate on how they controlled for potential confounding factors during the ablation studies, particularly when assessing the impact of weakening neurons? What steps were taken to ensure that the observed effects were indeed due to the ablation of weakening neurons and not other factors? These questions aim to clarify key methodological choices, assumptions, and limitations of the study, and to explore potential avenues for future research.
