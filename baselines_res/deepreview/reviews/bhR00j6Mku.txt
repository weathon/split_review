## Summary:

This paper delves into the critical issue of benchmark contamination in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), a problem that arises when evaluation benchmarks are inadvertently included in the training data, leading to inflated performance metrics. The authors investigate this problem in the context of LRMs, which are trained using a combination of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). They identify two key scenarios where contamination can occur: first, during the transition from a base model to an LRM via SFT and RL, and second, during the final SFT stage with chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. The core finding of this work is that existing contamination detection methods are inadequate for LRMs. Specifically, the authors demonstrate that contamination introduced during the SFT phase can be effectively concealed through subsequent RL training. Furthermore, they show that contamination introduced during the final SFT stage with CoT reasoning leaves minimal detectable evidence. These findings are supported by both empirical experiments across multiple benchmarks and a theoretical analysis focusing on PPO-style RL algorithms. The authors' theoretical analysis suggests that the importance sampling and clipping objectives used in PPO-style algorithms are the root cause of this concealment, implying that a broader class of RL methods may exhibit similar behavior. The paper's significance lies in highlighting the vulnerabilities in current evaluation practices for LRMs and underscoring the urgent need for more robust detection methods and trustworthy evaluation protocols. By revealing the ease with which contamination can be concealed, this work challenges the reliability of current LRM benchmarks and calls for a reevaluation of how these models are assessed. The authors propose two potential directions to address these issues: releasing more intermediate training checkpoints and developing contamination detection methods that go beyond memorization-driven approaches. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution by exposing a critical flaw in the evaluation of LRMs and setting the stage for future research in this area.

The identification of two distinct contamination scenarios—the base model's evolution into an LRM and contamination during the final SFT step—is a key strength, as it provides a structured framework for understanding the problem. The empirical findings are particularly compelling. The demonstration that RL training can effectively conceal contamination introduced during SFT is a novel and important observation. This finding challenges the assumption that contamination will always be detectable, even if the model undergoes further training.
