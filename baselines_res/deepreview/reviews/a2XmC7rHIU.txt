## Summary:

This paper introduces the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a large-scale dataset of human-evaluated proofs generated by state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs). The authors' primary aim is to address key open questions in the field of automated proof generation, specifically the gap between natural language and formal proof generation, the relationship between final answer accuracy and proof correctness, and the effectiveness of best-of-n selection strategies. The OPC comprises over 5,000 LLM-generated proofs, each annotated by one or two human judges for correctness. The authors also fine-tuned an 8B-parameter model on the OPC, achieving a performance that matches GEMINI-2.5-PRO and approaches GPT-5 in evaluating proof correctness. The dataset includes problems from top-tier national and international mathematics competitions, such as the USAMO and IMO, and the selection process was dynamically adjusted based on model performance to ensure the problems remained appropriately challenging. The paper presents a comprehensive analysis of the dataset, exploring the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in mathematical reasoning. The findings are well-supported by empirical evidence and analysis, providing valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs in this domain. The authors also explore the use of best-of-n selection strategies, demonstrating their effectiveness in improving the overall performance of LLMs in proof generation. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear methodology and presentation of results. The creation of the OPC is a significant contribution to the field, providing a valuable resource for future research in automated theorem proving and mathematical reasoning. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the dataset, including the distribution of competitions, the average accuracy of the best-performing model per competition as a proxy for difficulty, and the inter-annotator agreement for the human evaluations. The paper also includes a detailed description of the problem selection process, the criteria for choosing competitions, and the dynamic adjustment of the problem set based on model performance. The authors also provide a detailed description of the human evaluation process, including the background of the judges, the specific grading criteria, and the process for resolving disagreements between judges. The paper also includes a detailed analysis of the potential biases in the dataset, including the selection of problems, the distribution of difficulty levels, and the representation of different mathematical domains. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of automated theorem proving by introducing a large-scale, human-validated dataset of LLM-generated proofs and providing new insights into the strengths and limitations of LLMs in mathematical reasoning.

## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a valuable contribution, there are several weaknesses that warrant attention. Firstly, the human evaluation process, while rigorous in some aspects, could benefit from further detail. While the paper mentions that judges were selected from among former IMO participants or individuals who reached the final stages of IMO selection, and that they were provided with precise grading guidelines, the specific criteria used to determine proof correctness are not fully detailed in the main paper. The paper states that the full guidelines are available in the supplementary material, but the lack of these details in the main paper makes it difficult to fully assess the rigor of the evaluation process. Additionally, while the paper reports an inter-annotator agreement of 90.4% on a subset of the data, it does not specify the metric used to calculate this agreement, nor does it provide a detailed analysis of the types of disagreements that arose. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully assess the reliability of the human judgments, which are a cornerstone of the dataset's value. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the potential biases in the dataset. While the authors mention that problems were drawn from top-tier national and international mathematics competitions, and that the selection process was dynamically adjusted based on model performance, there is no explicit discussion of the representation of different mathematical domains. For example, the paper does not analyze the number of problems from each competition, the average difficulty level of problems from each competition, or the distribution of difficulty levels within each competition. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the potential biases in the dataset and their impact on the generalizability of the findings. The paper also does not explicitly discuss the limitations of the OPC. While the authors mention that the dataset is designed to capture a balanced mix of correct and incorrect proofs, they do not discuss the potential limitations of the dataset in terms of its ability to generalize to other types of mathematical problems or domains. This lack of discussion makes it difficult to assess the scope of the dataset and its applicability to other research areas. Finally, while the paper does fine-tune a model on the OPC, it does not explore the potential of using the OPC for training and fine-tuning LLMs for proof generation in a more comprehensive manner. The paper focuses primarily on evaluating models, and the dataset's potential for model training remains largely unexplored. This is a missed opportunity, as the OPC could be a valuable resource for training and fine-tuning LLMs for proof generation. The paper also does not explore different training strategies, such as varying the learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs, nor does it analyze the types of errors made by the fine-tuned model. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to fully leverage the potential of the OPC for improving LLM performance in mathematical proof generation. In summary, while the paper presents a valuable contribution, the lack of detail in the human evaluation process, the lack of analysis of potential biases, the lack of discussion of the limitations of the dataset, and the limited exploration of the dataset's potential for model training are significant weaknesses that warrant attention. The confidence level for these weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of specific details in the methodology and analysis.
