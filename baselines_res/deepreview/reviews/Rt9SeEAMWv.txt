## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for analyzing the generalization performance of stochastic optimization algorithms by focusing on the concept of *random set stability*. The authors move beyond traditional single-point generalization bounds by considering the entire trajectory of iterates or a data-dependent random set produced during the optimization process. This approach is motivated by the observation that the generalization error is often influenced by the collective behavior of the iterates, rather than just a single solution. The core methodological contribution is the definition of random set stability, a property that quantifies how much the output random set changes when a single data point in the training set is altered. This stability parameter, denoted as $\beta_n$, is then used to derive generalization bounds that depend on both the stability of the algorithm and the complexity of the hypothesis space. Specifically, the authors show that the expected worst-case generalization error can be bounded by a term involving the Rademacher complexity of the random set and a term proportional to the stability parameter. This framework allows for the derivation of computable generalization bounds, which is a significant advantage over existing approaches that rely on intractable mutual information terms. The authors demonstrate the applicability of their framework by deriving bounds based on fractal and topological complexity measures, providing the first fully computable topological bounds for practically used optimization algorithms. The empirical evaluation of the proposed bounds is conducted on practical settings, using a Vision Transformer (ViT) and a GraphSAGE model. The authors estimate the stability parameter by replacing 50 unseen samples per training set, retraining to obtain the random sets, and measuring the worst-case loss deviations across iterations. They then compare the estimated bounds with the actual worst-case generalization error, showing that the bounds are of the same order of magnitude and provide meaningful guarantees. The paper also investigates the interplay between stability and topological complexity, demonstrating that the bounds adapt meaningfully to model performance. Overall, this work presents a significant advancement in the field of generalization bounds for stochastic optimization algorithms by introducing a novel stability notion and providing computable bounds that are empirically relevant. The framework offers a new perspective on understanding the generalization behavior of these algorithms and opens up avenues for further research in this area.

## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a novel and valuable framework, there are several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper primarily focuses on providing *expected* bounds on the generalization error, as evidenced by Lemma 3.4 and Theorems 4.3 and 4.4, which explicitly bound the expected worst-case generalization error. This is a significant limitation because expected bounds do not capture the worst-case generalization error with high probability. In practical applications, it is often crucial to have guarantees that hold with high probability, rather than just on average. The absence of concentration inequalities in the analysis means that the paper does not address the potential for large deviations from the expected behavior, which could be problematic in certain scenarios. This is a significant weakness because it limits the applicability of the results in settings where high-probability guarantees are required. Second, the framework, as it stands, is primarily limited to Euclidean-based topological complexities. The definition of random set stability (Assumption 3.1) relies on the Euclidean distance in the loss function difference, and the experimental setup uses a Euclidean distance matrix. This reliance on the Euclidean metric restricts the applicability of the framework to scenarios where the geometry of the hypothesis space can be adequately captured by Euclidean distance. Many real-world problems involve more complex geometries that are better captured by data-dependent pseudometrics. The lack of a mechanism to incorporate these pseudometrics limits the framework's ability to provide a more nuanced understanding of the hypothesis space. This is a significant weakness because it restricts the scope of the framework and prevents it from being applied to a wider range of problems. Third, the empirical evaluation, while providing some evidence for the validity of the bounds, lacks a comprehensive comparison with existing generalization bounds, particularly those based on information-theoretic measures. The experiments compare the estimated bounds with the actual worst-case generalization error, but they do not directly compare the proposed bounds with other theoretical bounds. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the practical advantages and limitations of the proposed bounds relative to existing approaches. It is unclear whether the proposed bounds are tighter or more informative than existing bounds, and this makes it difficult to fully evaluate the significance of the contribution. This is a significant weakness because it limits the ability to contextualize the results and understand their practical implications. Finally, while the paper introduces the concept of a 'data-dependent selection' in Definition 3.1, the explanation of this concept is not entirely intuitive. The definition is formal, but it lacks a clear explanation of what this selection process entails and how it relates to the overall goal of bounding the generalization error. The paper states that it is a deterministic mapping, but it does not provide a concrete example of how such a selection is made in practice. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to fully grasp the implications of this definition and its role in the overall framework. This is a weakness because it makes the paper less accessible to readers who are not already familiar with the intricacies of stability analysis. The paper also mentions "mild measure-theoretic conditions" without specifying what these conditions are, which further reduces the clarity of the presentation. These conditions are crucial for the existence of the random variable in Definition 3.1, and the lack of specificity makes it difficult for readers to fully understand the theoretical underpinnings of the framework. These limitations, while not invalidating the contributions of the paper, highlight areas where further research and development are needed to fully realize the potential of the proposed framework.
