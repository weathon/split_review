## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for fine-tuning flow-matching generative models to enforce physical constraints and solve inverse problems in scientific systems. The core idea revolves around a differentiable post-training procedure that minimizes weak-form residuals of governing partial differential equations (PDEs), thereby promoting physical consistency and adherence to boundary conditions without distorting the underlying learned distribution. To infer unknown physical inputs, such as source terms, material parameters, or boundary data, the authors augment the generative process with a learnable latent parameter predictor and propose a joint optimization strategy. This approach leverages the adjoint-matching framework to reformulate reward fine-tuning for flow-based generative models as a stochastic optimal control problem, steering the base generative process towards high-reward samples by modifying the learned vector field. The method is validated on several canonical PDE problems, including Darcy flow, linear elasticity, Helmholtz, and Stokes flow, demonstrating improved satisfaction of physical constraints and accurate recovery of latent coefficients. The authors also showcase the cross-domain utility of their approach by fine-tuning natural-image models, suggesting the framework's potential beyond scientific simulations. The key methodological innovation lies in the use of weak-form residuals for enforcing physical constraints and the application of adjoint matching for fine-tuning, which allows for a stable and efficient training process. The empirical findings demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework in generating physically valid field solutions and estimating hidden parameters, even in the presence of noisy data. The overall significance of this work lies in its ability to bridge the gap between generative modeling and physics-informed learning, offering a promising approach for generating solutions that respect complex physical laws in a data-driven yet physics-aware manner. By leveraging flow-matching models and incorporating physical constraints through weak-form residuals, the method offers a novel approach to generating physically plausible solutions. The use of adjoint matching for fine-tuning is a technically sound approach, and the theoretical grounding provided through the stochastic optimal control formulation adds rigor to the proposed method. The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework across various PDE problems, including Darcy flow, linear elasticity, Helmholtz, and Stokes flow. The ability to reduce residuals and jointly generate solution-parameter pairs is a valuable contribution. The application to natural-image models showcases the potential for cross-domain utility, suggesting that the framework is not limited to scientific simulations.

## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling framework, several weaknesses warrant attention. First, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with existing methods for enforcing PDE constraints in generative models. While the authors compare against variants of their own method and PBFM, they do not include a direct quantitative comparison against standard Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) trained with a PDE residual loss or other methods that directly incorporate PDE constraints during training. This absence makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed post-training approach compared to methods that integrate physics constraints during training. For example, the experimental section (Section 4) primarily compares against "Base AM", "Base AM + $\varphi$", and "PBFM", but does not include a standard PINN baseline. This is a significant limitation, as it does not allow for a clear understanding of the performance gains achieved by the proposed approach compared to established methods. The lack of comparison against standard PINNs makes it harder to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed post-training approach compared to methods that integrate physics constraints during training. This is a high-confidence issue, as it is directly observable from the experimental results. Second, the paper lacks detailed implementation specifics for each experiment. While the authors mention the use of U-FNO for PDEs and a DiT-based latent FM for images (Section 4), they do not provide specific details on the neural network architectures used for each PDE, the choice of optimizers, learning rates, and the number of training epochs. Appendix E.3 contains a table of hyperparameter values, but it does not specify which values correspond to which experiment. This lack of detail hinders reproducibility and makes it difficult to understand the practical aspects of the method. The sensitivity analysis is limited to the $\lambda_x$, $\lambda_\alpha$, and $\lambda_f$ ablation studies in Section 4.1 and Figure 3. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper does not provide the necessary implementation details. Third, the paper does not thoroughly explore the limitations of the proposed method. The experiments primarily focus on linear PDEs, and there is no systematic evaluation of performance on highly nonlinear PDEs or robustness to noisy or incomplete boundary conditions. While the "Guidance on Sparse Observations" experiment (Section 4.2) touches on sensitivity to limited data, it is focused on latent parameters, not boundary conditions. The "Linear Elasticity" experiment (Section 4.3) introduces a "modified lower-boundary amplitude" to induce misspecification, but it is a controlled change, not a test of robustness to noisy or incomplete boundary conditions. The lack of exploration on more complex PDEs and boundary conditions limits the understanding of the method's robustness and applicability to real-world scenarios. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper does not provide the necessary experiments to demonstrate robustness. Fourth, the paper lacks a rigorous theoretical analysis of the proposed method's convergence properties, stability, and error bounds. While the method is grounded in the adjoint-matching framework, a detailed theoretical analysis of convergence, stability, and error bounds, particularly concerning the weak-form residuals and the proposed noise schedule scaling, is missing. The paper focuses on empirical validation, but a more in-depth theoretical treatment would be beneficial. The lack of a rigorous theoretical analysis weakens the understanding of the method's fundamental properties and limitations. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper does not provide the necessary theoretical analysis. Fifth, the computational cost of the fine-tuning procedure, especially the adjoint matching component, is not discussed in detail. While the "Darcy Flow" experiment (Section 4.1) mentions that "fine-tuning on noisy Darcy requires only 20 gradient steps... and completes in under 15 minutes on a single NVIDIA L40S", this provides only a high-level estimate for one specific case and lacks a detailed breakdown of the computational cost for each step and a scalability analysis. The lack of detailed computational cost analysis makes it difficult for practitioners to assess the practical applicability and resource requirements of the method. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper does not provide the necessary computational analysis. Finally, the paper does not fully explore the method's sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, such as the regularization strength and the parameters of the noise schedule. While the paper includes ablation studies for some hyperparameters ($\lambda_x$, $\lambda_\alpha$, $\lambda_f$), there is no systematic analysis of the sensitivity to the noise schedule parameter $\kappa$ or a broader range of hyperparameter values. The limited sensitivity analysis makes it harder to understand the robustness of the method and to provide guidance on hyperparameter selection. This is a medium-confidence issue, as the paper does include some sensitivity analysis, but it is not comprehensive.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with existing physics-informed machine learning techniques. This should involve a quantitative analysis against methods that directly incorporate PDE constraints during training, such as those using physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) or adjoint-based optimization. The comparison should not only focus on the final accuracy of the solutions but also on the computational cost and scalability of the different approaches. For example, the authors could compare their method against a PINN trained with a loss function that includes the PDE residual, and also against methods that use adjoint equations to enforce constraints during training. This would provide a clearer understanding of the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed post-training approach compared to methods that enforce constraints throughout the training process. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required by their method and how it scales with the complexity of the PDE and the size of the training data. Second, the paper would greatly benefit from a more detailed description of the implementation specifics for each experiment. This should include the exact neural network architectures used for each PDE, including the number of layers, the number of neurons per layer, and the activation functions. The authors should also specify the optimizers used, the learning rates, and the number of training epochs. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to include a discussion on the sensitivity of the method to these hyperparameters. For example, the authors could perform an ablation study to show how the performance of the method changes with different learning rates or different network architectures. This would enhance the reproducibility of the results and provide more insights into the practical aspects of the proposed method. The authors should also provide details on the data preprocessing steps, the batch size used for training, and the hardware used for the experiments. Third, the authors should explore the limitations of the proposed method in more detail. This should include an analysis of the method's performance on highly complex or nonlinear PDEs, as well as its sensitivity to the choice of initial conditions and boundary conditions. The authors could consider testing their method on PDEs that exhibit chaotic behavior or have strong nonlinearities. They should also investigate how the method's performance degrades with noisy or incomplete boundary conditions. This would provide a more complete understanding of the robustness of the method and its applicability to real-world problems. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the potential failure modes of the method and provide guidance on how to mitigate these issues. This would make the paper more impactful and useful for the broader research community. Fourth, the authors should provide a more in-depth theoretical analysis of the proposed method. While the empirical results are promising, a rigorous investigation into the convergence properties of the fine-tuning process is needed. Specifically, it would be valuable to explore how the choice of the reward function and the adjoint matching procedure affect the convergence rate and the stability of the fine-tuned model. Analyzing the error bounds of the approximated solutions and the impact of the noise schedule on the final results would also strengthen the theoretical foundation of the work. Furthermore, a discussion on the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the regularization strength and the parameters of the noise schedule, would be beneficial. This analysis should include a discussion of how these parameters affect the trade-off between the accuracy of the solution and the preservation of the base model's distribution. Fifth, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the computational cost and scalability of the proposed method. The authors should provide a breakdown of the computational resources required for each step of the fine-tuning process, including the pre-training phase, the adjoint matching phase, and the sampling phase. The authors should also discuss the memory requirements of the method and how they scale with the size of the problem. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis of the method to hyperparameter choices, such as the regularization strength and the parameters of the noise schedule, would be beneficial. This could involve exploring the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the quality of the generated solutions and the convergence of the fine-tuning process. This would help to identify optimal hyperparameter configurations and provide guidance for practitioners. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the proposed framework in scenarios where the base model is significantly misspecified or when the physical constraints are highly complex. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed sensitivity analysis of the method to hyperparameter choices, such as the regularization strength and the parameters of the noise schedule. This analysis should include a discussion of how these parameters affect the trade-off between the accuracy of the solution and the preservation of the base model's distribution.
