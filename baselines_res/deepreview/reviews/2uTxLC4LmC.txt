## Summary:

This paper introduces Intervened Preference Optimization (IPO), a novel method designed to enhance the safety of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) by focusing on the safety of the reasoning process itself, rather than solely on the final output. The authors observe that even when the final responses of LRMs are safe, the intermediate reasoning steps can contain harmful content, which poses a significant risk, especially in high-stakes applications. To address this, IPO identifies and replaces 'compliance cues'—indicators of potential unsafe reasoning trajectories—with 'safety triggers' that encourage safe reasoning paths. This is achieved through a preference learning framework, where the model is trained to prefer reasoning trajectories that incorporate safety triggers over those with compliance cues. The method involves identifying these cues and triggers using a combination of manual annotation and automated detection with large language models, specifically GPT-4o. The core of IPO lies in its iterative process of generating reasoning trajectories, identifying compliance cues, substituting them with safety triggers, and then training the model using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to favor the corrected trajectories. The empirical evaluation of IPO is comprehensive, covering multiple models and benchmarks, including jailbreak and adversarial safety benchmarks. The results demonstrate a significant improvement in safety, with a relative reduction of over 30% in harmfulness, while maintaining strong performance across diverse reasoning tasks. The authors also provide valuable insights into the characteristics of safe reasoning, identifying critical steps such as safety triggers and compliance cues, which are not only theoretically interesting but also practically useful for developing safer LRMs. The paper's focus on the reasoning process, rather than just the output, represents a significant advancement in the field of AI safety, and the introduction of IPO as a method to enforce safe reasoning paths is a creative and effective solution to the problem of low rollout diversity in reinforcement learning approaches. Overall, the paper makes a compelling case for the importance of reasoning safety and offers a practical approach to enhancing the safety of LRMs, which is particularly relevant as these models are increasingly deployed in complex and potentially high-stakes applications.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths, primarily centered around its novel approach to LRM safety and the thoroughness of its empirical evaluation. The most significant contribution is the shift in focus from output safety to reasoning safety. By recognizing that unsafe intermediate reasoning steps can persist even when final outputs are safe, the authors address a critical gap in current safety alignment methods. This focus is particularly important as LRMs are deployed in more complex and potentially high-stakes applications where unsafe intermediate steps could lead to harmful outcomes. The introduction of Intervened Preference Optimization (IPO) is a novel contribution that combines insights from preference learning with safety alignment. The method is innovative in its approach to substituting compliance steps with safety triggers, which is a creative way to enforce safe reasoning paths. This approach is not only theoretically sound but also practically effective, as demonstrated by the empirical results. The use of preference learning to enforce safe reasoning paths is a creative solution to the problem of low rollout diversity in reinforcement learning approaches. The paper also provides valuable insights into the characteristics of safe reasoning, identifying critical steps such as safety triggers and compliance cues. These insights are not only theoretically interesting but also practically useful for developing safer LRMs. The empirical evaluation is comprehensive, covering multiple models and benchmarks, including jailbreak and adversarial safety benchmarks. The results demonstrate a significant improvement in safety, with a relative reduction of over 30% in harmfulness, which is a substantial achievement. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of IPO, comparing it to other alignment methods like GRPO, and showing that IPO is computationally comparable. The paper is well-written and clearly explains the motivation, methodology, and results. The figures and tables are effective in conveying complex information, and the overall presentation is of high quality. The inclusion of an ablation study on the compliance cue detector further strengthens the paper by demonstrating the robustness of the method to different detection models. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach, technical innovation, empirical rigor, and clear presentation.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to enhancing LRM safety, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the paper's reliance on GPT-4o for identifying compliance cues. As stated in Section 3.4, the authors automate the detection of compliance cues by prompting GPT-4o with few-shot examples. While they validate GPT-4o's identification against manual annotation, obtaining a consistency rate over 80%, this reliance on a single, closed-source model introduces potential biases and inconsistencies. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the results to different prompts or configurations used with GPT-4o, which could significantly impact the identified safety triggers and compliance cues. This dependency on GPT-4o, a model whose understanding of safety may evolve or differ across contexts, limits the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method. The lack of transparency in GPT-4o's decision-making process also makes it challenging to debug or improve the system. Furthermore, the paper does not explore alternative methods for compliance cue detection, such as rule-based systems or other large language models, which could provide a more robust and transparent approach. This reliance on GPT-4o is a significant limitation, as it introduces a potential point of failure and makes it difficult to assess the consistency of the method across different versions or deployments of the model. My analysis confirms that the paper does not address the potential for GPT-4o's biases to influence the results, which is a critical oversight. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational cost of IPO. While the authors claim that the overall cost is comparable to other alignment methods, as mentioned in Section 4.3, the paper does not provide a detailed breakdown of the time and resources required for each step of the IPO process. The paper mentions the need for multiple generations to identify safety triggers and compliance cues, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the time complexity of each step, the memory requirements, or the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the overall computational cost. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, especially for large-scale models or real-time applications. The paper does mention that IPO requires at most 14 generations, while GRPO requires at least 40, but this is a high-level comparison and does not provide a granular view of the computational demands of each step. The paper also lacks a discussion of strategies to reduce the number of generations needed for identifying cues or triggers beyond the iterative intervention. This lack of a detailed computational analysis is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to assess the scalability and practical applicability of the method. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion on the limitations of IPO, particularly in scenarios where safety triggers might not be readily available or where the distinction between safe and unsafe reasoning is ambiguous. The current discussion lacks a thorough exploration of edge cases where the method might fail or underperform. The paper's methodology hinges on the ability to identify distinct safety triggers and compliance cues, as described in Section 3.1 and 3.4, but it does not address how the method would handle situations where the unsafe reasoning is subtle or indirectly harmful, or where the safety triggers are not clearly defined or easily identifiable. The experimental evaluation primarily focuses on clear-cut adversarial scenarios, and the paper lacks a discussion on how IPO would perform in more nuanced situations where the distinction between safe and unsafe reasoning is less clear. This lack of a detailed discussion on the limitations of IPO is a significant oversight, as it does not provide a balanced and realistic assessment of the method's applicability. My analysis confirms that the paper does not explore these edge cases, which is a critical limitation.


## Suggestions:

First, to mitigate the reliance on GPT-4o for identifying compliance cues, the authors should explore alternative methods for compliance cue detection. This could involve using multiple models, including open-source options, and comparing their performance. A comparative analysis of these different detection methods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and limitations of the current approach. Additionally, the authors should investigate the sensitivity of the results to different prompts and configurations used with GPT-4o. A more robust approach would involve a systematic evaluation of the impact of different prompt variations on the identified safety triggers and compliance cues. Furthermore, the authors could explore methods for incorporating human feedback or expert judgment into the process of identifying safety triggers, which could help to mitigate the biases and inconsistencies introduced by relying solely on a single model. This would also enhance the transparency and interpretability of the method. The authors should also consider exploring rule-based systems or other fundamentally different approaches to compliance cue detection, which could provide a more transparent and reliable alternative to relying on a single large language model. This would also help to address the concerns about the closed-source nature of GPT-4o and the lack of transparency in its decision-making process. Second, to improve the analysis of computational cost, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time and resources required for each step of the IPO process. This should include the time complexity of each step, the memory requirements, and the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the overall computational cost. The authors should also compare the computational cost of IPO with other alignment methods, providing a more comprehensive analysis of the trade-offs between safety and computational efficiency. This analysis should include a discussion of the scalability of the method to larger models and datasets, as well as its suitability for real-time applications. Furthermore, the authors should explore potential optimizations to reduce the computational cost of the method, such as using more efficient sampling techniques or parallelizing the computation. This would help to make the method more practical and applicable to a wider range of scenarios. Finally, to address the limitations of IPO, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of scenarios where the method might fail or underperform. This should include an analysis of edge cases where safety triggers are not readily available or where the distinction between safe and unsafe reasoning is ambiguous. The authors should also explore alternative approaches for handling these scenarios, such as using more sophisticated methods for identifying safety triggers or incorporating additional safety mechanisms. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the robustness of the method to adversarial attacks, which could potentially bypass the safety triggers. A more comprehensive analysis of these limitations would provide a more balanced and realistic assessment of the method's applicability and guide future research in this area. The authors should also consider incorporating a mechanism to detect and handle subtle or indirectly harmful reasoning, which is not explicitly addressed in the current method. This could involve using more advanced techniques for analyzing the reasoning process, such as semantic analysis or logical reasoning verification.
