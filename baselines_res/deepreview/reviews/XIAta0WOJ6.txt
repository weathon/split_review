## Summary:

This paper makes a significant contribution to the field of bilevel optimization by proposing a novel class of methods, F2SA-p, that leverages pth-order finite difference approximations to improve the upper complexity bounds for finding an ε-stationary point. The authors build upon the existing first-order method, F2SA, and demonstrate that by using higher-order approximations, they can achieve a better convergence rate, particularly for highly smooth problems. The paper provides a rigorous theoretical analysis, including both upper and lower complexity bounds, and shows that the proposed method is nearly optimal when p = Ω(log ε⁻¹ / log log ε⁻¹). The authors also conduct experiments on a learn-to-regularize problem using the '20 Newsgroup' dataset, comparing F2SA-p with existing methods. However, the paper's primary focus is theoretical, and it lacks a comprehensive empirical evaluation to validate the practical performance of the proposed methods, especially for small values of p, such as p=1. The gap between the upper and lower bounds for small p, and the limitations of the current analysis techniques, are acknowledged but not fully addressed, leaving room for future research. Overall, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear and structured presentation of its contributions and limitations.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant theoretical contributions, several limitations and gaps in the analysis and empirical validation are evident. One of the primary concerns is the lack of comprehensive empirical validation. While the paper does include an 'EXPERIMENTS' section, the experiments are limited in scope and do not fully address the practical performance of the proposed F2SA-p methods. Specifically, the experiments focus on a single 'learn-to-regularize' problem using the '20 Newsgroup' dataset, and the results are presented in a limited format. This makes it difficult to assess the real-world applicability of the methods, especially in terms of robustness to noisy gradients and hyperparameter sensitivity. The absence of a broader range of benchmark problems and a more detailed experimental setup leaves the reader with an incomplete picture of the practical trade-offs between higher-order methods and first-order methods. Another critical limitation is the gap between the upper and lower complexity bounds, particularly for small values of p, such as p=1. The paper acknowledges this gap in the 'Open problems' section, but it does not provide a detailed discussion of the potential reasons for this discrepancy or suggest specific directions for future research to address it. This gap is significant because the p=1 case is a common scenario in many applications, and the current analysis does not fully explain why the proposed method does not achieve the optimal rate in this setting. The analysis for odd values of p is also less tight, which raises questions about the potential for further improvement in the bounds. The paper's focus on the nonconvex-strongly-convex setting is another limitation. While this setting is important, the paper does not discuss the potential challenges and limitations of extending the proposed methods to other settings, such as nonconvex-nonconvex bilevel optimization problems. This omission leaves the reader with an incomplete understanding of the method's applicability and robustness in more general scenarios. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the assumptions made and their practical implications. For instance, the assumption of high-order smoothness for the lower-level variable is crucial for the theoretical improvements, but the paper does not explore how this assumption might affect the practical performance of the methods. A more in-depth analysis of these assumptions and their impact would provide a more complete picture of the theoretical landscape and the limitations of the proposed method.
