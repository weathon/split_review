## Summary:

This paper introduces Proper Velocity Neural Networks (PVNNs), a novel approach to hyperbolic neural networks that leverages the Proper Velocity (PV) model. The authors' core contribution lies in establishing a complete Riemannian geometric framework for the PV space, including the derivation of closed-form expressions for key operations such as the exponential and logarithmic maps, geodesic distance, and parallel transport. This theoretical foundation allows for the development of fundamental building blocks for neural networks within the PV space, including Multinomial Logistic Regression (MLR), Fully Connected (FC) layers, convolutional layers, activation functions, and batch normalization. The authors motivate their work by highlighting the numerical instabilities that can arise in existing hyperbolic models, particularly the Poincaré ball, and propose the PV space as an alternative due to its unconstrained nature. Empirically, the paper validates the stability and effectiveness of PVNNs through a series of experiments across four distinct tasks: numerical stability analysis, image classification on CIFAR datasets, graph node classification on several benchmark datasets, and genomic sequence learning. The numerical stability experiments demonstrate the robustness of PVNNs in scenarios where other models exhibit issues. In image classification, PVNNs achieve competitive performance compared to Poincaré-based methods. In graph learning, PVNNs show strong performance on some datasets but underperform on others, particularly Cora. Similarly, in genomic sequence learning, PVNNs demonstrate effectiveness. Overall, the paper presents a theoretically sound and empirically validated approach to hyperbolic neural networks using the PV space, offering a potential solution to the numerical instability issues of existing models. However, the paper also reveals some limitations, particularly in the performance of PVNNs on certain datasets and the lack of a detailed analysis of computational complexity and scalability, which warrant further investigation.

The derivation of closed-form expressions for the exponential and logarithmic maps, geodesic distance, and parallel transport provides a solid foundation for building neural networks in this space. This is not a trivial undertaking, and the authors have clearly demonstrated a strong understanding of the underlying mathematical principles. Furthermore, the development of fundamental building blocks for neural networks within the PV space, such as MLR, FC, convolutional, activation, and batch normalization layers, is a crucial step towards making this space practical for deep learning applications. The authors have successfully translated the theoretical framework into concrete implementations, which is a testament to their technical expertise. The paper's focus on numerical stability is also a major strength. The authors clearly articulate the limitations of existing hyperbolic models, particularly the Poincaré ball, in terms of numerical instability, and they propose the PV space as a potential solution. The experiments in Section 6.1, which demonstrate the superior numerical stability of PVNNs compared to Poincaré ball and hyperboloid models, provide strong empirical support for their claims. ## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, while the authors acknowledge the isometric relationship between the PV space and the Poincaré ball, the practical implications of using PV space over the Poincaré ball are not fully explored. As the reviewer 1 correctly points out, the isometry implies a mathematical equivalence in the Riemannian formalism, and the core operations, such as exponential and logarithmic maps, parallel transport, and geodesic distances, are fundamentally similar due to this isometry. While the paper does highlight the numerical stability advantages of PV space, a more detailed analysis of the computational benefits or drawbacks of using PV space compared to the Poincaré ball is needed. Specifically, a comparison of the condition numbers of the Jacobians of the exponential and logarithmic maps in both spaces, as well as an analysis of the gradient behavior during training, would be beneficial. This lack of a detailed computational analysis is a recurring theme, as noted by reviewer 2, who points out the absence of a breakdown of the time and space complexity of the core operations and how they scale with dimensionality and curvature. This is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of PVNNs, especially in high-dimensional settings. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of PVNNs. This should include a breakdown of the time and space complexity of the core operations, such as the exponential and logarithmic maps, and how these scale with the dimensionality of the input data and the curvature parameter. For instance, the authors could provide a table or a graph showing the runtime of these operations for different input sizes and curvature values. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to compare the computational cost of PVNNs with other hyperbolic neural network models, highlighting the trade-offs between accuracy and computational efficiency. This analysis should also consider the memory footprint of the model, which is crucial for large-scale applications. The authors should also investigate the impact of different optimization techniques on the training time and convergence of PVNNs, such as adaptive learning rate methods or second-order optimization algorithms. Second, to address the performance issues on specific datasets, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the data's intrinsic geometry and how it interacts with the chosen hyperbolic space. The paper should investigate why the proposed method underperforms on the Cora and PubMed datasets compared to the hyperboloid model. This analysis should include a study of the δ-hyperbolicity of these datasets and how it relates to the performance of different hyperbolic models. The authors should also explore whether the specific structure of the graphs in these datasets, such as the presence of bottlenecks or high-degree nodes, affects the performance of the proposed method. Furthermore, the paper should investigate the impact of different curvature parameters on the performance of the proposed method, as the optimal curvature may vary depending on the dataset's intrinsic geometry. The authors should also consider using adaptive curvature parameters that are learned during training, as this may improve the performance of the proposed method on datasets with varying degrees of hyperbolicity. Third, the paper needs to include a more comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art methods, particularly those that have demonstrated strong performance on the chosen datasets. The absence of specific baselines, such as those mentioned in the original review, makes it difficult to assess the true performance of the proposed method. The authors should include a more thorough comparison with existing methods, including both hyperbolic and Euclidean models. This comparison should include a detailed analysis of the performance of the proposed method across different datasets and tasks, as well as an analysis of the computational cost and memory usage of the different methods. The paper should also provide a clear explanation of the differences between the proposed method and the existing baselines, highlighting the advantages and disadvantages of each approach. This would provide a more robust evaluation of the proposed method's effectiveness and its position within the existing literature. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of PVNNs and the potential challenges in applying them to real-world problems. This should include a discussion of the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the curvature parameter, the computational cost of training, and the potential for overfitting. The authors should also investigate the impact of numerical precision on the stability of the method, especially when using lower precision formats. For example, the authors could conduct experiments to evaluate the performance of PVNNs under different numerical precision settings and analyze the potential for numerical instability. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the potential limitations of PVNNs in handling very high-dimensional data or very large-scale datasets, and suggest potential strategies for addressing these limitations. This would provide a more complete picture of the applicability of PVNNs and guide future research in this area.
