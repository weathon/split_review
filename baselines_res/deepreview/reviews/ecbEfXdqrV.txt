## Summary:

This paper delves into the intriguing phenomenon of likelihood inversion in anomaly detection, specifically examining why deep generative models, which often assign higher likelihoods to anomalous data in image domains, do not exhibit this behavior as prominently in tabular data. The authors introduce a domain-agnostic definition of this counterintuitive phenomenon, enabling a consistent evaluation across different data types. Through extensive experimentation on 57 datasets from the ADBench benchmark, encompassing both tabular and CV/NLP embedding data, they demonstrate that likelihood-based anomaly detection using normalizing flows is effective and reliable for tabular data. Their core finding is that the counterintuitive phenomenon, where anomalous data receives higher likelihood scores, is significantly less prevalent in tabular data compared to image data. This observation is linked to the lower dimensionality and weaker feature correlations typically found in tabular data, which contrasts with the complex, high-dimensional structure of images. The authors employ normalizing flows, specifically NICE, to model the data distribution and use likelihood scores as the primary anomaly detection metric. They compare their approach against a range of baseline anomaly detection methods, demonstrating that their likelihood-based approach performs competitively. The paper also includes a theoretical analysis, supported by visualizations and statistical analysis, to further understand the phenomenon and its implications. The authors argue that the success of likelihood-based anomaly detection in tabular data can be attributed to the fact that tabular data often has a lower intrinsic dimensionality and weaker feature correlations compared to images, making it easier for normalizing flows to learn a meaningful data representation. This work contributes to a deeper understanding of the behavior of deep generative models in different data domains and provides valuable insights into the applicability of likelihood-based anomaly detection for tabular data. The authors' findings suggest that the effectiveness of likelihood as an anomaly score is not universal across data types and is highly dependent on the characteristics of the data and the model's ability to capture its underlying structure. This paper provides a valuable contribution to the field by highlighting the importance of considering data characteristics when applying deep generative models for anomaly detection.

It would be valuable to understand how the method performs with limited training data, which is a common scenario in real-world applications. The paper suggests that the counterintuitive phenomenon is less prevalent in tabular data due to lower dimensionality and weaker correlations, but it would be valuable to test this hypothesis on datasets that challenge this assumption. Fifth, what are the limitations of using likelihood as an anomaly score, and are there alternative metrics that could be more robust to the counterintuitive phenomenon? While the paper focuses on likelihood, it would be valuable to explore other metrics, such as the reconstruction error or the latent space distance, and compare their performance. Finally, what are the practical implications of the counterintuitive phenomenon for real-world anomaly detection tasks? The paper provides valuable insights into the behavior of deep generative models, but it would be useful to discuss how these insights can be translated into practical guidelines for practitioners.
