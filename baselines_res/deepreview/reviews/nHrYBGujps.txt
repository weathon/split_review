## Summary:

This paper introduces BIRD-INTERACT, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) in dynamic, multi-turn text-to-SQL scenarios that more closely mirror real-world database interactions. Unlike existing benchmarks that often rely on static contexts or limited operations, BIRD-INTERACT provides a comprehensive interaction environment that includes a hierarchical knowledge base, metadata, and a function-driven user simulator. This environment allows models to engage in more natural and iterative dialogues, handling ambiguities, receiving feedback, and following up on previous interactions. The benchmark features two distinct evaluation settings: c-Interact, which provides a structured conversational protocol, and a-Interact, which offers more autonomy to the model. The task suite covers the full spectrum of CRUD operations across both business intelligence and data management domains, incorporating ambiguous initial tasks and follow-up sub-tasks that require dynamic interaction. The authors' experiments demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models struggle with BIRD-INTERACT, highlighting the significant challenges posed by interactive text-to-SQL tasks and the need for improved strategic interaction capabilities in LLMs. The benchmark includes a full set of 600 tasks and a lite set of 300 tasks, designed to provide a comprehensive overview of model performance and facilitate detailed behavioral analysis. The authors also provide a function-driven user simulator that enhances the robustness and reliability of the benchmark, addressing limitations of previous simulators. The paper's findings underscore the importance of developing LLMs that can effectively engage in complex, multi-turn interactions to handle real-world database tasks. The benchmark is publicly available, promoting reproducibility and further research in the field. The authors also explore the impact of interaction test-time scaling (ITS), showing that performance improves with more interaction turns, further emphasizing the importance of interactive capabilities. The paper's contribution lies in its realistic simulation of database interactions, its comprehensive task suite, and its rigorous evaluation of state-of-the-art LLMs, revealing the limitations of current models in handling interactive text-to-SQL tasks.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the introduction of BIRD-INTERACT as a novel benchmark is a significant contribution. The benchmark's design, which includes a hierarchical knowledge base, metadata, and a function-driven user simulator, provides a more realistic and comprehensive evaluation environment compared to existing multi-turn text-to-SQL benchmarks. This is a crucial step forward, as it pushes the boundaries of how LLMs are evaluated in interactive database tasks. The inclusion of both business intelligence and data management tasks, covering the full CRUD spectrum, ensures a broad and relevant evaluation scope. The two distinct evaluation settings, c-Interact and a-Interact, allow for a comprehensive assessment of model capabilities in diverse interaction scenarios. The function-driven user simulator is another notable strength. This technical innovation enhances the robustness and reliability of the benchmark by providing a controlled and predictable interaction environment. The authors' experiments, which demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models struggle with BIRD-INTERACT, highlight the difficulty of the benchmark and the need for improved strategic interaction capabilities in LLMs. The fact that the benchmark is publicly available is also a significant strength, as it promotes reproducibility and further research in the field. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology, experimental setup, and results. The figures and tables are informative and effectively support the narrative. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the performance of different LLMs on the benchmark, offering valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of various models. The findings highlight the importance of effective interaction for achieving success in dynamic text-to-SQL tasks. The inclusion of both open-source (O3-Mini, Qwen-3-Coder-480B) and closed-source models in the evaluation is also a positive aspect, providing a more comprehensive view of the current landscape of LLMs.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant attention. First, while the paper introduces a novel benchmark, it lacks a detailed comparison with existing interactive text-to-SQL benchmarks in the main body of the paper. While the authors do mention some existing interactive text-to-SQL datasets in the introduction and discuss their limitations, a direct comparison highlighting the unique features of BIRD-INTERACT against specific benchmarks like TPTU, TPTSL, TAT, and Agent-SQL is missing from the main text. The comparison is relegated to the Appendix, which makes it less accessible and impactful. This omission weakens the paper's contribution by not clearly positioning BIRD-INTERACT within the existing landscape of interactive text-to-SQL benchmarks. Second, the paper does not include an evaluation of open-source, non-LLM-based models, such as Supersparrow, DeepCoder, or Dr.Spider. The evaluation is limited to LLMs, which restricts the scope of the benchmark's applicability and does not provide a comprehensive assessment of its difficulty across different types of systems. Including these models would provide valuable insights into the performance of different types of systems and demonstrate the benchmark's utility across a broader range of approaches. Third, the analysis of model performance in Section 5.2 is somewhat limited. While the paper notes that GPT-5 performs better in the a-Interact setting than in the c-Interact setting, the reasons behind this performance difference are not thoroughly explored. A more in-depth analysis of the impact of ambiguity resolution or the effectiveness of different interaction strategies would provide valuable insights. The paper does include a 'Memory Grafting' experiment, which provides some insight into GPT-5's communication abilities in *c*-Interact, but a more direct analysis of why *a*-Interact suits GPT-5 better could be added. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed error analysis, which is a significant omission. The paper focuses on overall success rates and normalized rewards, but it does not provide a breakdown of error types, such as syntax errors, semantic errors, or interaction management errors. This lack of granularity makes it difficult to pinpoint the exact weaknesses of the models and to guide future research. A detailed error analysis would help identify common failure modes and areas for improvement in interactive text-to-SQL systems. Fifth, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed benchmark. While the conclusion briefly mentions future work, there is no dedicated section that addresses potential shortcomings, such as the complexity of the interactive environment or the potential for bias in the evaluation metrics. This omission weakens the paper by not providing a balanced perspective on the work. Finally, the evaluation is limited to a specific set of LLMs, and it is unclear how models with different architectures or training data would perform on the benchmark. While the evaluated models represent a range of architectures and training data, the absence of models specifically fine-tuned for SQL tasks is a limitation. This restricts the generalizability of the findings and makes it difficult to draw broad conclusions about the capabilities of LLMs in interactive text-to-SQL scenarios. Additionally, the paper does not discuss the potential biases present in the dataset or how these biases might affect the evaluation results. If the dataset contains a disproportionate number of tasks related to a specific domain or type of database operation, it could lead to skewed performance results. The lack of a bias analysis raises concerns about the fairness and representativeness of the benchmark. These limitations, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the conclusions and generalizability of the findings. My confidence in these identified weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of specific analyses.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a detailed comparison with existing interactive text-to-SQL benchmarks in the main body of the paper. This comparison should be presented in a table that outlines the key differences in terms of task complexity, interaction protocols, and evaluation metrics. Specifically, the paper should compare BIRD-INTERACT with benchmarks like TPTU, TPTSL, TAT, and Agent-SQL, highlighting the unique features and advantages of BIRD-INTERACT. This would help to clarify the novelty of the benchmark and justify its need in the field. Second, the authors should include an evaluation of open-source, non-LLM-based models in their experiments. This would demonstrate the applicability of the benchmark across different types of systems and provide a more complete picture of the challenges it poses. Specifically, the paper should evaluate models such as Supersparrow, DeepCoder, and Dr.Spider, and analyze their performance in both the c-Interact and a-Interact settings. This would help to identify the specific weaknesses of these models and highlight the areas where they struggle the most. Third, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the reasons behind the performance differences observed in Section 5.2, particularly why GPT-5 performs better in a-Interact than in c-Interact. This analysis should explore the impact of ambiguity resolution, the effectiveness of different interaction strategies, and the model's ability to handle the structured protocols of c-Interact. Fourth, the authors should include a detailed error analysis in the paper. This analysis should categorize the types of errors made by the models, such as syntax errors, semantic errors, or interaction management errors. This would help to identify the common failure modes of the models and suggest areas for improvement. The analysis should also include examples of failure cases, illustrating the types of errors that the models are making. Fifth, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed benchmark. This section should address potential shortcomings, such as the complexity of the interactive environment, the potential for bias in the evaluation metrics, and the generalizability of the results to real-world scenarios. This would provide a more balanced perspective on the work and help to guide future research in this area. Sixth, the authors should expand the evaluation to include a more diverse set of LLMs, including models with different architectures, training data, and fine-tuning strategies. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different models perform on the benchmark and help identify the factors that contribute to success or failure. Specifically, the authors should consider including models that are specifically fine-tuned for SQL tasks. Finally, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of potential biases in the dataset and discuss how these biases might affect the evaluation results. This should include an analysis of the distribution of tasks across different domains, database operations, and complexity levels. If the dataset is found to be biased, the authors should consider strategies for mitigating these biases, such as re-weighting tasks or augmenting the dataset with additional examples. These suggestions, which are directly linked to the identified weaknesses, would significantly enhance the paper's contribution and provide a more comprehensive and robust evaluation of interactive text-to-SQL systems.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe would further clarify the paper's contributions and limitations. First, could the authors provide more details on the function-driven user simulator? Specifically, how does it ensure robustness and reliability, and what are the specific mechanisms that prevent leakage of ground-truth information? Understanding the simulator's design and its limitations is crucial for assessing the validity of the benchmark. Second, how does the budget-constrained awareness mechanism affect the performance of different models, and what is the rationale behind the specific budget settings used in the experiments? A more detailed explanation of the budget constraints and their impact on model behavior would be beneficial. Third, the paper mentions that interaction test-time scaling (ITS) is observed. Could the authors elaborate on the implications of this finding for the development of interactive text-to-SQL systems? Specifically, how does the observed scaling behavior impact the design of interactive agents and what are the potential benefits of increased interaction turns? Fourth, could the authors provide more details on the types of ambiguities introduced in the benchmark and how they compare to those in existing benchmarks? A more detailed analysis of the ambiguities would help to understand the specific challenges posed by BIRD-INTERACT. Fifth, could the authors provide a more detailed breakdown of the types of SQL queries included in the benchmark, including the frequency of different SQL operations (SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE) and the complexity of the queries? This would help to understand the scope and difficulty of the benchmark. Finally, what are the authors' plans for future work to address the limitations of the benchmark, such as the lack of a detailed error analysis and the potential for bias in the dataset? Understanding the authors' future directions would be valuable for assessing the long-term impact of this work.
