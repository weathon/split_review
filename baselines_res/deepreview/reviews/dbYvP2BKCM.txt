## Summary:

This paper introduces ZNet, a novel deep learning model designed to automatically construct valid instrumental variables (IVs) from observed data. The core idea is to decompose observed variables into latent representations that satisfy the key assumptions of IV analysis: relevance, exclusion restriction, and unconfoundedness. The authors propose a neural network architecture that learns these representations by optimizing a loss function specifically designed to enforce these IV conditions. The method aims to address the challenge of unobserved confounding in causal inference by either recovering known instruments when they exist or constructing proxy latent instruments when explicit instruments are unavailable. The empirical evaluation of ZNet involves semi-synthetic datasets, where the ground truth causal relationships are known, allowing for a quantitative assessment of the method's ability to recover these relationships. The experiments compare ZNet's performance against other IV methods and a baseline that ignores confounding. The results suggest that ZNet can effectively recover ground-truth instruments and construct proxy latent instruments that reduce bias due to unobserved confounding. The paper's significance lies in its potential to automate the challenging process of identifying and validating IVs, which is a crucial step in causal inference from observational data. By leveraging deep learning, the authors aim to provide a more flexible and scalable approach to IV discovery compared to traditional methods. However, the paper also acknowledges the inherent limitations of IV estimation, particularly the difficulty in guaranteeing the validity of the learned instruments without strong assumptions about the data generating process. The authors emphasize that their method provides a practical framework for estimating causal effects, but further research is needed to address the theoretical limitations and improve the robustness of the approach. The paper contributes to the growing body of work on causal inference using machine learning, offering a new perspective on the problem of instrument discovery. The authors highlight the potential of their method to be applied in various domains where causal inference is important, such as economics, social sciences, and healthcare. The paper also discusses the potential for extending the framework to handle more complex scenarios, such as time-varying treatments and non-linear causal relationships, which are important directions for future research. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to automating IV discovery, but also acknowledges the need for further research to address the theoretical and practical challenges involved.

## Weaknesses:

My analysis reveals several significant weaknesses in the paper, primarily concerning the theoretical grounding of the method and the robustness of the experimental results. A major concern is the limited theoretical analysis supporting the claim that the learned representations, Z and C, truly satisfy the instrumental variable assumptions, particularly the unconfoundedness assumption. While the paper introduces Lemma 1 and a loss function designed to enforce unconfoundedness, it does not provide a rigorous proof or a clear set of conditions under which the learned latent variables would provably satisfy the IV assumptions. The method relies on decorrelating Z with the residuals of a predictor of Y, but this procedure does not guarantee independence from all unobserved confounders, not just the residuals of the specific predictor used. This is a critical limitation, as the validity of the IV approach hinges on the unconfoundedness assumption. The paper acknowledges the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem and the lack of general identifiability guarantees for IV estimation, but it does not fully address the implications of this limitation. The lack of a strong theoretical foundation raises concerns about the reliability of the learned instruments and the validity of the causal estimates obtained using ZNet. This weakness is supported by the paper's own statements in Section 3, where it introduces Lemma 1, and in Section 7, where it acknowledges the limitations of IV estimation. The experimental results also raise concerns about the robustness and reliability of the proposed method. ZNet's performance is not consistently better than other baselines, and the standard deviations are quite large, although not explicitly reported in the main table. The paper lacks a thorough analysis of why ZNet performs better in some cases and worse in others. It is also unclear how sensitive the method is to the choice of hyperparameters and the architecture of the neural network. The lack of consistent performance across different datasets and the large standard deviations raise concerns about the robustness and reliability of the proposed method. Furthermore, the paper's introduction of ZNet and its underlying principles could benefit from clearer explanations. While the paper provides a high-level description of the network architecture and training process, specific details like the exact number of neurons in each layer, the specific optimization algorithm used (beyond gradient descent), and a more detailed explanation of how the network structure enforces exclusion restriction could be clearer. The connection between loss functions and IV assumptions is explained, but could be further elaborated. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to fully understand the inner workings of the model and how it achieves its results. While comparing against other IV methods is relevant, including comparisons with non-IV methods that address confounding would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of ZNet's strengths and weaknesses. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more thorough discussion of the limitations of ZNet and potential areas for future research. The current approach relies on a heuristic of decorrelating the learned representation Z with the residuals of a predictor of Y. However, this does not guarantee that Z is independent of all unobserved confounders. A more formal analysis is needed to establish conditions under which the learned latent variables Z and C would provably satisfy the instrumental variable assumptions, particularly the unconfoundedness assumption. This could involve exploring the properties of the learned representations and their relationship to the underlying data generating process. For example, the authors could investigate whether the decorrelation procedure effectively removes all spurious correlations between Z and unobserved confounders, or if there are cases where residual confounding remains. This could involve a more in-depth analysis of the network architecture and the loss function, and how they interact to achieve this goal. Second, the experimental results need to be strengthened.
