## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling theoretical and empirical investigation into the injectivity properties of decoder-only transformer language models. The authors rigorously demonstrate that these models, despite their complex non-linearities, almost surely produce distinct last-token representations for different input sequences. This finding is significant because it challenges the common assumption that transformers, due to their many-to-one mappings, are inherently lossy. The core of the paper lies in its mathematical proofs, which establish that the mapping from input tokens to the final hidden state is injective with probability one. This injectivity is shown to hold across initialization and training, a result that is both novel and impactful. The authors then leverage this theoretical insight to develop SIFT (Sequence Inversion via Hidden States), an algorithm capable of reconstructing the exact input sequence from the hidden states at any given layer. SIFT operates by exploiting the injectivity property, iteratively testing candidate tokens and matching their generated hidden states to the target state. The algorithm's linear time complexity with respect to vocabulary size is a key feature, making it potentially practical for real-world applications. The paper's empirical validation is extensive, involving collision search experiments across multiple layers and model architectures, including different sizes of Gemma and GPT-2, as well as Llama-3.1, Mistral, and Phi models. These experiments provide strong evidence supporting the theoretical claims, demonstrating the robustness of the injectivity property in practical settings. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of SIFT in reconstructing original input sequences from hidden states, showcasing its potential for model interpretability and input reconstruction. The paper also explores the robustness of SIFT to quantization noise, showing that it can still accurately reconstruct inputs even when the model weights are quantized to FP4. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to our understanding of transformer models, providing a theoretical foundation for their behavior and presenting a practical algorithm that leverages this understanding. The findings have implications for model interpretability, security, and the broader field of machine learning.

## Strengths:

This paper's primary strength lies in its rigorous theoretical framework, which provides a novel and insightful perspective on the behavior of decoder-only transformer language models. The authors have successfully demonstrated, through formal proofs and lemmas, that these models are almost surely injective, meaning that different input sequences almost certainly produce distinct last-token representations. This is a significant contribution because it challenges the prevailing view that transformers are inherently lossy due to their non-linearities and many-to-one mappings. The mathematical proofs are well-structured and easy to follow, providing a solid foundation for the paper's claims. The authors' ability to establish this injectivity property across initialization and training is particularly impressive, as it suggests that this property is inherent to the architecture itself and not merely a consequence of specific training conditions. Furthermore, the paper's empirical validation is exceptionally thorough. The authors conduct extensive experiments on a wide range of state-of-the-art models, including different sizes of Gemma and GPT-2, as well as Llama-3.1, Mistral, and Phi models. The collision search experiments, which involve comparing the hidden states of different input sequences, provide strong empirical support for the theoretical claims. These experiments demonstrate that, in practice, collisions are extremely rare, confirming the robustness of the injectivity property. The inclusion of experiments across multiple layers and model architectures further strengthens the paper's findings. The introduction of the SIFT algorithm is another major strength of this work. SIFT leverages the proven injectivity property to efficiently reconstruct the original input sequence from the hidden states at any given layer. The algorithm's linear time complexity with respect to vocabulary size is a key advantage, making it potentially practical for real-world applications. The experimental results demonstrate that SIFT can accurately reconstruct input sequences, further validating the theoretical findings. The paper is also exceptionally well-written, with clear explanations of complex mathematical concepts and a logical flow that makes the theoretical results accessible. The authors provide sufficient background and motivation to understand the problem and their contribution, and they also provide a good overview of related work. The combination of strong theoretical results, extensive empirical validation, a practical algorithm, and clear writing makes this a significant contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents compelling theoretical and empirical results, there are several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's focus on decoder-only transformer models limits the generalizability of its findings. As the authors explicitly state, their analysis is specific to 'standard decoder-only Transformer language models'. The theoretical proofs and the SIFT algorithm are tailored to this architecture, and the paper does not address how the absence of a decoder's autoregressive attention mechanism might affect the injectivity proof. Specifically, the proofs rely on the properties of causal attention, which is not present in encoder-only models. The interaction between the encoder and decoder in encoder-decoder models could introduce additional complexities that are not considered in this work. This is a significant limitation because encoder-only and encoder-decoder models are widely used in various natural language processing tasks, and it remains unclear whether the injectivity property holds under these different architectural configurations. This limitation is supported by the paper's explicit focus on decoder-only models in the introduction and throughout the paper, as well as the specific components of the model discussed in the method section, such as causal attention. My confidence in this limitation is high. The theoretical complexity of SIFT is indeed linear with respect to vocabulary size, as stated in Theorem 3.1, and the algorithm's description shows a loop iterating through the vocabulary. However, the experiments primarily use models with smaller vocabularies, such as GPT-2 Small, Mistral-7B-v0.1, and Llama-3.1-8B, which have vocabularies in the tens or hundreds of thousands. While the paper shows efficiency in terms of percentage of vocabulary explored, it lacks a direct analysis and experimental validation of scalability to models with vocabularies in the millions. This is a critical factor for its applicability to larger models, as the brute-force nature of the algorithm could become computationally expensive with very large vocabularies. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is supported by the theoretical complexity statement, the algorithm description, and the relatively small vocabulary sizes used in the experiments. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the memory requirements of the SIFT algorithm. The algorithm requires storing the observed hidden states, the recovered sequence, and the set of tested candidates, and the memory footprint of these components could be significant, especially for longer sequences and larger vocabularies. The paper does not provide any experimental results regarding the memory footprint of the algorithm, nor does it compare the memory requirements of SIFT with other inversion methods. Theorem 3.2 discusses the robustness of SIFT to perturbations in the hidden states, and the experiments with FP4-quantized models show that SIFT can still accurately reconstruct inputs in this setting. This evaluation should include a range of perturbation techniques, such as adding noise to the input sequence, modifying the hidden states, or manipulating the attention weights. The paper does not consider the impact of different types of adversarial attacks on the algorithm's performance, such as targeted attacks that aim to reconstruct a specific incorrect prompt. Finally, the paper's focus on the injectivity of the last token's representation might limit its applicability to other aspects of transformer behavior. The paper explicitly focuses on the injectivity of the 'last-token representations' in its theoretical analysis and experimental setup. While the SIFT algorithm leverages information from all hidden states, the theoretical foundation and the core argument of injectivity are centered on the last token. This raises questions about the broader applicability of the injectivity result, particularly for tasks that rely on analyzing the full sequence of hidden states rather than just the final token representation. ## Suggestions:

To strengthen this paper, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should extend their analysis to other types of transformer architectures beyond decoder-only models. Specifically, an analysis of encoder-only models, such as those used in sequence classification tasks, would be valuable. This would involve adapting the theoretical framework to account for the absence of autoregressive attention and investigating whether the injectivity property still holds. Furthermore, the authors should consider the impact of the encoder-decoder structure on injectivity. This could involve analyzing the interaction between the encoder and decoder layers and determining if the injectivity property is preserved through the entire model. Such an analysis would significantly enhance the generalizability of the paper's findings and make it more relevant to a wider range of transformer-based models. Second, the authors should provide a more thorough analysis of the SIFT algorithm's scalability with respect to vocabulary size. This could involve conducting experiments on models with significantly larger vocabularies, such as those used in large-scale language models. The paper should also include a theoretical analysis of the algorithm's time complexity as a function of vocabulary size, embedding dimension, and sequence length. This would provide a better understanding of the algorithm's limitations and help identify potential bottlenecks. Furthermore, the authors could explore alternative approaches to input reconstruction that do not rely on a brute-force search over the entire vocabulary. This could involve using approximate nearest neighbor search techniques or other optimization methods to improve the algorithm's efficiency and make it more practical for large-scale models. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the SIFT algorithm's memory requirements. This analysis should consider how memory usage scales with the input sequence length, the vocabulary size, and the number of layers in the transformer. It would be helpful to provide a breakdown of the memory usage for each component of the algorithm, including the storage of the transformer model itself, the input sequence, and the intermediate hidden states. This analysis should also consider the impact of batch size on memory usage, as larger batch sizes may require more memory. Understanding the memory requirements of the algorithm is crucial for determining its feasibility on different hardware platforms and for different input sizes. A discussion of potential memory optimization techniques would also be valuable. Fourth, the authors should include a more detailed analysis of the algorithm's robustness to adversarial attacks. This evaluation should include a range of perturbation techniques, such as adding noise to the input sequence, modifying the hidden states, or manipulating the attention weights. The authors should also consider the impact of different types of adversarial attacks on the algorithm's performance, such as targeted attacks that aim to reconstruct a specific incorrect prompt. A thorough analysis of the algorithm's robustness would provide a better understanding of its limitations and potential vulnerabilities. This analysis should also discuss potential defenses against adversarial attacks, such as adversarial training or input sanitization techniques. Finally, the authors should consider expanding their analysis to include the injectivity of intermediate hidden states, not just the final token representation. This could involve investigating whether the mapping from input sequences to the hidden states at each layer is also injective, or if there are layers where information is lost. Such an analysis would provide a more complete picture of how information flows through the transformer and would broaden the applicability of the injectivity result. For example, if intermediate layers are also injective, it might be possible to develop new methods for analyzing the internal representations of the model, which could lead to a better understanding of its behavior. Furthermore, exploring the conditions under which intermediate representations lose information could provide insights into the limitations of transformers and suggest ways to improve their architecture.
