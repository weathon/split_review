## Summary:

This paper introduces Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI), a model-based reinforcement learning algorithm designed to learn compact state representations in competitive games. The core idea behind GOEI is to infer a minimal state space that is sufficient for predicting game outcomes, thereby reducing the complexity of the environment and improving the efficiency of learning. The algorithm achieves this through a Bayesian framework, employing Dirichlet processes to handle an indefinite number of states and variational Bayesian inference to learn the model parameters. Specifically, the method involves a state reduction model that maps observations to a smaller set of core states, an action selection model that predicts actions based on these core states, and a reward prediction model that estimates rewards based on the core states and actions. The paper focuses on applying GOEI to the two-player card game Hol's der Geier, a game with a relatively small state space but complex strategic interactions. The authors demonstrate that GOEI can learn a state representation that leads to a near-optimal policy, while significantly reducing the number of states compared to the original observation space. The experimental results show that GOEI achieves performance comparable to the Nash equilibrium strategy, while using only a small fraction of the possible states. The paper's main contribution lies in the practical application and empirical validation of the GOEI algorithm in a complex game environment, showcasing its ability to learn effective state abstractions. While the algorithm itself is based on prior work, the detailed implementation, experimental setup, and analysis of the learned state representations provide valuable insights into the algorithm's behavior and potential. The paper also highlights the potential of GOEI for improving the explainability of reinforcement learning agents by focusing on a reduced set of core states that are relevant for decision-making. However, the paper also acknowledges the limitations of the current work, particularly the restriction to episodic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and the lack of comparison with other state abstraction techniques. Overall, this paper presents a compelling case for the use of GOEI in learning compact state representations for competitive games, while also identifying areas for future research and improvement.

## Strengths:

This paper presents a compelling application of the Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI) algorithm to the complex card game Hol's der Geier, demonstrating its ability to learn compact and effective state representations. The core strength of this work lies in its empirical validation of the GOEI algorithm in a realistic setting. The authors have successfully implemented the algorithm and shown that it can achieve near-optimal performance while significantly reducing the state space. This is a crucial contribution, as state abstraction is a fundamental challenge in reinforcement learning, particularly in complex environments. The paper's detailed description of the GOEI algorithm, including its state reduction model, action selection model, and reward prediction model, provides a clear understanding of its inner workings. The use of Dirichlet processes for state reduction is a novel approach that allows the model to handle an indefinite number of states, which is a significant advantage. Furthermore, the experimental results are well-presented and clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of the algorithm. The comparison with Q-learning highlights the benefits of using a state abstraction method like GOEI, as it achieves comparable performance with a much smaller state space. The paper also touches upon the potential of GOEI for improving the explainability of reinforcement learning agents, which is a promising direction for future research. The authors have clearly articulated the motivation for their work, highlighting the limitations of traditional Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) in handling complex state spaces and the need for more efficient state representations. The paper is well-written and organized, making it accessible to a wide audience. The focus on a practical application of the GOEI algorithm, rather than just a theoretical analysis, is a significant strength. The authors have successfully demonstrated the potential of GOEI for learning effective strategies in competitive games, and this practical validation is a valuable contribution to the field. The paper also provides a detailed analysis of the learned state representations, which offers insights into the algorithm's behavior and the underlying game dynamics. This analysis is crucial for understanding how the algorithm achieves state reduction and how it identifies the core information necessary for predicting game outcomes. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its clear presentation, its empirical validation of the GOEI algorithm, and its demonstration of the algorithm's effectiveness in a complex game environment.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling application of GOEI, several weaknesses limit the scope and generalizability of its findings. First, the paper's evaluation is limited to a single game environment, Hol's der Geier. As noted by multiple reviewers, this narrow focus raises concerns about the generalizability of the algorithm to other games or environments with different characteristics. The paper does not provide any evidence to suggest that GOEI would perform equally well in games with larger state spaces, different reward structures, or more complex action spaces. This lack of evaluation on diverse environments is a significant limitation, as it restricts the conclusions that can be drawn about the algorithm's broader applicability. The paper's experimental setup, while well-executed for Hol's der Geier, does not provide a comprehensive benchmark against other state abstraction techniques. As pointed out by several reviewers, the paper only compares GOEI with Q-learning and simple strategies, but it lacks a comparison with other established state aggregation or state abstraction methods, such as those based on bisimulation or other forms of state abstraction. This absence of comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative strengths and weaknesses of GOEI compared to existing approaches. Without such a comparison, it is hard to determine whether GOEI offers a significant advantage over other state abstraction techniques. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the GOEI algorithm. While the paper describes the algorithm's components, it does not provide a theoretical analysis of its time and space complexity, nor does it provide empirical data on its runtime and memory usage for different problem sizes. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical limitations of the method, especially for more complex environments. Understanding how the computational cost scales with the size of the state and action spaces is crucial for determining the algorithm's applicability to real-world problems. Furthermore, the paper's method is restricted to episodic MDPs, as noted by one reviewer. The formulation of the state reduction problem and the experimental setup are tailored to the episodic nature of the card game, and the paper does not discuss how GOEI would handle continuous tasks or non-episodic environments. This limitation restricts the applicability of the method to a specific class of problems. The paper also lacks a theoretical analysis of the GOEI algorithm. While the authors provide an intuitive explanation of the algorithm, they do not provide any formal guarantees on its performance or convergence. This lack of theoretical analysis makes it difficult to understand the algorithm's properties and limitations. A theoretical analysis would provide insights into the convergence properties of the algorithm, its sample complexity, and the trade-off between the level of state abstraction and the quality of the resulting policy. Finally, while the paper mentions the potential of GOEI for explainability, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the learned state representations. The paper does not investigate the interpretability of the core states or how they relate to the underlying game dynamics. This lack of analysis limits the understanding of the algorithm's behavior and its potential for explainability. In summary, the paper's weaknesses include the limited evaluation environment, the lack of comparison with other state abstraction techniques, the absence of a computational complexity analysis, the restriction to episodic MDPs, the lack of a theoretical analysis, and the limited analysis of the learned state representations. These weaknesses limit the generalizability and practical applicability of the findings.
