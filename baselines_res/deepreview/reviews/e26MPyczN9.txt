## Summary:

This paper presents a critical re-evaluation of the claim that programmatic policies inherently generalize better than neural policies in reinforcement learning (RL). The authors meticulously investigate this claim by revisiting prior work and identifying key confounding factors that have led to misleading conclusions. Through a series of carefully controlled experiments, they demonstrate that, when these factors are addressed, neural policies can achieve comparable out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization to programmatic policies in several benchmark environments, including TORCS, Karel, and Parking. The core of their argument revolves around the concepts of 'expressivity' and 'discoverability.' Expressivity refers to the ability of a policy space to represent a generalizing solution, while discoverability refers to the ability of a search algorithm to find such a solution within that space. The authors argue that previous claims of superior generalization in programmatic policies often stem from limitations in the training pipelines of neural policies, rather than inherent representational advantages. They show that by using sparse observations, cautious reward functions, and other adjustments, neural policies can overcome these limitations and achieve comparable generalization. However, the paper also identifies a specific class of problems where programmatic representations do exhibit a genuine advantage: tasks that require working memory that grows with the input size. To demonstrate this, the authors present a proof-of-concept experiment using FUNSEARCH to synthesize a breadth-first search (BFS) algorithm for a modified Karel maze. This experiment highlights the ability of programmatic representations to handle instance-growing structures, which are challenging for standard neural architectures with fixed memory capacities. The paper's significance lies in its rigorous re-evaluation of existing claims, its clear articulation of the factors influencing generalization, and its identification of a specific domain where programmatic representations offer a clear advantage. By disentangling representational factors from experimental confounds, the authors provide valuable insights into the design of RL systems that can generalize effectively.

The paper primarily focuses on pathfinding as a concrete example of a task requiring growing memory, with a brief mention of NetHack. While the proof-of-concept experiment with the modified Karel maze is compelling, the discussion lacks concrete examples beyond pathfinding. The current examples, while illustrative, do not fully explore the breadth of potential applications for programmatic policies in memory-intensive scenarios. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand their discussion of tasks where programmatic policies excel due to growing memory requirements. Beyond pathfinding, they could explore domains like dynamic graph traversal, where the graph structure changes over time, or tasks involving complex state dependencies that require variable memory. For instance, consider a scenario where an agent must navigate a maze with moving walls or a task where the agent needs to remember a sequence of past events to make decisions. These examples would better illustrate the potential of programmatic policies in handling variable memory needs. Furthermore, the authors could include a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of these tasks and how programmatic policies can handle them more efficiently than neural networks. This would provide a more concrete understanding of the advantages of programmatic policies in memory-intensive scenarios. Second, to address the lack of formal theoretical framework, the authors should include a more rigorous analysis of the generalization properties of programmatic representations. This could involve a discussion of the bounding of the generalization error for programmatic policies versus neural networks. For example, they could explore how the structure of programmatic policies, which are often based on symbolic manipulation, allows for better generalization in certain tasks compared to the distributed representations of neural networks. A formal analysis of the expressivity and limitations of programmatic representations would also be beneficial. This could include a discussion of the types of functions that can be represented by programmatic policies and how this compares to the function space of neural networks. This would provide a more solid theoretical foundation for the claims made in the paper. Third, the authors should compare their programmatic policies against more sophisticated neural architectures, such as transformers, which are known to handle complex dependencies better. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the strengths and weaknesses of both approaches. For example, they could explore how transformers perform in tasks with growing memory requirements and compare their performance to programmatic policies. This would help to clarify the specific scenarios where programmatic policies offer a genuine advantage over neural networks. Additionally, the authors could investigate the impact of different training techniques on the performance of neural networks, such as curriculum learning or meta-learning, to ensure that the comparisons are as fair as possible. This would provide a more robust evaluation of the relative strengths of programmatic and neural policies. Finally, to strengthen the claims regarding programmatic representations with instance-growing memory, the authors should conduct more extensive empirical evaluations across a wider range of tasks and environments. This should include tasks with varying degrees of complexity and input size growth, to better understand the limitations and scalability of the proposed approach. For example, the authors could explore environments where the required memory grows non-linearly with the input size, or where the memory requirements are not predictable. It would also be beneficial to investigate the impact of different programmatic representations on generalization, such as using different programming languages or different types of program structures. This could involve comparing the performance of programs with varying depths, branching factors, and types of operations. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the computational cost of finding these programmatic solutions, and compare it to the cost of training neural policies with similar generalization capabilities. This analysis should include the time required for search, as well as the memory requirements for storing and executing the programmatic policies.
