## Summary:

This paper introduces Insertion Language Models (ILMs), a novel approach to sequence generation that departs from the traditional autoregressive paradigm. Unlike autoregressive models (ARMs) that generate tokens sequentially from left to right, ILMs insert tokens at arbitrary positions within a sequence. This is achieved by training the model to predict both the token to be inserted and its corresponding position. The authors propose a tailored network architecture and a simple denoising objective to facilitate this process. A key aspect of ILMs is their ability to handle variable-length sequences without relying on placeholder tokens, which is a limitation of Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs). The training process involves randomly removing tokens from a sequence and then training the model to predict both the removed tokens and their original positions. The model also incorporates a stopping prediction module to determine when the sequence generation is complete. The authors evaluate ILMs on a variety of tasks, including synthetic planning tasks, language modeling, and infilling. The results demonstrate that ILMs achieve performance comparable to ARMs and MDMs in language modeling and exhibit superior performance in infilling tasks. Specifically, the paper shows that ILMs outperform MDMs in terms of negative log-likelihood (NLL) and demonstrate better coherence and consistency based on qualitative evaluations. In planning tasks, ILMs also show strong performance, indicating their ability to handle complex dependencies and constraints. The paper's contribution lies in proposing a new perspective on language modeling that addresses some of the limitations of autoregressive models and masked diffusion models. By allowing for arbitrary insertion of tokens, ILMs offer a more flexible approach to sequence generation that can potentially handle complex constraints and dependencies more effectively. The authors also highlight the potential of ILMs for tasks that require non-left-to-right generation, such as infilling and certain types of planning problems. Overall, the paper presents a promising new direction for sequence generation that warrants further investigation and development.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the introduction of Insertion Language Models (ILMs) is a novel and significant contribution to the field of sequence generation. The idea of inserting tokens at arbitrary positions, rather than generating them sequentially from left to right, offers a fresh perspective on language modeling and addresses some of the limitations inherent in autoregressive models (ARMs). This approach allows for more flexible handling of complex constraints and dependencies, which is a notable advantage. The authors have also clearly articulated the differences between ILMs and Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs), highlighting how ILMs overcome the rigid fixed-length constraint imposed by MDMs. The training objective and network architecture proposed for ILMs are well-designed and effectively support the model's ability to predict both the token and its insertion position. The use of a stopping prediction module is also a clever way to determine when the sequence generation is complete. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The fact that ILMs achieve comparable performance to ARMs and MDMs in language modeling, while also demonstrating superior performance in infilling tasks, is a strong indication of the model's effectiveness. The quantitative results, particularly the lower negative log-likelihood (NLL) achieved by ILMs compared to MDMs, provide solid evidence of their superior performance. The qualitative evaluations, which show that ILMs generate more coherent and consistent text, further support this claim. The strong performance of ILMs on synthetic planning tasks also demonstrates their ability to handle complex dependencies and constraints, which is a significant achievement. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow. The authors have clearly explained the methodology, the experimental setup, and the results. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and findings. Overall, the paper presents a well-executed and innovative approach to sequence generation that has the potential to make a significant impact on the field. The combination of a novel model design, a well-defined training objective, and strong empirical results makes this a valuable contribution.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. A primary concern is the limited scope of the experimental evaluation. While the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of ILMs on planning tasks, language modeling, and infilling, the evaluation lacks a broader range of tasks commonly used in natural language processing. Specifically, the paper does not include experiments on tasks such as machine translation, text summarization, or question answering. This omission limits our understanding of the model's general applicability and robustness. As the paper focuses on specific tasks, it is difficult to assess how well ILMs would perform on more diverse and complex NLP tasks. The absence of such evaluations makes it challenging to determine the model's strengths and weaknesses in different contexts. Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the model's limitations. While the authors highlight the strengths of ILMs, they do not provide a thorough investigation into the model's failure cases or scenarios where it might struggle. For instance, there is no analysis of the model's performance on specific types of inputs, such as sentences with ambiguous syntax or complex semantic relationships. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the model's biases or limitations in handling certain linguistic structures. The paper also does not delve into the underlying reasons for the model's errors, such as examining attention patterns or internal representations. This lack of qualitative analysis limits our understanding of the model's behavior and potential areas for improvement. Another significant weakness is the limited scale of the datasets used in the experiments. While the authors use the One Billion Word Benchmark (LM1B) for language modeling, they refer to the datasets as "small-sized" and acknowledge the need to scale ILMs to larger datasets as future work. This raises concerns about the model's scalability and generalizability. The performance of ILMs on larger and more diverse datasets might differ significantly from the results presented in the paper. The lack of experiments on larger datasets makes it difficult to assess the model's practical applicability in real-world scenarios. Additionally, the paper's comparison with other models is somewhat limited. While the authors compare ILMs with ARMs and MDMs, they do not include comparisons with other relevant sequence generation models, such as those based on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or other transformer architectures. This limited comparison makes it difficult to fully contextualize the performance of ILMs and understand its unique advantages and disadvantages compared to other approaches. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of ILMs. While the authors provide a comparison of per-token generation time for MDM, ARM, and ILM, they do not provide specific training times, memory usage, or a detailed breakdown of the computational cost of different components of the ILM architecture. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of using ILMs for different applications. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of different hyperparameters on the model's performance. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the model's sensitivity to different hyperparameter settings and to identify optimal configurations. In summary, the lack of a comprehensive evaluation across a wider range of tasks, the limited analysis of the model's limitations, the use of small-scale datasets, the limited comparison with other models, the lack of detailed computational cost analysis, and the absence of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis are all significant weaknesses that need to be addressed to fully understand the potential of ILMs.
