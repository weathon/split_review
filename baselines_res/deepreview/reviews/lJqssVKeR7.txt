## Summary:

This paper introduces HiSo, a novel federated learning (FL) optimization method that leverages zeroth-order (ZO) techniques and incorporates a diagonal Hessian approximation to accelerate convergence while maintaining dimension-free communication. The core idea is to use the diagonal Hessian to guide the search direction, thereby improving the efficiency of the zeroth-order updates without incurring additional communication costs. The authors provide a rigorous theoretical analysis, demonstrating that HiSo can achieve an accelerated convergence rate that is independent of the model dimension and the Lipschitz constant, under certain assumptions about the Hessian approximation. Empirically, the paper shows that HiSo outperforms existing zeroth-order FL baselines in terms of communication rounds and final performance on various large language model (LLM) fine-tuning tasks. The method is particularly significant in the context of federated learning, where communication efficiency is crucial, and the use of Hessian information offers a promising approach to overcoming the limitations of standard zeroth-order methods. However, the paper's reliance on strong assumptions about the Hessian approximation and the limited scope of its experimental evaluation raise important questions about the practical applicability and generalizability of HiSo.

## Strengths:

The paper is well-organized and easy to follow, with a clear and logical structure that facilitates understanding. The theoretical analysis is comprehensive and provides convergence guarantees for non-convex settings, which is a significant contribution to the field of federated learning. The authors introduce a novel approach to zeroth-order optimization by incorporating a diagonal Hessian approximation, which is a significant advancement in the area. This method not only accelerates convergence but also maintains the dimension-free communication property, making it particularly suitable for large-scale models. The empirical results are promising, showing that HiSo delivers a 1~5× speedup in communication rounds over existing state-of-the-art zeroth-order FL baselines. The experiments are conducted on a variety of LLM fine-tuning tasks, including sentiment classification, question matching, and question answering, which demonstrates the practical effectiveness of the proposed method. The paper also includes an ablation study on the smoothing parameter, which provides insights into the sensitivity of the method to this hyperparameter. Overall, the paper makes a strong case for the use of Hessian information in zeroth-order federated learning, addressing a critical bottleneck in the field.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and concerns are evident. First, the convergence analysis relies on strong assumptions about the Hessian approximation quality, which may not hold in practice. Specifically, the assumption that the diagonal Hessian accurately captures the curvature of the loss landscape is questionable, especially in highly non-convex scenarios common in deep learning. The paper defines a 'well-approximate matrix of Hessian' in Section 5.1 and provides a theoretical framework based on this assumption. However, the practical accuracy of this approximation is not thoroughly validated, and the experimental results show sensitivity to the smoothing parameter, suggesting that the Hessian approximation can significantly impact performance. This discrepancy between the theoretical assumptions and practical performance is a critical concern, as it may lead to a significant gap between the theoretical convergence rate and the actual observed performance (High confidence, see Section 5.1 and Figure 5).

Second, the method introduces additional complexity in terms of Hessian estimation and preconditioning, which could increase the computational overhead per round. While the paper focuses on reducing communication costs, it lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with Hessian estimation and preconditioning. The Hessian update rule (Equation 12) and the update rule incorporating the Hessian (Equation 8) suggest that these steps involve additional computations. However, the paper does not provide a quantitative comparison of the computational cost of HiSo with existing zeroth-order FL methods, such as DeComFL. This omission makes it difficult to assess the overall efficiency of the method, especially in resource-constrained environments (High confidence, see Section 4.2 and lack of computational cost analysis in experiments).

Third, the experimental evaluation primarily focuses on LLM fine-tuning tasks, which limits the assessment of the method's generalizability. The paper includes experiments on various LLMs, such as OPT-350M, OPT-1.3B, and OPT-2.7B, but does not evaluate HiSo on other types of models or optimization problems. For instance, there are no experiments on training from scratch or fine-tuning smaller models with different architectures. This narrow focus raises questions about whether the observed performance gains would translate to other tasks, such as image classification or different types of neural networks. The inclusion of a small-scale experiment on MNIST (Figure 5) provides some broader context, but it is insufficient to fully address the generalizability concern (High confidence, see Section 6 and Figure 5).

Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. For example, the impact of inaccurate Hessian approximations on the performance of HiSo is not thoroughly explored. The paper briefly mentions in Appendix F.7.1 that if the Hessian approximation is poor, the performance of HiSo may degrade to that of DeComFL. However, a more in-depth analysis of this degradation and strategies to mitigate it would be beneficial. Additionally, the sensitivity of HiSo to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the smoothing parameter and the learning rate, is not fully addressed. The paper should provide guidelines for selecting these parameters in practice, as well as discuss the potential impact of data heterogeneity on the performance of HiSo (High confidence, see Appendix F.7.1 and lack of detailed discussion on limitations).

Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the Hessian approximation. While the Hessian is diagonal, the additional steps of updating and using it for preconditioning do introduce computational overhead compared to standard zeroth-order FL methods. The paper should include a breakdown of the computational cost per round, considering the number of operations and memory requirements, and compare it to the computational cost of the baselines. This analysis should also consider the impact of the Hessian estimation frequency on the overall computational cost, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the method's practical applicability (High confidence, see lack of computational cost analysis in experiments).


## Suggestions:

To address the identified limitations, the paper should provide a more thorough analysis of the Hessian approximation quality and its impact on convergence. Specifically, it would be beneficial to include experiments that evaluate the correlation between the quality of the diagonal Hessian approximation and the observed convergence rate. This could involve comparing the performance of HiSo with different levels of Hessian approximation accuracy, perhaps by varying the frequency of Hessian updates or using different approximation techniques. Furthermore, the theoretical analysis should be extended to consider scenarios where the Hessian approximation is not perfect, possibly by introducing a bound on the approximation error and analyzing its effect on the convergence rate. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the method's performance in practical settings (High confidence, see Section 5.1 and Figure 5).

To address the concern about computational overhead, the authors should include a detailed breakdown of the computational cost associated with each step of the HiSo algorithm, including Hessian estimation, preconditioning, and the zeroth-order gradient update. This analysis should compare the computational cost of HiSo with that of existing zeroth-order federated learning methods, such as DeComFL. It would also be helpful to investigate techniques for reducing the computational cost of Hessian estimation, such as using sparse approximations or low-rank methods. Additionally, the paper should discuss the trade-off between communication efficiency and computational overhead, providing guidance on when HiSo is most appropriate. For example, it would be useful to analyze how the computational cost scales with the model size and the number of clients (High confidence, see Section 4.2 and lack of computational cost analysis in experiments).

The experimental evaluation should be expanded to include a wider range of tasks and models. This could involve evaluating HiSo on tasks such as image classification, natural language processing, or reinforcement learning. It would also be beneficial to explore the performance of HiSo on problems with different levels of non-convexity and dimensionality. The paper should include experiments on both small and large-scale datasets, as well as different model architectures. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's generalization ability and its applicability to different scenarios. The authors should also compare the performance of HiSo with other federated learning methods that are not based on zeroth-order optimization, to better understand the trade-offs between different approaches (High confidence, see Section 6 and Figure 5).

The paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This discussion should include an analysis of how the performance of HiSo degrades when the Hessian approximation is inaccurate, and what are the practical challenges of implementing HiSo in real-world federated learning systems. The paper should also discuss the sensitivity of HiSo to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the smoothing parameter and the learning rate, and provide guidelines for selecting these parameters in practice. Furthermore, the paper should explore the potential impact of data heterogeneity on the performance of HiSo and how this issue can be addressed. The authors should also consider the potential for combining HiSo with other techniques, such as adaptive learning rate methods, to improve its robustness and performance (High confidence, see Appendix F.7.1 and lack of detailed discussion on limitations).

Finally, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the Hessian approximation. This analysis should not only consider the number of operations but also the memory requirements for storing the Hessian approximation. A detailed breakdown of the computational cost per round, including the cost of Hessian estimation, should be provided and compared to the computational cost of the baselines. This comparison should be done both theoretically and empirically, with experiments on different model sizes and hardware configurations. The paper should also discuss the trade-offs between communication efficiency and computational cost, and provide guidelines for selecting the appropriate method based on the available resources (High confidence, see lack of computational cost analysis in experiments).
