## Summary:

This paper introduces Agentic Reinforced Policy Optimization (ARPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm designed to enhance the performance of multi-turn, Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents, particularly in scenarios involving tool use. The core motivation behind ARPO stems from the observation that LLMs exhibit increased uncertainty, as measured by higher token entropy, after utilizing external tools. To address this, ARPO employs an entropy-based adaptive rollout mechanism. This mechanism encourages the model to explore alternative reasoning paths during high-entropy tool-use steps, effectively promoting step-level exploration rather than relying solely on trajectory-level exploration. The adaptive rollout is achieved by branching the sampling process when the entropy exceeds a predefined threshold, allowing the model to investigate multiple potential actions at critical decision points. Furthermore, ARPO incorporates advantage attribution estimation, which enables the model to better understand the effectiveness of different tool-use interactions by attributing the advantage to specific actions. This is achieved by calculating the difference between the Q-value of a specific action and the average Q-value of the policy, allowing the model to learn which actions are most beneficial in a given context. The authors conducted extensive experiments across 13 challenging benchmarks, demonstrating that ARPO consistently outperforms traditional trajectory-level RL algorithms, achieving better performance with only half the tool-use budget. This is a significant finding, as it suggests that ARPO is not only more effective but also more efficient in terms of tool usage. The paper also provides a theoretical justification for the ARPO algorithm, highlighting its adaptability in multi-turn training scenarios for LLM-based agents. The theoretical analysis, while not fully rigorous, provides a foundation for understanding the behavior of the algorithm. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for LLM-based agents, offering a novel and effective approach to training multi-turn agents with tool use. The core innovation lies in the fine-grained, step-level exploration of tool-use behaviors, which is a departure from traditional trajectory-level RL methods. The integration of advantage attribution further enhances the algorithm's ability to learn effective tool-use strategies. The empirical results are compelling, demonstrating the practical value of the proposed method. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, there are several areas where further analysis and clarification are needed to fully understand the practical implications and limitations of the proposed method.

## Strengths:

The paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall impact and significance. First and foremost, the proposed Agentic Reinforced Policy Optimization (ARPO) algorithm is a novel and well-motivated approach to reinforcement learning for LLM-based agents. The core idea of using an entropy-based adaptive rollout mechanism to encourage step-level exploration is a significant departure from traditional trajectory-level RL methods. This is a crucial innovation, as it directly addresses the challenge of tool use in multi-turn reasoning tasks, where LLMs often exhibit increased uncertainty after using tools. The authors clearly articulate the motivation behind this approach, highlighting the need for more fine-grained exploration in high-entropy tool-use steps. The integration of advantage attribution estimation further enhances the algorithm's ability to learn effective tool-use strategies. By attributing the advantage to specific actions, the model can better understand which actions are most beneficial in a given context. This is a crucial component of the algorithm, as it allows the model to learn from its explorations and improve its performance over time. The paper also provides a theoretical analysis of the ARPO algorithm, demonstrating its strong adaptability in multi-turn training scenarios for LLM-based agents. While the theoretical analysis is not fully rigorous, it provides a foundation for understanding the behavior of the algorithm and adds credibility to the proposed method. The empirical evaluation of ARPO is another significant strength of the paper. The authors conducted extensive experiments across 13 challenging benchmarks, demonstrating that ARPO consistently outperforms existing methods, achieving better performance with only half the tool-use budget. This is a compelling result, as it highlights the efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed algorithm. The experiments cover a diverse range of tasks, including mathematical reasoning, knowledge-intensive reasoning, and deep search tasks, which further strengthens the validity of the results. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide a clear and concise explanation of the proposed method, making it accessible to a broad audience. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and experimental results, further enhancing the paper's clarity. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for LLM-based agents, offering a novel and effective approach to training multi-turn agents with tool use. The combination of a well-motivated algorithm, a theoretical foundation, and compelling empirical results makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of the paper, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis of the ARPO algorithm. While the authors mention that ARPO reduces the computational complexity of each rollout from $O(n^2)$ to between $O(n \log n)$ and $O(n^2)$, this high-level statement lacks a thorough breakdown of the time complexity associated with each component of the algorithm, particularly the entropy calculation and the adaptive rollout mechanism. Specifically, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the time complexity of calculating token-level entropy, which involves summing over the vocabulary size $V$ at each step. This calculation, while seemingly straightforward, can become computationally expensive, especially with large vocabulary sizes and long sequences. Furthermore, the paper does not analyze the computational cost of the adaptive rollout mechanism, which involves branching the sampling process when the entropy exceeds a predefined threshold. The paper lacks a detailed breakdown of the time complexity of this branching process and how it scales with the number of tool-use steps and the size of the language model. This omission is significant because the adaptive rollout mechanism is a core component of ARPO, and understanding its computational cost is crucial for assessing the practical applicability of the method. The absence of a comparison with baseline methods, such as REINFORCE++ and GRPO, in terms of FLOPS or wall-clock time further exacerbates this issue. Without such a comparison, it is difficult to assess the practical trade-offs of using ARPO compared to other RL algorithms. The paper also fails to discuss the memory requirements of ARPO, which could be a limiting factor for large-scale applications. A detailed analysis of the memory footprint of the algorithm, particularly during the adaptive rollout phase, is missing. This is a critical oversight, as the memory requirements of RL algorithms can be substantial, especially when dealing with large language models and extensive tool-use scenarios. The lack of a discussion on the sensitivity of the algorithm to hyperparameter settings is another significant weakness. The paper introduces hyperparameters such as the entropy threshold for branching, but it does not explore how changes in these values affect the performance and computational cost of ARPO. This is a crucial omission because the performance of ARPO is likely to be sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, and without a sensitivity analysis, it is difficult to provide practical guidance for users. The paper also lacks a discussion of the potential limitations of the entropy-based exploration strategy. For example, it is unclear how the algorithm would perform in scenarios where the entropy is not a reliable indicator of uncertainty or where the optimal policy requires exploring low-entropy actions. This is a critical limitation because the entropy-based exploration strategy is a core component of ARPO, and understanding its limitations is crucial for assessing the applicability of the method. Finally, while the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of ARPO across 13 benchmarks, a more detailed analysis of the performance differences across these benchmarks is needed. Specifically, it would be useful to understand why ARPO performs better on some tasks than others and whether there are specific characteristics of the tasks that make them more or less suitable for the proposed method. The paper provides a qualitative discussion of the results, but a deeper quantitative analysis is missing. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the overall assessment of the method. The lack of a detailed computational analysis, the absence of a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, the limited discussion of limitations, and the lack of a detailed analysis of performance differences across benchmarks all contribute to a less complete understanding of the practical implications and limitations of ARPO. My confidence in these identified weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the absence of specific analyses and discussions within the paper.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the ARPO algorithm. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time complexity of each component of the algorithm, particularly the entropy calculation and the adaptive rollout mechanism. The analysis should consider how the computational cost scales with the number of tool-use steps, the size of the language model, and the number of samples used for training. Specifically, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the time complexity of calculating token-level entropy, including the impact of the vocabulary size. They should also analyze the computational cost of the adaptive rollout mechanism, including the branching process and the number of additional samples generated. Furthermore, the authors should compare the computational cost of ARPO with other RL algorithms, such as REINFORCE++ and GRPO, in terms of FLOPS or wall-clock time. This comparison should be conducted on the same hardware and with the same experimental setup to ensure a fair comparison. This analysis should also include a discussion of the memory requirements of ARPO, including the memory footprint during the adaptive rollout phase. This analysis should consider the memory needed to store the policy network, the value function, and the entropy estimates. It would also be helpful to provide an analysis of the sensitivity of the algorithm to hyperparameter settings, such as the entropy threshold for branching. This could be done by conducting experiments with different hyperparameter values and analyzing the impact on the performance of the algorithm. This analysis should also include a discussion of how these hyperparameters should be tuned for different tasks and datasets. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of the entropy-based exploration strategy. This should include an analysis of scenarios where the entropy is not a reliable indicator of uncertainty or where the optimal policy requires exploring low-entropy actions. The authors could consider alternative exploration strategies, such as Thompson sampling or upper confidence bound (UCB), and discuss how these strategies could be integrated into the ARPO framework. It would also be beneficial to provide a more detailed analysis of the performance differences across the 13 benchmarks used in the experiments. This analysis should include a discussion of the characteristics of the tasks that make them more or less suitable for the proposed method. For example, the authors could analyze the complexity of the tool-use interactions required for each task and how this relates to the performance of ARPO. This analysis could also include a discussion of the types of errors that ARPO makes on different tasks and how these errors could be addressed in future work. Finally, the authors should consider providing more practical guidance on how to implement the ARPO algorithm. This could include a discussion of the software and hardware requirements for running the algorithm, as well as practical tips for debugging and optimizing the implementation. The authors could also consider releasing the source code for the algorithm, which would allow other researchers to reproduce their results and build upon their work. This would also facilitate a more thorough evaluation of the algorithm by the research community. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly enhance the practical applicability and impact of their work. These suggestions are directly linked to the identified weaknesses and are aimed at providing a more complete and robust analysis of the proposed method.
