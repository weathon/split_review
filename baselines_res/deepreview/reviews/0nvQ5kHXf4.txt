## Summary:

This paper introduces Weight-Activation Subspace Iteration (WASI), a novel method designed to enhance the efficiency of training transformer models, particularly in resource-constrained environments. The core idea behind WASI is to leverage the inherent stability of both model weights and activation maps within low-dimensional subspaces during fine-tuning. By employing Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and subspace iteration, WASI aims to reduce memory consumption and computational costs while maintaining accuracy comparable to traditional training methods. The method combines two key components: Weight Subspace Iteration (WSI), which focuses on the low-rank decomposition of weight matrices, and Activation Subspace Iteration (ASI), which addresses the redundancy in activation maps. The authors hypothesize that the essential information of a model resides in a stable subspace, allowing for efficient training and inference by operating within these compressed representations. The experimental validation of WASI includes evaluations on vision transformer models such as ViT and SwinT, as well as on TinyLlama, a smaller language model. The results demonstrate significant reductions in memory usage, up to 62x, and computational costs, up to 2x, while achieving comparable accuracy to vanilla training. Furthermore, the paper presents on-device latency experiments on a Raspberry Pi 5, showcasing the practical applicability of WASI for on-device learning. The authors also explore the trade-offs between compression rates and accuracy by varying the explained variance threshold, demonstrating the flexibility of the method. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for enabling the deployment of transformer models on edge devices, addressing critical concerns about energy consumption and data privacy. The method's ability to significantly reduce resource requirements while maintaining accuracy makes it a valuable contribution to the field of efficient deep learning. The paper also highlights the potential for WASI to facilitate on-device learning, which is a growing area of interest in the field of AI. The authors' focus on both vision and language models demonstrates the broad applicability of their approach. The inclusion of on-device experiments further strengthens the practical relevance of the proposed method. The paper's findings suggest that WASI could be a crucial step towards enabling the widespread adoption of transformer models in resource-constrained environments.

## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling method, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. Firstly, the novelty of WASI, while present, might be perceived as incremental. As Reviewer 1 pointed out, the method appears to be an extension of Activation Subspace Iteration (ASI), with the primary addition being Weight Subspace Iteration (WSI). While the paper explicitly states that WASI combines WSI and ASI, the core contribution of WSI and its integration with ASI could be more clearly emphasized to highlight the novelty. The paper introduces WSI in Section 3.3, describing it as a low-rank weight decomposition strategy, and states that WSI and ASI together form WASI. However, the paper could benefit from a more detailed comparison between WASI and ASI, clarifying the exact differences in their mathematical formulations and algorithmic implementations. A table summarizing the key differences, such as the inclusion of weight subspace iteration, the dynamic programming strategy for rank selection, and the handling of 3D activation tensors, would be beneficial. Furthermore, a more in-depth analysis of the computational complexity of both methods, including the FLOPs and memory requirements, would help to better understand the advantages of WASI over ASI. The authors should also discuss the limitations of ASI that prevent it from being directly applicable to the experiments conducted in the paper, such as its inability to handle 3D activation tensors or its lack of a dynamic programming strategy for rank selection. This would further highlight the novelty and contribution of the proposed method. Secondly, the paper lacks clarity regarding the application of WASI to attention layers. As Reviewer 1 noted, the method description in Section 3 focuses on linear layers, defining the weight matrix and activation tensor in the context of linear layers. The examples provided in the method description are specific to linear layers. While the experiments include models with attention layers, the details of how WASI is applied to these layers are not explicitly provided in the method section. The paper needs to provide a detailed explanation of how the method is applied to the weight matrices and activation tensors within the attention mechanism. This should include a description of the specific SVD and subspace iteration steps performed on the query, key, and value matrices, as well as the attention output. A clear mathematical formulation of the low-rank approximation within the attention layer, including the equations for the low-rank weight update and the computation of gradients in the low-rank space, would be beneficial. The authors should also discuss any potential challenges or limitations of applying WASI to attention layers, such as the impact on the attention mechanism's ability to capture long-range dependencies or the computational cost of performing SVD on large attention matrices. A visual representation, such as a diagram illustrating the application of WASI to an attention layer, would further enhance the clarity of the explanation. Thirdly, the paper's experimental evaluation primarily focuses on vision transformer models and TinyLlama, with limited exploration of larger language models. As Reviewer 2 pointed out, WASI's effectiveness on models with more complex architectures or larger parameter counts remains unclear. The paper lacks experiments on models that are significantly larger than TinyLlama, which is crucial to understand the scalability of the proposed method. The behavior of subspace iteration in extremely high-dimensional spaces, common in large language models, is not addressed, raising concerns about its practical applicability in such scenarios. The experimental section confirms the focus on ViT, SwinT, and a partially fine-tuned TinyLlama. The limitation to the last 5 layers of TinyLlama suggests resource constraints and a lack of full evaluation on large language models. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between compression rates and accuracy across different tasks and datasets. While the paper presents results for different explained variance thresholds, which directly relate to compression rates, it does not explore the impact of varying compression rates on different layers of the transformer model. It is possible that some layers are more sensitive to compression than others, and a uniform compression rate across all layers may not be optimal. A more granular analysis of layer-wise compression impact would be beneficial. The paper does analyze the trade-off between compression rate (controlled by $\epsilon$) and accuracy. However, it lacks a granular, layer-wise analysis of compression impact. Furthermore, the paper could provide more insights into the practical challenges of implementing WASI on real-world edge devices. While the paper includes on-device experiments, providing some practical insights, it lacks a broader discussion of hardware compatibility, quantization, and the overhead of subspace iteration. The limited scope of the on-device experiments and the absence of discussion on quantization and subspace iteration overhead support the reviewer's claim. The paper does not discuss compatibility with different hardware architectures beyond the Raspberry Pi 5. The paper does not explicitly address the impact of quantization. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the overhead of the subspace iteration process itself. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, particularly in scenarios where the assumption of a stable subspace may not hold. As Reviewer 3 and 4 noted, the paper does not address how the method would perform if the optimal subspace shifts significantly during training or if the data distribution changes, which could lead to a degradation in performance. A more rigorous analysis of the sensitivity of the method to these factors is needed. While the paper validates the stability assumption, it lacks a discussion of its limitations and the method's behavior when the assumption is violated. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the WASI method itself. While the method aims to reduce computational costs, the overhead of the subspace iteration process should be quantified and compared to the savings achieved. This analysis should include the time and memory costs associated with the subspace identification and update steps, particularly in the context of on-device learning where resources are constrained. The paper shows overall efficiency gains but lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of the WASI method itself. The paper does not provide a separate breakdown of the computational cost of the subspace iteration process itself. These limitations, while not invalidating the method, highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to fully understand the capabilities and limitations of WASI.

A table summarizing the key differences, such as the inclusion of weight subspace iteration, the dynamic programming strategy for rank selection, and the handling of 3D activation tensors, would be beneficial. Furthermore, a more in-depth analysis of the computational complexity of both methods, including the FLOPs and memory requirements, would help to better understand the advantages of WASI over ASI. The authors should also discuss the limitations of ASI that prevent it from being directly applicable to the experiments conducted in the paper, such as its inability to handle 3D activation tensors or its lack of a dynamic programming strategy for rank selection. This would further highlight the novelty and contribution of the proposed method. Secondly, to address the concern regarding the applicability of WASI to attention layers, the authors should provide a detailed explanation of how the method is applied to the weight matrices and activation tensors within the attention mechanism. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the scalability and limitations of WASI.
