## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to understanding how language models acquire syntax by focusing on the learning dynamics of probabilistic context-free grammars (PCFGs) and their substructures. The authors define two types of subgrammars: inner subgrammars, which correspond to subtrees of context-free grammar (CFG) derivations, and outer subgrammars, which are simplified versions of the CFG. They then prove a suite of fundamental results demonstrating that the loss of language modeling obeys recurrences with respect to this subgrammar structure. This theoretical framework provides a new lens through which to analyze the learning process of language models. Empirically, the authors train small transformer models on synthetic CFGs and observe that these models learn all subgrammars in parallel, a finding that contrasts with the sequential manner in which children acquire language. This parallel learning is a key finding of the paper, and it raises questions about the nature of language learning in neural networks. Furthermore, the study explores the potential benefits of curriculum learning by pretraining models on subgrammars, finding that this approach can improve performance for smaller models. The authors also demonstrate that even models that achieve low training loss do not perfectly capture the subgrammar structure, particularly when dealing with deeper recursive structures. This highlights a fundamental challenge in how neural networks represent hierarchical syntax. The paper's core contribution lies in its novel framework for analyzing language model learning through the lens of subgrammars, its empirical findings on parallel learning, and its exploration of curriculum learning strategies. The work provides valuable insights into the learning dynamics of language models and their ability to acquire syntax, while also revealing limitations in their ability to handle complex recursive structures. Overall, the paper presents a compelling study that advances our understanding of how neural networks learn grammatical structures, while also raising important questions about the nature of language acquisition.

The empirical findings are also a major strength of the paper. ## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents several strengths, there are also some notable weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the primary concerns lies in the theoretical framework presented in Section 4, which relies on a strong assumption of context-insensitivity for the model's subgrammars. Specifically, Corollary 4.5 assumes that the model's behavior on a subgrammar is independent of the context in which it appears within the larger grammar. This assumption, as stated in the paper, is that for two contexts *s* and *s'*, if the probability of a subgrammar *A_i* given *s* and *s'* is greater than zero, then the model's prediction for *A_i* given *s* is the same as its prediction for *A_i* given *s'*. This is a significant simplification, and it's not immediately clear that a neural network trained on a full grammar would exhibit such context-insensitive behavior at the subgrammar level. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the conditions under which this assumption is valid, nor does it explore the potential impact of its violation on the theoretical results. This lack of justification weakens the theoretical framework, as the validity of the results hinges on this assumption. The paper acknowledges that the model learns all subgrammars in parallel, which is a surprising and counterintuitive result. However, the paper does not provide a clear explanation for *why* this happens. While the authors propose a potential explanation based on the 'independence' of gradient updates (Corollary 4.7), this is presented as a starting point for future research rather than a definitive explanation. The paper acknowledges that the reasons behind this phenomenon are not fully understood, and this lack of a clear explanation is a limitation of the current study. The experimental setup in Section 5 also presents some limitations. The experiments are conducted on synthetic CFGs with limited complexity. The paper describes the grammars used in the experiments (e.g., in Figure 1 and 2 descriptions), and they appear to be relatively simple, with limited recursion depth and a small number of production rules. The paper does not explicitly discuss the limitations of using these simple CFGs or the challenges of generalizing the findings to more complex grammars or natural language. This raises concerns about the applicability of the results to more complex, real-world grammars. The paper also presents a CKA analysis in Section 5.2, which is intended to provide insights into the model's internal representations. However, the CKA analysis does not offer a clear interpretation of the results. While the paper presents CKA results (Table 1) and attempts to interpret them by comparing representational similarity using cosine similarity (Table 3), the reviewer's assessment might stem from a perceived lack of depth or surprisingness in the findings. The paper concludes that pretrained models have higher alignment and are better at segregating sequences with and without subgrammars, which could be seen as incremental insights. Finally, the paper does not explore the question of grammar induction, which is an important aspect of language acquisition. The paper focuses on the learning dynamics of language models given a fixed grammar, but it does not address the problem of grammar induction, which is a crucial aspect of language acquisition. The paper should discuss the limitations of not addressing grammar induction and suggest future directions for research in this area. These limitations, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be strengthened through further analysis and experimentation. The reliance on strong assumptions, the lack of a clear explanation for parallel learning, the limited complexity of the experimental setup, the lack of clear insights from the CKA analysis, and the absence of a discussion on grammar induction all represent areas for improvement.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the paper should conduct a more thorough investigation into the context-insensitivity assumption. Specifically, the authors should explore the extent to which the model's behavior on subgrammars is influenced by the surrounding context within the larger grammar. This could involve conducting experiments where the context of subgrammars is systematically varied and analyzing the impact on the model's performance. Furthermore, the authors could investigate whether specific architectural modifications or training techniques could encourage context-insensitivity at the subgrammar level. A more detailed analysis of the conditions under which this assumption holds, and the potential consequences of its violation, would significantly strengthen the theoretical results. It would also be beneficial to explore the use of techniques such as attention visualization to understand how the model attends to different parts of the input sequence when processing subgrammars in different contexts. Second, to address the limitations of the experimental setup, the authors should consider expanding their experiments to include more complex and realistic CFGs. This could involve using grammars with deeper recursion, a larger number of production rules, and more intricate dependencies between non-terminals. The paper should also discuss the challenges of scaling the experiments to larger models and more complex datasets. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of techniques such as curriculum learning to facilitate the learning of complex grammars. It would be valuable to investigate how the model's performance scales with the complexity of the grammar and to identify the specific factors that contribute to the difficulty of learning complex language structures. The paper should also consider experiments on grammars that are more representative of natural language, even if they are still synthetic. Third, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the parallel learning behavior observed in the experiments. This could involve investigating the relationship between the learning rates of different subgrammars and the architecture of the model. The authors should also explore whether this parallel learning behavior is specific to the transformer architecture or whether it is a more general property of neural networks. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the implications of this parallel learning behavior for our understanding of language acquisition. It would be valuable to compare the model's learning behavior to human language acquisition data and to identify the key differences and similarities. The paper should also explore the potential benefits and drawbacks of parallel learning compared to more sequential learning approaches. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the CKA analysis and its implications. The paper should clarify whether the observed changes in CKA scores are meaningful and what they imply about the model's internal representations. It would be beneficial to explore alternative methods for analyzing the model's internal representations and to compare the results with the CKA analysis. Finally, the paper should address the question of grammar induction. While this may be outside the scope of the current paper, the authors should acknowledge the limitations of not addressing this important aspect of language acquisition and suggest future directions for research in this area. By addressing these weaknesses, the paper could be significantly strengthened and provide a more comprehensive understanding of how language models learn syntax.
