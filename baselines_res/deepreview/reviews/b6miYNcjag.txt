## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to assessing the reliability of datasets, particularly those collected from potentially strategic sources where the true underlying data is unobserved. The core idea revolves around the concept of a 'reliability score' that aims to quantify how well reported data aligns with the unknown ground truth. The authors propose the Gram determinant score, which measures the volume spanned by vectors representing the empirical distribution of observed data and experiment outcomes. This score, they argue, preserves several ground-truth-based reliability orderings and exhibits a property termed 'experiment agnosticism,' meaning it yields consistent reliability rankings across different experiments. The paper's methodology involves defining these reliability orderings based on how reported data deviates from the truth, and then demonstrating that the Gram determinant score adheres to these orderings. The authors also introduce a kernelized version of the score to handle continuous or general observation spaces. The empirical evaluation includes experiments on synthetic noise models, CIFAR-10 embeddings, and real employment data, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed score in capturing data quality across diverse observation processes. The paper's significance lies in addressing the critical problem of data reliability in the absence of ground truth, offering a potentially valuable tool for various domains where data integrity is crucial. The authors motivate their work with examples from insurance, finance, and public health, highlighting the practical relevance of their approach. Overall, the paper presents a well-defined problem, a novel solution, and empirical evidence supporting its effectiveness, making a significant contribution to the field of data reliability assessment. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened, particularly in terms of practical applicability and a more thorough analysis of limitations.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the problem of reliability scoring for datasets collected from potentially strategic sources is both well-motivated and highly relevant. The authors effectively highlight the importance of reliable data in various domains, such as insurance, finance, and public health, where strategic reporting or noise can significantly impact decision-making. The introduction of the Gram determinant score as a novel measure of reliability is a significant contribution. The authors provide a clear theoretical foundation for this score, demonstrating its ability to preserve several ground-truth-based reliability orderings. The concept of 'experiment agnosticism' is also a valuable contribution, as it ensures that the reliability ranking of datasets remains consistent across different experiments. The mathematical formulation of the score and its properties is well-presented, and the proofs are rigorous. Furthermore, the empirical evaluation of the proposed score is a strength. The authors conduct experiments on synthetic noise models, CIFAR-10 embeddings, and real employment data, demonstrating the effectiveness of the Gram determinant score in capturing data quality across diverse observation processes. The use of both synthetic and real-world datasets provides a good balance between controlled experiments and practical validation. The inclusion of a kernelized version of the score to handle continuous or general observation spaces is another positive aspect, as it broadens the applicability of the method. Finally, the paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, making the complex concepts accessible to a wider audience. ## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of a comprehensive comparison with existing methods for reliability scoring. While the authors discuss related work and include comparisons to Hamming distance and $\ell_2$ error as baselines, they do not provide a thorough analysis of how the Gram determinant score compares to other established approaches in the field. This is a significant omission, as it makes it difficult to assess the true contribution of the proposed method. Without a detailed comparison, it is unclear what advantages the Gram determinant score offers over existing methods, and in what specific scenarios it might be preferred. This lack of comparative analysis is a recurring issue, as noted by multiple reviewers. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the proposed method. The authors define the Gram determinant score and mention the use of Gram matrices, but they do not analyze the time and space requirements of computing this score, especially for large datasets. The computation of the Gram matrix and its determinant can be computationally expensive, particularly for high-dimensional data. The absence of a computational complexity analysis is a significant weakness, as it limits the practical applicability of the method. Without a clear understanding of the computational cost, it is difficult to assess whether the proposed method is scalable for real-world applications. The paper also lacks a comprehensive discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors include a section on impossibility results and propose extensions to handle continuous data, they do not thoroughly address the potential challenges and limitations of their approach. For example, the paper does not discuss how the method performs when the data is highly correlated or when the underlying distribution is non-Gaussian. The sensitivity of the method to the choice of the experiment is also not discussed in detail. The paper also assumes that the true data and the reported data have the same dimensionality. This assumption may not hold in all real-world scenarios, and the authors do not discuss the limitations of their method in cases where the dimensionality of the true data and reported data differ. Specifically, the paper does not address how the Gram determinant score would behave if the reported data is a lower-dimensional projection or a noisy version of the true data with a different number of features. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world datasets suffer from such dimensionality discrepancies. Finally, while the paper provides examples of how auxiliary observations ($\mathbf{y}$) might be obtained in practice, it does not fully address the practical challenge of obtaining these observations when the ground truth is not directly accessible. The paper relies on the assumption that such observations are available, but it does not provide a general method for obtaining them in all scenarios. This is a critical limitation, as the practical utility of the proposed measure is severely limited without a clear path to obtaining $\mathbf{y}$. The paper also does not explicitly discuss limitations related to noisy or incomplete data in detail. These limitations, taken together, significantly impact the practical applicability and generalizability of the proposed method. My confidence in these identified weaknesses is high, as they are supported by direct examination of the paper's content and are independently validated by multiple reviewers.
