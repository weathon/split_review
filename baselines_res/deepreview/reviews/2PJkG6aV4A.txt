## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for evaluating societal bias in large vision-language models (LVLMs), addressing a critical limitation of existing benchmarks that often trigger safety guardrails, leading to high refusal rates. The authors propose a guardrail-agnostic approach that uses person-irrelevant prompts and treats images as user context rather than direct subjects of inference. This method allows for a more reliable assessment of bias by circumventing the safety mechanisms that cause models to refuse to answer questions about people's attributes in images. The core idea is to present the model with an image as user context and then ask questions unrelated to the person in the image, such as writing a fictional story or explaining a term. By comparing model responses across different user demographics, the authors can measure societal bias without triggering safety refusals. This approach is applied to 20 recent LVLMs, including both open-source and proprietary models, revealing that all models exhibit undesirable societal bias. Notably, proprietary models like GPT-5 show lower bias than open-source ones. The authors also explore potential factors behind this difference, suggesting that continuous model monitoring and improvement, along with safety-aware training, may play a key role in reducing bias. The study employs diverse tasks such as story generation, term explanation, and exam-style QA, and analyzes gender and racial biases. The paper's findings highlight the pervasive nature of societal bias in LVLMs and the importance of developing robust evaluation methods that can effectively measure bias without being hindered by safety mechanisms. The authors also emphasize the need for continuous model monitoring and improvement to mitigate bias in these models. The paper's contribution lies in its innovative methodology for bias evaluation and its empirical findings that shed light on the prevalence and characteristics of societal bias in a wide range of LVLMs.

## Strengths:

This paper makes a significant contribution by addressing a critical issue in the evaluation of large vision-language models: the limitations of existing bias benchmarks due to safety guardrails. The authors' proposed guardrail-agnostic framework is a novel and effective solution that allows for reliable bias measurement even in models with strong safety mechanisms. The shift from attribute-infering prompts to person-irrelevant prompts, and the use of images as user context rather than direct subjects of inference, is a key innovation. This approach enables the evaluation of societal bias without triggering refusals, which is a major advancement over previous methods. The paper's methodology is rigorously validated across 20 recent LVLMs, including both open-source and proprietary models, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method in measuring societal bias across different model architectures and training paradigms. The use of diverse tasks such as story generation, term explanation, and exam-style QA provides a robust assessment of bias in various contexts. The authors also employ rigorous statistical methods to quantify bias, ensuring the validity of their findings. The paper is well-structured and clearly written, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide detailed explanations of their methodology, including the rationale behind their choices and the steps involved in the evaluation process. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates key concepts and results, enhancing the clarity and impact of the paper. The empirical findings, which demonstrate that all models exhibit societal bias, with proprietary models showing less bias than open-source ones, are significant and have important implications for the development and deployment of LVLMs. The paper's discussion of potential factors behind the observed differences, such as safety-aware training and continuous model monitoring, is also insightful and provides a valuable starting point for future research. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its innovative methodology, rigorous empirical validation, and significant findings that contribute to our understanding of societal bias in LVLMs.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a valuable contribution to the field, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's focus on gender and racial biases, while important, does not fully capture the complexity of societal bias. As the authors themselves acknowledge, societal bias encompasses a much wider range of demographic attributes, including age, socioeconomic status, and physical ability. The current evaluation, while comprehensive in terms of models and tasks, is narrow in the types of biases it considers. For instance, the impact of ageism or ableism in these models remains unexplored, potentially overlooking significant sources of societal bias. This limitation is evident in the paper's explicit focus on gender and race, as stated in the 'Dataset' section of the 'Evaluation Settings', where they mention using the FairFace dataset, which provides annotations for gender and race. The 'Target LVLMS' section also confirms this focus, stating that they follow prior work that focuses on gender and racial biases. This narrow scope limits the generalizability of the findings and the applicability of the proposed framework to a broader range of societal biases. Second, the paper's methodology relies on statistical disparities in model outputs as a measure of bias, but it does not directly assess the harm or impact of these biases. While statistical disparities can indicate the presence of bias, they do not capture the potential harm or impact of these biases. A more comprehensive evaluation framework should incorporate measures of bias harm or severity. For example, the paper does not explore whether the model's outputs perpetuate harmful stereotypes or have negative social consequences. This limitation is evident in the paper's description of the bias score calculation, which is based on statistical disparity using Total Variation Distance (TVD), as described in the 'INSTANTIATION ACROSS TASKS' section. The paper lacks a mechanism or metric to assess the potential harm or severity of the observed biases. Third, while the paper discusses potential sources of bias, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how these biases manifest within the models. The 'DISCUSSION: BIAS SOURCES AND DEPLOYMENT RECOMMENDATIONS' section mentions 'safety-aware training' and 'continuous monitoring and iterative refinement' as potential factors, but it does not delve into a detailed analysis of the training data, model architecture, or specific safety alignment techniques used in these models. This lack of granular analysis limits our understanding of the mechanisms behind the observed biases and hinders the development of effective mitigation strategies. The paper also does not adequately address the potential for bias in the evaluation dataset itself. While the authors attempt to control for confounding factors by aligning non-target demographic distributions, as stated in the 'Task details' section, they do not explicitly discuss the potential biases inherent in the FairFace dataset itself, such as representation imbalances or annotation biases. This could lead to inaccurate bias measurements. Finally, while the paper does explore the relationship between model size and bias, as shown in the 'Observation 2.5' section and Figure 4, the analysis is not as detailed as it could be. A more in-depth analysis of the training data, model architecture, and safety alignment techniques used in these models could provide valuable insights into the origins of bias and how to mitigate it. The paper's analysis of model size and performance is limited to correlations and does not provide a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's findings, highlight areas where further research is needed to develop a more comprehensive understanding of societal bias in LVLMs. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and methodology.


## Suggestions:

To address the limitations identified, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand the scope of their evaluation to include a more diverse set of demographic attributes beyond gender and race. This could involve incorporating datasets that contain images with annotations for age, socioeconomic status, and physical ability, among others. The evaluation should also include tasks that are specifically designed to elicit these types of biases. For example, the story generation task could be modified to assess whether the model generates different narratives based on the perceived age or socioeconomic status of the person in the image. Furthermore, the term explanation task could be adapted to examine if the model provides different levels of technical detail based on these attributes. By broadening the range of biases considered, the study would provide a more comprehensive assessment of societal bias in LVLMs. This would also require a careful consideration of how to define and measure bias for these additional attributes, which may require the development of new metrics and evaluation protocols. Second, the authors should explore methods for directly assessing the harm or impact of biases, rather than relying solely on statistical disparities. This could involve analyzing the sentiment or toxicity of model outputs, or by evaluating the potential for biased outputs to perpetuate harmful stereotypes. For example, if the model consistently generates negative or stereotypical descriptions of certain demographic groups, this would indicate a more severe form of bias than if the model simply generates different types of outputs for different groups. It is also important to consider the context in which the model is being used and the potential for bias to cause harm in real-world scenarios. This could involve conducting user studies or analyzing the impact of model outputs on different demographic groups. Third, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the potential sources of bias in the evaluated models. This could involve analyzing the training data used to train the models, examining the architecture of the models, or conducting ablation studies to identify the components that contribute most to bias. For example, if the training data contains biased representations of certain demographic groups, this could lead to the model learning and perpetuating these biases. Similarly, if the model architecture is not designed to be robust to bias, this could also contribute to the problem. Understanding the mechanisms behind bias is crucial for developing effective mitigation strategies. Additionally, the paper should address the potential for bias in the evaluation dataset itself. This could involve analyzing the distribution of images across different demographic groups and ensuring that there are no confounding factors that could lead to inaccurate bias measurements. Finally, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the relationship between model size, training data, and bias. This could involve examining the demographic distribution of the training data and identifying potential biases in the representation of different groups. The authors should also investigate the specific architectural choices and safety alignment techniques used by proprietary models, which may contribute to their lower bias scores. This could involve analyzing the impact of different training objectives, regularization techniques, and data augmentation strategies on the model's bias. Furthermore, the authors should explore the use of interpretability techniques to identify the specific features or patterns in the input data that contribute to biased outputs. By combining these approaches, the authors could gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of bias and develop more effective mitigation strategies. These recommendations, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of societal bias in LVLMs.


## Questions:

Several questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how does the proposed guardrail-agnostic framework handle cases where the image context is ambiguous or contains multiple individuals? Is there a risk that the model's responses could be influenced by factors other than the intended demographic attribute? This is important to understand the robustness of the method in real-world scenarios. Second, the paper observes that proprietary models exhibit less societal bias than open-source models. What specific factors contribute to this difference? Is it primarily due to differences in training data, model architecture, or safety alignment techniques? A more detailed analysis of these factors would provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of bias and how to mitigate it. Third, how does the proposed method perform when applied to models with different types of safety guardrails? Are there any specific types of guardrails that are more likely to interfere with the evaluation process? This is important to assess the generalizability of the method across different models and safety mechanisms. Fourth, the paper focuses on gender and racial biases. How well does the proposed framework generalize to other forms of societal bias, such as ageism, ableism, or socioeconomic bias? What modifications would be needed to adapt the framework to these other forms of bias? This is crucial to understand the limitations of the current framework and how it can be extended to address a broader range of biases. Fifth, the paper uses a fixed set of person-irrelevant prompts for evaluation. How sensitive are the results to the choice of prompts? Would different prompts lead to significantly different bias scores? This is important to assess the reliability of the method and the potential for prompt engineering to influence the results. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required to implement the proposed framework. What are the main computational bottlenecks, and how can they be addressed to make the framework more accessible to researchers with limited resources? This is important to ensure that the method is practical and can be widely adopted by the research community. These questions highlight key areas where further investigation is needed to fully understand the strengths and limitations of the proposed framework.
