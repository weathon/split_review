## Summary:

This paper presents a rigorous investigation into the performance of neural network policy ensembles compared to their linear counterparts in control systems. The authors develop a theoretical framework, primarily using the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation and control Lyapunov functions, to demonstrate that neural policy ensembles are inherently suboptimal and less stable than linear policy ensembles, even when individual policies within the ensemble are stable. This theoretical analysis is grounded in the fundamental differences in how linear and nonlinear functions behave, particularly in the context of temporal coupling in control systems. The core argument is that the temporal coupling of actions in control systems can amplify errors in nonlinear ensembles, while linear ensembles benefit from the superposition principle. The authors further show that using a neural network to mix policies results in suboptimal performance compared to convex mixing. To validate these theoretical claims, the authors conduct a series of empirical studies on linear and nonlinear dynamical systems, including a multi-regime linear system, pendulum, and vadDerPol systems. These experiments demonstrate a significant performance gap between neural and linear ensembles, with neural ensembles often underperforming by orders of magnitude. Additionally, the experiments highlight stability issues with neural ensembles, which can exhibit significant instability even under identical operating conditions where linear ensembles remain stable. The paper's findings have significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning and agentic-AI, particularly in safety-critical applications where stability and optimality are paramount. The authors' work challenges the widespread adoption of neural network ensembles in control, suggesting that linear ensembles may be a more reliable and effective choice in many scenarios. The paper's contribution lies in its rigorous theoretical analysis and empirical validation of the limitations of neural policy ensembles, providing valuable insights for future research in control and reinforcement learning.

## Weaknesses:

While I appreciate the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the theoretical analysis, while rigorous, primarily focuses on linear and relatively simple nonlinear dynamical systems. As the authors themselves acknowledge, this focus may limit the generalizability of their findings to more complex, high-dimensional, or non-stationary environments commonly encountered in reinforcement learning. The theoretical framework is built upon continuous-time nonlinear dynamical systems, and the proofs are derived specifically for stabilizable linear systems and general nonlinear systems. The experiments are conducted on a multi-regime linear dynamical system and relatively low-dimensional systems like pendulum and vadDerPol. This focus on simpler systems raises concerns about whether the sub-optimality results would hold in more complex settings. The paper does not explicitly discuss the limitations of its theoretical framework regarding high-dimensional or non-stationary environments, which is a significant omission. Second, the study mainly considers well-established ensemble methods and LQR-based linear policies. The neural network policy is a general feedforward network, and the neural policy ensemble is a simple weighted sum. The linear policy ensemble is also a weighted sum. The paper does not explore a broader range of neural network architectures, such as recurrent or transformer-based policies, nor does it consider more sophisticated ensemble techniques like boosting or bagging. This narrow focus limits the scope of the analysis and may not capture the full potential of neural network ensembles. The paper's experiments use basic feedforward networks and weighted ensembles, and the paper does not discuss or compare against more sophisticated ensemble techniques. Third, the paper demonstrates the suboptimality of neural ensembles but does not offer concrete solutions or strategies to mitigate this issue. The conclusion suggests future research directions, such as ensemble methods that operate within stable subspaces or adding temporal consistency regularization, but these are suggestions for future work, not solutions presented in the paper. The paper's focus is on proving sub-optimality, not on proposing solutions. The experiments are designed to demonstrate the performance gap, not to test mitigation strategies. Fourth, the paper lacks sufficient detail about the neural network controller. While the paper mentions that the NN controller is a feedforward neural network with configurable depth, width, and activation function, it does not provide specific details about the architecture, activation functions, or training algorithm used in the experiments. The training algorithm is mentioned as "gradient descent" but without specifics like the optimizer or learning rate. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and understand the specific characteristics of the neural network used. Fifth, the paper lacks sufficient detail about the Bayesian updates used for learning ensemble weights. The paper mentions that the weights are learned using Bayesian updates based on individual controller performance, but it does not provide details on the prior distributions, likelihood function, or specific update rules. This lack of detail makes it difficult to understand how the ensemble weights are determined and potentially replicate this aspect of the experiments. Sixth, the paper lacks sufficient detail about the experimental setup.
