## Summary:

This paper introduces DeepScientist, an innovative AI-driven system designed for autonomous scientific discovery. The core contribution of this work lies in its novel formalization of the scientific discovery process as a goal-oriented Bayesian Optimization problem. This approach moves beyond previous AI scientist systems by focusing on long-term, goal-directed research rather than simply generating novel combinations of existing ideas. DeepScientist employs a three-stage iterative workflow, mirroring the human research process, which includes strategizing and hypothesizing, implementation and verification, and analysis and reporting. A key component of this system is its persistent 'Findings Memory,' which accumulates both successful and failed experiments, allowing the system to learn and improve over time. This memory, coupled with a Bayesian optimization framework, enables DeepScientist to balance the exploration of new research avenues with the exploitation of promising ideas. The system's architecture is composed of specialized agents for various tasks, including coding, planning, and reasoning, all coordinated through the shared Findings Memory. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of DeepScientist through large-scale experiments on three cutting-edge AI research tasks: Agent Failure Attribution, LLM Inference Acceleration, and AI Text Detection. In each of these tasks, DeepScientist not only matched but also surpassed human-designed state-of-the-art methods, showcasing its ability to autonomously advance scientific frontiers. The paper provides the first large-scale empirical evidence that an AI system can continuously make significant scientific progress on complex AI tasks, rivaling human researchers under comparable compute budgets. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the system's performance, including scaling laws and the iterative trajectory of autonomous exploration, offering valuable insights into the scientific discovery process. The paper concludes by highlighting the challenges and bottlenecks in autonomous scientific discovery, particularly the need for more efficient exploration and the reduction of implementation errors. Overall, DeepScientist represents a significant step forward in the field of autonomous scientific discovery, demonstrating the potential of AI to accelerate scientific progress.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The most significant strength is the novel formalization of scientific discovery as a goal-oriented Bayesian Optimization problem. This approach is a marked departure from previous AI scientist systems, which often lack clear scientific goals and tend to focus on short-term exploration. By framing the discovery process as a long-horizon optimization problem, DeepScientist is able to pursue meaningful scientific progress in a way that mimics human researchers. The introduction of the 'Findings Memory' is another crucial innovation. This persistent memory, which accumulates both successful and failed experiments, allows the system to learn from its mistakes and improve over time. This is a significant advantage over systems that do not retain information about past failures, as it enables DeepScientist to avoid repeating the same mistakes and to build upon its previous discoveries. The system's architecture, with its specialized agents for various tasks, is also well-designed and allows for efficient information sharing and learning. The use of a shared knowledge base, the Findings Memory, ensures that all agents have access to the same information, which facilitates coordination and collaboration. The empirical results presented in the paper are also very impressive. The fact that DeepScientist was able to surpass human-designed state-of-the-art methods on three complex AI tasks demonstrates its potential to accelerate scientific progress. The large-scale experiments provide strong evidence that the system is capable of making meaningful scientific discoveries. Furthermore, the paper provides a detailed analysis of the system's performance, including scaling laws and the iterative trajectory of autonomous exploration. This analysis offers valuable insights into the scientific discovery process and provides a foundation for future research in this area. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain the technical details of the system and provide sufficient context for readers to understand the significance of their work.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, I have identified several weaknesses in the DeepScientist paper that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the system's reliance on high computational resources, which may limit its accessibility and practical implementation for some research groups. The paper explicitly states that the system consumed 'over 20,000 GPU hours' and utilized 'two servers, each with 8 Nvidia H800 GPUs' (Abstract, Section 4). This substantial computational demand, coupled with the lack of a detailed comparison to human-led research efforts, makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the approach for researchers without access to such resources. The scaling experiment in Section 4.3 further highlights the dependence on parallel resources, showing that the number of progress ideas increases with the number of GPUs used. This dependence on high computational resources is a significant limitation that needs to be addressed in future work. Another significant weakness is the system's reliance on human oversight to verify outputs and filter out hallucinations. The paper acknowledges that 'Three human experts supervise the process to verify outputs and filter out hallucinations' (Section 4). This reliance on human intervention indicates that the system is not yet fully autonomous and may demand significant human effort. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the types of errors and hallucinations encountered or the frequency of human intervention, making it difficult to assess the true level of autonomy. This lack of detail raises questions about the reliability of the system and its ability to operate independently. Furthermore, the paper acknowledges that the success rate of LLM-generated ideas is low, with approximately 60% of failed trials due to implementation errors (Section 4.3). This highlights a potential bottleneck in the system's efficiency, as a large number of trials are needed to achieve a successful outcome. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the types of implementation errors that occur or discuss potential strategies for improving the reliability of LLM-generated code. This low success rate and the lack of error analysis suggest that the system's exploration process is not as efficient as it could be. Additionally, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the specific algorithms and techniques used in the Bayesian Optimization process. While the paper mentions the use of the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm, it does not provide details on the choice of kernel function for the Gaussian Process, if one is used, or the specific implementation of the surrogate model. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the robustness and generalizability of the approach. The paper also lacks a comprehensive analysis of the computational resources required by DeepScientist compared to human-led research. While the authors mention that their compute budget is 'comparable to a "medium-sized lab"' (Section 4), this statement is vague and lacks quantitative support. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the computational cost, including the number of GPU hours, memory usage, and energy consumption, making it difficult to evaluate the practical feasibility of the system. Finally, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the Findings Memory. While the concept is valuable, the paper does not address potential issues such as the scalability of the memory, the efficiency of information retrieval, and the potential for bias in the stored findings. The paper mentions using a 'separate retrieval model' to overcome LLM context length constraints (Section 3), but it does not discuss the limitations of this model or how the system handles noisy or irrelevant findings. These limitations of the Findings Memory could affect the system's performance and its ability to scale to more complex tasks.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should focus on reducing the computational demands of DeepScientist. This could involve exploring more efficient search algorithms or implementing techniques like knowledge distillation to reduce the computational burden of individual experiments. The authors should also investigate the scalability of the system with respect to the complexity of the scientific tasks, providing a clearer understanding of the computational requirements for different types of problems. This would allow potential users to better assess the feasibility of implementing DeepScientist in their own research environments. Second, the authors should work towards reducing the need for human oversight. This could involve incorporating more robust verification mechanisms within the system or training the LLM on a dataset of verified scientific findings. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the types of errors and hallucinations encountered and the frequency of human intervention. This would help to understand the limitations of the system's reasoning and generation capabilities and guide the development of more effective error mitigation strategies. Third, the authors should focus on improving the reliability of LLM-generated code. This could involve incorporating more robust code synthesis techniques, such as using formal methods to verify the correctness of generated code, or employing more sophisticated debugging and error analysis tools. The authors should also analyze the types of implementation errors that occur most frequently and develop strategies to avoid them. Additionally, the authors should explore methods for improving the search efficiency, such as using more informed search strategies or incorporating prior knowledge into the search process. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the Bayesian Optimization process. Specifically, they should elaborate on the choice of kernel function for the Gaussian Process, justifying why a particular kernel was selected over others. They should also discuss the acquisition function used, explaining how it balances exploration and exploitation, and why it is suitable for the specific scientific tasks addressed in the paper. Furthermore, the authors should provide details on the implementation of the surrogate model, including the specific algorithms and techniques used. Fifth, the authors should include a comprehensive analysis of the computational resources required by DeepScientist. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the computational cost, including the number of GPU hours, memory usage, and energy consumption. The authors should also compare the computational resources required by DeepScientist to those typically used in human-led research for similar tasks. This would provide a better understanding of the practical feasibility of the system and its potential impact on scientific discovery. Finally, the authors should address the limitations of the Findings Memory. They should discuss how the system handles noisy or irrelevant findings, and how it ensures that the memory does not become saturated with redundant information. The authors should also explore the scalability of the memory, discussing how it can be efficiently managed as the number of findings grows. This discussion should include details on the data structures and algorithms used for storing and retrieving findings, as well as any techniques used to compress or summarize the information stored in the memory.
