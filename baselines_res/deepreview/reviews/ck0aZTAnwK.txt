## Summary:

This paper investigates pre-training strategies for language models under the constraint of limited data, a departure from the typical focus on computational limitations. The authors begin by demonstrating that simply increasing the number of training epochs or model parameters under fixed data constraints leads to overfitting, a critical issue that is often overlooked in the pursuit of larger models and more compute. To address this, they propose a 'regularized recipe' that involves significantly increasing weight decay, up to 30 times the standard practice, along with careful tuning of learning rates and epoch counts. This regularization strategy effectively mitigates overfitting, allowing for more efficient pre-training. Furthermore, the authors introduce an 'ensembling recipe,' where they train multiple models independently and combine their predictions, achieving a lower loss asymptote than the regularized single model approach. They also explore a 'joint scaling recipe' that combines parameter scaling with ensembling, further improving performance. A key aspect of their methodology is evaluating the asymptotic performance of these recipes, which provides a more comprehensive view of model potential than evaluating at a fixed compute budget. The authors also demonstrate that the benefits of ensembling can be transferred to smaller models through distillation, maintaining most of the performance gains while reducing model size. Finally, they show that these improvements in validation loss translate to better performance on downstream tasks, achieving a 9% improvement on pre-training evals. The paper's core contribution lies in its focus on data efficiency in language model pre-training, a timely and relevant problem as high-quality data becomes an increasingly scarce resource. The authors' systematic exploration of regularization, ensembling, and distillation, coupled with their novel evaluation methodology, provides valuable insights into optimizing pre-training under data constraints. The findings suggest that careful tuning of regularization, combined with ensembling and distillation, can lead to significant improvements in data efficiency, allowing for the training of high-performing models with less data and potentially lower computational costs.
