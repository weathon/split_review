## Summary:

This paper introduces Thoughtbubbles, a novel transformer architecture designed to dynamically allocate computational resources within the model's latent space. The core idea revolves around learning to fork or delete residual streams, effectively creating 'bubbles' of computation for tokens that require more processing. This adaptive mechanism is trained end-to-end using only a standard language modeling loss, without any explicit supervision or task-specific data. The authors demonstrate that Thoughtbubbles outperforms both standard decoder language models and non-adaptive parallel computation approaches in terms of perplexity on OpenWebText and pes2o datasets. Furthermore, they show improvements in zero-shot evaluations on tasks such as HellaSwag and LAMBADA. The method's key innovation lies in its ability to learn which tokens require more computation, allocating more resources to high-entropy tokens, which are often the more challenging ones. This is achieved through a forking mechanism that duplicates residual streams, allowing for more processing steps, and a pruning mechanism that discards less important streams. The authors also provide some analysis of the learned behavior, showing that the model tends to allocate more computation to tokens with higher uncertainty. The overall significance of this work lies in its ability to introduce adaptive computation into transformer models in an unsupervised manner, potentially leading to more efficient and effective models for a variety of tasks. The method is trained from pretraining and evaluated in zero-shot manner. The authors also conduct analysis on the learned behavior, which is interesting. The method is simple and effective, and can be trained with only language modeling loss. The method shows improved perplexity on OpenWebText and pes2o. The method is evaluated on multiple model scales and shows good scaling. The analysis on the learned behavior is interesting. The method is novel. It learns to dynamically allocate additional parallel residual streams in latent space, which is a new approach to adaptive computation in transformers. The method is trained with only language modeling loss, which is a significant advantage as it does not require any additional supervision or task-specific training data. The proposed method outperforms both standard decoder LMs and non-adaptive parallel computation approaches on OpenWebText and pe2o perplexity and in zero-shot evaluations such as HellaSwag and LAMBADA. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method in improving the performance of language models.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this work, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the presentation of the method, particularly the forking procedure, is unnecessarily complicated and difficult to understand. The paper dives directly into technical details without providing a high-level, intuitive explanation of how the method works. For example, the forking procedure is described in Section 2.3, involving multiple steps and equations (1-6), which makes it hard to grasp the core idea. Figure 2, which is intended to illustrate the forking procedure, is also confusing and does not effectively convey the flow of tokens through the network. This lack of clarity makes it challenging to fully appreciate the method's mechanics and potential limitations. The method is only evaluated on perplexity. It would be nice to see how it performs on downstream tasks. The method is only evaluated on two datasets. It would be nice to see how it performs on other datasets. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the dynamic forking mechanism. It is unclear how the additional computation required for forking and managing multiple residual streams impacts the overall efficiency of the model, especially in terms of inference time and memory usage. A more thorough analysis, including a breakdown of the computational costs associated with different components of the Thoughtbubbles architecture, would be beneficial. The paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other adaptive computation methods. While the authors compare against standard decoder LMs and non-adaptive parallel computation approaches, a more detailed comparison with existing adaptive computation techniques, such as those based on dynamic neural networks or conditional computation, would provide a better understanding of the advantages and disadvantages of Thoughtbubbles. This comparison should include not only performance metrics but also computational costs and model complexity. The current implementation of Thoughtbubbles has lower wall-clock efficiency compared to computation-matched baselines, which may limit its practical applicability in resource-constrained environments. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the dynamic forking mechanism, such as the cost of calculating and applying the cumulative scores, and how this overhead scales with model size and input sequence length. This lack of detailed profiling makes it difficult to assess the true efficiency bottleneck. The top-k selection for residual stream management can lead to a gradient bottleneck, potentially hindering the performance of models with deep forking. The paper does not explore alternative selection mechanisms or provide a thorough analysis of the impact of different top-k values on the model's performance and training dynamics. This raises concerns about the robustness of the method to different hyperparameter settings. The model's performance on downstream reasoning tasks is not fully explored due to hardware limitations, leaving questions about its scalability to more complex tasks. The evaluation is limited to perplexity and zero-shot evaluations, which do not fully capture the model's ability to perform complex reasoning. The paper lacks experiments on more challenging reasoning benchmarks, such as those requiring multi-step inference or symbolic manipulation. In Section 3.1, the authors say that they insert the first forking layer after a few regular transformer blocks to ensure that the forking score judgments see a broader context window. However, this means that only tokens that appear earlier in the context can have multiple residual streams. It would be more fair to compare to other methods if all tokens had the possibility of having multiple residual streams. Is there a reason you made this design choice? In Section 5, the authors say that their method allocates relatively less budget at tokens of the highest uncertainty, forming a concave parabolic relationship between entropy and computation allocation. They hypothesize that this is due to the relatively higher utility of further computation at areas of moderate (but not low) uncertainty. Is there any way to test this hypothesis? The paper only evaluate their method on a small scale and only compare to strong baselines (other models with the same number of parameters or FLOPs). It would be good to see the method applied to larger scales and compared to a wider range of baselines. It would also be good to ablate various design choices of the method. Finally, the analysis of the learned behavior is a good starting point, but it could be further enhanced by providing more detailed insights into the specific types of tokens or contexts that trigger the forking of residual streams. For example, it would be interesting to analyze the frequency and distribution of forking events across different layers of the model and different types of input sequences. This could provide a better understanding of how the model learns to allocate compute and how this allocation affects performance. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to investigate the relationship between the forking behavior and the model's internal representations, to understand how the dynamic allocation of compute affects the model's ability to capture different types of information. This could involve analyzing the attention patterns and activation patterns of the model's layers, to identify the specific features that are being processed by the forked residual streams. The confidence level for all these weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the absence of certain analyses or comparisons.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly improve the clarity of the method's presentation. This could be achieved by starting with a high-level, intuitive explanation of how the dynamic computation allocation works, before diving into the technical details. For example, they could begin by explaining the motivation behind allocating more computation to certain tokens, and then describe how the forking mechanism achieves this. The forking procedure, in particular, needs a clearer explanation. The authors should consider using a step-by-step approach, perhaps with a simple example, to illustrate how tokens are duplicated and how the cumulative scores are used to decide which tokens to keep or discard. The current Figure 2 is not helpful in this regard and should be replaced with a more intuitive diagram that clearly shows the flow of tokens through the network. Additionally, the authors should consider adding a table that summarizes the key notations used in the paper, which would make it easier for the reader to keep track of the different variables and parameters. Second, the authors should expand the evaluation of their method to include a wider range of tasks and datasets. This should include more challenging reasoning tasks, such as those requiring multi-step inference or symbolic manipulation. For example, the authors could evaluate their method on datasets such as the ARC challenge or the MATH dataset. The evaluation should also include a more detailed analysis of the model's performance on different types of reasoning tasks, such as logical reasoning, arithmetic reasoning, and common-sense reasoning. Furthermore, the authors should explore the use of techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting to improve the model's reasoning abilities. The authors should also evaluate their method on datasets with different characteristics, such as datasets with different genres, topics, or writing styles. This would help to identify potential biases or limitations of the method and provide a more comprehensive understanding of its performance. For example, evaluating on datasets with more structured data, such as code or mathematical text, could reveal whether the method is effective in domains beyond natural language. Additionally, it would be useful to analyze the performance of the model on datasets with varying levels of noise or ambiguity, to understand how the dynamic allocation of compute affects the model's robustness to these factors. Third, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the dynamic forking mechanism. This should include a breakdown of the time spent on different operations, such as calculating the cumulative scores, performing the forking operation, and executing the attention mechanism. The analysis should also explore how these costs scale with model size and input sequence length. Furthermore, the authors should investigate hardware-aware optimizations, such as custom kernels for the forking and gathering operations, to improve the practical efficiency of the method. The paper should also include a thorough analysis of the impact of different top-k values on the model's performance and training dynamics. This analysis should investigate how the choice of k affects the diversity of the residual streams and the stability of the training process. Additionally, the authors could consider incorporating techniques such as residual connections or skip connections to improve the flow of gradients through the network. A comparison of different selection mechanisms and their impact on model performance would provide valuable insights into the robustness of the method. Fifth, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive comparison with other adaptive computation methods. This should include a detailed discussion of existing techniques, such as dynamic neural networks and conditional computation, and a comparison of their architectural differences, computational costs, and performance characteristics. The authors should also provide a more detailed comparison of the performance of Thoughtbubbles with these methods on a range of tasks and datasets. This comparison should include not only perplexity but also other relevant metrics, such as accuracy and F1-score. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the robustness of Thoughtbubbles to different hyperparameter settings and compare it with other adaptive computation methods. For example, the authors could compare the sensitivity of Thoughtbubbles to the number of residual streams and the forking probability with other methods. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the interpretability of the Thoughtbubbles architecture. Furthermore, the authors should investigate whether the learned forking patterns are consistent across different tasks and datasets. This analysis would provide a better understanding of the inner workings of the Thoughtbubbles architecture and its potential for further improvement. The authors should also conduct ablation studies to investigate the impact of various hyperparameters, such as the number of forks, the size of the residual streams, and the specific scoring function used. This would help to understand which design choices are most important for the method's performance and provide guidance for future research.
