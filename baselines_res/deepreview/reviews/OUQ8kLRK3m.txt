## Summary:

This paper introduces DRE-Bench, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the fluid intelligence of large language models (LLMs) through dynamic abstract reasoning tasks. The benchmark is structured around a hierarchical cognitive framework, encompassing four levels of cognitive complexity: Attribute, Spatial, Sequential, and Conceptual. Each level is associated with specific latent rules and abstract reasoning tasks, and the authors employ a code-based generator and solver to create approximately 4,000 dynamic task variants. This dynamic generation aims to mitigate data contamination issues prevalent in static benchmarks. The authors evaluate a range of state-of-the-art LLMs, including both general-purpose models and those specifically designed for reasoning, and find that while reasoning-oriented models generally outperform general-purpose models, their accuracy declines as the cognitive level and task complexity increase. The paper highlights the gap between current LLM capabilities and human-like fluid intelligence, suggesting that DRE-Bench can serve as a valuable tool for tracking progress in LLM reasoning abilities. The core contribution of this work lies in the creation of a dynamic, cognitively-inspired benchmark that allows for a more nuanced evaluation of LLMs' abstract reasoning abilities. The use of a code-based generation pipeline enables the creation of a large number of task variants, which helps to address the issue of data contamination. The empirical findings reveal that while some LLMs demonstrate proficiency in lower-level reasoning tasks, they struggle with higher-level cognitive tasks, indicating a significant gap between current LLM capabilities and human-level fluid intelligence. The authors also explore the impact of in-context learning, showing that increasing the number of examples can improve performance, particularly on more complex tasks. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and innovative approach to evaluating LLMs' reasoning abilities, and the findings provide valuable insights into the current state of the field. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the depth of analysis of the models' reasoning processes and the lack of detailed experimental setup information, which I will discuss in more detail in the following sections. The paper's focus on dynamic task generation and cognitive alignment represents a significant step forward in the evaluation of LLMs, but further work is needed to address the identified limitations and fully realize the potential of DRE-Bench.

## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the models' reasoning processes. This could involve techniques such as probing or attention analysis to gain insights into the internal representations and reasoning steps of the LLMs. For example, they could analyze the activation patterns of different layers in the model when solving specific tasks to identify which parts of the model are responsible for different aspects of the reasoning process. They could also use attention maps to visualize which parts of the input the model is focusing on when making predictions. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of explainable AI (XAI) methods to generate human-interpretable explanations of the model's reasoning steps. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of how the models are solving the tasks and whether they are truly grasping the underlying reasoning rules. Additionally, the authors should consider incorporating metrics beyond accuracy, such as the number of steps taken to reach a solution or the complexity of the reasoning path, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the models' reasoning abilities. Second, to enhance the reproducibility and transparency of the study, the authors should provide a more detailed description of the experimental setup. This should include the specific prompts used for each task, including any variations or modifications made for different models. The authors should also provide the hyperparameter settings for each LLM, such as the temperature, top-p, and maximum token length. Furthermore, the criteria for determining the complexity levels of the generated tasks should be clearly defined. For example, how is the number of steps in a planning task related to its complexity? What are the specific parameters that control the complexity of the spatial tasks? Providing this level of detail would allow other researchers to replicate the experiments and build upon the findings of this paper. The authors should also consider releasing the code and data used in the study to further enhance reproducibility. Third, to mitigate the potential for data contamination, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the possibility of contamination, even with the code-verifiable data generation process. They could test the models on adversarial examples that are designed to be similar to the generated tasks but are not present in the training data. They could also analyze the training data of the models for the presence of the generated code or similar patterns. Furthermore, they could explore techniques such as data augmentation or the use of synthetic data to generate additional training data that is free from contamination. The authors should also consider the possibility that the models may have learned to solve the tasks through memorization or pattern matching, rather than genuine rule understanding. They could address this by evaluating the models on tasks that require generalization to unseen scenarios or by using techniques such as few-shot learning to assess the models' ability to adapt to new tasks with limited training data. Fourth, to improve the human study, the authors should provide more detailed information about the participants and the experimental procedure. This should include details on the participants' backgrounds, such as their education levels, experience with abstract reasoning tasks, and any other relevant demographic information. The authors should also provide a detailed description of the experimental procedure, including the instructions given to the participants, the measures taken to ensure data quality, and the methods used to analyze the results. This would allow for a more thorough assessment of the validity and generalizability of the human study results. Furthermore, the authors should consider comparing the human performance on DRE-Bench with existing human performance data on similar abstract reasoning tasks to provide a more robust validation of the benchmark. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of DRE-Bench. This should include a discussion of potential biases in the benchmark design, as well as the types of reasoning skills that are not covered by the benchmark. The authors should also compare DRE-Bench to existing benchmarks for fluid intelligence, highlighting its unique advantages and disadvantages. This would help to contextualize the paper's findings and demonstrate the significance of its contributions. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can further strengthen the validity and impact of their work.
