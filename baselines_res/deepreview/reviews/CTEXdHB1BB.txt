## Summary:

This paper introduces CANON, a novel reinforcement learning framework designed to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by leveraging human priors on training metrics without assuming a specific directional impact on performance. The core idea behind CANON is to regroup sampled responses based on the values of a target metric, such as entropy or response length, and then compute inter-group and intra-group advantages. This approach allows the model to adaptively amplify metric changes, identifying beneficial behaviors without relying on handcrafted priors. The method involves sorting responses based on a chosen metric and dividing them into two groups. The inter-group advantage is calculated by comparing the rewards of responses from different groups, while the intra-group advantage is computed by comparing responses within the same group. These two advantages are then combined to guide the model's learning process. The authors demonstrate CANON's effectiveness across various LLMs, including Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, and Llama3.1-8B, and on a range of reasoning tasks, primarily focusing on math reasoning benchmarks like AIME, Olympiad, AMC, MATH-500, and GSM8k, as well as high-complexity logic reasoning tasks using ZebraLogic subsets. The empirical results show that CANON consistently outperforms existing methods, such as DR.GRPO, in terms of both performance and efficiency. Specifically, CANON based on entropy improves performance across different models and tasks, while CANON based on response length yields a more favorable Pareto frontier in the performance-cost trade-off. The authors also provide a theoretical analysis of CANON, including proofs of its properties and relationships to existing methods. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel approach to advantage estimation, which avoids handcrafted priors and adaptively amplifies metric changes, leading to improved reasoning capabilities in LLMs. The authors present a comprehensive experimental evaluation, demonstrating the effectiveness of CANON across multiple models and benchmarks. Overall, this work presents a significant advancement in the field of reinforcement learning for LLMs, offering a more flexible and adaptive approach to enhancing reasoning capabilities.

This is a significant limitation because it is unclear how the method would generalize to more than two groups. The paper does not provide any theoretical justification or experimental evidence for the generalization of CANON to scenarios where responses are divided into three or more groups based on different metrics. This lack of theoretical grounding for more than two groups raises concerns about the method's robustness and applicability in more complex scenarios.
