## Summary:

This paper introduces LLM4GCL, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the performance of large language models (LLMs) in graph continual learning (GCL) scenarios, specifically addressing the challenge of catastrophic forgetting. The authors identify a critical flaw in existing GCL evaluation setups, namely task ID leakage during local testing, which can lead to inflated performance metrics. To address this, they propose a more rigorous evaluation protocol. Furthermore, they present SimGCL, a simple yet effective method that leverages LLMs and a prototype network to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in GCL. SimGCL employs an ego-graph-derived prompt for each node, capturing both textual and structural features, and utilizes a training-free prototype classifier in incremental sessions to avoid catastrophic forgetting. The method is designed to be efficient, using parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with LoRA in the initial session and avoiding parameter updates in subsequent sessions. The authors demonstrate that SimGCL achieves a substantial performance improvement, approximately 20%, over existing GNN-based baselines under rehearsal-free conditions. The paper also provides an open-source platform to facilitate further research in this area. The core contribution of this work lies in the identification of a flaw in existing GCL evaluation methods, the introduction of a new benchmark, and the proposal of an effective method for mitigating catastrophic forgetting in GCL using LLMs. The empirical findings highlight the potential of LLMs in addressing GCL challenges, but also reveal limitations in current GLM-based approaches. The authors provide a comprehensive analysis of the performance of various methods, including LLM-based and GLM-based approaches, across multiple datasets. The overall significance of this work is in its contribution to improving the evaluation standards in the field of GCL and in demonstrating the effectiveness of LLMs in addressing catastrophic forgetting, while also highlighting the challenges and limitations of current GLM-based methods.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the authors' identification of task ID leakage in current GCL experimental setups is a significant contribution. This overlooked flaw can lead to inaccurate evaluations of GCL methods, and the authors' efforts to address it by proposing a more rigorous evaluation protocol are commendable. This contribution enhances the validity of GCL research and sets a higher standard for future studies. Second, the introduction of LLM4GCL as a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating LLMs in GCL is a substantial achievement. This benchmark provides a standardized platform for researchers to assess and compare different methods, which is crucial for the advancement of the field. The inclusion of various datasets and evaluation metrics makes LLM4GCL a valuable resource for the community. Third, the proposed SimGCL method demonstrates a substantial performance improvement over existing baselines. The method's simplicity, combined with its effectiveness in mitigating catastrophic forgetting, is impressive. The use of ego-graph-derived prompts to capture both textual and structural features, along with the training-free prototype classifier, is a novel approach that yields significant results. The reported 20% performance increase over existing GNN-based baselines under rehearsal-free conditions is a testament to the effectiveness of SimGCL. Finally, the authors' provision of an open-source platform further enhances the impact of their work by facilitating future research and development in this area. This commitment to open science is crucial for the rapid progress of the field.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's primary focus on node classification tasks limits its exploration of other important GCL scenarios, such as edge prediction or graph-level classification. The problem formulation, method description, and experimental setup are all centered around node classification. For example, the paper states, "Given a TAG with labeled nodes $\mathcal{V}^l$, the node classification task aims to predict the labels of the unlabeled nodes $\mathcal{V}^u$." (Section 2). The method SimGCL is designed to predict node labels, as evidenced by the description: "Then during prediction, for node $v_i$ with its embedding $\mathbf{h}_i$, its probability of belonging to class $j$ is formulated as follows..." (Section 3.3). The experimental setup also reinforces this focus, with the training tasks defined as $\mathcal{T}_{s_i} = \{\mathcal{G}_{s_i}, \mathcal{C}_{s_i}, \mathcal{V}_{s_i}^l\}$, where $\mathcal{V}_{s_i}^l$ represents labeled nodes (Section 2). The evaluation metrics used, such as accuracy after each training task, are specific to node classification. This narrow focus reduces the generalizability of the findings to other graph learning tasks. The absence of experiments on tasks like edge prediction or graph classification limits the scope of the benchmark and the applicability of SimGCL to a wider range of real-world problems. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world applications involve predicting relationships between nodes or properties of the graph as a whole. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's explicit focus on node classification is evident throughout the methodology and experimental sections. Second, while the paper claims that SimGCL is efficient, it lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and resource requirements, especially when scaling to larger graphs. The paper mentions efficiency as an advantage of SimGCL, stating, "Technically, SimGCL only requires a single round of instruction tuning in the first session, dramatically reducing training costs." (Section 3.3). However, there is no quantitative analysis of the time and memory requirements of the proposed method. The paper does not provide any explicit measurements or analysis of training time, inference time, or memory usage for SimGCL or the baselines. There is no comparison of computational costs between SimGCL and GNN-based methods. The absence of a detailed computational complexity analysis, such as Big O notation, makes it difficult to assess the scalability of SimGCL. This lack of analysis is a significant oversight, as the practical applicability of any method depends on its computational efficiency. Without this analysis, it is difficult to determine whether SimGCL is suitable for large-scale graphs. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's claims of efficiency are not supported by any empirical evidence or theoretical analysis. Finally, the paper does not provide a thorough investigation into the reasons behind the underperformance of GLM-based methods in GCL. While the authors offer some explanations, such as overfitting and architectural gaps, a more in-depth analysis is needed. The paper states, "Contrary to expectations, deliberately designed GLMs fail to achieve competitive performance..." (Section 4). The authors attribute this to overfitting and architectural gaps, stating, "We attribute this phenomenon to these models’ tendency to overfit training samples..." (Section 4) and "On the one hand, the architectural gap between shallow GNNs and deep LLMs leads to divergent parameter updates during continual learning..." (Section 4). However, these explanations are not supported by detailed analysis or ablation studies. The paper does not include ablation studies on the components of GLM architectures, nor does it analyze training dynamics like gradient behavior. This lack of in-depth analysis limits the understanding of why GLMs struggle with catastrophic forgetting in GCL. A more thorough investigation, including ablation studies and gradient analysis, would be necessary to fully understand the limitations of GLMs in this context. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's explanations for GLM underperformance are high-level and lack the necessary empirical support.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should broaden the scope of their evaluation beyond node classification. This could involve including tasks such as edge prediction, graph classification, and link prediction in the LLM4GCL benchmark. For edge prediction, the benchmark could include tasks that involve predicting the existence or type of relationships between nodes, which are common in many real-world applications. For graph classification, the benchmark could include tasks that require predicting properties of the graph as a whole, such as categorizing molecules based on their properties or classifying social networks based on their structure. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method and its applicability to a wider range of graph learning problems. The authors should also consider including datasets with varying graph sizes and densities to assess the scalability of the proposed approach. This would help to determine how well SimGCL performs on different types of graphs. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity and resource requirements of SimGCL. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time and memory consumption for each step of the algorithm, such as the ego-graph construction, prompt generation, and prototype classifier training. The analysis should also consider the impact of graph size, number of nodes, and number of edges on the overall performance and resource consumption. A comparison with existing GNN-based approaches would be beneficial to demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method. This analysis should be supported by empirical results on datasets of varying sizes and complexities. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of their approach in terms of scalability and resource usage, and suggest potential solutions for addressing these limitations. This would provide a more complete picture of the practical feasibility of SimGCL. Finally, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the reasons behind the underperformance of GLM-based methods in GCL. This investigation should include ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components of the GLM architecture on the performance in GCL tasks. For example, the authors could investigate the impact of different attention mechanisms or layer configurations on the performance of GLMs in GCL. Additionally, the authors should analyze the gradients during training to understand why these models struggle with catastrophic forgetting. This analysis could provide valuable insights into the limitations of GLMs in GCL and guide the development of more effective methods for addressing these challenges. The authors should also consider exploring alternative training strategies or regularization techniques that could improve the performance of GLMs in GCL. This would help to identify the specific factors that contribute to the underperformance of GLMs and guide future research in this area.
