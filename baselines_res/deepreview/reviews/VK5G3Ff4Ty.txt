## Summary:

This paper investigates the potential of small language models (SLMs) and small vision-language models (SVLMs) to perform clinical text summarization and radiology report generation tasks, comparing their performance against larger, domain-adapted language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs). The authors explore the effectiveness of these smaller models in zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned settings, aiming to determine if they can achieve comparable performance to their larger counterparts while reducing computational costs and addressing privacy concerns associated with deploying large models on-premise in clinical environments. A key contribution of this work is the introduction of a "Collapse Analysis" framework, which is used to assess the quality trade-offs in smaller models across dimensions such as task adherence, hallucination rate, and prompt robustness. This framework helps to identify performance degradation points in smaller models, providing insights into their limitations. The study employs a diverse set of models, including both general-purpose and domain-adapted models, to provide a broad perspective on the capabilities of smaller models in clinical NLP tasks. The empirical findings suggest that while SLMs can approach or sometimes exceed the performance of larger models with fine-tuning, SVLMs still lag in generating detailed radiology reports, highlighting the need for larger models in visual reasoning tasks. The paper's overall significance lies in its exploration of the feasibility of using smaller, more efficient models in clinical settings, which could have significant implications for resource allocation and model deployment. The authors also contribute a novel analysis framework for evaluating model performance, which could be useful in other contexts. The study's findings are particularly relevant given the increasing interest in deploying AI models in healthcare, where computational resources and data privacy are major concerns. By demonstrating that smaller models can achieve comparable performance in certain tasks, the paper opens up new possibilities for more efficient and accessible AI solutions in clinical practice. However, the paper also highlights the limitations of smaller models, particularly in tasks that require complex visual reasoning, suggesting that larger models are still necessary for certain applications. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by systematically evaluating the trade-offs between model size, performance, and computational efficiency in clinical NLP tasks.

## Strengths:

This is particularly important given the high computational costs and privacy concerns associated with deploying large language models (LLMs) on-premise in clinical settings. The authors' investigation into whether smaller models can achieve comparable performance to larger models is a significant step towards making AI more accessible and practical in healthcare. Second, the experimental setup is comprehensive, evaluating models across various adaptation methods, including zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning scenarios. This thorough approach provides a nuanced understanding of the capabilities of smaller models under different conditions. The use of multiple metrics to assess different aspects of generation quality further strengthens the validity of the findings. Third, the inclusion of a "Collapse Analysis" framework is a valuable methodological contribution. This framework provides a granular approach to evaluating model performance across key dimensions like task adherence, hallucination rate, and prompt robustness. This framework is a significant contribution, as it allows for a more detailed understanding of the limitations of smaller models and helps to identify the points at which their performance degrades. Finally, the study includes a diverse set of models, including both general-purpose and domain-adapted models, which provides a broad perspective on the capabilities of smaller models in clinical NLP tasks. This diversity of models allows for a more robust and generalizable analysis of the findings. The paper's focus on practical applications and its thorough experimental design make it a valuable contribution to the field. The inclusion of the "Collapse Analysis" framework and the diverse set of models further enhances the paper's contribution.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents valuable insights, several weaknesses limit the scope and generalizability of its findings. First, the paper lacks a clear and explicit statement of its main contributions in the introduction. While the research question is interesting, the absence of a dedicated contributions section makes it difficult to immediately grasp the novelty and significance of the work. The introduction should be revised to include a clear statement of the paper's key contributions, such as the identification of a minimum viable model size for clinical tasks, the development of the "Collapse Analysis" framework, and the empirical findings regarding the performance of SLMs and SVLMs. This lack of clarity makes it harder for the reader to quickly understand the main takeaways of the paper and appreciate its contributions to the field. The abstract does mention key contributions, but the introduction itself does not explicitly list them, which is a significant oversight. Second, the experimental evaluation is limited to a small number of datasets, specifically MeQSum and MIMIC-CXR. This limited scope may not fully represent the diversity of clinical text summarization and report generation tasks. The specific nature of these tasks, which involve structured clinical information, might not reflect the challenges in more complex medical scenarios such as open-ended question answering or longitudinal patient data analysis. The performance of small models in these tasks might be inflated due to the relatively constrained nature of the clinical text and report structures. The study should consider including additional datasets, such as those containing discharge summaries or progress notes, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the models' generalizability. The current datasets focus on specific types of clinical text, and expanding the evaluation to include a wider range of clinical documents would strengthen the paper's conclusions. Third, the paper does not provide sufficient detail on the experimental setup, including hyperparameter settings, training procedures, and the specific prompts used for each task. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and assess the robustness of the findings. While the paper mentions using in-context learning and PEFT techniques, it lacks specific information on hyperparameter settings for fine-tuning, such as learning rates, batch sizes, and the number of training epochs. Additionally, the exact prompts used for zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning scenarios are not fully detailed. This lack of transparency hinders the reproducibility of the study and makes it difficult for other researchers to validate the findings. The paper should include a detailed description of the experimental setup in the appendix, including the specific values for learning rates, batch sizes, optimization algorithms, and the exact prompts used for each task. This level of detail is crucial for other researchers to replicate the experiments and validate the results. Fourth, the evaluation of generation quality relies heavily on automatic metrics, such as BLEU, ROUGE, and BERTScore, which may not fully capture the nuances of clinical language and the factual accuracy required in medical reports. These metrics, while useful for evaluating text similarity, do not adequately capture the semantic correctness and clinical validity of the generated text. For example, a generated report might score well on these metrics but still miss critical clinical findings or include irrelevant information. The study lacks a direct evaluation of the clinical utility of the generated summaries and reports by medical professionals. The authors should incorporate human evaluations by clinical experts to assess the clinical relevance and accuracy of the generated summaries and reports. This would provide a more reliable assessment of the models' performance in real-world clinical settings. Finally, the study's focus on a limited set of tasks (clinical summarization and radiology report generation) may not fully generalize to other medical applications. The specific nature of these tasks, which involve structured clinical information, might not reflect the challenges in more complex medical scenarios such as open-ended question answering or longitudinal patient data analysis. The performance of small models in these tasks might be inflated due to the relatively constrained nature of the clinical text and report structures. The study should consider expanding the scope of evaluation to include a broader range of medical NLP tasks. Additionally, the comparison primarily involves models up to 3 billion parameters, leaving uncertainty about how the findings would scale to larger SLMs or more complex architectures. It is unclear if the observed 'safety collapse' would occur at higher parameter counts within the 3B range, or if different architectural choices would alter the identified thresholds. The study does not explore the impact of varying the number of layers or attention heads within this parameter range. The "Collapse Analysis" is insightful but relies heavily on automatic metrics, which may not fully align with clinical accuracy or practitioner preferences. The study lacks a detailed exploration of the architectural features that contribute to stability and performance in SLMs. The study does not delve into the specific mechanisms within the models that lead to the observed 'safety collapse.' This lack of mechanistic understanding limits the practical applicability of the findings for model design and fine-tuning. These limitations, taken together, significantly impact the generalizability and practical implications of the study's findings.

In addition to MeQSum and MIMIC-CXR, the authors should consider incorporating datasets that contain discharge summaries, progress notes, and other types of clinical documents. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the models' ability to handle different types of clinical text and would strengthen the paper's conclusions regarding the applicability of SLMs and SVLMs in real-world clinical settings. The inclusion of diverse datasets would also help to identify potential limitations of the models and would provide a more nuanced understanding of their performance across different clinical tasks. The authors should also consider using datasets that represent different medical specialties to further assess the generalizability of their findings. Third, to enhance the reproducibility and robustness of the findings, the paper should include a detailed description of the experimental setup in the appendix. This description should include specific information about the hyperparameter settings, training procedures, and the exact prompts used for each task. For example, the authors should specify the learning rates, batch sizes, optimization algorithms, and the number of training epochs used for fine-tuning. Additionally, the exact prompts used for zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning scenarios should be provided. This level of detail is crucial for other researchers to replicate the experiments and validate the results. Furthermore, the authors should consider providing the code and trained models to facilitate reproducibility and further research in this area. Fourth, to address the limitations of relying solely on automatic metrics, the study should incorporate a more robust evaluation framework that includes clinical validation. This could involve a panel of medical experts who assess the quality and accuracy of the generated summaries and reports, focusing on clinical relevance, diagnostic accuracy, and overall utility. The experts could use a standardized rubric to evaluate the outputs, providing a more nuanced and clinically relevant assessment. This would help to bridge the gap between automatic metrics and real-world clinical application. Moreover, the study should investigate the correlation between automatic metrics and clinical expert ratings to better understand the strengths and limitations of each evaluation approach. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's performance and its potential impact on clinical practice. Fifth, the study should delve deeper into the architectural features that contribute to model stability and performance. This could involve analyzing the internal representations of the models, examining the attention patterns, and identifying the specific layers that are most critical for task adherence and hallucination control. Techniques such as layer ablation studies, attention visualization, and probing experiments could be used to gain a more mechanistic understanding of the models. This analysis could reveal specific architectural modifications or training strategies that could improve the performance and stability of small language models. Furthermore, the study should explore the impact of different pre-training strategies on the observed safety thresholds, as this could provide insights into how to optimize model training for clinical applications. Finally, the study should explore the impact of different fine-tuning strategies on the performance of small models, as this could reveal methods to improve their robustness and accuracy across various medical tasks. By implementing these suggestions, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its impact on the field.
