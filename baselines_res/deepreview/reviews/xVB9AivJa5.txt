## Summary:

This paper introduces Blueprint-Bench, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the spatial reasoning capabilities of AI models by tasking them with converting apartment photographs into accurate 2D floor plans. The authors assess a diverse range of models, including large language models (LLMs), image generation models, and agent systems, using a scoring algorithm that measures the similarity between generated and ground-truth floor plans based on room connectivity graphs and size rankings. The results reveal a significant performance gap, with most models performing at or below a random baseline, while human performance is substantially superior. This highlights a critical blind spot in current AI capabilities, particularly in tasks requiring complex spatial understanding. The benchmark is model-agnostic, allowing for the evaluation of various types of models, and the authors have open-sourced the code and a sample dataset, promoting reproducibility and further research. The paper's core contribution lies in the creation of a challenging and relevant benchmark that exposes the limitations of current AI models in spatial reasoning, an area crucial for the development of general intelligence. The authors' methodology involves a detailed process of image collection, ground truth floor plan adaptation, and a carefully designed scoring algorithm. The empirical findings clearly demonstrate that current AI models struggle with this task, indicating a need for further research into spatial reasoning capabilities. The overall significance of this work is in its identification of a critical weakness in current AI systems and the provision of a valuable tool for future research in this area. The paper's emphasis on a model-agnostic approach and the open-sourcing of resources further enhance its impact on the AI research community. The authors' focus on a practical, real-world task makes the benchmark particularly relevant and insightful. By highlighting the disparity between human and AI performance, the paper underscores the importance of developing more robust spatial reasoning capabilities in AI systems. The paper's findings suggest that current AI models, despite their advancements in other areas, still lack the fundamental spatial understanding required for tasks like converting photographs to floor plans. This work serves as a call to action for researchers to address this gap and develop more spatially intelligent AI systems. The paper's clear articulation of the problem, methodology, and results makes it a valuable contribution to the field, and the open-sourced benchmark will undoubtedly facilitate further research and development in this critical area.

## Weaknesses:

Upon careful examination, several weaknesses in this paper warrant attention. Firstly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the impact of image quality on model performance. While the input modality is photographic images, the paper does not explore how variations in image resolution, quality, camera angles, and lighting conditions affect the models' ability to extract relevant spatial information. This is a significant oversight, as these factors can substantially influence the performance of vision-based models. The experimental setup focuses on evaluating models on the provided dataset without manipulating these image characteristics. The absence of such analysis is a valid concern, as it leaves open the question of how robust the models are to real-world variations in input data. The paper's methodology section describes the dataset as consisting of 50 apartments, each with approximately 20 images of the interior, but it does not mention any control or analysis of image quality. This lack of analysis limits the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the models' performance in less-than-ideal conditions. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper provides no evidence to counter this claim. Secondly, the scoring algorithm, while well-designed, has limitations that are not fully addressed. The algorithm focuses on room connectivity and size rankings but does not account for the accuracy of individual room shapes and orientations. This could lead to an incomplete assessment of model performance, as models might score well by correctly identifying connectivity and relative sizes but still produce inaccurate room shapes and orientations. The paper acknowledges this limitation, stating, "Another limitation of our scoring method is that it does not account for the shape of the room." This is a critical limitation because two floor plans with identical connectivity and size rankings could still differ significantly in terms of individual room shapes, aspect ratios, and the precise angles at which walls intersect. This could lead to an overestimation of model performance if the generated floor plans are structurally similar but geometrically inaccurate. The paper's evaluation section describes the scoring components, which include Jaccard similarity for edge overlap, degree correlation for connectivity patterns, graph density matching, room count accuracy, door count accuracy, and door orientation distribution similarity, but none of these metrics directly assess the geometric accuracy of individual rooms. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation. Thirdly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the types of errors made by the models. A more granular error analysis, categorizing the specific types of mistakes (e.g., incorrect room connections, inaccurate size estimations, misidentification of room types), would provide valuable insights into the limitations of current models and guide future research directions. The "Results and Discussion" section primarily focuses on the mean similarity scores across different models and comparisons to baselines. While the discussion mentions specific model failures (e.g., GPT-4o and NanoBanana's poor instruction following), it does not provide a systematic categorization and analysis of the types of errors made by the models. This lack of detailed error analysis limits the paper's ability to provide specific guidance for future research. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's results section focuses on aggregate scores rather than a detailed breakdown of error types. Fourthly, the paper lacks a clear and concise formulation of the problem. While the paper describes the task intuitively, it lacks a formal problem formulation with explicit definitions of the input and output spaces and the mapping function. The "Introduction" describes the task as "The task is to create a 2D floor plan from photographs of an apartment interior," and the "Method" section describes the input as "photographs of an apartment interior" and the output as "a 2D floor plan image." However, there isn't a formal definition of the input space (e.g., image resolution, perspective), output space (e.g., scale, level of detail), or a mathematical formulation of the mapping function. This lack of formalization makes it difficult to assess the validity of the benchmark and the significance of the results. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide a formal mathematical definition of the problem. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable improvements. Firstly, to mitigate the lack of analysis on image quality, the authors should conduct experiments using images with varying resolutions, levels of detail, camera angles, and lighting conditions. This could involve creating a subset of the dataset with these variations and evaluating the models' performance on this subset. The analysis should not only report the overall performance scores but also provide a detailed breakdown of how each factor affects the models' ability to extract spatial information. For instance, the authors could analyze the performance drop when using lower resolution images or images with poor lighting. This would help in understanding the robustness of the models to real-world variations in input data. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques to mitigate the impact of low-quality images, such as image enhancement or data augmentation methods. This would make the benchmark more robust and applicable to real-world scenarios. Secondly, to improve the scoring algorithm, the authors should incorporate metrics that capture the accuracy of individual room shapes and orientations. This could involve using techniques such as polygon similarity metrics (e.g., Intersection over Union - IoU) to compare the generated room shapes with the ground truth. Additionally, the authors could consider using a combination of graph-based metrics (for connectivity) and shape-based metrics (for individual room accuracy) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. The weighting of these metrics should be carefully considered and justified based on the importance of each aspect of spatial reasoning. The authors could also explore the use of more sophisticated image comparison techniques that take into account the spatial relationships between rooms, rather than just their individual shapes and sizes. This would provide a more nuanced and accurate assessment of model performance. Thirdly, a detailed error analysis is crucial for understanding the limitations of the models. The authors should categorize the types of errors made by the models, such as incorrect room connections, inaccurate size estimations, misidentification of room types, and errors in room shapes and orientations. This analysis should be quantitative, providing statistics on the frequency of each error type. The authors should also provide qualitative examples of these errors, illustrating the specific challenges that the models face. This analysis should be used to guide future research directions, focusing on the specific weaknesses of the models. For example, if models consistently struggle with certain room configurations, this could indicate a need for more sophisticated architectural designs or training strategies. Fourthly, the paper needs a more rigorous definition of the problem being addressed. The authors should explicitly define what constitutes a 'photograph of an apartment' (e.g., resolution, perspective, lighting conditions) and what constitutes a '2D floor plan' (e.g., scale, level of detail, coordinate system). Furthermore, the mapping function, which transforms the input photograph into the output floor plan, should be formally defined, even if it is a black-box model. This would involve specifying the domain and codomain of the function, as well as any constraints or assumptions that are imposed on it. Without a clear problem formulation, it is difficult to assess the validity of the benchmark and the significance of the results. Fifthly, to improve the writing quality, the authors should thoroughly proofread the manuscript to eliminate grammatical errors and typos. The presence of such errors significantly detracts from the credibility of the work and makes it difficult to follow the authors' arguments. Furthermore, the authors should consider using more precise and technical language to describe the methods and results. For example, instead of using vague terms like 'spatial reasoning,' they should specify the particular spatial abilities that are being tested, such as 'depth perception,' 'object recognition,' 'scene understanding,' or 'geometric reasoning.' This would allow for a more nuanced and detailed analysis of the models' performance. The authors should also ensure that the figures and tables are clearly labeled and easy to understand, with sufficient captions and explanations. Finally, regarding the dataset, the authors should provide a more detailed description of the data collection process, including the source of the photographs, the method used to generate the ground truth floor plans, and any data augmentation techniques that were applied. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the dataset's diversity, including the range of apartment layouts, the number of rooms, the types of furniture, and the lighting conditions.
