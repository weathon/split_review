## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the mechanisms by which large language models (LLMs) bind and retrieve entities within a given context. The authors challenge the prevailing view that LLMs rely solely on positional information for this task, proposing instead that these models employ a combination of three distinct mechanisms: positional, lexical, and reflexive. Through a series of carefully designed controlled experiments, they demonstrate that while the positional mechanism is effective for entities at the beginning or end of a context, it becomes unreliable for entities in middle positions. To compensate for this limitation, LLMs utilize lexical and reflexive mechanisms, which provide more precise signals for entity retrieval. The authors conduct extensive experiments across nine different models, ranging in size from 2 to 72 billion parameters, and across ten distinct binding tasks. Their findings consistently show that these three mechanisms are employed across different model scales and task variations. Furthermore, the authors develop a causal model that integrates all three mechanisms, achieving near-perfect agreement with the observed behavior of LLMs. This causal model is a significant contribution, as it provides a formal framework for understanding how these mechanisms interact to enable entity binding and retrieval. The authors also explore the generalizability of their findings by conducting experiments with more open-ended text, which further strengthens their conclusions. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to our understanding of how LLMs process and utilize contextual information, and it provides a more nuanced perspective on the inner workings of these complex models.

## Strengths:

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper's core contribution lies in its novel perspective on entity binding and retrieval in LLMs. By moving beyond the traditional focus on positional mechanisms, the authors have identified and characterized two additional mechanisms—lexical and reflexive—that are crucial for accurate entity retrieval, especially in the middle of a context. This is a significant step forward in our understanding of how LLMs handle contextual information. The experimental design is another notable strength. The authors have conducted a thorough set of experiments across a variety of models and tasks, providing strong evidence for the existence and importance of the three proposed mechanisms. The use of controlled experiments, including counterfactual inputs, is particularly innovative and allows for a precise analysis of each mechanism's contribution. The development of a causal model that combines all three mechanisms is also a significant achievement. This model not only provides a formal framework for understanding the interplay of these mechanisms but also achieves near-perfect agreement with the observed behavior of LLMs, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing the underlying processes. Finally, the authors' efforts to test the generalizability of their findings by using more open-ended text is commendable. This step strengthens the paper's conclusions by showing that the identified mechanisms are not limited to simple, templated tasks but are also relevant in more realistic settings. The clarity of the writing and the logical flow of the arguments further enhance the paper's impact, making it accessible and engaging for a broad audience.


## Weaknesses:

While I found the paper to be generally strong, there are several weaknesses that I believe warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by multiple reviewers, is the limited generalizability of the findings due to the reliance on templated tasks. The paper's core experiments are based on 'templatic in-context reasoning tasks with a similar structure,' as stated in Section 2. The examples provided, such as '*Pete loves jam, Ann loves pie. Who loves pie?*', are indeed simple and repetitive. While the authors mention using ten binding tasks, including *boxes* and *music*, and later introduce padding with filler sentences to make the input more natural (Section 5), the fundamental structure of the tasks remains constrained. This simplicity raises questions about whether the identified mechanisms would behave similarly in more complex, real-world scenarios where entity relationships are less predictable and the context is more nuanced. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the identified mechanisms might interact with more complex syntactic structures, ambiguous references, or indirect relationships between entities. This limitation is significant because it casts doubt on the extent to which the findings can be generalized beyond the specific experimental setup used in the paper. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the paper's own descriptions of the experimental tasks and the lack of evidence demonstrating generalizability to more complex settings. 

Another significant weakness, also independently validated, is the lack of a detailed intuitive explanation of the causal model, particularly for readers who may not be familiar with causal modeling. The paper introduces the causal model in Section 4, defining the positional, lexical, and reflexive mechanisms as causal models with single intermediate variables. While the mathematical formulation of the combined causal model is presented in Equation 2, the paper does not provide a clear, intuitive explanation of *why* this specific combination and formulation are chosen, or how they relate to the underlying cognitive processes. The paper cites Pislar et al. (2025) for the approach of combining causal models, but it does not elaborate on the fundamental principles of causal modeling that underpin their approach for a broader audience. This lack of intuitive explanation makes it difficult for readers to fully grasp the significance of the model and its implications. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the paper's presentation of the causal model, which focuses on mathematical formulation without providing a clear, intuitive explanation. 

Furthermore, the paper lacks sufficient detail regarding the open-ended text used in the experiments. While the authors mention using '1,000 filler sentences that are “entity-less”, meaning they do not contain sequences that signal the need to track or bind entities' (Section 5), they do not provide any details on the source or generation process of these sentences. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the potential impact of the filler sentences on the experimental results. For example, if the filler sentences were generated using a simple random process, they might not accurately reflect the complexity and diversity of real-world text. This lack of transparency makes it difficult to evaluate the generalizability of the findings to more realistic settings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's lack of detail regarding the generation and characteristics of the filler sentences. 

Finally, the paper does not include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the study or suggesting future research directions. While the paper presents a thorough analysis of the three mechanisms, it does not explicitly address the potential limitations of the proposed approach or suggest avenues for future research. For example, the paper does not discuss the potential impact of different model architectures or training data on the observed mechanisms. It also does not explore the robustness of the findings to adversarial attacks or noisy inputs. This lack of discussion limits the paper's ability to provide a comprehensive understanding of the identified mechanisms and their implications. ## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to mitigate the concerns about generalizability, the authors should expand the complexity of the entity binding tasks. This could involve exploring scenarios with multiple entities of the same type, or introducing tasks where entities have hierarchical or relational dependencies. For example, they could design tasks where entities are related through family relationships (e.g., 'John's mother loves apples') or organizational structures (e.g., 'The manager of the team likes coffee'). This would provide a more robust test of the proposed mechanisms and better reflect real-world scenarios where entity relationships are often intricate and multifaceted. Furthermore, the authors should explore a broader range of template structures, including those with more complex syntactic structures and varied semantic content. This could involve incorporating templates with nested clauses, ambiguous references, or indirect relationships between entities. For example, they could use templates that involve conditional statements (e.g., 'If John likes apples, then Mary likes oranges') or temporal relationships (e.g., 'Before John left, he liked apples'). Additionally, the authors could consider using a template-free approach, where the tasks are constructed from naturally occurring text, to further enhance the ecological validity of their findings. This would help to ensure that the observed mechanisms are not artifacts of the specific templates used in the experiments. It would also be beneficial to analyze how the identified mechanisms behave when the context is more ambiguous or when the entity references are less explicit. Such an analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the robustness and limitations of these mechanisms. 

Second, to improve the intuitive understanding of the causal model, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how the positional, lexical, and reflexive mechanisms are represented within the model and how their interactions are formalized. Specifically, they should elaborate on how the model assigns weights to each mechanism based on the input context. Providing a concrete example of how the model processes a specific input and assigns weights to each mechanism would greatly enhance understanding. Furthermore, a discussion of the assumptions underlying the causal model and how these assumptions might affect the results would be valuable. It would also be helpful to clarify how the model handles cases where the mechanisms might conflict or provide competing predictions. This would allow readers to better assess the model's robustness and applicability to different scenarios. The authors could also consider including a visual representation of the causal model to further aid in understanding its structure and operation. 

Third, to address the lack of detail regarding the open-ended text, the authors should provide more information on the source of the text, the process by which it was generated (if synthetic), and the criteria used for selecting the text. For example, if the text was generated using a language model, the authors should specify the model used, the prompts provided, and any filtering or post-processing steps applied. Additionally, it would be beneficial to analyze the characteristics of the open-ended text, such as its length, complexity, and the types of entities it contains. This would allow readers to better understand the generalizability of the findings and identify potential limitations. A more detailed analysis of the model's performance on different types of open-ended text would also be valuable. 

Finally, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of their study and suggesting potential avenues for future research. This could include discussing the potential impact of different model architectures or training data on the observed mechanisms. For example, it would be interesting to investigate whether the relative importance of the positional, lexical, and reflexive mechanisms varies across different model families or sizes. Additionally, the authors could explore the robustness of their findings to adversarial attacks or noisy inputs. Furthermore, the authors could discuss the potential for extending their work to other tasks, such as question answering or text summarization. By addressing these limitations and suggesting future directions, the authors can further strengthen the impact of their work.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe would be beneficial to address. First, how do the identified mechanisms interact with each other when multiple entities of the same type are present in the context? The paper's experiments primarily focus on scenarios with unique entities, but in real-world settings, it is common to encounter multiple instances of the same entity type. Are there specific conditions under which one mechanism might dominate the others, and how do these mechanisms resolve conflicts when they provide competing predictions? Second, how do the authors envision the application of their findings in the development of more robust and reliable LMs, particularly in handling long-context tasks? The paper identifies the limitations of the positional mechanism in the middle of a context, but it does not provide specific strategies for mitigating this issue. Could the lexical and reflexive mechanisms be explicitly incorporated into model architectures or training methods to improve performance in long-context scenarios? Third, could the authors provide more details on the counterfactual inputs used in the experiments? How were these inputs designed to isolate the effects of each mechanism, and what steps were taken to ensure that they do not introduce unintended biases? A more detailed explanation of the counterfactual design would help to clarify the validity of the experimental results. Finally, how do the authors handle cases where the model's predictions are ambiguous or uncertain? The paper focuses on cases where the model makes a clear prediction, but in real-world settings, model predictions are often uncertain or ambiguous. How can the proposed mechanisms be used to quantify and mitigate this uncertainty, and how can they be used to improve the reliability of model predictions?
