## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to merging large language models (LLMs) by operating within a learned latent space, rather than directly manipulating model weights. The core idea revolves around encoding the weights of different LLMs into a lower-dimensional latent space using a transformer-based variational autoencoder (VAE). This latent space is designed to capture the functional aspects of the model weights, enabling more flexible and robust merging operations. The authors propose a two-stage training curriculum for the VAE, which first learns a high-capacity latent representation and then distills it into a compact code, aiming to improve generalization and stability. To address the challenge of merging models with different architectures, the paper introduces a dimensionality-matching projection technique that aligns the latent spaces of models with varying depths and widths. This allows for interpolation between models from different families, facilitating heterogeneous merging. The empirical validation of the method involves extensive experiments on various benchmarks and model architectures, demonstrating that latent-space interpolation consistently outperforms weight-space averaging, particularly for heterogeneous merges. The authors evaluate their approach on tasks such as MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HellaSwag, and GSM8k, using models like Gemma and LLaMA. They also explore merging LoRA experts, showcasing the method's ability to combine specialized models. The results indicate that the latent space merging approach not only achieves better performance on downstream tasks but also provides a more robust and architecture-agnostic way to combine LLMs. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components, such as the VAE architecture and compression ratio. Overall, this work presents a significant advancement in the field of model merging, offering a more flexible and effective alternative to traditional weight-space methods. The use of a latent space, coupled with a two-stage VAE training and dimensionality-matching projection, provides a robust framework for merging LLMs, even those with different architectures. The empirical results consistently demonstrate the superiority of this approach, highlighting its potential for practical applications in combining and enhancing the capabilities of large language models.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to merging large language models (LLMs) within a learned latent space. This is a significant departure from traditional weight-space merging methods, which often struggle with architectural differences between models. By encoding model weights into a lower-dimensional latent space using a transformer-based variational autoencoder (VAE), the authors have created a more flexible and robust framework for merging. This latent space is designed to capture the functional aspects of the model weights, allowing for meaningful interpolation and combination of models. The two-stage training curriculum for the VAE is another notable strength. By first learning a high-capacity latent representation and then distilling it into a compact code, the authors have improved the generalization and stability of the VAE, which is crucial for handling the complex weight distributions of LLMs. The dimensionality-matching projection technique is also a key contribution, enabling the alignment of latent spaces from models with different architectures. This allows for heterogeneous merging, which is a significant advancement over existing methods that are typically limited to models with similar architectures. The empirical validation of the method is extensive and well-designed. The authors have conducted experiments on various benchmarks and model architectures, consistently demonstrating that latent-space interpolation outperforms weight-space averaging, especially for heterogeneous merges. The inclusion of experiments merging LoRA experts further highlights the method's versatility and practical applicability. The ablation studies provide valuable insights into the impact of different components, such as the VAE architecture and compression ratio, enhancing the understanding of the method's behavior. The paper also provides a detailed analysis of LLM weight distributions, justifying the choice of VAE over simpler methods like PCA. This theoretical analysis strengthens the empirical results and provides a solid foundation for the proposed approach. Overall, the paper presents a well-executed and innovative approach to model merging, with significant contributions to the field of large language model research. The architecture-agnostic nature of the method, coupled with its robustness to heterogeneity, makes it a valuable tool for combining and enhancing the capabilities of LLMs.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to merging large language models, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by all four reviewers, is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with the proposed method. The paper introduces a two-stage VAE training process and a latent encoding/decoding step, both of which introduce additional computational costs compared to simpler weight-space methods. However, the paper does not provide a breakdown of the computational resources required for each stage of the VAE training, such as the time and memory consumption for the initial deterministic autoencoder training and the subsequent fine-tuning with the KL term. This absence of concrete data makes it difficult to assess the practical scalability of the approach, especially for very large models. Furthermore, the paper lacks a direct comparison of the computational cost of their method against other merging techniques, such as Task Arithmetic, in terms of actual runtime and GPU memory usage. This omission makes it challenging to evaluate the efficiency of the proposed method relative to existing alternatives. The lack of this analysis is a significant limitation, as it hinders the practical adoption of the method, especially in resource-constrained environments. The confidence level for this weakness is high, as all reviewers independently identified this issue, and the paper lacks any quantitative analysis of the computational cost. Another significant weakness, also highlighted by multiple reviewers, is the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices, particularly the VAE architecture and compression ratio. While the paper explores the impact of compression ratio and compares two VAE architectures (Transformer-VAE and ConvNet-VAE), it lacks a comprehensive sensitivity analysis of various VAE hyperparameters on the merging performance. Specifically, the paper does not systematically explore the impact of different VAE architectures, such as the number of layers, the dimensionality of the latent space, and the size of the hidden layers, on the quality of the latent space and the resulting merged model performance. This lack of analysis makes it challenging to determine the optimal VAE configuration for different model sizes and architectures. The paper also does not explore the impact of different compression ratios on the quality of the latent space and the resulting merged model performance. This lack of sensitivity analysis is a concern, as it makes the method less reliable and more difficult to apply in practice. The confidence level for this weakness is medium, as the paper does explore some hyperparameter variations, but not in a comprehensive manner. Finally, the paper's analysis of merging strategies is limited, primarily focusing on linear interpolation in the latent space. While the paper mentions extending latent-space linear merging to N models via a convex barycenter, the core merging operation in the latent space is linear interpolation. The paper does not investigate the impact of different interpolation methods, such as non-linear interpolation techniques or weighted averaging based on model performance. This lack of exploration limits the understanding of the full potential of latent space merging and may overlook more effective merging strategies. The confidence level for this weakness is medium, as the paper does explore some merging strategies, but not in a comprehensive manner. The absence of a thorough analysis of non-linear interpolation techniques and weighted averaging is a missed opportunity to fully explore the potential of latent space merging.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First and foremost, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with the proposed method. This analysis should provide a breakdown of the computational resources required for each stage of the VAE training, including the time and memory consumption for the initial deterministic autoencoder training and the subsequent fine-tuning with the KL term. This analysis should also include a comparison of the computational cost of their method against other merging techniques, such as Task Arithmetic, in terms of actual runtime and GPU memory usage. This would provide a more practical understanding of the method's scalability and allow for a more fair comparison with other merging techniques. Furthermore, the authors should explore techniques to reduce the computational cost of the VAE training, such as using more efficient VAE architectures or employing techniques like knowledge distillation to transfer the learned latent space to a smaller model. This would make the method more practical for large-scale applications. Second, the paper should conduct a more comprehensive sensitivity analysis of the VAE architecture on the final merging performance. This analysis should include a systematic exploration of the impact of different VAE architectures, such as the number of layers, the dimensionality of the latent space, and the size of the hidden layers, on the quality of the latent space and the resulting merged model performance. The authors should also investigate the impact of different compression ratios on the quality of the latent space and the resulting merged model performance. This analysis should provide guidelines for selecting the optimal VAE configuration for different model sizes and architectures. Additionally, the authors should explore techniques to make the method less sensitive to hyperparameter choices, such as using adaptive learning rate schedules or employing regularization techniques. Finally, the paper should explore more complex merging strategies beyond linear interpolation in latent space. This could include investigating non-linear interpolation techniques, such as spherical interpolation or other non-linear transformations, and weighted averaging based on model performance or other criteria. The authors should also explore the impact of different interpolation methods on the final merged model performance. This exploration would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the full potential of latent space merging and may uncover more effective merging strategies. The authors should also investigate the impact of different merging strategies on the robustness of the merged model to different tasks and datasets. These changes would significantly improve the practical applicability and robustness of the proposed method, making it more valuable for the research community.
