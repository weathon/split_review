## Summary:

This paper investigates the capacity of a single-layer transformer to solve the 0-1 knapsack problem, a well-known NP-complete problem. The authors employ a combination of interpretability techniques, including attention pattern analysis, singular value decomposition, logit lens, and activation patching, to understand why the model fails to learn an effective solution. The core methodology involves training a small transformer model on a dataset of 0-1 knapsack instances and then analyzing its internal behavior using the aforementioned techniques. The dataset is constrained to instances with only four objects due to computational limitations. The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer for up to 100,000 epochs. The main empirical finding is that the single-layer transformer struggles to generalize to the 0-1 knapsack problem. The authors observe a lack of robustness in the model's ability to model an algorithm for solving the knapsack problem, particularly in its failure to effectively integrate the capacity constraint during the initial embedding phase and the lack of layers, which restricts the power of the model. They hypothesize that transformer-based models struggle to generalize to NP-complete tasks due to the combinatorial explosion in considerations involved. Additionally, they propose that transformer-based models with *k* layers will only be able to generalize to tasks which can be solved using O(*n*^*k*) time complexity algorithms. The paper concludes that transformer-based models, at least in their single-layer form, are not well-suited for solving NP-complete problems like the 0-1 knapsack problem. This work is significant because it explores the limitations of transformer models in the context of computationally hard problems, using mechanistic interpretability to understand the model's internal behavior. However, the paper's limitations, particularly the use of a single-layer transformer and a small dataset, raise questions about the generalizability of its findings. The authors acknowledge these limitations, citing computational constraints as a major factor. Despite these limitations, the paper provides valuable insights into the challenges of using transformers for combinatorial optimization problems and highlights the need for further research in this area. The study also underscores the importance of interpretability techniques in understanding the inner workings of complex models and identifying the reasons for their successes and failures.

## Strengths:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the ability of transformer models to tackle the 0-1 knapsack problem, an NP-complete challenge. I find the core idea of using mechanistic interpretability to understand why a transformer struggles with this problem to be quite innovative. The authors' approach of combining various interpretability techniques, such as attention pattern analysis, singular value decomposition, logit lens, and activation patching, is a significant strength. This multi-faceted approach provides a more comprehensive view of the model's internal behavior than relying on a single technique. The use of these techniques to analyze the model's internal representations and identify the limitations of the model is a valuable contribution. The paper is also well-motivated, clearly stating the research question and the importance of understanding the limitations of transformer models in the context of complex problems. The authors' attempt to link the model's failure to the combinatorial nature of the knapsack problem is a thought-provoking hypothesis. The paper's structure is generally easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methods and results. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their study, which is a sign of intellectual honesty. The paper's focus on a single-layer transformer, while a limitation in some respects, allows for a more focused analysis of the model's behavior without the added complexity of multiple layers. This choice, while limiting generalizability, simplifies the analysis and makes it easier to interpret the results. The use of the TransformerLens library is also a positive aspect, as it provides a framework for analyzing transformer models. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its innovative approach, the use of multiple interpretability techniques, and the clear presentation of the research question and findings. The authors have made a valuable contribution to the field by exploring the limitations of transformers in the context of NP-complete problems.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses significantly impact the validity and generalizability of its conclusions. One major concern is the limited scope of the experiments. The authors focus solely on a single-layer transformer and a small dataset of 0-1 knapsack problems with only four objects. As stated in the paper, this constraint is due to computational limitations. This narrow focus makes it difficult to extrapolate the findings to larger models or more complex problems. The paper acknowledges this limitation, stating, "This work is limited by computational constraints. As a result of these constraints, the authors were unable to run further experiments on transformers with more layers, as well on a wide-range of tasks." This limitation is significant because it is unclear whether the observed failure is inherent to transformers or specific to the single-layer architecture and small dataset used. The lack of exploration of different hyperparameters and model architectures further compounds this issue. The paper mentions the use of the AdamW optimizer and training for up to 100,000 epochs, but lacks details on other crucial hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and the specific architecture of the transformer, including the number of attention heads and the dimensionality of the embeddings. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and assess the validity of the findings. The paper also lacks a clear definition of the term "grok," which is used in the introduction. While the general meaning might be inferable, a formal definition would improve clarity. The paper states, "In this study, we analyze the attention patterns of the trained small-scale transformer, and hypothesize why it is unable to perform well," but the term "grok" is not defined, which makes it difficult to understand the authors' intent. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a clear definition of the "given input" used for training the linear regressor in the probing experiments. The paper states, "We train a linear regressor to predict the given input based on the internal representations," but it does not specify what this input is, making it difficult to understand the probing experiment. The experimental design also suffers from a lack of baseline comparisons. The paper does not compare the performance of the transformer model against any other algorithms for solving the 0-1 knapsack problem, such as greedy approaches or dynamic programming. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of the transformer model. The learning curves presented in Figure 3 suggest that the model is not well-trained, as the loss plateaus before reaching a satisfactory level. This raises concerns about the conclusions drawn from the study, as the model may not have reached its full potential. The paper's conclusions are also not entirely convincing. The authors claim that transformer-based models struggle to generalize to NP-complete tasks due to the combinatorial explosion, but the paper does not provide a clear explanation of why this is the case. The paper states, "We hypothesize that: 1. Transformer-based models struggle to generalize to NP-complete tasks due to the **combinatorial explosion** in considerations involved," but this is presented as a hypothesis without strong mechanistic proof. The second hypothesis, that transformer-based models with *k* layers will only be able to generalize to tasks solvable in O(*n*^*k*) time complexity, is speculative and lacks substantial backing within the presented experiments. The paper states, "2. Transformer-based models with  $k$  layers will only be able to generalize to tasks which can be solved using  $O(n^k)$  time complexity algorithms," but this is not supported by any empirical evidence. Finally, the paper lacks a formal definition of the 0-1 knapsack problem and its significance in the context of the study. The paper states, "We train these transformers to solve the 0-1 knapsack problem, where the transformer has to give the best possible price as output," but it does not provide a detailed explanation of the problem or its relevance to the research question. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the impact and generalizability of the paper's findings. The lack of detail in the experimental setup, the limited scope of the experiments, and the speculative nature of some of the conclusions raise serious concerns about the validity of the paper's claims. My confidence in these identified weaknesses is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of crucial details.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the scope of their experiments. This includes exploring the impact of varying hyperparameters such as the number of attention heads, the dimensionality of the hidden layers, the learning rate, and the batch size. A systematic study of these parameters could reveal whether the model's failure is due to a fundamental limitation of the architecture or simply a suboptimal choice of hyperparameters. This could be done through a grid search or a more sophisticated hyperparameter optimization technique. Furthermore, the authors should experiment with different model architectures, such as adding more layers or using a different type of attention mechanism. This would help to determine if the single-layer transformer is inherently unsuitable for this task or if a more complex architecture could overcome the observed limitations. The authors should also consider using a larger dataset with more objects in the knapsack problem. This would allow them to assess the generalizability of their findings to more complex instances of the problem. To address the lack of baseline comparisons, the authors should compare the performance of their transformer model against other algorithms for solving the 0-1 knapsack problem, such as greedy approaches, dynamic programming, or other machine learning models. This would provide context for the model's performance and help to determine whether the observed limitations are specific to transformers or are a more general issue. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the model's internal behavior, beyond simple visualizations. For example, they could investigate the attention patterns of the transformer to understand which parts of the input sequence are being attended to and how this relates to the problem's structure. They could also analyze the learned representations of the input data to see if the model is capturing meaningful features. This could involve techniques such as feature importance analysis or concept activation vectors. To improve the clarity of the paper, the authors should provide a formal definition of the term "grok" and clearly define the "given input" used for training the linear regressor in the probing experiments. They should also provide a more detailed explanation of the transformer model's architecture, including the number of attention heads, the dimensionality of the embeddings, and the specific activation functions used. The training process also needs to be thoroughly documented, including the learning rate, batch size, optimizer, and the number of training epochs. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of the 0-1 knapsack problem, including its mathematical formulation and its significance as a benchmark for computational complexity. They should clearly articulate why this specific problem is chosen and how it relates to the broader question of transformer model limitations. Finally, the authors should temper their conclusions and frame their hypotheses as conjectures rather than definitive statements. The claim that transformer-based models struggle with NP-complete tasks due to combinatorial explosion is not novel and lacks a clear explanation of why this is the case. The hypothesis that transformer-based models with *k* layers can only generalize to tasks solvable in O(*n*^*k*) time complexity is speculative and needs further justification. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly improve the rigor and clarity of their paper and make a more substantial contribution to the field.

However, I would like to see a more detailed explanation of the underlying mechanisms causing this failure. Do the authors have a more specific hypothesis about what is going wrong? The paper does not include any baseline comparisons, which makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of the transformer model. Have the authors considered comparing their model to other machine learning models or traditional algorithms? This would provide valuable context for their findings. Fourth, can the authors provide a more detailed explanation of the 0-1 knapsack problem and its significance in the context of this study? The paper briefly describes the task but does not provide a formal definition or elaborate on its significance in the context of evaluating transformer models. This is important for understanding the relevance of the research question. Fifth, can the authors provide more details about the experimental setup, including the size of the dataset, the training procedure, and the evaluation metrics used? The paper lacks specific details about the transformer architecture and comprehensive training parameters, which makes it difficult to reproduce the results. Finally, can the authors provide more evidence to support their conclusion that transformer-based models struggle to generalize on NP-complete problems? The paper provides several interpretability analyses as evidence, but I would like to see more direct quantitative evidence of generalization failure on the knapsack problem itself. These questions are crucial for understanding the limitations of the current study and for guiding future research in this area.
