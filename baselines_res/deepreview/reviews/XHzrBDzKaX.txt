## Summary:

This paper introduces VisFactor, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the visual cognitive abilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). The benchmark is grounded in the Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT), a well-established suite of human cognitive assessments, and includes 20 vision-centric subtests spanning four key domains: Visualization and Spatial Processing, Perceptual and Closure, Memory, and Reasoning. The authors' core contribution lies in their adaptation of these cognitive tasks into an automated, image-text-based format suitable for MLLMs, coupled with a parametric generator that allows for the creation of an unlimited number of test cases with controllable difficulty. This approach aims to provide a more fine-grained evaluation of visual reasoning abilities compared to existing holistic benchmarks. The methodology involves a careful selection of subtests from the FRCT, a digitization process to convert these into image-text problems, and the implementation of a parametric generator to create varied test instances. The authors systematically evaluate 23 MLLMs, encompassing both proprietary and open-source models, using this benchmark. The empirical findings reveal a significant gap between human and MLLM performance, with even the best-performing model achieving only 30.17% accuracy. The paper highlights specific areas where MLLMs struggle, such as mental rotation, spatial relation inference, and figure-ground discrimination. The authors also conduct a failure analysis to understand the underlying reasons for these limitations, suggesting that models may rely on concept-level recognition rather than low-level perception. The overall significance of this work lies in its attempt to ground MLLM evaluation in human cognitive science, providing a more nuanced understanding of their visual reasoning capabilities and limitations. The paper also contributes a valuable resource in the form of the VisFactor benchmark, which can be used to drive further research in this area. The authors' approach of using parametric generation to create an unlimited supply of test cases is a significant contribution, ensuring the benchmark's long-term viability and relevance. The paper's findings challenge the assumption that large-scale pretraining alone is sufficient for human-like visual cognition, highlighting the need for more targeted research into the visual reasoning abilities of MLLMs. The authors' work provides a valuable foundation for future research aimed at developing MLLMs that can truly reason about visual information in a manner comparable to humans.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in the introduction of VisFactor, a novel benchmark that directly grounds MLLM assessment in human cognitive factors. This approach provides a more fine-grained evaluation of visual reasoning abilities compared to existing holistic benchmarks, which often focus on task-specific performance rather than foundational visual skills. The use of the Factor-Referenced Cognitive Test (FRCT) as a basis for the benchmark is a significant strength, as it leverages a well-established suite of cognitive assessments that have been extensively studied in human psychology. This grounding in human cognition provides a valuable framework for understanding the strengths and limitations of MLLMs in visual reasoning. The authors' careful selection of 20 vision-centric subtests from the FRCT, spanning four key domains of visual cognition, ensures a comprehensive evaluation of MLLMs' visual abilities. The digitization of these subtests into an automated, image-text-based format is another notable achievement, allowing for the systematic evaluation of a wide range of MLLMs. The implementation of a parametric generator to create an unlimited supply of test cases with controllable difficulty is a significant technical innovation. This approach ensures the benchmark's long-term viability and relevance, addressing the potential for overfitting to specific test instances. The ability to modulate the difficulty of the test cases through key parameters, such as rotation angle, occlusion level, and grid size, allows for a more nuanced evaluation of MLLMs' visual reasoning abilities. The paper also presents a comprehensive evaluation of 23 state-of-the-art MLLMs, offering valuable insights into the current capabilities and limitations of these models in visual reasoning tasks. The systematic evaluation, coupled with a detailed failure analysis, provides a solid foundation for future research aimed at improving the visual reasoning abilities of MLLMs. The authors' efforts to reduce chance-level accuracy through the design of the benchmark is also commendable, ensuring that the reported performance metrics reflect genuine model capabilities rather than random guessing. The paper's findings, which highlight significant gaps in MLLMs' visual reasoning capabilities despite high performance on other benchmarks, are a crucial contribution to the field. These findings challenge the assumption that large-scale pretraining alone is sufficient for human-like visual cognition, emphasizing the need for more targeted research into the specific visual reasoning skills that MLLMs lack. The paper's focus on foundational visual skills, rather than task-specific performance, is a valuable contribution that can guide future research in this area.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a valuable contribution, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of a detailed analysis of the synthetic data generation process and its potential limitations. Although the authors mention using parametric generation to create an unlimited supply of test cases, they do not provide a thorough analysis of how this process ensures diversity and avoids creating images that are too similar to each other. This is a critical issue because if the generated images are not sufficiently diverse, models might learn to exploit specific patterns in the synthetic data rather than developing genuine visual reasoning abilities. For instance, the paper lacks a discussion on the distribution of parameters used in the generation process and how this distribution is designed to cover the full range of possible visual scenarios. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the impact of different parameter settings on model performance, which is crucial for understanding the robustness of the benchmark. This lack of analysis raises concerns about potential overfitting to specific types of synthetic images. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of how well the synthetic tasks mirror the complexity of real-world visual reasoning. While the authors demonstrate the ability to generate synthetic data and show some performance differences, they do not delve into the specific cognitive skills that might be affected by the synthetic nature of the data. The results in Section 3.3 hint at this, showing a performance drop on abstract CF2 images, suggesting a limitation in handling certain types of synthetic visual information. However, a more explicit discussion and analysis of these limitations would strengthen the paper. The paper's analysis of model failures, while present, is somewhat limited in its depth. While the authors identify specific tasks where models struggle and provide some explanations, they do not delve deeply into the underlying reasons for these failures. For instance, the paper does not explore whether the models are failing due to a lack of spatial reasoning, an inability to handle abstract patterns, or other factors. A more detailed error analysis, including qualitative examples of model outputs and a discussion of the specific visual features that are causing difficulties, would be beneficial. The paper also lacks a discussion on the types of errors that are most common for each task, which could provide valuable insights into the models' limitations. While the paper mentions controllable difficulty and shows performance trends, it lacks a detailed explanation of how the difficulty levels are calibrated and validated. There's no discussion of the specific metrics used to quantify difficulty or a rigorous validation process to ensure that the "Easy", "Normal", and "Hard" labels accurately reflect the cognitive load required for each task. This lack of a detailed explanation makes it difficult to assess the validity of the difficulty levels and their impact on model performance. Finally, the paper does not provide a thorough comparison with existing benchmarks that evaluate visual reasoning in MLLMs. While it mentions that VisFactor is more fine-grained, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how VisFactor differs from these benchmarks in terms of the types of visual reasoning tasks included, the complexity of the tasks, and the evaluation metrics used. This makes it difficult to assess the unique contributions of VisFactor and its potential to advance the field. For example, the paper does not discuss how VisFactor compares to benchmarks that use real-world images or those that focus on specific aspects of visual reasoning, such as object recognition or scene understanding. This lack of a detailed comparison makes it difficult to position VisFactor within the existing landscape of MLLM evaluation and to understand its unique value proposition.

Furthermore, the authors should explore methods for generating images that are more robust to variations in visual features, such as adding noise or introducing slight distortions. This would help to ensure that models are learning to reason about visual information rather than simply memorizing specific patterns. The authors could also consider using techniques such as adversarial training to generate images that are specifically designed to challenge the models' visual reasoning abilities. This would help to ensure that the benchmark is truly testing the models' visual reasoning abilities and not just their ability to memorize specific patterns. Second, the authors should conduct a more detailed error analysis, including qualitative examples of model outputs and a discussion of the specific visual features that are causing difficulties. The authors should also explore the relationship between model architecture and performance on different types of tasks, which could provide valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different MLLM architectures. This analysis should also include a discussion of the limitations of the current evaluation metrics and whether they are adequately capturing the models' visual reasoning abilities. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how the difficulty levels are calibrated and validated. While the authors mention that the difficulty is controlled by key question parameters, a more rigorous analysis is needed to understand how these parameters affect the cognitive load required to solve the tasks. For example, the paper could include a psychometric analysis of the generated tasks, which would help in quantifying the difficulty of each task and validating the effectiveness of the difficulty control mechanism. This analysis should also explore the relationship between the difficulty parameters and the types of errors made by the models, which could provide insights into the specific cognitive skills that are affected by the difficulty level. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the limitations of the current difficulty control mechanism and suggest potential improvements for future work. Finally, the authors should provide a more thorough comparison with other relevant benchmarks. This should include a detailed analysis of the types of visual reasoning tasks included in each benchmark, the complexity of the tasks, and the evaluation metrics used. The authors should also discuss how VisFactor differs from these benchmarks in terms of its focus on human cognitive factors and its use of parametric generation to create an unlimited supply of test cases. This comparison should also include a discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of each benchmark, as well as the specific types of models that are best suited for each benchmark. This would help to clarify the unique contributions of VisFactor and its potential to advance the field of MLLM evaluation.
