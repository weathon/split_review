## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach for learning the parameters of a discrete Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) from a single trajectory. The core contribution lies in combining spectral methods, typically used for learning Predictive State Representations (PSRs), with tensor decomposition techniques to estimate the similarity transform that relates the learned PSR to the underlying POMDP parameters. The key idea is to first learn a PSR from trajectory data using singular value decomposition (SVD), and then leverage tensor decomposition to recover the similarity transform that maps the PSR to the POMDP's transition and observation matrices. This approach allows for learning POMDPs without requiring full state observability or full-rank transition matrices for all actions, thus broadening the class of POMDPs that can be effectively learned. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method through experiments on several standard POMDP domains, showing that it can accurately recover the underlying POMDP parameters and achieve comparable planning performance to PSRs when used with standard sampling-based POMDP solvers. The paper provides a theoretical analysis, including a theorem characterizing the conditions under which the similarity transform can be recovered up to a partition of the state space, and offers a detailed proof in the appendix. The experimental results indicate that the proposed method can learn accurate models, and the learned models can be used for planning with comparable performance to other methods. The paper also explores the method's ability to learn partition-level transitions and observations, which are essential for directing agent behavior in POMDPs with very noisy observations. The authors also show that the method can be used to specify rewards based on observations, which is a useful feature for real-world applications. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of POMDP learning by providing a novel method that combines spectral and tensor decomposition techniques to learn POMDP parameters from a single trajectory, relaxing some of the assumptions of previous methods. The method's ability to learn accurate models and achieve good planning performance, along with its theoretical grounding, makes it a valuable addition to the literature.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First and foremost, the core methodological approach of combining spectral methods for learning PSRs with tensor decomposition for estimating the similarity transform is a novel and valuable contribution. This combination allows for learning POMDPs without requiring full state observability or full-rank transition matrices for all actions, which is a significant advancement over existing methods. This generalization expands the class of POMDPs that can be effectively learned, making the method more applicable to real-world scenarios. The theoretical analysis provided in the paper, including the theorem characterizing the conditions under which the similarity transform can be recovered, adds substantial credibility to the proposed approach. The detailed proof of this theorem in the appendix further strengthens the theoretical foundation of the work. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the method and sufficient background information on POMDPs and PSRs. The experimental results are presented in a clear and concise manner, and the authors provide a thorough discussion of their findings. The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in learning accurate POMDP models, and the method achieves comparable performance to PSRs in terms of planning accuracy, while also providing explicit transition and observation likelihoods, which are valuable for downstream tasks. The paper also highlights the method's ability to learn partition-level transitions and observations, which are essential for directing agent behavior in POMDPs with very noisy observations. This is a particularly important contribution, as it addresses a key challenge in learning from partial observations. Furthermore, the method's ability to specify rewards based on observations is a useful feature for real-world applications, as it allows for more flexible and intuitive reward design. The paper's focus on learning from a single trajectory is also a strength, as it reduces the data requirements for learning POMDPs, making the method more practical for many applications. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its novel methodological approach, its strong theoretical foundation, its clear presentation, and its empirical validation, all of which contribute to its overall significance and impact.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of a detailed, step-by-step algorithm description, including a formal analysis of its time complexity. While the paper outlines the method conceptually in Section 4, it does not provide a numbered algorithm or a rigorous analysis of how the computational cost scales with the number of states, actions, and observations. This absence makes it difficult to assess the practical implementation details and the scalability of the method, particularly for larger POMDPs. The paper mentions the use of SVD and tensor decomposition, but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the steps involved in these processes within the context of their method, nor does it discuss the memory requirements for storing the intermediate matrices and tensors. This lack of clarity makes it challenging to understand the computational bottlenecks and the practical limitations of the approach. The paper also lacks a thorough comparison with existing methods for learning POMDPs. While the experimental section includes comparisons with linear PSRs and Expectation-Maximization (EM), the discussion primarily focuses on performance metrics (state estimation, error, reward) rather than a detailed analysis of the inherent advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to a broader range of existing POMDP learning techniques. Specifically, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with methods based on variational inference or other state-of-the-art techniques, which would be valuable for understanding the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. The paper also does not provide a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between the proposed method and EM in terms of computational complexity, sample efficiency, and robustness to noise. This lack of comparative analysis makes it difficult to gauge the practical value of the proposed method compared to the state-of-the-art. Furthermore, the paper's experimental evaluation is limited to a few standard POMDP domains, such as Tiger, T-Maze, and Sense-Float-Reset. While these domains are useful for demonstrating the method's ability to recover POMDP parameters, they do not fully capture the challenges of real-world POMDPs, which often have larger state and action spaces, more complex dynamics, and noisier observations. The paper does not evaluate the method on more complex and realistic domains, such as those found in robotics or natural language processing, which would provide a more convincing demonstration of its practical relevance. The paper also relies on several assumptions, such as the ergodicity of the POMDP and the full-rank assumption on the forward and backward matrices. These assumptions may not hold in all practical scenarios, limiting the applicability of the method. The paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the implications of violating these assumptions or guidance on how to assess whether these assumptions are likely to hold in a given application. The ergodicity assumption, while common, can be a significant limitation in real-world scenarios where state spaces are large and exploration is not guaranteed to reach all states. The full-rank assumption on the forward and backward matrices is also a strong condition that may not hold if the observation space is not rich enough to distinguish between different states, or if the action space does not provide sufficient transitions to explore the entire state space. Finally, the paper does not thoroughly explore the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors mention that their approach can handle POMDPs without full state observability, they do not discuss the types of POMDPs where their method might fail or perform poorly. For example, it is unclear how the method would perform in scenarios with highly stochastic transitions or when the observation space is very large and sparse. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the method's robustness to noise and its sensitivity to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the rank of the PSR and the number of samples used for tensor decomposition. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from a lack of detailed algorithmic description, insufficient comparative analysis with existing methods, limited experimental evaluation, strong assumptions, and a lack of thorough exploration of the method's limitations.
