## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel post-training quantization (PTQ) method for large language models (LLMs), focusing on the challenging task of 1-bit quantization. The authors identify a critical flaw in existing 1-bit PTQ methods: the naive layer-wise output alignment leads to error accumulation and performance degradation, particularly in deeper layers. To address this, they propose a selective layer-wise output alignment strategy that accounts for accumulated quantization errors. This strategy modifies the quantization objective to explicitly consider the impact of quantization errors from previous layers, thereby mitigating the error accumulation problem. Furthermore, the paper introduces an attention-aware masking mechanism called Attention Matrix Preservation (AMP) to maintain the integrity of attention behavior during quantization. AMP aims to preserve the crucial relationships within the attention matrix, which is vital for the performance of LLMs. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through extensive experiments on various LLMs and NLP tasks, showing significant improvements over existing 1-bit PTQ techniques. The core contribution of this work lies in its analysis of the limitations of naive layer-wise output alignment in 1-bit PTQ, and the introduction of a selective alignment strategy and AMP mechanism to overcome these limitations. The paper's findings suggest that careful consideration of error accumulation and attention preservation is crucial for achieving high performance in extremely quantized LLMs. The proposed method offers a promising solution for deploying LLMs on resource-constrained devices, where extreme quantization is often necessary. The empirical results show that the proposed method consistently outperforms existing 1-bit PTQ techniques across various LLMs and NLP tasks, highlighting its potential for practical applications. The paper's analysis of the limitations of existing methods and the introduction of novel techniques make it a valuable contribution to the field of model compression and deployment.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First, the authors provide a thorough and insightful analysis of the limitations of existing 1-bit post-training quantization (PTQ) methods. They correctly identify that naive layer-wise output alignment leads to error accumulation, which significantly degrades performance, especially in deeper layers. This analysis is crucial for understanding the challenges associated with extreme quantization of large language models (LLMs). Second, the proposed selective layer-wise output alignment strategy is a novel and effective approach to address the error accumulation problem. By modifying the quantization objective to explicitly account for accumulated quantization errors, the method mitigates the negative impact of quantization on model performance. This is a significant technical innovation that demonstrates a deep understanding of the underlying issues. Third, the introduction of the Attention Matrix Preservation (AMP) mechanism is another key strength. AMP is designed to maintain the integrity of attention behavior during quantization, which is essential for the performance of LLMs. The authors demonstrate that AMP significantly improves performance by preserving the crucial relationships within the attention matrix. This is a valuable contribution that addresses a critical aspect of LLM quantization. Finally, the paper provides extensive experimental results that demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The authors evaluate their approach on various LLMs and NLP tasks, showing significant improvements over existing 1-bit PTQ techniques. These empirical results provide strong evidence for the practical value of the proposed method. The combination of a thorough analysis, novel techniques, and strong empirical results makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with the proposed method. Although the authors mention 'minimal overhead' in the abstract and introduction, they do not provide quantitative comparisons of time complexity, memory footprint, or energy consumption against baseline methods. Specifically, the paper does not quantify the cost of the data-aware optimization process, including the time taken for forward and backward passes during the PTQ process. This omission is significant because the practical applicability of any quantization technique depends not only on its performance but also on its computational efficiency. The absence of this analysis makes it difficult to assess the trade-offs between performance gains and computational costs. The reference to an 'Overhead Analysis' in the Appendix suggests that this information might exist, but it is not included in the provided text, further highlighting this gap. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the lack of computational cost metrics is a clear and demonstrable omission. Second, the paper does not explore the potential of combining the proposed method with other quantization techniques, such as quantization-aware training (QAT). The paper focuses solely on post-training quantization (PTQ) and does not discuss the potential synergies or challenges of integrating the proposed method with QAT. This is a missed opportunity because QAT often achieves better performance than PTQ by training the model with quantization in mind. The authors do not discuss whether their method could be used as a pre-processing step before QAT or if it could be combined with QAT in a more integrated manner. This lack of exploration limits the potential impact of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's exclusive focus on PTQ and the absence of any discussion or experiments related to QAT is evident. Third, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, particularly its sensitivity to hyperparameter settings and its applicability to different LLM architectures. The paper does not investigate how sensitive the method is to hyperparameter choices, such as the learning rate and the number of optimization steps. Furthermore, the paper does not analyze the performance of the method on a wider range of LLM architectures, including those with different attention mechanisms and activation functions. This lack of analysis limits the generalizability of the proposed method and makes it difficult to assess its robustness. The experiments are primarily conducted on OPT and LLaMA models, and the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to support the claim that the method would perform equally well on other architectures. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the lack of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis and limited architectural scope in the experimental evaluation is clear. Fourth, the paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the impact of different calibration datasets on the performance of the proposed PTQ method. The choice of calibration data can significantly affect the accuracy of quantized models, and it is important to understand how sensitive the proposed method is to this choice. The paper consistently uses the C4 dataset for calibration but does not explore the impact of using other calibration datasets. This is a significant limitation because the performance of the PTQ method might vary depending on the characteristics of the calibration dataset. The paper does not investigate whether using calibration datasets that are closely aligned with the target downstream tasks would lead to better performance. This lack of analysis limits the understanding of the AMP mechanism and its potential limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the 'Attention Matrix Preservation' section focuses on the formulation and the ablation study shows performance impact but lacks a detailed analysis of the attention matrices.
