## Summary:

This paper introduces DiSTAR, a novel zero-shot text-to-speech (TTS) framework that operates entirely within a discrete residual vector quantization (RVQ) code space. The core contribution of DiSTAR lies in its innovative approach to speech synthesis, which tightly couples an autoregressive language model (AR LM) with a masked diffusion model. This coupling allows for the generation of speech without relying on forced alignment or a separate duration predictor, which are common components in traditional TTS systems. The framework operates by first using the AR LM to draft block-level RVQ tokens, effectively predicting the sequence of discrete codes that represent the speech. Subsequently, a parallel masked diffusion process infills the remaining tokens within each block, conditioned on the draft from the AR LM. This approach enables long-form synthesis with blockwise parallelism, mitigating the exposure bias often associated with autoregressive models. A key advantage of operating in the discrete RVQ space is the explicit control it affords at inference time. This control allows for trade-offs between robustness and diversity, as well as variable bit-rate and controllable computation through the pruning of RVQ layers at test time. The authors present extensive experimental results, including ablation studies, that demonstrate DiSTAR's superior performance compared to state-of-the-art zero-shot TTS systems. These results highlight improvements in robustness, naturalness, and speaker/style consistency, while maintaining rich output diversity. The paper's significance lies in its novel approach to TTS, which leverages the strengths of both autoregressive and diffusion-based models within a discrete representation space, offering a promising direction for future research in speech synthesis. The framework's ability to generate high-quality speech without explicit duration modeling and its inherent controllability at inference make it a valuable contribution to the field. The authors also demonstrate the practical benefits of their approach through extensive experimentation, showcasing the potential of DiSTAR for real-world applications. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and compelling argument for the efficacy of the proposed DiSTAR framework, positioning it as a significant advancement in the field of zero-shot TTS.

For example, the evaluation could include experiments where the model is tested on datasets with different acoustic conditions or speaker characteristics than those used during training. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of DiSTAR in relation to existing state-of-the-art methods.
