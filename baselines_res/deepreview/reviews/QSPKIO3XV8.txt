## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework, termed Dimension Domain Co-Decomposition (3D), for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) using physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). The core idea behind 3D is to combine dimension decomposition, which factorizes solutions along individual coordinates, with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)-based domain decomposition, which automatically partitions the solution space into regions handled by specialized sub-networks. The authors propose three key innovations: an interpretable dimension decomposition strategy using a shared multilayer perceptron (MLP) with indexed inputs, a novel metric called Variable Interpretability (VI) to quantify the alignment between learned latent representations and ground-truth components, and an MoE-driven domain decomposition that eliminates the need for predefined subdomains. The shared MLP architecture significantly reduces the number of parameters compared to using independent MLPs for each dimension, leading to improved computational efficiency. The VI metric provides a quantitative measure of the interpretability of the learned solution components, while the MoE framework allows for adaptive domain partitioning, which is particularly useful for problems with complex solution features. The authors evaluate their method on several PDE benchmarks, including the Poisson equation, wave equation, Burgers equation, and transport equation, demonstrating improved accuracy and computational efficiency compared to standard PINNs. The experimental results show that the proposed method can effectively capture the underlying physics of these problems, and the VI metric provides a means to assess the quality of the learned representations. The paper also explores the scalability of the method by applying it to higher-dimensional problems, such as the 10D Poisson equation. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for solving PDEs with PINNs, combining dimension and domain decomposition in a novel and efficient way. The introduction of the VI metric is a valuable contribution, offering a way to quantify the interpretability of the learned solutions. The experimental results, while limited in scope, demonstrate the potential of the proposed method for solving complex PDEs. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, there are several areas where the paper could be improved to further strengthen its claims and impact.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The core idea of combining dimension and domain decomposition within a single framework is a novel and potentially powerful approach for solving PDEs with PINNs. The authors' introduction of the Variable Interpretability (VI) metric is a significant contribution, providing a quantitative measure for assessing the alignment between learned latent representations and the ground-truth components of the solution. This is a crucial step towards making PINN solutions more interpretable, which is often a challenge with black-box neural network approaches. The use of a shared MLP architecture for dimension decomposition is another notable strength, as it significantly reduces the number of parameters compared to using independent MLPs for each dimension. This parameter efficiency translates to improved computational efficiency, as demonstrated in the experimental results. The MoE-driven domain decomposition is also a clever idea, as it allows the solution space to be automatically partitioned without the need for predefined subdomains or interface conditions. This adaptivity is particularly useful for problems with complex solution features, such as shocks or boundary layers. The experimental results, while limited in scope, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method on a range of PDE benchmarks. The authors show that their method achieves improved accuracy and computational efficiency compared to standard PINNs, and the VI metric provides a means to assess the quality of the learned representations. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the proposed method and its components. The authors have done a good job of presenting their ideas in a logical and coherent manner, making it easy for the reader to understand the key contributions of the paper. The combination of these strengths makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of physics-informed machine learning.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the experimental evaluation is limited in scope, focusing on a relatively narrow set of PDE benchmarks. While the chosen benchmarks, such as the Poisson, wave, Burgers, and transport equations, are relevant, they do not fully explore the method's robustness in more realistic scenarios. Specifically, the paper lacks experiments on problems with more complex geometries, such as irregular domains or curved boundaries, and problems with more challenging boundary conditions, such as time-dependent or non-homogeneous conditions. This limitation is evident in the experimental section (Section 4), where the focus is primarily on simple geometries and boundary conditions. This lack of diversity in the test cases makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the proposed method to more complex problems. Second, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method compared to existing approaches. While the authors mention training time and memory usage for the shared MLP compared to independent MLPs in Section 4.2, they do not provide a comprehensive comparison against other PINN variants or traditional numerical methods. This lack of a thorough computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios. For example, the paper mentions that the shared MLP requires a higher per-epoch cost, resulting in a total training time of 1579 s versus 1184 s for independent MLPs on the 10d Poisson problem. However, this is not sufficient to understand the method's efficiency compared to other methods. A more detailed breakdown of the computational cost, including the cost of evaluating the PDE residual, computing gradients, and updating network parameters, is needed. Furthermore, the scalability of the method with respect to the number of dimensions and the size of the domain is not thoroughly investigated. Third, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the conclusion briefly mentions that the VI metric relies on reference solutions that are dimension-separable, there is no discussion of how the method performs on problems with highly non-smooth solutions or solutions with multiple scales. The sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices, particularly those related to the MoE architecture and the dimension decomposition, is also not addressed. This lack of discussion on limitations makes it difficult to understand the potential drawbacks and challenges of the proposed method. Fourth, the proposed method relies on the availability of reference solutions for computing the Variable Interpretability (VI) metric. As stated in Section 3.2, the VI metric requires the ground-truth matrix by evaluating the exact j-th factor. This reliance on analytical solutions limits the applicability of the VI metric in purely data-driven settings, where such solutions are not available. This is a significant limitation, as it restricts the use of the VI metric to problems where analytical solutions are known. Fifth, the paper does not provide a strong justification for why the proposed method is specifically better at handling sharp features compared to other domain decomposition methods. While the method is evaluated on the Viscous Burgers equation, which involves a shock, there is no direct comparison against other domain decomposition methods specifically designed for handling sharp features. The paper claims that the MoE-driven domain decomposition can automatically capture sharp features, but there is no concrete evidence or analysis to support this claim. Sixth, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of how the proposed method is different from existing methods that use dimension decomposition. While the related work section discusses dimension decomposition methods, a more detailed, point-by-point comparison highlighting the specific advantages of the 3D framework's dimension decomposition strategy is needed. The paper introduces the shared MLP architecture as a key difference, but a more explicit comparison with existing methods is needed to highlight the novelty of the approach. Seventh, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the proposed method to the choice of hyperparameters. While the paper mentions specific hyperparameter settings, there is no systematic analysis of how varying these hyperparameters affects the performance of the method. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to choose appropriate hyperparameters for different problems. Finally, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method. While the authors present experimental results, a theoretical analysis of the convergence rate is missing. The paper shows empirical convergence curves for the 5d Poisson equation (Figure 2), but there is no formal analysis of the convergence rate or theoretical guarantees. This lack of theoretical analysis makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the method. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be improved to further strengthen its claims and impact. My confidence in these identified weaknesses is high, as they are all supported by direct evidence from the paper's content and a lack of specific analyses or comparisons.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the experimental evaluation should be significantly expanded to include a wider range of PDE benchmarks. This should include problems with more complex geometries, such as those involving irregular domains or curved boundaries, and problems with more challenging boundary conditions, such as time-dependent or non-homogeneous conditions. Furthermore, the method should be tested on PDEs with varying degrees of nonlinearity and stiffness to assess its robustness and generalizability. For example, the method could be applied to problems arising in fluid dynamics, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, or in heat transfer, such as the heat equation with variable thermal conductivity. These additional benchmarks would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. Second, a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method is essential. This analysis should include a breakdown of the computational resources required, such as memory usage, training time, and inference time, as a function of the problem size and complexity. A comparison with existing methods, such as standard PINNs and other domain decomposition techniques, should be provided to demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed approach. This comparison should consider both the absolute computational cost and the scaling behavior with respect to the problem dimension and the number of training data points. Furthermore, the analysis should investigate the impact of different hyperparameter choices on the computational cost, such as the number of experts in the MoE architecture and the rank of the dimension decomposition. Third, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This discussion should go beyond simply stating that the method has limitations and should instead provide specific examples of scenarios where the method might fail or perform poorly. For instance, it would be useful to discuss how the method performs when the PDE has discontinuous coefficients or when the solution has sharp gradients. Additionally, the authors should discuss the potential challenges of applying the method to more complex PDEs, such as those arising in fluid dynamics or electromagnetism. This discussion should also include a consideration of the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of experts in the mixture-of-experts model or the rank of the dimension decomposition. Providing guidelines for choosing appropriate hyperparameters would be valuable for practitioners. Fourth, the authors should explore alternative ways to compute the VI metric without relying on analytical solutions. This could involve using numerical approximations or other techniques to estimate the ground-truth components. This would broaden the applicability of the VI metric to purely data-driven settings. Fifth, the paper should provide a more detailed justification for why the proposed method is specifically better at handling sharp features compared to other domain decomposition methods. This could involve a direct comparison against other domain decomposition methods specifically designed for handling sharp features, as well as a more detailed analysis of the MoE approach's ability to capture these features. Sixth, the paper should provide a more detailed comparison with existing methods that use dimension decomposition, highlighting the specific advantages of the proposed approach. For example, the authors could compare their method with separable PINNs, which also use dimension decomposition. The comparison should focus on the differences in the architecture, training procedure, and performance of the methods. Seventh, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the proposed method to the choice of hyperparameters. This analysis should include a systematic study of the impact of different hyperparameter values on the solution accuracy and computational cost. This would provide guidance on how to choose appropriate values for different problems. Finally, a more rigorous analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method is needed. This could involve proving bounds on the error between the numerical solution and the true solution as a function of the number of training iterations or the number of network parameters. Furthermore, it would be helpful to investigate the conditions under which the proposed method is guaranteed to converge to a solution that satisfies the PDE. Such a theoretical analysis would provide a more solid foundation for the proposed method and would increase its credibility. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.
