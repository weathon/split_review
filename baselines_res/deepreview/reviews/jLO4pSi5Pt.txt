## Summary:

This paper introduces Long-tailed Test-Time Adaptation (L-TTA), a novel framework designed to address the challenges of adapting Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to long-tailed distributions in test sets. The core contribution of this work lies in its recognition of the unique difficulties posed by long-tailed data during test-time adaptation, where some classes are significantly underrepresented. To tackle this, the authors propose a three-pronged approach: Synergistic Prototypes (SyPs), Rebalancing Shortcuts (RSs), and Balanced Entropy Minimization (BEM). SyPs aim to enhance the representation of tail classes by accumulating multi-modal knowledge through the creation of Deterministic Prototypes (DPs) and Exclusionary Prototypes (EPs). DPs are updated using embeddings with low prediction entropy, while EPs are updated based on the most improbable features of each class. RSs, implemented via cross-attention with shared hyper-class vectors, dynamically balance the accumulated knowledge from SyPs, ensuring that both head and tail classes are adequately represented. Finally, BEM modifies the standard entropy minimization loss to mitigate biases towards head classes during adaptation. The method's effectiveness is demonstrated through extensive experiments across 15 datasets, showing superior performance in both accuracy and class balancing compared to existing methods. The authors also provide an efficiency study, showing that L-TTA is computationally efficient compared to other methods. The paper's significance lies in its novel approach to long-tailed test-time adaptation for VLMs, addressing a gap in existing research and providing a practical solution for real-world scenarios where data distributions are often imbalanced. The authors' work highlights the importance of considering long-tailed distributions during test-time adaptation and provides a valuable contribution to the field of vision-language understanding.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the introduction of L-TTA as a method specifically designed for long-tailed test-time adaptation in VLMs is a significant contribution. The authors have correctly identified a gap in existing research, where most test-time adaptation methods do not explicitly address the challenges posed by imbalanced data distributions. The proposed framework, consisting of Synergistic Prototypes (SyPs), Rebalancing Shortcuts (RSs), and Balanced Entropy Minimization (BEM), is both novel and well-motivated. The idea of using prototypes to capture class-specific features, and then dynamically rebalancing them using cross-attention, is a clever approach to handling the long-tailed distribution. The use of both Deterministic Prototypes (DPs) and Exclusionary Prototypes (EPs) to capture different aspects of class features is also a notable innovation. The authors' decision to modify the standard entropy minimization loss through BEM to mitigate biases towards head classes is another strong point. The experimental results presented in the paper are convincing, demonstrating the effectiveness of L-TTA across a wide range of datasets and imbalance ratios. The authors have conducted extensive experiments, comparing L-TTA with several existing methods, and have shown that their approach consistently outperforms these baselines in terms of both accuracy and class balancing. Furthermore, the inclusion of an efficiency study, which compares the computational cost of L-TTA with other methods, is a valuable addition. This study demonstrates that L-TTA is not only effective but also computationally efficient, making it a practical solution for real-world applications. The paper is also well-organized and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a wider audience. The authors have clearly explained each component of their method and have provided sufficient details for others to reproduce their results. Overall, the paper presents a novel, effective, and well-validated approach to long-tailed test-time adaptation for VLMs, making a significant contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

While I appreciate the contributions of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. The most significant concern, echoed by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a clear and cohesive explanation of how the three proposed components—Synergistic Prototypes (SyPs), Rebalancing Shortcuts (RSs), and Balanced Entropy Minimization (BEM)—interact within the L-TTA framework. While each component is individually described in Section 3.2, the paper does not provide a sufficiently detailed narrative that clarifies their synergistic effects. Specifically, it remains unclear how the outputs of SyPs, which are designed to capture class-specific features, are directly utilized by RSs to dynamically adjust the model's adaptation process. The paper states that RSs are applied to the prototypes (DPs and EPs), which are the outputs of SyPs, but the exact mechanism of how the *modified* prototypes from SyPs influence the rebalancing performed by RSs is not explicitly stated. For instance, the paper mentions that RSs use cross-attention with shared hyper-class vectors, but it does not provide a concrete example illustrating how the prototypes generated by SyPs influence the attention mechanism and subsequent feature clustering. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately explain how BEM, which aims to balance the entropy of different classes, interacts with the other two components. While the paper states that BEM operates on the final predictions, it does not clarify whether it directly modifies the outputs of SyPs or RSs, or if it operates independently on the final predictions. The paper provides the final objective function in equation (11) as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LTTA}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BEM}}(\mathbb{P}_{\text{LTTA}}) + \eta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CRA}}$, which shows that BEM operates on the predictions from L-TTA, but the exact flow of information and the synergistic effect is not clear. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to fully understand the combined impact of these components on long-tailed test-time adaptation. The paper also lacks a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research. While the conclusion briefly mentions the extension of TTA to long-tailed scenarios, it does not delve into specific limitations or future directions. This omission is a significant weakness, as it prevents a more balanced view of the method's capabilities and hinders further research in this area. For example, the paper does not discuss the sensitivity of L-TTA to the choice of hyperparameters, the potential for overfitting to the long-tailed distribution, or the generalizability of the method to different types of datasets and tasks. Finally, while the paper introduces a novel approach, the method does introduce several hyperparameters that need to be tuned, including $\theta$ (entropy threshold for DP update), $\beta$ (penalty degree in BEM), $\eta$ (balance factor for the loss), $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ (affinity function parameters), and $K$ (number of hyper-class vectors). While the ablation study explores the sensitivity of some of these hyperparameters, the overall complexity could be a practical limitation. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of each component, including SyPs, RSs, and BEM, and how the computational cost scales with the number of classes or the size of the input images. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of L-TTA in real-world scenarios. My confidence in these weaknesses is high, as they are based on direct examination of the paper's content and are supported by the reviewers' comments.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how the Synergistic Prototypes (SyPs), Rebalancing Shortcuts (RSs), and Balanced Entropy Minimization (BEM) components interact within the L-TTA framework. Specifically, they should clarify how the multi-modal knowledge accumulated by SyPs is leveraged by RSs to dynamically balance class knowledge. A concrete example illustrating the flow of information from SyPs to RSs, and how this interaction leads to improved feature clustering, would be beneficial. For instance, the authors could provide a step-by-step breakdown of how the prototypes generated by SyPs influence the attention mechanism in RSs, and how this leads to a more balanced representation of head and tail classes. Furthermore, the authors should elaborate on how BEM influences the adaptation process, detailing how it mitigates biases towards head classes and how this interacts with the other two components. A step-by-step explanation of the adaptation process, highlighting the role of each component at each stage, would greatly improve the reader's understanding of the framework's synergistic effects. This could be achieved by providing a detailed algorithm or a visual representation of the information flow. Second, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed method and potential avenues for future research. This section should address the sensitivity of L-TTA to the choice of hyperparameters, the potential for overfitting to the long-tailed distribution, and the generalizability of the method to different types of datasets and tasks. For example, the authors could discuss how the method performs when the test distribution is significantly different from the training distribution, beyond the long-tailed nature. They could also explore potential extensions of L-TTA, such as incorporating uncertainty estimation or exploring different prototype generation techniques. This discussion would help contextualize the contribution of L-TTA and guide further work in this area. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of each component of L-TTA, including SyPs, RSs, and BEM. This analysis should consider the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the computational cost. Furthermore, the authors should compare the computational overhead of L-TTA with existing methods, using metrics like training time, inference time, and memory usage. This comparison should be performed on a common hardware platform to ensure a fair evaluation. The authors should also discuss the scalability of L-TTA to larger datasets and more complex models. This would help assess the practical applicability of L-TTA in real-world scenarios. Finally, the authors should explore methods to reduce the number of hyperparameters, perhaps through techniques like Bayesian optimization or by identifying the most sensitive parameters and focusing on tuning those. This would make the method more practical and easier to use. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make it more impactful.


## Questions:

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are crucial for a deeper understanding of the proposed method. First, how does the performance of L-TTA vary with different choices of the base VLM architecture? The paper does not explicitly discuss the impact of the base VLM on the performance of L-TTA. Does the method perform consistently across different VLM architectures, or are there specific models where it is more effective? This is important for understanding the generalizability of the proposed method. Second, what is the impact of the number of hyper-class vectors ($K$) on the performance of L-TTA? While the ablation study explores the sensitivity of the model to $K$, it would be beneficial to have a more detailed analysis of how the choice of $K$ affects the feature clustering and the overall adaptation process. How does the performance vary with different values of $K$, and what is the optimal value of $K$ for different datasets and tasks? Third, how does the method perform when the test distribution is significantly different from the training distribution, beyond the long-tailed nature? The paper focuses on long-tailed distributions, but it would be valuable to understand how the method performs under other types of distribution shifts. For example, how does the method perform when the test data contains novel classes that were not seen during training? Fourth, what is the impact of the choice of the affinity function parameters ($\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$) on the performance of L-TTA? While the ablation study explores the sensitivity of the model to these parameters, it would be beneficial to have a more detailed analysis of how these parameters affect the rebalancing process and the overall adaptation performance. Fifth, how does the method handle noisy or adversarial examples? The paper does not discuss the robustness of L-TTA to noisy or adversarial examples. It would be valuable to understand how the method performs under such conditions and what strategies could be used to improve its robustness. These questions target core methodological choices and assumptions, and addressing them would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method.
