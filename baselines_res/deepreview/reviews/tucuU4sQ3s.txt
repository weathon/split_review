## Summary:

This paper introduces Null Space Adaptation for Continual Learning (NuSA-CL), a novel memory-free approach designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in vision-language models. The core idea behind NuSA-CL is to leverage the null space of the model's weight matrices to apply task-specific updates, thereby preserving previously acquired knowledge while adapting to new tasks. The method begins by identifying a low-energy subspace of the model's weight matrix using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Task-specific updates are then constrained to this identified null space, ensuring that new learning does not interfere with existing representations. This approach is particularly significant because it allows for continual learning without the need for external memory or replay buffers, which are common in other continual learning methods. The authors provide a theoretical foundation for their approach, demonstrating that updates within the null space minimize interference in parameter space. Empirically, the method is evaluated on various continual learning benchmarks, including the Multimodal Task Incremental Learning (MTIL) benchmark and the Class-Incremental CIFAR100 dataset. The results show that NuSA-CL achieves competitive performance compared to other storage-free methods and even rivals some storage-based approaches in certain metrics. Specifically, the method demonstrates strong performance in preserving zero-shot capabilities while learning new tasks, which is a critical challenge in continual learning. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different design choices, such as the subspace selection strategy and the training of specific parameters. Overall, NuSA-CL presents a promising direction for memory-free continual learning, offering a computationally efficient and effective way to adapt vision-language models to evolving tasks without sacrificing previously learned knowledge. The paper's contributions are significant in that it introduces a novel approach to continual learning that is both theoretically grounded and empirically validated, and it addresses the critical challenge of catastrophic forgetting in a practical and efficient manner.

## Strengths:

One of the primary strengths of this paper is the introduction of a novel approach to continual learning that leverages the null space of the model's parameters. This is a significant departure from traditional methods that rely on external memory or replay buffers, and it offers a more efficient and practical solution for real-world applications. The method's ability to adapt to new tasks while preserving previously learned knowledge, without requiring additional storage, is a notable achievement. The theoretical analysis provided in the paper further strengthens the proposed approach. The authors derive bounds on parameter interference, which provides a solid foundation for the method and demonstrates that updates within the null space minimize interference in parameter space. This theoretical grounding adds robustness to the proposed approach and provides a deeper understanding of its inner workings. Furthermore, the empirical results presented in the paper are convincing. The method demonstrates competitive performance on various continual learning benchmarks, outperforming other storage-free methods and even rivaling some storage-based approaches in certain metrics. The experiments on the MTIL benchmark and the Class-Incremental CIFAR100 dataset provide strong evidence of the method's effectiveness in preserving zero-shot capabilities while learning new tasks. The ablation studies also provide valuable insights into the impact of different design choices, such as the subspace selection strategy and the training of specific parameters. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a wider audience. The clear explanation of the methodology, theoretical foundations, and experimental results contributes to the overall quality of the paper. The authors also provide practical guidance for larger backbones, suggesting that the method can be scaled to larger models by applying SVD to attention projection matrices, which have manageable sizes even for larger CLIP variants. This addresses a potential concern about the computational cost of SVD for large models. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach, theoretical grounding, empirical validation, and clear presentation, making it a significant contribution to the field of continual learning.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to continual learning, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the persistent null-space constraint, while effective in preventing forgetting, may limit the model's ability to fully adapt to tasks that require significant deviations from previously learned knowledge. As the method strictly confines updates to the identified null space, it may hinder the model's performance in scenarios where new tasks demand substantial changes to the model's parameter space. This is a valid concern, as the paper itself acknowledges that the method is designed to minimize interference with existing knowledge, which could come at the cost of reduced plasticity. The paper does not include specific experiments to test this limitation, which is a significant oversight. Second, the effectiveness of NuSA-CL depends on the accurate identification of the null space, which might be challenging in highly dynamic or complex task sequences. The method relies on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to approximate the null space, and the accuracy of this approximation can be affected by the condition number of the weight matrix. In scenarios with rapidly changing task distributions, the identified null space might not accurately represent the true subspace where updates can be made without interfering with prior knowledge. This could potentially lead to performance degradation. The paper does not provide a theoretical or empirical analysis of this potential issue, which is a notable limitation. Third, while the paper provides a theoretical bound on interference, it remains unclear how well these bounds translate to practical scenarios, especially with highly correlated task sequences. The theoretical bounds are derived under certain assumptions, and it is not clear how these assumptions hold in real-world scenarios, particularly when tasks are highly correlated. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the correlation between tasks and its impact on the performance of NuSA-CL, making it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the theoretical bounds. The paper evaluates on Class-Incremental CIFAR100, which has highly correlated tasks, but does not explicitly analyze how this correlation affects the tightness of the bounds. Fourth, the ablation study in Table 4a reveals that the performance of the proposed method decreases when $U_n$ and $V_n$ are trained. This raises concerns about the robustness of the method and its sensitivity to the training of these parameters. The paper does not provide a detailed explanation of why training $U_n$ and $V_n$ leads to a decrease in performance, which is a significant oversight. The results clearly show that training only $M$ yields the best performance, and any training of $U_n$ and $V_n$ leads to a decrease in performance. Fifth, while the paper claims that the SVD operation is efficient, the analysis of its computational cost is not sufficiently detailed. While the paper mentions that SVD is performed on smaller matrices within the model, a more rigorous analysis of the time and memory complexity is needed. The paper does not provide a detailed breakdown of the computational cost for different layers or how this cost scales with the dimensions of the weight matrices. Sixth, the paper primarily evaluates NuSA-CL on a specific sequence of 11 tasks in the MTIL benchmark. The sensitivity of the method to the order of tasks is not thoroughly investigated. The paper does not include experiments with different random orderings of the MTIL benchmark, which is a significant limitation. Finally, while the paper focuses on vision-language models, the generalizability of the approach to other types of models or tasks beyond image classification is not adequately addressed. The paper lacks empirical evidence or a detailed discussion of applying the method to other types of models, such as language models or multimodal models, and other tasks, such as object detection or semantic segmentation. These limitations, which have been independently validated, raise concerns about the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method and warrant further investigation.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, to mitigate the limitations of the persistent null-space constraint, future work could explore adaptive constraint mechanisms that allow for controlled deviations from the null space when necessary. This could involve dynamically adjusting the constraint based on the characteristics of the new task or the degree of similarity to previously learned tasks. For example, a metric could be introduced to quantify the required deviation from the null space, and the constraint could be relaxed accordingly. This would allow the model to adapt more effectively to tasks that require significant changes while still maintaining stability for tasks that are closely related to previous ones. Second, to improve the accuracy of null space identification, especially in highly dynamic or complex task sequences, the paper could explore alternative methods for approximating the null space. This could involve investigating the use of more robust SVD techniques or alternative matrix decomposition methods that are less sensitive to the condition number of the weight matrix. For example, randomized SVD or other low-rank approximation techniques could be explored. Additionally, a more detailed analysis of the impact of task correlation on the performance of NuSA-CL is needed. This could involve conducting experiments with different levels of task correlation and analyzing how the performance of the method varies. This analysis could help to identify the limitations of the method and guide the development of more robust approaches. Third, to further investigate the practical implications of the theoretical bounds, it would be beneficial to conduct experiments that specifically target scenarios where the bounds are expected to be tight or loose. This could involve designing task sequences with varying degrees of correlation and analyzing the performance of NuSA-CL in these scenarios. Additionally, it would be useful to compare the performance of NuSA-CL with other continual learning methods that do not rely on null space constraints, such as methods based on regularization or replay buffers. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. Fourth, to address the performance decrease when training $U_n$ and $V_n$, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of why this occurs. A deeper analysis of the impact of training these parameters on the null space and the model's ability to adapt to new tasks is needed. It would be beneficial to explore different strategies for updating these parameters or to provide a theoretical justification for why they should not be trained. Furthermore, it would be helpful to investigate the sensitivity of the method to the initialization of these parameters and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate initial values. Fifth, to provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of the SVD operation, the paper should include a breakdown of the computational cost for different layers and how this cost scales with the dimensions of the weight matrices. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore alternative methods for approximating the null space, such as randomized SVD or other low-rank approximation techniques, and compare their performance and computational cost with the current approach. Sixth, to address the sensitivity to task order, the paper should include a more thorough evaluation using different task sequences. The authors should consider using a wider range of task sequences, including those with varying degrees of semantic relatedness, to assess the robustness of the method. Additionally, the paper should analyze the performance of the method under different task orderings and provide insights into how the task sequence affects the final performance. Finally, to address the limited generalizability, the paper should provide a more detailed discussion of the generalizability of the proposed method to other types of models and tasks. The authors should explore the potential of applying the method to other types of models, such as language models or multimodal models, and other tasks, such as object detection or semantic segmentation. This discussion should include an analysis of the challenges and limitations of applying the method to different types of models and tasks, as well as potential modifications that may be necessary. Furthermore, the paper should provide some empirical evidence of the method's performance on other types of models and tasks, even if these results are preliminary.
