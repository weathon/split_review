## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to lifelong learning in vision-and-language navigation (VLN), addressing the challenge of catastrophic forgetting when deploying agents across diverse scenes and environments. The authors formalize this challenge as the all-day multi-scenes lifelong VLN (AML-VLN) problem, emphasizing the need for agents that can adapt to new scenarios without losing previously acquired knowledge. To tackle this, they propose Tucker Adaptation (TuKA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that leverages Tucker decomposition to represent hierarchical navigation knowledge within a high-order tensor. This allows for the decoupling of shared subspaces and scenario-specific experts, enabling the agent to learn new tasks while preserving existing knowledge. The core idea is to represent the model's weights as a tensor, then decompose this tensor into a core tensor and several factor matrices using Tucker decomposition. This decomposition allows the model to learn shared knowledge in the core tensor and specific knowledge in the factor matrices. The authors also introduce a decoupled knowledge incremental learning strategy (DKIL) to consolidate shared subspaces while constraining specific experts, further mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The paper presents AllDayWalker, a VLN agent that continually learns across multiple navigation scenarios, achieving all-day multi-scenes navigation. The authors extend an existing embodied navigation simulator with diverse imaging models to create a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating VLN agents in various environmental conditions, including low-light, scattering, and overexposure. The empirical results demonstrate that AllDayWalker outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in lifelong VLN tasks, showcasing the effectiveness of the proposed TuKA method. The paper's main contributions include the novel AML-VLN problem formulation, the TuKA method for hierarchical knowledge representation, the AllDayWalker agent, and the extended benchmark for evaluation. The authors conduct extensive experiments to validate the effectiveness of their approach, comparing it with several baselines and demonstrating its superior performance in terms of success rate and resistance to forgetting. Overall, this paper presents a significant contribution to the field of lifelong learning in VLN, offering a promising approach for deploying agents in diverse and dynamic environments.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First, the introduction of the all-day multi-scenes lifelong VLN (AML-VLN) problem is a well-motivated and timely contribution. By highlighting the challenges of deploying VLN agents in diverse scenes and environments, the authors address a critical issue in the field. The formalization of this problem as a lifelong learning challenge, where agents must adapt to new scenarios without forgetting previous knowledge, is a significant step forward. Second, the proposed Tucker Adaptation (TuKA) method is a significant technical innovation. The use of Tucker decomposition to represent multi-hierarchical navigation knowledge in a high-order tensor is a creative and effective solution. This approach allows for the decoupling of shared and specific knowledge, which is a significant advancement over existing methods that often struggle with catastrophic forgetting. The mathematical formulation of TuKA, while not overly complex, is well-explained and provides a solid foundation for the method. The decoupled knowledge incremental learning strategy (DKIL) further enhances the method's ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by consolidating shared subspaces while constraining specific experts. Third, the development of the AllDayWalker agent and its demonstrated performance is a strong point. The agent's ability to continually learn across multiple navigation scenarios and achieve superior performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines is a testament to the effectiveness of the proposed TuKA method. The extensive experiments conducted by the authors provide strong empirical evidence for the method's capabilities. Finally, the creation of a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating VLN agents across diverse environments is a valuable contribution to the research community. The inclusion of low-light, scattering, and overexposure conditions makes the benchmark more realistic and challenging, pushing the boundaries of current VLN methods. The benchmark will undoubtedly serve as a valuable resource for future research in this area. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its novel problem formulation, technical innovation, strong empirical results, and the creation of a valuable benchmark.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and memory requirements of the proposed TuKA method compared to existing approaches. While the authors mention parameter efficiency, they do not provide a rigorous analysis of the computational overhead introduced by TuKA. Specifically, the paper does not include a detailed breakdown of the FLOPs required for both training and inference, making it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method, especially in resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, a comparison of training time per epoch and inference latency against baseline methods is absent, which would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the computational trade-offs. This lack of computational analysis is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to evaluate the practical feasibility of the method in real-world scenarios. The absence of this analysis is evident throughout the method and experiments sections, where no such metrics are reported. Second, the generalization experiments are limited to only six unseen tasks. While the authors do evaluate the method's ability to generalize to new scenarios, the evaluation set is small and lacks diversity. Expanding the evaluation to a larger and more diverse set of unseen tasks would provide a more robust assessment of the method's generalization capabilities. The current evaluation does not fully explore the boundaries of the method's applicability, and it is unclear how performance would degrade under more challenging or novel scenarios. The selection criteria for these six tasks are also not clearly defined, making it difficult to assess the representativeness of the evaluation set. This limitation is explicitly stated in the paper, where the authors mention testing on six unseen tasks, and the results are presented in Table 5. The lack of a more extensive generalization evaluation weakens the claims of the method's broad applicability. Third, the paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art continual learning methods beyond those specifically designed for VLN. While the authors compare their method with several LoRA-based methods and some continual learning techniques, they do not include comparisons with a broader range of continual learning methods that utilize different knowledge retention strategies, such as replay-based approaches or regularization techniques. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the method's performance relative to the broader field of continual learning. Specifically, comparisons with methods that utilize different knowledge retention strategies, such as replay-based approaches or regularization techniques, would be beneficial to understand the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach in a broader context. This limitation is evident in the experiments section, where the baselines are primarily focused on VLN and parameter-efficient adaptation. Finally, the paper does not discuss the potential limitations of the proposed method in scenarios with a large number of scenes and environments. It would be valuable to explore how the performance of TuKA scales with an increasing number of tasks and whether there are any bottlenecks in terms of computational resources or memory requirements. The analysis should also consider the impact of task similarity on the performance of the method, as highly similar tasks might lead to interference and reduced performance. The paper introduces a benchmark with 24 tasks, but there is no explicit discussion or experiment analyzing the performance of TuKA with a significantly larger number of scenes and environments. This lack of analysis leaves open questions about the scalability and robustness of TuKA in more complex and diverse lifelong learning scenarios. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be improved to provide a more complete and robust evaluation of the proposed method.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to address the lack of detailed computational analysis, the authors should include a comprehensive breakdown of the computational costs associated with the TuKA method. This should include a comparison of FLOPs for both training and inference, as well as a detailed analysis of memory usage. Furthermore, the authors should provide a comparison of training time per epoch and inference latency against baseline methods, such as standard fine-tuning and other parameter-efficient adaptation techniques. This analysis should be conducted on a consistent hardware setup to ensure a fair comparison. It would also be beneficial to explore the scalability of the method by evaluating its performance with varying numbers of navigation scenarios and environments. This would provide a better understanding of how the computational cost scales with the complexity of the problem. The authors should also investigate the impact of the Tucker decomposition rank on the computational cost and performance, providing a trade-off analysis between model size, computational efficiency, and navigation performance. This would provide a more complete picture of the method's practical applicability. Second, to strengthen the generalization analysis, the authors should significantly expand the evaluation to include a larger and more diverse set of unseen tasks. This could involve incorporating tasks from different datasets or creating novel scenarios that are significantly different from the training environments. The selection criteria for these tasks should be clearly defined and justified. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the performance of the method across different types of scenarios to identify any potential biases or limitations. It would also be valuable to investigate the sensitivity of the method to variations in the environment, such as changes in lighting conditions, object arrangements, or the presence of obstacles. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's robustness and generalization capabilities. Third, to provide a more comprehensive comparison with other continual learning methods, the authors should include experiments with a broader range of baselines. This should include methods that utilize different knowledge retention strategies, such as replay-based approaches, regularization techniques, and methods that dynamically expand the network architecture. Specifically, comparisons with methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), Learning without Forgetting (LwF), and other parameter-efficient adaptation techniques would be beneficial. The comparison should not only focus on the final performance but also on the learning dynamics, such as the convergence speed and the stability of the learning process. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the sensitivity of the proposed method to different hyperparameter settings, such as the learning rate, batch size, and the rank of the Tucker decomposition, and compare it with the sensitivity of other continual learning methods. Finally, to address the potential limitations of the proposed method in scenarios with a large number of scenes and environments, the authors should conduct experiments with a significantly larger number of tasks. This should include an analysis of how the performance of TuKA scales with an increasing number of tasks and whether there are any bottlenecks in terms of computational resources or memory requirements. The analysis should also consider the impact of task similarity on the performance of the method, as highly similar tasks might lead to interference and reduced performance. The authors should also explore potential strategies to mitigate these limitations, such as using more efficient data structures or optimizing the Tucker decomposition process. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different task ordering on the performance of the method, as some task orderings might lead to better performance than others. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and provide a more complete and robust evaluation of the proposed method.
