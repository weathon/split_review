## Summary:

This paper introduces the Autoformalizer with Tool Feedback (ATF), a novel framework designed to enhance the autoformalization of mathematical statements. Autoformalization, the process of translating natural language mathematical problems into formal language statements, is a crucial step in automated theorem proving. The core contribution of this work lies in the integration of tool feedback into the formalization process, specifically leveraging syntactic and semantic validation tools. The authors develop two key tools: a syntax check tool that utilizes the Lean 4 compiler to provide compilation feedback, and a consistency check tool that employs a multi-LLMs-as-judge approach to validate the semantic equivalence between informal and formal expressions. The ATF framework is trained through a three-phase process: a cold-start phase using synthetic tool-calling data, an expert iteration phase to refine formalization capabilities, and a Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) phase to reduce ineffective revisions. The experimental results demonstrate that ATF significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art formalizers across various benchmarks, achieving superior performance in both syntactic validity and semantic consistency. Furthermore, the authors contribute a dataset, Numina-ATF, containing 750,000 formal statements, which serves as a valuable resource for future research in this area. The paper's approach of iteratively refining formalizations based on tool feedback represents a significant advancement in the field, addressing the limitations of previous methods that relied solely on language models for direct formalization. The use of a multi-LLM judge for consistency, while not without its limitations, offers a practical approach to a challenging problem. The authors' comprehensive evaluation, including human assessment, further strengthens the validity of their findings. Overall, this paper presents a well-structured and impactful contribution to the field of automated theorem proving, offering a robust and effective method for autoformalization.

## Strengths:

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to autoformalization through the integration of tool feedback. The use of Lean 4 compilers for syntax checking and a multi-LLM judge for consistency validation represents a significant departure from previous methods that relied solely on language models to generate formal statements without explicit feedback mechanisms. This approach directly addresses the key challenges of autoformalization, namely the need for syntactic correctness and semantic consistency. The development of two evaluation tools, one for syntactic validity and another for semantic consistency, is a valuable contribution in itself. These tools provide a means for accurately measuring the quality of formalized statements, which is crucial for both training and evaluation. The experimental results convincingly demonstrate the superiority of ATF over existing baseline formalizers across various benchmarks. The improvements in both syntactic validity and semantic consistency are substantial, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The authors' decision to open-source the Numina-ATF dataset, containing 750,000 formal statements, is a significant contribution to the research community. This dataset provides a valuable resource for further research in autoformalization and automated theorem proving. The paper is also well-written and clearly explains the methodology, experimental setup, and results. The figures and tables are informative and effectively support the claims made in the paper. The use of a multi-LLM judge for consistency, while not without its limitations, offers a practical approach to a challenging problem. The authors' comprehensive evaluation, including human assessment, further strengthens the validity of their findings. The iterative refinement process, guided by tool feedback, is a key innovation that allows the model to learn from its mistakes and improve its formalization capabilities. This approach is particularly effective in addressing the complexities of formal language and the nuances of mathematical reasoning.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to autoformalization, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's reliance on specific tools and models for syntax and consistency checks raises concerns about the generalizability and reproducibility of the approach. The syntax check tool is explicitly based on Lean 4 compilers, as stated in Section 3.1: "For syntactic validity, the tool processes formal statements and returns comprehensive compilation feedback from the Lean 4 compilers..." This dependence on a specific formal language makes it difficult to apply ATF to other formal languages that use different compilers or have different syntax rules. Similarly, the consistency check tool uses a multi-LLMs-as-judge approach, which depends on the availability and performance of specific LLMs. As detailed in Section 3.1.2, the authors use Gemini-2.5-Pro for generating perturbations and QWQ-32B and Qwen3-32B for the multi-LLMs-as-judge approach. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the approach to different LLM choices or the potential impact of LLM biases on the consistency checks. While the authors use an ensemble vote method to mitigate bias, they do not investigate the performance of ATF with different LLMs or analyze the robustness of the approach to variations in LLM quality. This lack of analysis makes it unclear how the performance of ATF would vary with different LLMs or if the approach is robust to variations in LLM quality. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states its reliance on specific tools and models, and does not provide any evidence to the contrary. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost and efficiency of the tool feedback mechanism. The integration of external tools into the formalization process may introduce additional overhead and latency, especially for large-scale datasets or complex formal statements. The paper does not provide a quantitative evaluation of the time and resources required for each tool call, as well as the overall impact on the inference time of ATF. While the paper mentions efforts to improve efficiency, such as the pre-check stage and grouped execution for syntax checks (Section 3.1.1), it does not provide specific timings for tool calls or the overall inference process. The paper reports Pass@k metrics, which implicitly involve multiple inference attempts, but does not break down the time spent on tool calls versus model inference. The hardware specification (128 NPUs) is provided, but detailed timing information is missing. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the approach, especially for researchers with limited computational resources. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide any quantitative data on the computational cost of the tool feedback mechanism. Finally, the paper does not fully explore the limitations and potential failure modes of ATF. While the paper acknowledges that ATF may exhibit consecutive identical errors or struggle with increasingly challenging revisions, as stated in Section 3.2: "However, we observe that the model sometimes exhibits consecutive identical errors (e.g., the same syntax error appearing multiple times without being resolved)," it does not provide a systematic analysis of the types of errors that ATF is prone to make. The paper focuses on overall performance improvements but does not delve into the specific weaknesses of ATF in terms of the types of formal statements or mathematical concepts it struggles with. The paper does not provide a detailed categorization of errors or specific examples of failure cases. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the limitations of ATF and to guide future research directions. This interface could define a set of common operations for syntax validation, allowing researchers to plug in different compilers or syntax checkers as needed. Similarly, the multi-LLMs-as-judge approach for consistency checking could be made more robust by exploring techniques for ensembling different LLMs or by incorporating methods for uncertainty estimation. This would help to mitigate the impact of individual LLM biases and improve the reliability of the consistency checks. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the sensitivity of ATF to different LLM choices and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate LLMs for different tasks. A thorough analysis of the performance of ATF with different LLMs would be valuable to understand the robustness of the approach and to identify potential limitations. This should include a breakdown of the time spent on each tool call, as well as the time spent on other parts of the formalization process. The authors should also investigate the scalability of the approach by evaluating its performance on larger datasets and more complex formal statements. This analysis should consider the impact of different hardware configurations and optimization techniques on the inference time. Furthermore, the authors should explore methods for reducing the computational overhead of the tool feedback mechanism, such as caching tool results or using more efficient tool implementations. A detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the tool feedback mechanism is needed to assess its scalability and practicality. Finally, to better understand the limitations and potential failure modes of ATF, the authors should conduct a more in-depth error analysis. This should include a categorization of the types of errors that ATF is prone to make, as well as specific examples of failure cases. The authors should investigate whether ATF struggles more with certain types of mathematical concepts, logical structures, or formal language features. This analysis should also consider the impact of the iterative revision process on error propagation. The authors should also explore techniques for mitigating the identified failure modes, such as incorporating additional training data or refining the tool feedback mechanism. A detailed analysis of the limitations of ATF would be valuable to guide future research directions and to improve the overall robustness of the approach. In addition to these specific suggestions, the authors should consider exploring the potential for incorporating human feedback into the training process. While the current approach relies on automated validation tools, human experts could provide valuable insights into the nuances of formalization that are difficult to capture with automated tools. This could involve a human-in-the-loop approach, where human experts review the formalizations generated by ATF and provide feedback that is used to further refine the model.
