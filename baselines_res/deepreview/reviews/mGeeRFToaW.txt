## Summary:

This paper introduces Quantized Zeroth-Order Optimization (QZO), a novel method designed to fine-tune quantized neural networks with minimal memory usage. The core idea behind QZO is to combine zeroth-order optimization, which approximates gradients without backpropagation, with model quantization, which reduces the memory footprint of network weights. Instead of perturbing the discrete quantized weights directly, QZO perturbs the continuous quantization scale to estimate gradients. This approach circumvents the challenges associated with applying zeroth-order optimization to discrete weights. To stabilize the training process, which can be unstable due to the high variance of zeroth-order gradient estimates, QZO employs a directional derivative clipping technique. This clipping method helps to reduce the variance of the gradient estimates, leading to more stable convergence. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of QZO through extensive experiments on large language models (LLMs), showing that it achieves significant memory savings compared to traditional fine-tuning methods while maintaining competitive performance. Specifically, QZO achieves an 18x reduction in memory usage compared to 16-bit fine-tuning, enabling the fine-tuning of large models like Llama-2-13B on a single 24GB GPU. This work is significant because it addresses the growing challenge of fine-tuning large models on resource-constrained hardware, making it more accessible to a wider range of researchers and practitioners. The method is compatible with both scalar-based and codebook-based quantization methods, further enhancing its versatility. The paper also provides a theoretical justification for the directional derivative clipping method, showing that it reduces the variance of gradient estimates. Overall, QZO presents a promising approach for efficient fine-tuning of large models, combining the benefits of zeroth-order optimization and quantization to achieve substantial memory savings without sacrificing performance.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the core idea of combining zeroth-order optimization with quantization is both novel and well-motivated. The authors have identified a critical challenge in the field of efficient deep learning—the memory bottleneck in fine-tuning large models—and have proposed a creative solution. The approach of perturbing the continuous quantization scale, rather than the discrete weights, to estimate gradients is an elegant way to overcome the limitations of applying zeroth-order methods to quantized networks. This is a significant technical innovation that directly addresses the core problem. Furthermore, the introduction of directional derivative clipping is a valuable contribution. The authors not only propose this technique but also provide a theoretical justification for its effectiveness in reducing the variance of gradient estimates. This adds a layer of rigor to the method and demonstrates a deep understanding of the underlying optimization challenges. The experimental results are also compelling. The authors have conducted comprehensive evaluations on various LLMs and NLP tasks, demonstrating that QZO achieves significant memory savings while maintaining competitive performance. The ability to fine-tune Llama-2-13B on a single 24GB GPU is a remarkable achievement that highlights the practical impact of this work. The compatibility of QZO with both scalar-based and codebook-based quantization methods further enhances its versatility and applicability. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide clear explanations of the proposed method and its implementation details, making it accessible to a broad audience. The logical flow of the paper, from the introduction of the problem to the presentation of the solution and its experimental validation, is well-structured and contributes to the overall clarity of the work.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of a detailed convergence analysis for the proposed Q-SPSA method, particularly in the context of quantized neural networks. While the paper builds upon existing zeroth-order optimization techniques, the modification of perturbing the quantization scale and the introduction of directional derivative clipping necessitate a specific convergence analysis. The paper does not address how the perturbation of the quantization scale interacts with the non-convex loss landscape of the quantized network, nor does it analyze whether the directional derivative clipping introduces any bias in the gradient estimation that could affect convergence. Furthermore, the analysis should consider the impact of quantization error on the stability of the optimization process. The absence of such an analysis leaves a gap in the theoretical understanding of QZO's behavior. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper itself acknowledges the dependence of QZO on the quality of the initial quantization, stating, 'First, QZO’s performance depends on how good the quantization method is. Specifically, if the quantization method has a large quantization error, this makes the forward passes in ZO noisy and therefore could make the gradient estimation less accurate.' (Section 5). This dependence on quantization quality, coupled with the lack of a specific convergence analysis, raises questions about the robustness of QZO under different conditions. Another significant weakness is the absence of a direct comparison with other zeroth-order optimization methods, such as ZO-JADE and ZO-SGD. While the paper compares QZO against MeZO, which is a memory-efficient zeroth-order method, a comparison with other methods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of QZO's advantages and disadvantages. The paper does not analyze how the specific characteristics of each zeroth-order method interact with the quantization process, nor does it demonstrate whether perturbing the quantization scale is more suitable for quantized networks compared to existing methods. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative merits of QZO compared to other zeroth-order approaches. This is a high-confidence concern, as the experimental section (Section 4.1) lists the baselines used for comparison, which do not include ZO-JADE or a standalone implementation of ZO-SGD. This makes it difficult to fully assess the computational efficiency of the method, especially when considering the potential for increased training time due to the need for multiple perturbation steps. The analysis should also consider the impact of the quantization error on the stability of the optimization process. The authors should analyze how the specific characteristics of each zeroth-order method interact with the quantization process, and whether the proposed method is more suitable for quantized networks compared to existing methods.
