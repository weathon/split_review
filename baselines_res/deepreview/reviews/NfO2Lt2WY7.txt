## Summary:

This paper delves into the intricacies of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a reinforcement learning technique designed to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). The authors embark on a systematic investigation of GRPO's loss function, aiming to discern the necessity of its various components. Through a series of ablation experiments, they challenge the conventional wisdom surrounding the inclusion of PPO-style constraints, such as policy ratio clipping, in the optimization process. A key finding of their study is that negative feedback, or the incorporation of actions that do not improve performance, is crucial for effective learning, while PPO-style constraints may not be essential for improving mathematical reasoning. Building upon these insights, the authors propose a simplified variant of GRPO, termed REINFORCE with Group Relative Advantage (RGR). This streamlined approach retains the core advantage estimation of GRPO but discards the PPO-style clipping and policy ratio terms. The empirical evaluation of RGR is conducted across a range of mathematical benchmarks, including multilingual datasets, demonstrating that it achieves comparable or even superior performance to the original GRPO. The authors' work suggests that simpler reinforcement learning methods, rooted in the REINFORCE algorithm, can be effective for enhancing reasoning in LLMs, offering a more transparent and computationally efficient alternative to the more complex GRPO. The paper's core contribution lies in its systematic deconstruction of GRPO, its identification of key components, and the development of a simplified yet effective alternative. This challenges the necessity of complex loss functions in this domain and opens up avenues for more efficient and interpretable reinforcement learning techniques for LLMs. The authors' findings have implications for the design of future reinforcement learning algorithms for reasoning tasks, suggesting that a more streamlined approach may be sufficient to achieve strong performance. The paper's focus on mathematical reasoning, while providing a solid foundation, also highlights the need for further investigation into the generalizability of these findings to other reasoning domains. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the simplification of reinforcement learning algorithms for LLMs, backed by empirical evidence and a clear articulation of the underlying principles.
