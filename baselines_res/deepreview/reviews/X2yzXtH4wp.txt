## Summary:

This paper introduces AMBIG-SWE, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the ability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to handle underspecified instructions in software engineering tasks. The authors' core contribution lies in their detailed framework for assessing LLMs across three critical stages: detecting when instructions are underspecified, asking targeted clarifying questions, and leveraging interactions to improve task completion. The methodology involves a simulated user environment, where LLMs are presented with ambiguous software engineering issues and tasked with resolving them through interactive clarification. The authors employ both proprietary and open-weight models, providing a comprehensive view of current LLM capabilities and limitations in this context. The empirical findings highlight that while LLMs often struggle with underspecified inputs, their performance significantly improves when they are allowed to engage in interactive clarification. This underscores the importance of interactive capabilities in real-world software development scenarios, where ambiguity is common. The authors also demonstrate that even when models fail to detect ambiguity, their performance improves significantly when they are allowed to ask clarifying questions. This finding underscores the value of interactive capabilities in real-world software development scenarios. The paper's significance lies in its systematic approach to evaluating LLMs in a realistic software engineering setting, revealing both the potential and the limitations of current models in handling ambiguous instructions. The study's findings suggest that interactive clarification is a crucial component for the effective use of LLMs in software development, and that future research should focus on improving models' ability to detect underspecification and ask effective clarifying questions. The paper also highlights the need for more sophisticated simulation techniques to better capture the complexity of real-world user interactions, which is a critical area for future work. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by offering a structured framework and a benchmark for evaluating LLMs in a challenging, real-world scenario, paving the way for more robust and reliable AI-assisted software development tools.

## Strengths:

The paper's primary strength lies in its introduction of AMBIG-SWE, a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate LLMs' ability to handle underspecified instructions in software engineering tasks. This is a significant contribution because it addresses a critical gap in how AI agents are currently used in software development. The authors' decision to break down the problem into three distinct steps—detection, clarification, and resolution—provides a clear and structured framework for evaluation. This approach allows for a detailed assessment of LLM capabilities and offers a clear pathway for future improvements in each area. The use of both proprietary and open-weight models is another notable strength, as it provides a comprehensive view of current model capabilities and limitations. This diverse set of models allows for a more robust analysis and ensures that the findings are not specific to a particular model architecture. Furthermore, the paper effectively highlights the importance of interaction in handling underspecified issues. The empirical results demonstrate that even when models fail to detect ambiguity, their performance improves significantly when they are allowed to ask clarifying questions. This finding underscores the value of interactive capabilities in real-world software development scenarios, where ambiguity is common. The authors' methodology, which involves a simulated user environment, is a practical approach to evaluating LLMs in a controlled setting. While the simulated user has limitations, it provides a valuable starting point for assessing the models' ability to handle ambiguous instructions. The paper's focus on practical software engineering tasks, using real-world GitHub issues, adds to its relevance and impact. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by offering a structured framework and a benchmark for evaluating LLMs in a challenging, real-world scenario, paving the way for more robust and reliable AI-assisted software development tools. The paper's clear and concise writing style also contributes to its overall strength, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors effectively communicate their methodology, findings, and implications, ensuring that the paper is both informative and impactful.


## Weaknesses:

One significant weakness of this paper is its reliance on a simulated user for clarification. While the authors acknowledge that the simulated user is designed to avoid hallucinations and provide information injection, it does not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world human interactions. As the paper states, "The goal is not to simulate real users but provide the information injection to the trajectory and analyze model behaviors." This simplification is a limitation because real users may provide ambiguous, contradictory, or incomplete information, and their communication styles and levels of expertise can vary significantly. The simulated user, as described in the paper, responds only with information explicitly present in the full issue or with the phrase "I don't have that information." This limited response set does not reflect the nuances of human communication, potentially leading to an overestimation of the models' performance in practical scenarios. This limitation is further supported by the fact that the simulated user is based on prior work that uses LLMs to simulate users, which, while useful, is not a perfect substitute for real human interaction. The confidence level in this weakness is high, as it is explicitly acknowledged by the authors and is a well-known limitation of simulation-based evaluations. The implication of this weakness is that the generalizability of the findings to real-world software development scenarios is limited. Another critical weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the types of clarifying questions that are most effective in resolving underspecified instructions. While the paper evaluates models based on their ability to ask clarifying questions and the overall information gain, it does not provide a systematic analysis of the *types* of questions asked and their impact on task resolution. The paper does include a qualitative analysis of question-answer pairs, revealing three distinct approaches with different tradeoffs, but this is not a comprehensive categorization and analysis of question effectiveness. The paper also identifies "informational" and "navigational" information, but this is about the content of the information requested, not the strategy of the question itself. The absence of a detailed analysis of question types is a significant limitation because it hinders the development of actionable insights for improving model training and interaction protocols. The paper's focus on overall information gain, rather than a granular analysis of question effectiveness, means that we do not fully understand which question strategies lead to better outcomes. For example, the paper does not analyze whether open-ended questions are more effective than closed-ended questions, or whether questions that target specific aspects of the task are more effective than general questions. The confidence level in this weakness is also high, as it is evident from the paper's methodology and experimental design. The implication of this weakness is that the paper does not provide a complete understanding of how LLMs can effectively leverage interactive clarification, and it limits the practical applicability of the findings for improving model training and interaction protocols.


## Suggestions:

To address the limitations of the simulated user, I recommend that future work explores more sophisticated simulation techniques that better capture the complexity of real-world user interactions. This could involve incorporating a diverse set of simulated user behaviors, including varying levels of technical expertise, different communication styles, and the ability to provide ambiguous or contradictory information. The simulation should also account for the iterative nature of real-world interactions, where users might refine their requests based on the agent's responses. This could involve incorporating a more dynamic model of user behavior that adapts to the agent's actions, rather than a static set of pre-defined responses. Furthermore, future studies should investigate the impact of different user personas (e.g., novice vs. expert developers) on the agent's performance and the types of clarifying questions that are most effective. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of how LLMs can be used in different software development contexts. To enhance the analysis of clarifying questions, I suggest that the authors categorize the types of questions asked by the models and evaluate their effectiveness in resolving specific types of underspecification. This could involve a qualitative analysis of the questions, identifying patterns and strategies that lead to successful task completion. For instance, some questions might focus on clarifying the functional requirements of the task, while others might target implementation details or constraints. The study should also investigate the impact of question ordering and the level of detail requested. A more granular analysis of the question-answer pairs would provide valuable insights into the reasoning process of the models and how they leverage the information provided by the user. This could involve techniques such as ablation studies, where specific types of questions are removed to assess their impact on performance. Additionally, the study should explore the use of different question-asking strategies, such as open-ended questions versus closed-ended questions, and analyze their effectiveness in different scenarios. The authors should also consider the computational cost of different question-asking strategies, as some strategies might be more effective but also more computationally expensive. Finally, the paper should delve deeper into the relationship between the quality of the clarifying questions and the final task performance. It is not sufficient to simply measure the number of questions asked or the amount of information gained; the study should also assess how well the models utilize the acquired information to complete the task. This could involve analyzing the agent's reasoning process after receiving clarification, identifying any bottlenecks or limitations in its ability to integrate new information. The authors should also investigate whether the models are able to identify and correct errors based on the user's feedback. This could involve analyzing the agent's ability to revise its plan and adapt to new information. Furthermore, the study should explore the use of different evaluation metrics that capture the quality of the agent's reasoning process, rather than just the final outcome. This could involve techniques such as human evaluation of the agent's reasoning traces or the use of automated metrics that measure the coherence and consistency of the agent's plan. By addressing these limitations, future work can build upon the contributions of this paper and develop more robust and reliable AI-assisted software development tools.


## Questions:

A primary concern regarding the simulated user is how well it reflects the behavior of real users in providing clarification. The paper acknowledges that the simulated user is not intended to be a perfect representation of real users, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the potential differences between simulated and real user interactions. I am curious about the specific design choices made in creating the simulated user and how these choices might impact the generalizability of the findings. For example, how does the simulated user handle situations where the requested information is not explicitly stated in the full issue, but might be implied or inferred by a real user? Additionally, I would like to understand how the simulated user's responses were generated and whether any measures were taken to ensure that the responses are natural and realistic. Another key question relates to the analysis of clarifying questions. The paper evaluates the overall effectiveness of interactive clarification but does not provide a detailed analysis of the types of questions that lead to the best outcomes. I am interested in understanding how the authors plan to categorize and analyze the questions asked by the models. What specific criteria will be used to evaluate the effectiveness of different question types? For example, will the analysis consider the level of detail requested, the relevance of the question to the task, and the ability of the question to elicit the necessary information from the user? Furthermore, I am curious about the potential biases that might be present in the simulated user responses and how these biases might affect the results. The paper mentions that the simulated user responds only with information explicitly present in the full issue or with the phrase "I don't have that information." This limited response set might introduce biases that favor certain types of questions or interaction strategies. I would like to understand how the authors plan to address these potential biases and ensure that the findings are not skewed by the limitations of the simulated user. Finally, I am interested in the authors' perspective on the practical implications of their findings for real-world software development. How do they envision the integration of interactive LLMs into existing development workflows? What are the potential challenges and risks associated with using LLMs in software development, and how can these risks be mitigated? I believe that addressing these questions will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential and limitations of interactive LLMs in software engineering.
