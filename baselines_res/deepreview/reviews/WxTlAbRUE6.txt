## Summary:

This paper introduces GMD-25, a novel benchmark designed to rigorously evaluate the compositional generalization capabilities of machine learning force fields (MLFFs). The authors address a critical gap in the field by focusing on the ability of MLFFs to extrapolate to out-of-distribution (OOD) data, a crucial requirement for their practical application in molecular discovery and materials science. The benchmark consists of four distinct tasks, each targeting a specific aspect of compositional generalization: length extrapolation, functional group composition, functional group duplication, and functional group combination. These tasks are designed to challenge MLFFs by systematically altering molecular structures, such as varying chain lengths and combining different functional groups. The authors generated the benchmark dataset using the GFN2-xTB method, ensuring high-fidelity energy and force labels. They then evaluated five state-of-the-art MLFF architectures on GMD-25, revealing a significant performance degradation on OOD samples compared to in-distribution (ID) data. This finding highlights a critical limitation of current MLFFs, indicating that even advanced models struggle to generalize to chemically novel molecules. The paper's core contribution lies in the introduction of a systematically designed benchmark that directly addresses the issue of compositional generalization, a problem that has been largely overlooked in previous evaluations of MLFFs. The authors' approach involves creating OOD test sets by modifying the structure of molecules, which is a more direct and relevant test of generalization than simply using molecules not seen during training. The empirical results demonstrate that current MLFFs, despite their success on in-distribution data, often fail to generalize to even slightly modified chemical structures. This work is significant because it exposes the limitations of current MLFFs and provides a valuable tool for the community to develop more robust and generalizable models. The benchmark's focus on compositional generalization, rather than just overall accuracy, is a crucial step towards making MLFFs more reliable and applicable in real-world scenarios. The paper's findings suggest that future research should focus on developing models with stronger inductive biases that can capture the underlying physics of interatomic interactions, rather than simply fitting the training data. The authors' work provides a clear direction for future research in the field of MLFFs, emphasizing the need for models that can generalize beyond the training distribution. The GMD-25 benchmark is a valuable contribution that will likely have a significant impact on the development of more reliable and generalizable MLFFs.

## Strengths:

This paper presents several notable strengths, primarily centered around the introduction of a novel and well-designed benchmark for evaluating the compositional generalization of MLFFs. The core strength lies in the authors' recognition of a critical gap in the field: the lack of systematic evaluation of MLFFs' ability to generalize to out-of-distribution data. The GMD-25 benchmark directly addresses this issue by providing a set of tasks that specifically test the models' ability to extrapolate to novel molecular structures. The design of the four tasks—length extrapolation, functional group composition, functional group duplication, and functional group combination—is a significant contribution. These tasks are well-defined and systematically target different aspects of compositional generalization, providing a comprehensive evaluation framework. The use of the GFN2-xTB method for generating high-fidelity energy and force labels ensures the quality of the dataset, which is crucial for reliable evaluation. The paper's experimental results are also a strength. The authors evaluate five state-of-the-art MLFF architectures on the GMD-25 benchmark, and the results clearly demonstrate that these models exhibit significant performance degradation when generalizing to OOD data. This finding is valuable for the community, as it highlights the limitations of current MLFFs and motivates further research into more robust models. The paper is also well-written and organized, making it easy to follow the methodology and results. The authors clearly explain the design of the benchmark, the evaluation metrics, and the experimental setup. The figures and tables are well-presented and effectively communicate the key findings. The paper's focus on compositional generalization, rather than just overall accuracy, is a crucial step towards making MLFFs more reliable and applicable in real-world scenarios. The authors' work provides a clear direction for future research in the field of MLFFs, emphasizing the need for models that can generalize beyond the training distribution. The GMD-25 benchmark is a valuable contribution that will likely have a significant impact on the development of more reliable and generalizable MLFFs. The paper's clear articulation of the problem, the well-designed benchmark, and the insightful results make it a significant contribution to the field.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper makes a valuable contribution with the GMD-25 benchmark, several weaknesses limit its scope and impact. One significant limitation is the relatively narrow chemical diversity of the dataset. Although the authors state that the dataset comprises 118 molecules, these molecules are primarily based on linear alkanes and a limited set of functional groups, such as alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines, and amides. This focus on carbon-based molecules with relatively simple functional groups, as detailed in Section 3.1, restricts the benchmark's ability to assess MLFFs' performance on more complex chemical systems. The lack of heteroatoms and more complex functional groups, such as those found in inorganic materials or large biomacromolecules, means that the benchmark may not fully reveal the models' limitations when applied to more challenging chemical environments. This is a concern because MLFFs are often used to study systems with diverse chemical compositions, and the current benchmark may not adequately capture the complexities of these systems. The reliance on linear alkane chains as the main scaffold for the tasks, as described in Section 3.1, is another weakness. While the functional groups introduce some complexity, the core structure remains relatively simple. This simplicity may not adequately challenge the generalization capabilities of MLFFs to more complex molecular architectures, such as branched chains, rings, or molecules with significant conformational flexibility. The systematicity of the tasks, while based on variations of the carbon chain length and functional group combinations, may still be too easily learned by MLFFs, limiting the benchmark's ability to assess true compositional generalization. The evaluation metrics used in the paper also present a weakness. The authors rely solely on the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for energy and forces, as stated in Section 4.2. While these metrics are standard, they provide a limited view of model performance in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The absence of metrics related to dynamic properties, such as diffusion coefficients, radial distribution functions, or vibrational frequencies, limits the ability to assess the models' performance in realistic simulation scenarios. These dynamic properties are crucial for understanding the behavior of molecules in different environments and are not captured by the static energy and force MAEs. This limitation means that the benchmark may not fully reveal the models' ability to accurately simulate real-world molecular dynamics. Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed benchmark. While the authors describe the design choices for the benchmark in Section 3, they do not explicitly dedicate a section to discussing its limitations. This omission is a weakness because it leaves the reader to infer the limitations based on the presented tasks and molecules. A more explicit discussion of the benchmark's limitations, including its scope and the types of systems it does not cover, would be beneficial. Finally, the paper does not provide a thorough analysis of the reasons behind the poor generalization performance of the evaluated models. While the results in Section 4.3 clearly show that current models struggle with compositional generalization, the authors do not delve deeply into the underlying causes. The analysis sections within each experiment primarily focus on describing the observed trends in the MAE values for different models and tasks, without investigating the specific architectural limitations of the models, such as the receptive field of the neural networks or the ability of the models to capture the underlying physics of interatomic interactions. This lack of in-depth analysis limits the paper's ability to provide insights into the failure modes of the models and guide the development of more robust models. The absence of a detailed investigation into the model's learned representations also prevents a deeper understanding of why the models fail to generalize. These weaknesses, taken together, limit the scope and impact of the paper. While the GMD-25 benchmark is a valuable contribution, the limitations in dataset diversity, evaluation metrics, and analysis need to be addressed in future work.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made to the GMD-25 benchmark and the associated research. First, the dataset's chemical diversity should be significantly expanded. This could involve incorporating molecules with a wider range of functional groups, including those with heteroatoms such as sulfur, phosphorus, and halogens. The inclusion of inorganic molecules and molecules with more complex bonding environments, such as metal-organic complexes, would also be beneficial. Furthermore, the dataset should include molecules with ring systems, branched chains, and significant conformational flexibility. This expansion would make the benchmark more representative of real-world chemical systems and provide a more robust evaluation of MLFFs' generalization capabilities. The authors should also consider incorporating molecules with varying charge states and protonation levels to assess the models' ability to handle different chemical environments, which is crucial for modeling biological systems. This could involve generating datasets with diverse chemical compositions, structural motifs, and bonding environments. For example, the benchmark could include proteins with varying secondary structure content, such as alpha-helices and beta-sheets, or inorganic materials with different crystal structures and bonding types. To address the limitations of the evaluation metrics, the authors should incorporate dynamic properties that are relevant to molecular dynamics simulations. This could include calculating properties such as diffusion coefficients, radial distribution functions, vibrational frequencies, and reaction rates. These metrics would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the models' ability to accurately simulate molecular dynamics. Additionally, the evaluation should consider the stability of the simulations, such as the conservation of energy and momentum. The use of more sophisticated metrics, such as the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the simulated trajectories, would provide a more detailed analysis of the models' performance. Furthermore, the inclusion of metrics that assess the models' ability to reproduce experimental observables would be valuable. The authors should also consider using metrics that are specific to the type of molecular system being simulated. For example, for systems with periodic boundary conditions, metrics such as the mean squared displacement or the structure factor could be used. To better understand the reasons behind the poor generalization performance, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the model's learned representations. This could involve visualizing the feature maps of the neural networks or analyzing the sensitivity of the models to different types of structural variations. For example, the authors could investigate whether the models are overly sensitive to the presence of specific functional groups or whether they struggle to capture the long-range interactions between different parts of the molecule. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different architectural choices on the generalization performance. This could involve comparing the performance of different types of neural networks, such as graph neural networks and transformers, or investigating the effect of different regularization techniques. A more detailed analysis of the model's failure modes would provide valuable insights into the limitations of current MLFFs and guide the development of more robust models. Finally, the authors should consider incorporating more physically-informed architectures or training strategies to improve the generalization performance of MLFFs. This could involve incorporating symmetry constraints into the model architecture or using physics-based loss functions. For example, the authors could explore the use of equivariant neural networks, which are designed to respect the symmetries of the physical system, or incorporate a loss function that penalizes deviations from known physical laws. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of transfer learning techniques to leverage knowledge from related tasks or datasets. By incorporating more physically-informed approaches, the authors could develop more robust and generalizable MLFFs that are better suited for real-world applications. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly enhance the GMD-25 benchmark and make it a more valuable tool for the MLFF community.
