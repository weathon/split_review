## Summary:

This paper introduces a streamlined spectral algorithm for community detection within the two-community stochastic block model (SBM), focusing on scenarios with constant edge density. The core contribution lies in simplifying an existing spectral algorithm by removing a degree-based preprocessing step, which was originally intended to handle outliers and degree variations. The authors argue that this preprocessing step is unnecessary and that its removal leads to a more efficient algorithm without sacrificing performance. They achieve this simplification by directly leveraging the spectral properties of the adjacency matrix, specifically exploiting the characteristics of the second eigenvalue. The theoretical analysis demonstrates that the proposed algorithm achieves error rates comparable to the original algorithm, but with reduced computational complexity. The authors provide rigorous theoretical bounds using Chernoff bounds, normal approximations, and Monte Carlo validation. They show that the simplified algorithm's error rates approach information-theoretic limits, indicating a significant improvement over existing methods. The paper includes an experimental section that validates the theoretical findings, demonstrating the practical effectiveness of the simplified approach. The experiments compare the proposed algorithm against theoretical predictions, Chernoff-derived bounds, and Monte Carlo simulations, showing that the algorithm performs well in practice and can accurately recover the community structure. The results suggest that algorithmic simplification, rather than increasing complexity, can lead to both computational efficiency and enhanced performance in spectral community detection. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field by demonstrating that a simplified spectral algorithm can achieve near-optimal performance without the need for complex preprocessing steps, which has implications for the design of efficient and scalable algorithms for community detection in large-scale networks. The work challenges the assumption that complex preprocessing is always necessary for robust community detection, highlighting the potential of direct spectral methods.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's focus on the two-community stochastic block model (SBM) with equal-sized communities and constant edge density is a significant limitation. As the authors themselves state, they propose a streamlined spectral algorithm for community detection in this specific setting. The theoretical analysis and experimental results are tailored to this scenario, and it is unclear how the algorithm would perform in more complex settings. Specifically, the paper does not discuss the potential extensions of the proposed algorithm to more general SBMs, such as those with more than two communities or unbalanced community sizes. The analysis and experimental results are limited to the balanced two-community case, and it is unclear how the algorithm would perform when community sizes are significantly different or when the number of communities increases. This limits the practical applicability of the proposed method, as real-world networks often exhibit more complex community structures. This limitation is evident throughout the paper, with the introduction explicitly stating the focus on two equal-sized blocks, and the experimental setup using parameters that enforce this balance. The absence of any discussion or analysis regarding the algorithm's performance in more general SBM settings is a significant oversight. Second, the paper lacks a detailed comparison of the proposed algorithm with other state-of-the-art community detection algorithms. While the authors compare against the original spectral algorithm, a more comprehensive comparison against other established methods is needed to fully assess the performance of the proposed algorithm. Specifically, a comparison with semidefinite programming (SDP) based methods, which are known to achieve optimal performance in certain regimes of the SBM, is missing. This makes it difficult to understand the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. The paper primarily compares the proposed simplified Spectral Partition against the original Spectral Algorithm (with the correction step), and the experimental section only includes comparisons with theoretical bounds and the original spectral algorithm, with no mention of SDP or other advanced methods. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to position the proposed algorithm within the broader landscape of community detection methods. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm. While the authors claim that the algorithm is simplified, they do not provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity. This makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the algorithm for large-scale networks. A rigorous analysis of the computational cost, including the dependence on the number of nodes and edges, is necessary to fully understand the practical implications of the proposed simplification.
