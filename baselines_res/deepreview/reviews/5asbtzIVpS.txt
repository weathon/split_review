## Summary:

This paper introduces Forest-based Graph Learning (FGL), a novel graph learning paradigm designed to address the trade-off between computational efficiency and the ability to capture long-range dependencies in graph neural networks. The core idea of FGL is to reinterpret message passing as transportation over a forest of spanning trees, which facilitates efficient long-range information propagation. The authors propose a homophily estimator-based tree sampler to generate homophilous trees with higher probability and a linear-time tree aggregator that enables quadratic node-pair interactions. The method is theoretically grounded, with a detailed analysis of the relationship between edge-homophily estimates and the quality of the induced tree distribution. Empirically, FGL achieves competitive results on semi-supervised node classification tasks, outperforming state-of-the-art methods while maintaining efficiency. However, the paper's focus on node classification and its limited exploration of other graph-related tasks, such as link prediction and graph classification, restrict its broader applicability. Additionally, the computational complexity analysis is high-level, and the practical considerations for implementing FGL, such as hyperparameter sensitivity and the impact of different tree sampling strategies, are not thoroughly addressed. Despite these limitations, FGL represents a significant contribution to the field of graph neural networks, offering a new perspective on efficient and effective long-range information propagation.

## Strengths:

I find several strengths in this paper that make it a valuable contribution to the field of graph neural networks. Firstly, the introduction of a forest-based graph learning paradigm is innovative and provides a fresh perspective on how to model long-range dependencies in graphs. The authors effectively argue that traditional graph neural networks struggle with the trade-off between computational efficiency and the ability to capture global information, and FGL offers a compelling solution by leveraging spanning trees. The theoretical support for the proposed method is robust, with a detailed analysis of the relationship between edge-homophily estimates and the quality of the induced tree distribution. This theoretical foundation adds credibility to the framework and helps to justify the design choices. Secondly, the empirical results are promising, demonstrating that FGL achieves competitive performance on semi-supervised node classification tasks while maintaining efficiency. The authors conduct extensive experiments on a variety of datasets, including both homophilous and heterophilous graphs, and show that FGL outperforms state-of-the-art methods in many cases. The ablation studies and hyperparameter analyses further validate the effectiveness of the proposed components, such as the homophily estimator and the tree aggregator. Lastly, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to both experts and non-experts in the field. The clear structure and detailed explanations of the method and experiments contribute to the overall quality of the paper.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and weaknesses are evident. Firstly, the paper's focus on semi-supervised node classification may limit its applicability to other graph-related tasks, such as link prediction or graph classification. The method is designed to generate node embeddings suitable for classification, but it lacks a clear mechanism for producing graph-level embeddings or handling multi-graph datasets. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world scenarios involve tasks beyond node classification, and the ability to generalize to these tasks is crucial for the broader impact of the method. The authors could explore methods for generating graph-level embeddings, such as incorporating pooling or readout layers, to address this gap. Secondly, the computational complexity analysis is high-level and lacks a detailed breakdown of the time and space complexity of each step in the FGL framework. While the authors claim that their method is efficient, a more rigorous analysis of the computational cost of tree sampling, message passing, and aggregation processes would be valuable. This would allow for a more direct comparison with other graph learning methods, especially those that employ attention mechanisms or other computationally intensive operations. The current analysis, which is limited to a few high-level statements, does not provide a comprehensive understanding of the method's efficiency. Thirdly, the paper could provide more insights into the practical considerations of implementing the FGL framework. For instance, the impact of the number of trees in the forest on performance and computational cost is not thoroughly explored. Similarly, the sensitivity of the method to different graph structures, such as sparse graphs, dense graphs, and graphs with varying degrees of homophily, is not adequately addressed. A more systematic study of these practical aspects would be beneficial for practitioners seeking to apply the FGL framework to real-world problems. The current discussion is limited to a few hyperparameter studies and ablation experiments, which do not fully capture the method's behavior under different conditions. Furthermore, the paper does not sufficiently differentiate its approach from existing tree-based and subgraph-based methods. The use of trees for graph representation is not new, and the paper does not provide a detailed comparison to these methods, leaving the reader unsure of the unique contributions of FGL. A more thorough analysis of the similarities and differences between FGL and existing techniques, along with a demonstration of how FGL improves upon them, would strengthen the paper's claims. Lastly, the motivation for using a forest of spanning trees is somewhat weak. The authors argue that trees achieve global coverage with lower computational cost, but they do not provide a strong theoretical justification for why this specific ensemble approach is beneficial for graph learning. The connection to established graph learning principles, such as message passing or spectral graph theory, is not clearly articulated, making it difficult to understand the method's underlying mechanisms and potential advantages over existing approaches. The 'bagging' analogy is relevant, but the paper could benefit from a more detailed explanation of how the ensemble nature of the method contributes to its performance.


## Suggestions:

To enhance the applicability of the FGL framework beyond node classification, the authors should explore methods for generating graph-level embeddings. This could involve incorporating a pooling or readout layer that aggregates node embeddings into a single vector representation of the entire graph. Techniques such as global mean pooling, max pooling, or attention-based pooling could be considered. Additionally, the authors should investigate how the FGL framework can be adapted for link prediction tasks. This might involve generating node embeddings that are specifically designed for predicting the existence of edges between nodes, possibly by incorporating a separate message passing mechanism that focuses on edge information or by using a contrastive learning approach to learn embeddings sensitive to the presence or absence of edges. These extensions would significantly broaden the scope of the FGL framework and make it more versatile for various graph learning tasks. To address the lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis, the authors should provide a more rigorous breakdown of the time and space complexity of each step in the FGL framework. This should include a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the tree sampling process, the message passing operations within each tree, and the aggregation of messages at each node. The analysis should also consider the impact of different hyperparameters, such as the number of trees in the forest, on the overall computational cost. Furthermore, the authors should compare the computational complexity of the FGL framework with that of other state-of-the-art graph learning methods, such as graph neural networks with attention mechanisms. This comparison should be based on both theoretical analysis and empirical measurements on benchmark datasets. Such a detailed analysis would provide a clearer understanding of the efficiency of the FGL framework and its suitability for large-scale graph learning tasks. To improve the practical implementation of the FGL framework, the authors should provide more detailed guidance on the choice of hyperparameters and the sensitivity of the method to different graph structures. This should include a systematic study of the impact of different tree sampling strategies on the performance of the method. For example, the authors could investigate the effect of using different random sampling techniques or biased sampling methods that prioritize certain types of edges or nodes. Additionally, the authors should explore the sensitivity of the method to different graph structures, such as sparse graphs, dense graphs, and graphs with varying degrees of homophily. This could involve conducting experiments on a wider range of benchmark datasets with different characteristics. The authors should also provide practical recommendations for choosing appropriate hyperparameters based on the characteristics of the input graph. This would make the FGL framework more accessible and easier to use for practitioners. To better motivate the proposed method, the authors should provide a more rigorous theoretical justification for using a forest of spanning trees. This could involve analyzing the properties of the sampled trees and their relationship to the underlying graph structure. The authors should also explain why this specific ensemble approach is beneficial for graph learning, perhaps by relating it to concepts such as diversity or robustness. A more detailed comparison to existing ensemble methods for graphs would also be beneficial. Furthermore, the authors should clarify the connection between their method and established graph learning principles, such as message passing or spectral graph theory. This would help to better understand the method's underlying mechanisms and its potential advantages over existing approaches. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of how the method's components, such as the tree sampler and the tree aggregator, contribute to its overall performance. To address the lack of novelty, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison to existing tree-based and subgraph-based methods. This should include a thorough analysis of the similarities and differences between the proposed method and these existing techniques, highlighting the specific advantages of the proposed approach. The authors should also provide a more detailed explanation of how their method improves upon these existing techniques, perhaps by demonstrating superior performance on specific tasks or by showing that it can capture different types of graph structures. The authors should consider incorporating ideas from existing tree-based and subgraph-based methods into their approach, perhaps by using them as a basis for their forest sampling or aggregation strategies. This would help to better position their method within the existing literature and to highlight its unique contributions.
