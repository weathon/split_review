## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel approach to causal bandit problems, focusing on the scenario where interventions are conditional rather than hard or soft. The authors tackle the challenge of identifying the optimal node for intervention within a causal graph, aiming to maximize the expected reward. Their core contribution lies in providing a graphical characterization that pinpoints the optimal node for conditional intervention directly from the causal graph structure. This characterization is then leveraged to develop an efficient algorithm, termed C4, which identifies the minimal set of nodes (mGISS) guaranteed to contain the optimal intervention node. The C4 algorithm operates with a time complexity of O(|V| + |E|), where |V| is the number of nodes and |E| is the number of edges in the causal graph, making it scalable for larger problems. The authors provide rigorous theoretical proofs to support their graphical characterization and the correctness of the C4 algorithm. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that the C4 algorithm significantly reduces the search space for the optimal intervention, leading to faster convergence rates when integrated with a UCB-based bandit algorithm. The experimental results, conducted on both synthetic and real-world datasets, validate the theoretical findings and highlight the practical benefits of the proposed approach. The paper's significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between causal inference and bandit optimization, offering a structured approach to identify optimal interventions in complex systems. By focusing on conditional interventions, the authors address a more realistic scenario compared to hard or soft interventions, enhancing the applicability of their work to real-world problems. The paper's approach of using the causal graph to reduce the search space, rather than directly accelerating learning, distinguishes it from other works in the field. Overall, this paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of causal bandits, offering a theoretically sound and empirically validated method for identifying optimal interventions in complex systems.

## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified two key weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed C4 algorithm, particularly concerning its computational complexity and scalability. While the authors claim a linear time complexity of O(|V| + |E|), they do not delve into the practical implications of this complexity, especially for very large causal graphs. The paper states, "The Closure Computation via Children with Multiple Connectors (C4) Algorithm (Algorithm 1) computes the closure  $\mathcal{L}^\infty(\mathbf{U})$  in  $O(|\mathbf{V}| + |E|)$  time" (Algorithm 1 C4). However, this statement is not accompanied by an analysis of the constants involved or an empirical evaluation of the algorithm's runtime on large graphs. The absence of such analysis leaves a gap in understanding the practical scalability of the algorithm. For instance, the paper does not discuss how the algorithm's performance might vary on sparse versus dense graphs, or graphs with varying degrees of connectivity. Furthermore, the memory requirements of the algorithm are not addressed, which could be a limiting factor for very large graphs. The experimental section only uses datasets with a maximum of 109 nodes, which is not sufficient to evaluate the algorithm's performance on very large graphs. This lack of detailed analysis and empirical validation on larger datasets raises concerns about the algorithm's practical applicability in real-world scenarios involving complex systems with thousands or millions of nodes. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the time complexity but fails to provide any further analysis or empirical evidence to support its practical scalability. Second, the paper makes a significant assumption that there are no latent confounders in the causal graph. This assumption is explicitly stated in the introduction: "While Lee & Bareinboim (2018) study causal bandits with multi-node hard interventions in the presence of latent confounders, we focus on single-node conditional interventions under the assumption of no latent confounders." This assumption severely restricts the applicability of the method in many real-world scenarios where unobserved variables are common. The paper does not discuss the potential impact of violating this assumption on the validity of the results. The consequences of unobserved confounders could lead to biased estimates of the causal effects and potentially incorrect identification of the optimal intervention node. The paper also does not explore potential avenues for extending the proposed method to handle such scenarios. While the conclusion mentions that "Addressing latent confounding would also require substantially more research and is thus left as future work," this acknowledgement does not mitigate the limitation's impact on the current work. The absence of a discussion on the implications of this assumption and potential mitigation strategies significantly limits the generalizability of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as the paper explicitly states the assumption and does not address its limitations or potential impact on the results. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more thorough comparison with existing causal bandit algorithms, particularly those that handle hard and soft interventions. While the paper cites relevant works in the field, the comparison is relatively high-level and does not provide a detailed analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to existing approaches. The paper states, "All of the works described above proposed algorithms which aim at accelerating learning by utilizing knowledge of the causal model. As explained in Section 1, this contrasts with our work, which, like the work of Lee & Bareinboim (2018; 2019), uses knowledge of the causal graph to find a minimal search space (over the nodes) for causal bandits." This statement highlights the difference in approach but does not delve into the specific mechanisms, assumptions, and performance trade-offs of different algorithms. The paper does not compare its method to algorithms that use causal graphs to guide exploration in bandit problems, or those that explicitly model intervention mechanisms. This lack of a detailed comparison makes it difficult to understand the specific scenarios where the proposed method is most effective and its limitations compared to existing methods. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's comparison is conceptual rather than a detailed analysis of specific algorithms and their trade-offs.

I suggest that the authors compare their method to algorithms that use causal graphs to guide exploration in bandit problems, or those that explicitly model intervention mechanisms.
