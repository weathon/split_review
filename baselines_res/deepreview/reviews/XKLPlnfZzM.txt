## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel diffusion-based trajectory generation model, termed TDDM (Temporal Deaggregation Diffusion Model), which aims to address the limitations of existing methods by decoupling spatial and temporal aspects of human mobility. The core idea behind TDDM is to first generate spatial priors, representing the marginal distribution of geographical occupancy, and then use these priors to guide the generation of temporal trajectories. This hierarchical approach, the authors argue, allows for more scalable and generalizable trajectory generation, as it avoids the need for strong sample-specific conditioning often required by other methods. The spatial prior is computed by dividing each region into a grid and calculating the proportion of trajectories that fall within each cell, effectively capturing the spatial distribution of mobility. The temporal trajectories are then generated using a denoising diffusion process conditioned on these spatial priors. The authors employ a transformer encoder within the diffusion model to capture the temporal dependencies in the trajectory data. The model is evaluated on three large-scale human trajectory datasets: Geolife, Porto, and Cabspotting, representing diverse urban environments in Beijing, Porto, and the San Francisco Bay Area, respectively. The empirical results demonstrate that TDDM consistently outperforms several baseline methods, including other diffusion-based models, in terms of distributional alignment, achieving up to four times lower KL divergences. The paper also includes ablation studies to verify the effect of map-matching and the contribution of the deaggregation framework. The authors claim that TDDM's improvements in performance stem from this deaggregation framework, which allows for better generalization across different regions. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to trajectory generation that could have significant implications for various applications, including urban planning, transportation management, and location-based services. The separation of spatial and temporal components is a conceptually sound idea that aligns with the inherent structure of trajectory data. The empirical results, while promising, also highlight the need for further investigation into the model's limitations and potential areas for improvement. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to trajectory generation, its comprehensive evaluation, and its potential to advance the state-of-the-art in this field.

While TDDM effectively captures spatial occupancy and the temporal ordering of movements, it overlooks crucial temporal patterns such as time of day, day of the week, and seasonal variations. These patterns are known to significantly influence human mobility, and their absence could lead to unrealistic trajectory generation. For instance, the model might generate similar traffic patterns during peak hours and off-peak hours, which is not representative of real-world scenarios. The paper's method description focuses on spatial priors and the diffusion process for temporal generation without explicitly incorporating these temporal features. The experimental setup also doesn't indicate the use of time-based features. This limitation is further highlighted by the fact that the model is trained on data resampled to one observation per second, without any explicit consideration of the time of day or day of the week associated with each observation.
