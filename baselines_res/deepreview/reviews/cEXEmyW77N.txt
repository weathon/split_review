## Summary:

This paper presents a compelling investigation into the ability of large language models (LLMs) to generate reference lists that are indistinguishable from those curated by humans. The authors construct citation graphs for both ground-truth and LLM-generated references, employing a dataset of 10,000 focal papers. They then analyze these graphs using a combination of structural features, such as centrality measures and clustering coefficients, and semantic embeddings derived from the titles and abstracts of the focal papers and their references. The core methodology involves comparing the structural and semantic properties of human-generated citation graphs with those generated by two LLMs, GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4.5. The authors utilize Random Forest classifiers and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to assess the separability of these different graph types. The key empirical finding is that while LLM-generated citation graphs exhibit structural properties that closely resemble those of human-generated graphs, they differ significantly in their semantic embeddings. Specifically, the study demonstrates that structural features alone are insufficient to reliably distinguish LLM-generated references from human-generated ones. However, semantic embeddings of titles and abstracts provide a strong signal for differentiation. Furthermore, the authors show that GNNs incorporating semantic embeddings can effectively differentiate between human and LLM-generated references, achieving high accuracy. This finding suggests that LLMs, while capable of mimicking the structural patterns of citation networks, leave detectable semantic fingerprints. The overall significance of this work lies in its timely exploration of the challenges posed by the increasing use of LLMs in scientific writing, particularly in the context of literature review and bibliography generation. The study highlights the need to focus on semantic content rather than just structural features for effective detection of LLM-generated scientific content, which has important implications for the development of tools to detect and mitigate biases in LLM-generated scientific content.

## Strengths:

This paper makes several notable contributions to the growing field of LLM analysis, particularly in the context of scientific writing. First and foremost, the paper addresses a highly relevant and timely problem: the potential for LLMs to generate seemingly plausible but ultimately unreliable reference lists. The authors' approach of comparing LLM-generated references to human-curated ones through the lens of citation graphs is both novel and insightful. This method allows for a systematic analysis of the structural and semantic properties of these references, providing a quantitative basis for understanding the similarities and differences between human and machine-generated content. The methodology is rigorously designed, employing a large dataset of 10,000 focal papers and constructing paired ground-truth and LLM-generated citation graphs. The use of both Random Forest classifiers and Graph Neural Networks to analyze the data provides a robust and comprehensive approach. The inclusion of a field-matched random baseline provides a strong control for comparison, ensuring that the observed differences are not simply due to chance. The paper's findings are clearly presented, with detailed explanations of the methodology, including the construction of citation graphs, feature extraction, and model training. The results are supported by clear visualizations and statistical analysis, making the paper accessible and easy to follow. The key finding that LLMs can mimic the structural properties of human citation networks but leave detectable semantic fingerprints is a significant contribution. This highlights the need to focus on semantic content rather than just structural features for effective detection of LLM-generated scientific content. The paper's emphasis on the limitations of topology-based detection methods and the importance of content-aware approaches is a crucial insight for the development of future detection and debiasing methods. Finally, the paper's overall clarity and organization make it a valuable contribution to the field, providing a solid foundation for future research in this important area.


## Weaknesses:

While this paper presents a valuable contribution to the field, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the study's scope is limited to title-abstract-level references, which may not fully represent the complexity of actual reference-generating behavior in LLMs. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the inclusion of full-text references or fine-tuning on bibliography generation tasks could provide a more comprehensive evaluation. This limitation is significant because LLMs might exhibit different behaviors when processing more comprehensive information. The paper explicitly states that embeddings are extracted from titles and abstracts, and references are generated based on this limited information, which may not capture the nuances of full-text content. This focus on title-abstract information is a clear limitation that impacts the generalizability of the findings. Second, while the paper demonstrates the ability to distinguish between human and LLM-generated references, it lacks a detailed analysis of which specific semantic or structural features most strongly influence separability. The paper employs Random Forest classifiers, which offer feature importance scores, but these scores are not presented or analyzed in detail. For instance, the paper does not identify which specific centrality measures (e.g., betweenness, closeness, eigenvector) or clustering coefficients are most indicative of LLM-generated graphs. Similarly, on the semantic side, the paper does not explore which aspects of the title/abstract embeddings contribute most to the separability. This lack of granular analysis limits our understanding of the underlying mechanisms that differentiate the two types of graphs. The results sections focus on overall accuracy and F1-scores, but do not delve into the specific feature contributions. This is a significant weakness because it prevents us from understanding *why* the separation occurs. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the specific types of errors or biases that LLMs introduce in reference generation. While the study shows that semantic embeddings can distinguish LLM-generated references, it does not delve into the nature of these semantic differences. For example, the paper does not investigate whether LLMs are more likely to cite papers from specific venues or with certain keywords, or whether there are systematic biases in the recency or age of the cited papers. This lack of error analysis is a significant limitation because it prevents us from understanding the specific weaknesses of LLMs in reference generation. The paper focuses on overall distinguishability rather than a granular error analysis, which limits its practical implications. Fourth, the study focuses on a specific set of LLMs (GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4.5) and embedding models (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large and SPECTER2). The generalizability of the findings to other LLMs and embedding models is not fully explored. It is unclear whether the observed patterns would hold for other models with different architectures or training data. For instance, models with different attention mechanisms or pre-training corpora might exhibit different citation behaviors, and this is not addressed. The paper explicitly states the LLMs and embedding models used, and while it shows some robustness by using Claude and SPECTER2, it doesn't explore a wider range of models to fully address generalizability. Finally, the paper could benefit from a deeper investigation into the practical implications of its findings, particularly in terms of how the observed limitations of topology-based detection might inform the development of more robust detection and debiasing methods. While the paper suggests a focus on content signals, it does not delve into specific strategies for mitigating the risks of LLM-generated references in scientific literature. The discussion section mentions that auditing and debiasing should prioritize content signals, but it does not provide specific, actionable strategies for mitigation and robust method development. The paper's discussion is more high-level, and a deeper investigation into practical applications is needed. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's findings, highlight areas where further research is needed to fully understand the implications of LLM-generated references.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses and further strengthen this research, several concrete steps can be taken. First, future work should explore the impact of using full-text references instead of relying solely on titles and abstracts. This would provide a more realistic evaluation of LLM-generated bibliographies, as LLMs might exhibit different behaviors when processing more comprehensive information. This could involve comparing the structural and semantic properties of citation graphs generated using only titles and abstracts versus those generated using full-text data. Such an analysis would provide a more nuanced understanding of how the availability of different levels of information affects the citation behavior of LLMs. Second, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the specific semantic and structural features that contribute most to the separability between human and LLM-generated references. This could involve techniques such as feature importance analysis or ablation studies to determine which embedding dimensions or graph centrality measures are most indicative of LLM-generated content. For example, examining the distribution of specific word embeddings or the variance in centrality measures across different types of references could reveal patterns that are unique to LLM-generated content. This would not only provide a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms but also inform the development of more targeted detection methods. Additionally, exploring the use of more sophisticated graph neural network architectures that can capture higher-order interactions between nodes could potentially improve the detection accuracy. Third, a more detailed analysis of the specific types of errors and biases introduced by LLMs in reference generation is needed. This could involve examining the citation patterns of LLMs in more detail, looking at the distribution of cited venues, the age of cited papers, and the semantic similarity between cited papers and the focal paper. The authors should also investigate whether LLMs exhibit biases towards certain topics or authors. This analysis should include a discussion of the potential implications of these biases for the scientific community. The authors should also consider how these biases might be mitigated in future work. This could involve developing methods for detecting and correcting biased citation patterns, or for training LLMs to generate more balanced and accurate reference lists. Fourth, the authors should investigate the generalizability of their findings across a wider range of LLMs and embedding models. This could involve including models with different architectures, training data, and sizes. For example, models like Llama or other open-source alternatives could be included to see if the observed patterns hold. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of different embedding models beyond OpenAI's and SPECTER2, such as Sentence-BERT or other domain-specific embeddings. This would help to determine if the observed semantic differences are consistent across various representation spaces. The analysis should also include a discussion of the potential limitations of the chosen models and how these limitations might affect the results. Finally, the practical implications of the findings should be explored in more detail. The study should investigate how the observed limitations of topology-based detection can be addressed in real-world scenarios. This could involve developing hybrid detection methods that combine structural and semantic features, or exploring the use of adversarial training techniques to create more robust detection models. Furthermore, the study should consider the potential impact of LLM-generated references on the scientific community and propose strategies for mitigating the risks associated with their use. This could include developing tools for verifying the authenticity of references or establishing guidelines for the responsible use of LLMs in scientific writing. Such practical considerations are crucial for ensuring the integrity of scientific literature in the age of LLMs.


## Questions:

Several questions arise from this study that warrant further investigation. First, have the authors explored the impact of different prompting strategies on the generated references? The paper describes the prompting approach at a high level, but it is unclear how variations in prompts might affect the structural and semantic characteristics of the generated graphs. How sensitive are the results to variations in prompts, and could different prompts lead to more easily detectable patterns? Second, are there specific semantic or structural features that consistently contribute most to the separability between human and LLM-generated references? While the paper uses Random Forest classifiers, which offer feature importance scores, a deeper dive into the most influential structural and semantic features is needed. Which specific centrality measures, clustering coefficients, or embedding dimensions are most indicative of LLM-generated content? An analysis of feature importance could provide deeper insights into the underlying mechanisms that differentiate the two types of graphs. Third, how do the results generalize to other LLMs or different domains? The study is limited to two LLMs (GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4.5) and a specific dataset from SciSciNet. Are there specific characteristics of these LLMs that might influence the results, and would the observed patterns hold for other models with different architectures or training data? Furthermore, how would the results change if the study was conducted on different datasets from other domains? Fourth, what is the impact of using full-text information in addition to titles and abstracts on the generated citation graphs? Would this change the structural and semantic properties of the graphs, and how would it affect the ability to distinguish LLM-generated references? The paper focuses on title-abstract information, but a more comprehensive analysis using full-text data is needed to fully understand the capabilities of LLMs in reference generation. Finally, what are the specific types of errors or biases that LLMs introduce in reference generation? Are there patterns in the semantic or structural features of the generated references that could provide more insights into the limitations of LLMs in this task? A more granular analysis of these error patterns would be beneficial for developing more robust detection and mitigation strategies.
