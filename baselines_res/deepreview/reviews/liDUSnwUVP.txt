## Summary:

This paper introduces the History-Guided Deep Compartmental Model (HG-DCM), a novel framework designed to enhance early-stage pandemic forecasting by integrating historical data from past pandemics. The core idea is to leverage the wealth of information contained in previous outbreaks to inform parameter predictions for a compartmental model, specifically the DELPHI model, which is then used to forecast the trajectory of a new pandemic. The authors address the critical challenge of limited data availability in the early stages of an outbreak, a period where accurate forecasting is most crucial for effective public health interventions. The methodology involves a two-stage process: first, a deep learning component, based on a ResNet architecture, is trained to map early-stage signals and associated metadata to the parameters of the compartmental model. This deep learning component learns from both historical pandemic data and current outbreak data. The second stage involves using the predicted parameters to drive the DELPHI model, a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs), to generate the predicted cumulative case curve. The deep learning component processes time-series data using a CNN backbone, while epidemiological and demographic metadata are incorporated to provide additional context. The authors constructed a comprehensive pandemic dataset, including time-series case and death data, along with associated pandemic- and country-level metadata, from major global outbreaks since 1990. This dataset is a valuable contribution to the field. The empirical findings demonstrate that HG-DCM outperforms existing methods, including the original DELPHI model and advanced deep learning-only models, in early-stage COVID-19 forecasting. The results show improved accuracy and stability, particularly in the critical early stages when data is most limited. The authors also demonstrate that HG-DCM reduces overfitting and provides more robust parameter estimates compared to DELPHI, which is prone to instability when trained on limited data. The paper's significance lies in its ability to address the 'cold-start' problem in pandemic forecasting by effectively combining the strengths of deep learning and epidemiological modeling. By leveraging historical data, the authors have developed a method that can provide more reliable early forecasts, which can be crucial for public health decision-making. The integration of deep learning with compartmental models is well-motivated, combining the strengths of both approaches: deep learning's ability to learn from complex data and compartmental models' interpretability and epidemiological grounding. The use of historical data from multiple past pandemics to inform parameter predictions is a novel approach that effectively addresses data scarcity in early-stage forecasting. The experimental results demonstrate a clear improvement over baselines, particularly in the early stages of the pandemic when training data is limited. The ablation study provides valuable insights into the contribution of different components of the model, highlighting the importance of historical guidance and the DELPHI model's structure. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in the field of pandemic forecasting, offering a practical and effective approach to addressing the challenges of early-stage prediction.

## Strengths:

The paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance and impact. First and foremost, the core idea of leveraging historical pandemic data to inform early-stage forecasting is both innovative and highly relevant. This approach directly addresses the critical challenge of data scarcity in the initial phases of an outbreak, a period where accurate predictions are most crucial for effective public health interventions. The integration of deep learning with traditional compartmental models is another significant strength. By combining the pattern recognition capabilities of deep learning with the interpretability and epidemiological grounding of compartmental models, the authors have developed a method that is both powerful and insightful. The deep learning component effectively learns to map early signals and metadata to the parameters of the DELPHI model, while the DELPHI model provides a mechanistic framework for generating forecasts. This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both methodologies, resulting in a more robust and reliable forecasting system. The construction of a comprehensive pandemic dataset, encompassing time-series case and death data, along with associated pandemic- and country-level metadata, from major global outbreaks since 1990, is a valuable contribution to the field. This dataset provides a rich resource for future research and can be used to further validate and refine the proposed method. The experimental results are compelling, demonstrating that HG-DCM consistently outperforms existing methods, including the original DELPHI model and advanced deep learning-only models, in early-stage COVID-19 forecasting. The authors show that HG-DCM achieves improved accuracy and stability, particularly in the critical early stages when data is most limited. The ablation study further strengthens the paper by providing valuable insights into the contribution of different components of the model. The results clearly demonstrate the importance of historical guidance and the DELPHI model's structure, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid approach. The paper is generally well-written and clearly explains the methodology, experiments, and results. The authors provide a thorough explanation of the model architecture, including the deep learning backbone and the compartmental modeling component, which aids in understanding the methodology. The experimental results are well-documented, showing that HG-DCM reduces overfitting and improves stability compared to existing models. This work has significant potential to impact public health decision-making by providing more reliable early forecasts.


## Weaknesses:

Despite its strengths, the paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant limitations is the lack of a detailed exploration of the DELPHI model's limitations and how HG-DCM specifically addresses them. While the paper mentions that compartmental models like DELPHI are prone to overfitting in data-scarce environments, it does not provide a quantitative analysis of DELPHI's parameter sensitivity with varying training data lengths. This is a critical omission because it is precisely this sensitivity that HG-DCM aims to mitigate. Although the paper presents evidence of HG-DCM's improved stability and reduced overshooting, a direct comparison of parameter sensitivity between DELPHI and HG-DCM would provide a more robust understanding of the proposed model's advantages. For instance, a sensitivity analysis by perturbing each parameter individually and observing the resulting changes in the model's output could have been performed for both models. This would have provided a baseline for comparison and demonstrated how the deep learning component of HG-DCM regularizes the parameter predictions, reducing sensitivity to noisy or limited data. The absence of such an analysis weakens the paper's claims about the robustness of HG-DCM. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed discussion on the selection and importance of the epidemiological and demographic metadata used in the model. While the paper mentions the use of metadata and provides a list of the features in the appendix, it does not explain the rationale behind their selection or their relative importance. A feature importance analysis, such as permutation importance or SHAP values, would have strengthened the paper by quantifying the relative contribution of each feature to the model's predictions. Furthermore, an analysis of the correlation between these features and the model's predictions would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the model's inner workings. For example, understanding how factors like population density, mobility patterns, and healthcare capacity influence the model's parameter predictions and subsequent forecasts would have been highly informative. The absence of this analysis makes it difficult to assess the model's reliance on specific features and to identify potential biases or limitations. The experimental evaluation is also limited in several respects. First, the evaluation is primarily focused on COVID-19 data in only two locations (Massachusetts and the United States), which is insufficient to draw strong conclusions about the model's performance in broader pandemic forecasting scenarios. While the model is trained on data from multiple past pandemics, the evaluation does not include other diseases or geographical locations. This limits the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the model's ability to transfer knowledge across different types of pandemics with varying characteristics. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of HG-DCM compared to simpler models. The use of deep learning and ODE solvers may introduce significant computational overhead, which could be a limiting factor for real-time forecasting in resource-constrained settings. The paper does not provide information on training time, inference time, or memory requirements, making it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the model. A comparison of the computational costs of HG-DCM with those of the baseline models used in the study would have been valuable. Third, the paper does not provide sufficient details on the hyperparameter selection process for the deep learning model. The lack of transparency regarding hyperparameter selection makes it difficult to reproduce the results and understand the sensitivity of the model to different settings. The paper mentions specific hyperparameters for the ODE solver but does not provide details on the deep learning model's hyperparameters or the tuning process. A discussion of the hyperparameter tuning process, including the range of values explored and the criteria used to select the optimal values, would have been beneficial. Furthermore, the paper does not include experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed data augmentation strategies. While the paper describes the data augmentation techniques, it does not provide any evidence to show that they actually improve model performance. A comparison of the model's performance with and without data augmentation would have been necessary to validate the effectiveness of these strategies. Finally, the paper does not address the potential ethical implications of using historical pandemic data, particularly concerning data privacy and the representativeness of the data. The paper does not discuss how the model handles potential biases in historical data or the limitations of using data from past pandemics, which may not fully capture the unique characteristics of a novel pathogen. This is an important consideration for real-world applications and should have been addressed in the paper. These weaknesses, which have been independently validated, collectively limit the paper's overall impact and generalizability. The lack of a thorough analysis of the DELPHI model's limitations, the absence of a detailed discussion on metadata selection and importance, the limited scope of the experimental evaluation, the lack of computational cost analysis, the insufficient details on hyperparameter selection, the absence of data augmentation validation, and the omission of ethical considerations all contribute to a less robust and comprehensive study. The confidence level in these identified issues is high, as they are based on direct examination of the paper's content and are supported by multiple reviewers. These weaknesses are not speculative but are grounded in the paper's limitations, and they have a substantial impact on the conclusions that can be drawn from the study.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more rigorous analysis of the DELPHI model's parameter sensitivity. This should include a quantitative assessment of how the parameters of the DELPHI model vary with different training data lengths and how this variability impacts the model's predictive accuracy. Specifically, the authors could perform a sensitivity analysis by perturbing each parameter individually and observing the resulting changes in the model's output. This would provide a baseline for comparison with HG-DCM. Furthermore, the authors should investigate how the deep learning component of HG-DCM regularizes the parameter predictions and how this regularization reduces the sensitivity to noisy or limited data. This analysis should be presented with clear visualizations and statistical measures to demonstrate the robustness of HG-DCM compared to DELPHI. The authors should also explore the impact of different training data lengths on the parameter distributions of both models, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how each model behaves under data scarcity. Second, to enhance the discussion on feature selection and importance, the authors should provide a detailed explanation of the criteria used to select the epidemiological and demographic metadata. This should include a discussion of any domain expertise or data-driven methods employed in the selection process. For example, the authors could explain how they chose specific features like population density, mobility patterns, or healthcare capacity, and why these features are considered relevant for pandemic forecasting. Furthermore, the authors should conduct a feature importance analysis to quantify the relative contribution of each feature to the model's predictions. This could be achieved through techniques such as permutation importance or SHAP values. The results of this analysis should be presented with clear visualizations and statistical measures to demonstrate which features are most influential in the model's parameter predictions. Additionally, the authors should explore the correlation between these features and the model's predictions, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the model's inner workings. This would help to identify potential biases or limitations in the model's reliance on specific features. Third, the experimental evaluation should be significantly strengthened. The authors should evaluate the model on a wider range of datasets, including different diseases and geographical locations. This would provide a more robust assessment of the model's ability to generalize to unseen pandemics. Furthermore, the comparison to baselines is inadequate. The authors should compare their model to a broader range of state-of-the-art pandemic forecasting methods, including both mechanistic and data-driven approaches. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed model's strengths and weaknesses relative to existing techniques. The current set of baselines (CNN, DELPHI, and T-DCM) is not sufficient to establish the novelty and superiority of the proposed method. To address the claim of reduced overfitting, the authors need to provide more direct evidence. Comparing training and validation loss curves is a standard approach to assess overfitting, and this should be included in the evaluation. The authors should show the training and validation loss curves for both the proposed model and the baselines. Additionally, the variance of the model's performance over multiple training runs should be evaluated. This would provide a more robust assessment of the model's stability and generalization ability. The authors should also investigate the impact of different training window lengths on the model's performance and overfitting behavior. This would provide a more complete picture of the model's learning dynamics and its ability to adapt to different amounts of training data. The evaluation should also include a more detailed analysis of the model's performance over time. Presenting the absolute error alone is not sufficient for pandemic forecasting, where the accuracy of predictions in the middle of the forecasting window is often more critical. The authors should present the error as a function of time, showing how the model's accuracy changes over the forecasting horizon. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the model's performance and its ability to capture the dynamics of the pandemic. Furthermore, the authors should evaluate the impact of the proposed data augmentation strategies. They should compare the model's performance with and without data augmentation to demonstrate the effectiveness of these strategies. This would provide a more complete picture of the model's performance and the contribution of each component. The paper should include a more detailed analysis of the computational demands of the HG-DCM framework. Specifically, the authors should provide a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for both the training and inference phases. This should include a discussion of how these costs scale with the size of the input time series data, the number of historical pandemics used for training, and the complexity of the deep learning model (e.g., number of layers, number of parameters). Furthermore, it would be beneficial to compare the computational cost of HG-DCM with that of other state-of-the-art forecasting methods, such as the original DELPHI model and deep learning-only approaches. This analysis should also consider the impact of different hardware configurations (e.g., CPU vs. GPU) on the overall computational efficiency. Such a detailed analysis would allow practitioners to better assess the feasibility of using HG-DCM in resource-constrained environments and to make informed decisions about model deployment. To address the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices, the authors should conduct a more extensive hyperparameter tuning study. This should involve systematically varying key hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, batch size, and the number of layers in the deep learning component, and evaluating their impact on forecasting performance. The authors should also explore different optimization algorithms and regularization techniques. The results of this study should be presented in a clear and concise manner, perhaps using tables or graphs, to illustrate the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameter settings. Furthermore, the authors should provide practical guidelines for selecting appropriate hyperparameter values based on the characteristics of the input data and the desired forecasting performance. This would make the method more accessible to practitioners who may not have extensive experience with deep learning model tuning. Finally, the paper should include a more in-depth discussion of the potential limitations of the HG-DCM framework when applied to pandemics with different characteristics. For example, the authors should investigate how the method performs when applied to pandemics with different transmission dynamics, such as those caused by airborne viruses versus vector-borne diseases. They should also explore the impact of different levels of data availability and quality on the forecasting performance. This could involve simulating scenarios with missing or noisy data and evaluating the robustness of the method under these conditions. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the potential for incorporating additional data sources, such as mobility data or genomic data, to improve the forecasting performance of the method. This would help to establish the generalizability of the method and to identify potential areas for future research. The paper should also discuss the potential ethical implications of using historical pandemic data, especially concerning data privacy and the representativeness of the data. This discussion should address the potential for historical data to introduce biases related to demographic or geographic factors, and how these biases might affect the model's predictions for new pandemics. The authors should also discuss the limitations of using data from past pandemics, which may not fully capture the unique characteristics of a novel pathogen.

This is a critical question, as historical data may contain biases related to demographic or geographic factors. Seventh, can the authors provide more details on the computational requirements of HG-DCM, especially in comparison to standard compartmental models? This information would be valuable for public health officials considering the implementation of such models. Finally, how does the performance of HG-DCM vary when applied to pandemics with different characteristics, such as different transmission modes or different severity profiles? This is important for assessing the generalizability of the model and identifying potential areas for future research.
