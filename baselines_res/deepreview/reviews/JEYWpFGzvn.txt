## Summary:

This paper introduces INFOTok, a novel adaptive video tokenizer grounded in information theory, specifically leveraging Shannon's principles to optimize token compression rates based on video content complexity. Unlike traditional fixed-rate tokenizers, INFOTok dynamically adjusts token lengths using an Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)-based router, aiming to achieve near-optimal compression. The core idea is to allocate more tokens to complex video segments and fewer tokens to simpler ones, thereby improving overall token efficiency. The authors present a rigorous theoretical framework, demonstrating the suboptimality of fixed-rate tokenizers and the near-optimality of their adaptive approach through theorems rooted in information theory. The method involves an encoder-decoder architecture where the encoder maps video frames to a latent space, and the decoder reconstructs the video from these latent representations. The ELBO is calculated based on the reconstruction error and a KL divergence term, which is then used by the router to determine the number of tokens to allocate for each video segment. The adaptive compressor then uses these variable-length token representations. Empirically, the paper demonstrates significant improvements over existing tokenization methods. The results show that INFOTok achieves approximately 20% token savings without any loss in reconstruction quality compared to state-of-the-art fixed-length tokenizers. Furthermore, it outperforms previous adaptive tokenizers by an average compression rate of 2.3× and reduces the number of evaluations (NFEs) by 11×. These findings are validated across multiple video datasets, including TokenBench and DAVIS, using metrics such as PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and FVD. The authors also provide ablation studies to validate the effectiveness of their adaptive compressor and compare their approach to other adaptive mechanisms. The paper's contribution lies in its principled approach to adaptive video tokenization, combining theoretical insights with empirical validation to achieve significant improvements in compression efficiency. The use of an ELBO-based router for dynamic token allocation is a key innovation, and the results suggest that this approach can lead to more efficient video representation. The paper also provides a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of the proposed method, showing that it is more efficient than previous adaptive tokenization methods. Overall, this paper presents a significant advancement in video tokenization, with potential implications for various video processing and understanding tasks.

## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling approach to adaptive video tokenization, several weaknesses warrant further consideration. First, the ELBO-based routing mechanism, while theoretically sound, introduces a computational overhead that needs more thorough analysis. The paper acknowledges that the ELBO calculation requires an additional decoder pass to estimate video complexity, which may impact real-time applications, especially for long video sequences. While the paper provides a comparison of the number of function evaluations (NFEs) and wall-clock latency in Appendix D, it lacks a detailed breakdown of the time cost associated with this additional decoder pass, particularly as a function of video length. This is a critical concern because the additional decoding step could become a bottleneck in real-time scenarios, and the paper does not fully explore this aspect. My analysis confirms that the method description explicitly states the need for an additional decoder pass for ELBO calculation, and the experimental section acknowledges the overhead but lacks a granular analysis of its impact on long sequences. This oversight is significant because it limits the practical applicability of the method in time-sensitive contexts. Second, the evaluation is primarily focused on reconstruction quality, with limited exploration of downstream tasks. While high reconstruction fidelity is important, the ultimate goal of video tokenization is often to enable downstream tasks such as video generation, action recognition, or video editing. The paper does not include experiments on these tasks, making it unclear how the adaptive tokenization strategy impacts performance in these contexts. My analysis confirms that the experiments section focuses solely on reconstruction metrics, and the introduction and conclusion mention the importance of downstream tasks but do not provide any experimental results for them. This is a significant limitation because it leaves a gap in our understanding of the practical benefits of the proposed method. Third, the router and adaptive compressor add complexity to the model architecture, and the sensitivity of the model to the hyperparameters of these components is not thoroughly explored. The paper introduces a router with a hyperparameter β and an adaptive compressor based on a transformer architecture, but it lacks a detailed ablation study on how different hyperparameter settings affect the performance of INFOTok. Fourth, the paper primarily demonstrates INFOTok's effectiveness on standard video datasets, such as TokenBench and DAVIS. The authors could present a graph showing the time cost of the additional decoder pass as a function of video length, which would provide a clearer picture of the computational overhead.
