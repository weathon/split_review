## Summary:

This paper introduces CATS-Bench, a novel benchmark designed for time series captioning and reasoning, addressing a gap in existing resources by incorporating real-world data, metadata, and visual representations. The benchmark is constructed from 11 diverse datasets spanning various domains, including climate, safety, demography, health, sales, and agriculture. The authors propose a scalable pipeline for generating high-quality captions using an oracle Large Language Model (LLM), Gemini 2.0 Flash, and further refine a subset of these captions through human revision. This pipeline also includes a human detectability study and diversity analysis to ensure the quality and lack of bias in the generated captions. The benchmark includes five tasks: time series captioning, time series matching, caption matching, plot matching, and time series comparison, providing a comprehensive evaluation framework for vision-language models (VLMs). The authors also introduce tailored evaluation metrics that go beyond standard linguistic metrics, focusing on numeric fidelity and statistical inference. The paper benchmarks several state-of-the-art VLMs, highlighting their strengths and limitations in the context of time series data. The results reveal that while VLMs demonstrate some capabilities in time series captioning, they struggle with visual reasoning and often rely on textual priors rather than effectively utilizing visual information. The paper also explores the impact of fine-tuning VLMs on the benchmark, showing improvements in performance. Overall, CATS-Bench provides a valuable resource for the community, offering a more realistic and challenging evaluation framework for time series understanding and reasoning than previous synthetic benchmarks. The authors' analysis of VLM performance underscores the need for further research in multimodal time series analysis and the development of models that can effectively integrate visual and textual information.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most significant contribution is the introduction of CATS-Bench, a comprehensive and diverse benchmark for time series captioning and reasoning. The use of 11 real-world datasets across various domains is a major strength, making the benchmark more realistic and challenging than previous synthetic benchmarks. This diversity ensures that models are evaluated on a wide range of time series patterns and characteristics, enhancing the benchmark's generalizability. The authors' approach to generating high-quality captions through a scalable pipeline is also commendable. The use of an oracle LLM, combined with rigorous validation through factual checks, human indistinguishability studies, and diversity analyses, ensures the reliability of the generated captions. Furthermore, the inclusion of a human-revisited subset, although small, provides a valuable resource for evaluating the quality of model-generated captions. The paper's introduction of tailored evaluation metrics is another notable strength. These metrics go beyond standard linguistic metrics, focusing on numeric fidelity and statistical inference, which are crucial for accurate time series captioning. The benchmark's inclusion of five different tasks, including time series matching, caption matching, plot matching, and time series comparison, provides a comprehensive evaluation framework for VLMs. The authors' thorough benchmarking of several state-of-the-art VLMs is also a significant contribution. The results highlight the strengths and weaknesses of these models in the context of time series data, providing valuable insights for future research. The analysis of the role of the visual modality, which reveals that models often rely on textual priors rather than effectively utilizing visual information, is particularly insightful. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a wide audience.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a valuable contribution, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, while the paper describes the use of diverse real-world datasets, it lacks a detailed analysis of the statistical properties and representativeness of these datasets. As noted by a reviewer, it is unclear how well the benchmark captures the complexity of real-world time series data and whether it adequately covers the range of patterns and characteristics encountered in practice. For instance, the paper does not analyze the presence of seasonality, trend, noise, or other statistical properties of the time series, nor does it discuss the length of the time series segments and their impact on the captioning task. This lack of analysis limits my confidence in the benchmark's ability to generalize to all real-world time series scenarios. Secondly, while the paper acknowledges the use of an oracle LLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) for caption generation, it does not provide a detailed sensitivity analysis of the impact of this choice on the quality of the generated captions. Although the paper mentions refining captions from multiple LLMs in the human-revisited subset, it does not explore how different LLMs might produce different captions, which could affect the results of the evaluation. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the potential biases and limitations introduced by the choice of the oracle LLM. Thirdly, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of generating captions using the proposed pipeline. This is a significant limitation, as the computational cost is a crucial factor for practical applications. The paper does not discuss the time and resources required for each step of the pipeline, such as data preprocessing, caption generation, and evaluation. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the scalability and practicality of the benchmark creation process. Fourthly, the human-revisited subset, while valuable, is relatively small compared to the entire benchmark. As noted by a reviewer, this small size may not be sufficient to ensure the overall quality of the benchmark. While the human-revisited subset provides high-quality references, its limited size raises concerns about its representativeness of the overall benchmark's quality. Fifthly, while the paper includes a section on the quality validation of semi-synthetic captions, it does not provide a dedicated and detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed benchmark. This includes potential biases in the datasets beyond caption accuracy and limitations in the caption generation pipeline. Although the paper discusses some limitations, such as the limited contribution of the visual modality, a more comprehensive analysis of the benchmark's inherent limitations is needed. Finally, while the paper introduces new evaluation metrics, it does not provide a detailed discussion of their limitations. As noted by a reviewer, the paper does not explore how these metrics handle captions that are technically accurate but lack important contextual information, or captions that are verbose but do not add significant value. A more in-depth analysis of the correlation between the automated metrics and human judgments is needed, including a discussion of cases where the metrics disagree with human evaluators. These limitations, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be strengthened to provide a more robust and reliable benchmark.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the paper should include a more thorough analysis of the statistical properties of the time series data used in the benchmark. This analysis should include measures of stationarity, autocorrelation, distribution, seasonality, trend, and noise. The paper should also discuss the length of the time series segments and their impact on the captioning task. This analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the benchmark's representativeness and help identify potential biases or limitations. Secondly, the paper should conduct a sensitivity analysis of the generated captions with respect to different LLMs. This analysis should compare the quality, diversity, and potential biases of the captions generated by different models. The paper should also discuss the potential limitations of using LLMs as oracles, such as the possibility of generating captions that are not factually accurate or that do not accurately reflect the underlying time series data. This analysis would help to better understand the impact of the choice of LLM on the benchmark's results. Thirdly, the paper should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of generating captions using the proposed pipeline. This analysis should include the time and resources required for each step of the pipeline, such as data preprocessing, caption generation, and evaluation. The paper should also discuss the scalability of the pipeline and its ability to handle larger datasets. This analysis would help to assess the practicality and scalability of the benchmark creation process. Fourthly, the authors should expand the human-revisited subset to ensure that it is more representative of the overall benchmark. This would involve increasing the number of human-revised captions and ensuring that they cover a wide range of time series patterns and characteristics. This would improve the reliability of the benchmark and provide a more robust evaluation framework. Fifthly, the paper should include a dedicated section that discusses the limitations of the proposed benchmark in detail. This section should address potential biases in the datasets, limitations in the caption generation pipeline, and the limitations of the evaluation metrics. This would provide a more transparent and comprehensive assessment of the benchmark's strengths and weaknesses. Finally, the paper should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the evaluation metrics used. This discussion should explore how the metrics handle captions that are technically accurate but lack important contextual information, or captions that are verbose but do not add significant value. The paper should also consider the use of additional evaluation metrics that could capture different aspects of caption quality, such as the degree of detail, the clarity of expression, and the relevance to the time series data. These improvements would significantly enhance the robustness and reliability of the benchmark.
