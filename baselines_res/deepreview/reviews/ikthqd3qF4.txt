## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel method for evaluating unsupervised, anonymous record linkage, specifically focusing on scenarios where labeled training data is unavailable. The core contribution lies in deriving observable lower bounds on precision and relative recall, which are crucial metrics for assessing the quality of record linkage. The authors achieve this by leveraging a structural constraint common in many datasets: the limitation on the number of positive outcomes a single individual can have. For instance, in the context of mortgage applications, an individual can only originate one first-lien mortgage. This constraint forms the basis for their evaluation framework. The method involves clustering applications based on a distance function and a threshold, then filtering out clusters with multiple originations, which are considered false positives. The authors apply their method to the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA) dataset to identify loan applicants who submit multiple applications, demonstrating its practical utility. The theoretical framework is supported by a simulation study, although this study simplifies the real-world scenario by only considering clusters of size two. The paper's approach is particularly relevant in situations where privacy concerns or data silos prevent the use of labeled data for training supervised models. By providing a way to evaluate record linkage algorithms without ground truth, the authors address a significant challenge in unsupervised learning. The paper's main empirical finding is the identification of cross-applicants within the HMDA dataset, showcasing the method's ability to detect individuals who submit multiple loan applications. This is a valuable contribution to the field of fair machine learning, as it allows for the analysis of potential biases in lending practices without relying on sensitive personal identifiers. Overall, the paper presents a significant step forward in the evaluation of unsupervised record linkage, offering a principled approach for scenarios where labeled data is scarce or unavailable. The method's reliance on a structural constraint limits its generalizability, and the lack of a comprehensive comparison with existing methods leaves some questions about its relative performance. However, the paper's theoretical contributions and practical application to the HMDA data make it a valuable contribution to the field.
