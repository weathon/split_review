## Summary:

This paper introduces VOLTBench, a novel benchmark designed to systematically evaluate and address the length volatility problem in long-form text generation by large language models (LLMs). The authors identify and define internal patterns of length volatility, such as Attention Collapse and Attention Instability, through a detailed analysis of attention traces. They propose Structural Enforcement via Logits Boosting (SELB), a decoding-stage method that enhances length accuracy and output stability without requiring additional training. The paper demonstrates that SELB significantly reduces length volatility by 69% and improves mean output length by 148% while maintaining generation quality. The benchmark and method are evaluated across a diverse set of tasks, including both structured and unstructured text generation, and support multiple languages. However, the paper's contributions are somewhat limited by the lack of a quantitative analysis of attention patterns, the high difficulty of the benchmark, and the task-dependent effectiveness of SELB. These limitations suggest that while the paper makes valuable contributions, further work is needed to fully understand and address the complexities of long-form generation in LLMs.

## Strengths:

One of the most significant strengths of this paper is the introduction of VOLTBench, a benchmark that systematically quantifies length volatility across diverse tasks, including both structured and unstructured text generation. This benchmark is a valuable contribution to the field, as it provides a standardized way to evaluate and compare the performance of different LLMs in long-form generation. The authors have put considerable effort into designing a benchmark that is both comprehensive and challenging, covering a range of tasks such as story writing, code generation, and mathematical problem-solving. The inclusion of multiple languages, particularly English and Chinese, further enhances the benchmark's utility and relevance in a global context. The paper also provides an in-depth analysis of the internal mechanisms behind length volatility, identifying patterns like Attention Collapse and Attention Instability through attention trace analysis. This analysis offers valuable insights into the challenges of long-form generation and provides a foundation for developing more effective mitigation strategies. The proposed SELB method is another strength, as it is a lightweight, training-free approach that significantly improves length accuracy and stability during long-form generation. The fact that SELB can be applied without additional training makes it a practical solution for enhancing the performance of existing LLMs. The empirical results, which show a 69% reduction in length volatility and a 148% improvement in mean output length, are impressive and demonstrate the method's effectiveness. The paper's focus on both the theoretical and practical aspects of length volatility, combined with its empirical validation, makes it a well-rounded and impactful contribution to the field of LLM research.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and concerns are evident. Firstly, the internal mechanisms behind length volatility, specifically Attention Collapse and Attention Instability, are not well-supported by empirical evidence. The authors describe these patterns qualitatively, but there is a lack of quantitative measures to validate their claims. For instance, the paper does not provide a formal definition of what constitutes 'collapsed' attention or how it differs from normal attention behavior during long sequence generation. A more rigorous analysis, such as quantifying the entropy or variance of attention weights over time, would strengthen the paper's theoretical foundation. The absence of such measures makes it difficult to assess the true nature of these attention patterns and their impact on length volatility. Secondly, the proposed benchmark appears to be too difficult for current LLMs, even for specialized models like LongWriter. The low quality scores and high volatility observed in LongWriter's performance suggest that the benchmark might be testing the limits of model capabilities rather than isolating length volatility. This raises concerns about the practical relevance of the benchmark and whether it truly measures the intended construct. The paper acknowledges a 'quality-length trade-off' in LongWriter's performance, which supports the reviewer's concern that the benchmark might be measuring a combination of factors, not just length volatility. A more detailed analysis of the benchmark's difficulty and its impact on different models would be beneficial. Thirdly, the proposed SELB method relies on predefined section titles, which limits its applicability in more general scenarios. The core mechanism of SELB's structural enforcement involves boosting logits for tokens corresponding to the next section title, making it less suitable for tasks where the structure is not explicitly defined or where the user does not have control over the section titles. This limitation is particularly evident in the paper's focus on chapter-based structured tasks, and the lack of a detailed exploration of SELB's performance on tasks with varying levels of logical complexity or dynamic structural requirements. The paper does mention a 'SELB-Hybrid strategy' in Appendix I, which attempts to address this limitation, but the main text does not provide a comprehensive evaluation of this approach. Additionally, the paper could benefit from a more extensive comparison with other existing methods for long-form generation, particularly those that also focus on improving output stability. While the paper compares SELB against several strong baseline models and common decoding strategies, it lacks a direct comparison with methods that employ different strategies, such as reinforcement learning or adversarial training. This makes it challenging to fully assess SELB's novelty and relative strengths and weaknesses. Finally, the generalization ability of SELB needs further validation, especially on tasks or datasets not included in the VOLTBench. The evaluation primarily focuses on the VOLTBench dataset, and it is unclear how well SELB would generalize to other datasets with different characteristics, such as different domain-specific vocabularies or reasoning requirements. The paper should include experiments on a wider range of datasets to demonstrate the robustness and generalizability of SELB. These limitations, while not undermining the paper's overall contributions, highlight areas where further research and validation are necessary.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified limitations, several concrete and actionable improvements can be made. Firstly, the authors should provide a more rigorous definition and analysis of 'attention collapse' and 'attention instability.' This should include a quantitative measure of attention patterns, such as the entropy or variance of attention weights, and demonstrate how these measures correlate with the observed length volatility. For example, the authors could track the attention weights of specific tokens related to length constraints and show how their attention patterns change during the generation process. Visualizing the attention weights against the generated tokens would also help to illustrate the correlation and provide a more concrete basis for their claims. The analysis should consider the impact of different attention heads and layers, as some heads might be more sensitive to length constraints than others. Secondly, to address the issue of the benchmark's difficulty, the authors should consider creating a series of benchmarks with varying levels of difficulty. This would allow for a more nuanced evaluation of LLMs' length volatility at different scales and help to isolate the length volatility problem from other limitations of current models. For example, the authors could create a benchmark with shorter sequences and fewer sections to test the models' basic ability to control length, and then gradually increase the difficulty to test the limits of the models. Additionally, a more detailed analysis of the performance of different models on the benchmark, including the types of errors they make and the specific scenarios where they struggle the most, would provide a better understanding of the challenges posed by the benchmark and how it relates to the length volatility problem. The authors should also compare the benchmark to existing long-form generation benchmarks to highlight its unique contributions. Thirdly, to improve the generalizability of the proposed method, the authors should explore ways to adapt SELB to scenarios without predefined section titles. One possible approach is to use the model itself to generate section titles dynamically during the generation process. This could be done by adding a mechanism that prompts the model to generate a section title after a certain number of tokens or when a specific condition is met. The authors could also explore the use of reinforcement learning to train the model to generate section titles that are optimal for controlling length volatility. Furthermore, a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed method and potential future directions for research would help to clarify its scope and practical applications. To better position SELB within the broader landscape of long-form generation techniques, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods. This comparison should not be limited to methods that focus solely on output stability but should also include methods that employ different strategies for long-form generation, such as reinforcement learning or adversarial training. The comparison should include a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, highlighting the specific scenarios where SELB outperforms or underperforms compared to other approaches. This would provide a more complete understanding of SELB's relative advantages and limitations. Finally, to validate the generalization ability of SELB, the authors should evaluate its performance on a wider range of datasets and tasks beyond the VOLTBench. This should include datasets with different characteristics, such as different domain-specific vocabularies, different types of reasoning requirements, and different output structures. For example, the authors could evaluate SELB on datasets from different domains, such as scientific writing, legal documents, or creative writing. The evaluation should also include a discussion of the specific challenges posed by each dataset and how SELB performs in these different contexts. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's generalization ability and help identify potential areas for improvement. The authors should also consider exploring the use of transfer learning techniques to adapt SELB to new datasets and tasks.


## Questions:

1. How does SELB perform on tasks with highly dynamic or unpredictable structures, such as those requiring multi-step logical inference or the generation of complex, nested data structures? The current evaluation primarily focuses on chapter-based structured tasks, and it would be valuable to understand SELB's effectiveness in more challenging scenarios.
2. Can the authors provide more insights into how different models exhibit Attention Collapse and Attention Instability? Are there specific model architectures or training methods that make models more susceptible to these patterns? Understanding the underlying causes of these attention patterns could help in developing more targeted mitigation strategies.
3. How does SELB impact the computational efficiency of the generation process? Are there any trade-offs in terms of speed or resource consumption? This is an important factor for practical applications, and a detailed analysis would help to assess the method's feasibility in real-world settings.
4. What is the internal mechanism behind the length volatility? Why does the attention trace help to mitigate the length volatility? The paper provides a qualitative description of attention patterns, but a more detailed explanation of the underlying mechanisms would be beneficial.
5. How does the proposed method affect the quality of the generated responses? Does the proposed method help to improve the quality of the generated responses, or does it primarily focus on length accuracy and stability? A more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between length control and generation quality would provide a more comprehensive understanding of SELB's impact.
