## Summary:

This paper introduces a self-supervised learning framework for EEG-based dementia classification, leveraging a multi-band approach to process EEG signals. The core idea revolves around decomposing EEG signals into five standard frequency bands—delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma—and processing each band independently through dedicated CNN encoders. This multi-band processing is coupled with a multi-head SimCLR architecture, where each frequency band is associated with its own projection head. The authors also incorporate an adaptive temperature parameter within the NT-Xent loss function, aiming to account for the varying learning difficulties and distribution properties of different frequency bands. The proposed method, termed DGNet, is evaluated on a single publicly available dataset, where it demonstrates improved performance compared to several benchmark models, including both supervised and self-supervised approaches. The authors report a 31.5% relative performance improvement over training from scratch and a 25.4% improvement over a single-head approach. The empirical results suggest that the multi-band approach, combined with the adaptive temperature parameter, can enhance the model's ability to learn discriminative representations for dementia classification. The paper's primary contribution lies in the application of a multi-head self-supervised learning framework to multi-band EEG data, aiming to capture more fine-grained and meaningful features associated with dementia. While the results are promising, the paper's methodological novelty and experimental evaluation have several limitations that warrant further investigation. The authors' approach, while practically motivated, lacks a strong theoretical foundation and does not introduce novel algorithmic components or address fundamental limitations of existing self-supervised learning approaches. The adaptive temperature parameter, while presented as a key contribution, is essentially a dynamic adjustment of a existing parameter within the NT-Xent loss, and its theoretical justification is not thoroughly explored. The experimental evaluation is limited to a single dataset, which raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method. The authors do not provide any evidence that the method would perform well on other EEG datasets, particularly those with different characteristics or task requirements. The lack of evaluation on diverse datasets makes it difficult to assess the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed approach. The authors also do not compare their method against recent state-of-the-art methods in self-supervised learning for EEG analysis, such as those based on contrastive learning or generative modeling. The comparison is limited to older supervised learning methods, which does not provide a fair assessment of the proposed method's performance relative to current best practices. The absence of a comparison with relevant SSL techniques makes it difficult to determine the true contribution of the proposed method. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a valuable exploration of multi-band EEG processing within a self-supervised learning framework, highlighting the potential of this approach for dementia classification.

## Strengths:

The paper's primary strength lies in its exploration of a multi-band approach for EEG signal processing within a self-supervised learning framework. The idea of decomposing EEG signals into five standard frequency bands and processing each band independently is intuitively appealing, as different frequency bands are known to carry distinct neurophysiological information. This approach allows the model to potentially capture more fine-grained and meaningful features associated with dementia, which is a significant challenge in EEG analysis. The authors' implementation of this idea, using independent CNN encoders and projection heads for each frequency band, is a practical and effective way to realize this multi-band processing. The empirical results presented in the paper demonstrate that the proposed method, DGNet, achieves improved performance compared to several benchmark models, including both supervised and self-supervised approaches. Specifically, the authors report a 31.5% relative performance improvement over training from scratch and a 25.4% improvement over a single-head approach. These results suggest that the multi-band approach, combined with the adaptive temperature parameter, can enhance the model's ability to learn discriminative representations for dementia classification. The ablation study, while not statistically significant, does show a trend of performance decrease when components are removed, suggesting their contribution. The authors also provide a clear description of their methodology, making it easy to understand the proposed approach. The paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the methodology and results. The authors have conducted a comprehensive set of experiments, including comparisons with major benchmark models, leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, and ablation studies. This thorough evaluation helps to demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed model. The paper's focus on a practically relevant problem, dementia classification, and the exploration of a multi-band approach within a self-supervised learning framework, is a valuable contribution to the field. The authors' work highlights the potential of this approach for improving the performance of EEG-based dementia classification systems.


## Weaknesses:

My analysis reveals several significant weaknesses in this paper, primarily concerning the methodological novelty, theoretical justification, experimental evaluation, and comparison with existing methods. First, the methodological novelty of the proposed approach is limited. The core of the method is an adaptation of the SimCLR framework, a well-established self-supervised learning technique. While the authors introduce a multi-head architecture to process multiple frequency bands, this is essentially a straightforward extension of the existing SimCLR framework. The use of independent CNN encoders and adaptive temperature parameters for each frequency band, while practically motivated, does not represent a significant theoretical or technical advancement. The paper itself acknowledges the adaptation of SimCLR, stating, "Our implementation of SimCLR, as shown in Figure 1(a), is specifically tailored for EEG signals. This advanced adaptation applies the original image-based SimCLR framework to neural signal analysis." This lack of fundamental innovation raises concerns about the incremental contribution of this work. Second, the paper lacks a rigorous theoretical analysis explaining why the adaptive multi-band approach should outperform existing methods. The justification for the adaptive temperature parameter is particularly weak, lacking a clear connection to the underlying optimization landscape or convergence properties. The paper does not offer any formal proofs or theoretical guarantees regarding the convergence or generalization capabilities of the proposed method, relying solely on empirical results. The authors describe the adaptive temperature parameter as accounting for the "unique distribution properties and learning difficulty of different frequency bands," but this is an intuitive explanation rather than a mathematically rigorous justification. The absence of a theoretical framework makes it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms that drive the performance improvements observed in the experiments. Third, the ablation study does not provide sufficient evidence to support the effectiveness of the proposed components. The performance gains observed when comparing the full model to the ablated versions are marginal, and it is unclear whether these gains are statistically significant or simply due to random variations in the training process. For example, the difference in accuracy between the full model and the single-head model is only 19.38%, which may not be substantial enough to justify the added complexity of the multi-head architecture. Furthermore, the ablation study lacks a systematic exploration of the parameter space, and the reported results do not convincingly demonstrate the necessity of each component. The paper does not explicitly mention statistical significance testing for the ablation study results, which further weakens the conclusions drawn from this analysis. Fourth, the experimental evaluation is limited to a single dataset, which raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method. The authors do not provide any evidence that the method would perform well on other EEG datasets, particularly those with different characteristics or task requirements. The lack of evaluation on diverse datasets makes it difficult to assess the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed approach. The paper explicitly states the use of only one dataset for evaluation: "The dataset (Miltiadou et al., 2023b) used in this study consists of resting-state, eyes-closed EEG recordings from a total of 88 participants." This limitation significantly restricts the scope of the conclusions that can be drawn from the study. Finally, the authors do not compare their method against recent state-of-the-art methods in self-supervised learning for EEG analysis, such as those based on contrastive learning or generative modeling. The comparison is limited to older supervised learning methods, which does not provide a fair assessment of the proposed method's performance relative to current best practices. The absence of a comparison with relevant SSL techniques makes it difficult to determine the true contribution of the proposed method. Table 1, which lists the baseline models used for comparison, primarily includes supervised learning baselines, and there is a lack of comparison with recent, relevant self-supervised learning methods for EEG. These weaknesses collectively undermine the strength of the paper's claims and highlight the need for further investigation and validation of the proposed method.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the authors should focus on enhancing the methodological novelty of their approach. Instead of merely adapting existing techniques, they should explore more innovative ways to leverage the multi-band nature of EEG signals. This could involve developing novel contrastive learning objectives that explicitly encourage the model to learn relationships between different frequency bands, or exploring the use of attention mechanisms to dynamically weigh the contributions of different bands based on the task at hand. The authors could also investigate the use of more sophisticated neural network architectures that are specifically designed to handle multi-frequency data, rather than relying on independent CNN encoders. Second, the authors should provide a more rigorous theoretical analysis of their proposed method. This should include a mathematical justification for the adaptive temperature parameter, explaining how it affects the optimization landscape and convergence properties of the model. The authors should also explore the generalization capabilities of their method, providing theoretical guarantees or bounds on its performance. This could involve analyzing the properties of the learned representations, such as their dimensionality and separability, and relating these properties to the model's generalization ability. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough ablation study, including statistical significance testing and a systematic exploration of the parameter space. The ablation study should not only remove components but also explore variations within each component. For example, the authors could investigate the impact of different numbers of frequency bands, different types of frequency band decompositions, and different adaptive temperature parameter settings. The results of the ablation study should be presented with statistical significance values, such as p-values, to provide a more robust assessment of the effectiveness of each component. Fourth, the authors should evaluate their method on multiple diverse EEG datasets, including datasets with different characteristics and task requirements. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the generalizability and robustness of the proposed method. The authors should also compare their method against recent state-of-the-art self-supervised learning methods for EEG analysis, including those based on contrastive learning and generative modeling. This would provide a more accurate assessment of the proposed method's performance relative to current best practices. The comparison should include a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, highlighting the specific scenarios where the proposed method performs better or worse than existing approaches. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the learned representations, visualizing the feature space and examining the contribution of each frequency band to the final classification performance. This could involve techniques such as t-SNE or UMAP for visualizing the feature space, and ablation studies that selectively remove or mask different frequency bands to assess their individual contributions. This analysis would provide a deeper understanding of the model's behavior and the underlying mechanisms that drive its performance. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly strengthen their work and make a more substantial contribution to the field of EEG-based dementia classification.
