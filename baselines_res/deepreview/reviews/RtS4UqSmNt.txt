## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel framework for studying controlled social learning, where an information-mediating planner, such as a large language model (LLM), strategically influences the precision of signals received by agents. The core contribution lies in integrating a dynamic control problem for a centralized information planner with the mechanism of sequential social learning. The model considers two types of planners: an altruistic planner aiming to maximize social welfare and a biased planner seeking to induce a specific action, regardless of the true state. The paper employs a dynamic programming approach to characterize the optimal policies for both types of planners, demonstrating how they can strategically influence public opinion and social welfare by adjusting the precision of information. The analysis reveals that the optimal planner operates in different modes depending on the range of belief values, including investing maximum resources, not investing at all, or adjusting investment based on the belief. Notably, the biased planner may even intentionally obfuscate agents' signals in certain belief ranges. The paper also presents empirical simulations using LLMs, where LLMs act as both planners and agents. These simulations show that LLM-based planners exhibit strategic behavior that aligns with the theoretical predictions, despite the non-Bayesian nature of LLM agents. The results demonstrate that a planner that accounts for social learning can dramatically influence public opinion and social welfare, far more than a myopic one. The paper's findings highlight the potential for strategic information control to significantly impact social outcomes, both positively and negatively. The authors also show that even under stringent transparency constraints, information mediation can substantially shift social welfare in either direction. The paper's approach is significant because it addresses the increasing role of LLMs in information dissemination and the potential for these models to be used as strategic information mediators. The combination of theoretical analysis and empirical validation using LLMs provides a valuable contribution to the understanding of strategic information control in social learning environments. The paper's exploration of both altruistic and biased planners offers a nuanced perspective on the potential motivations and impacts of information mediators. The findings suggest that the strategic behavior of LLM-based planners is robust to non-Bayesian agent behavior, which is a significant insight given the known limitations of Bayesian reasoning in both humans and LLMs. Overall, this work provides a solid foundation for further research into the strategic control of information in social learning settings, particularly in the context of increasingly prevalent LLM-based information mediators.

As stated in Section 3, the state space is defined as $\omega \in \Omega := \{G, B\}$, and the signals are binary, matching the state with a certain probability. The cost function is defined as a non-negative, increasing, continuous, and concave function, and later specified as a linear function in the experiments. These assumptions, while simplifying the analysis, may not accurately reflect the complexities of real-world social learning scenarios. The binary state and action space, in particular, restricts the complexity of the information environment, potentially overlooking nuances in how agents process and act on information. Furthermore, the assumption of symmetric signals, where the cost of increasing or decreasing precision is the same, is a significant simplification that may not hold in practice, where different types of information manipulation could have varying costs.
