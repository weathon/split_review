## Summary:

This paper introduces PromptArmor, a novel defense mechanism against prompt injection attacks, leveraging the reasoning capabilities of modern large language models (LLMs). The core idea is to employ a separate LLM, termed the 'guardrail' LLM, to detect and remove malicious prompts before they reach the primary LLM. This approach contrasts with previous methods that often relied on fine-tuning or other techniques. The authors demonstrate that by carefully designing a system prompt, PromptArmor can effectively identify and sanitize injected prompts, achieving low false positive and negative rates across multiple benchmarks. The paper's methodology involves prompting the guardrail LLM to analyze input text and identify any instructions that deviate from the intended task. If an injected prompt is detected, the guardrail LLM removes it before passing the sanitized input to the backend LLM. The authors conduct a comprehensive evaluation of PromptArmor using several benchmark datasets, including AgentDojo, and compare its performance against seven representative baseline defenses. The results show that PromptArmor significantly reduces the attack success rate while maintaining high utility. Furthermore, the paper explores the impact of different prompting strategies, model size, and adaptive attacks on PromptArmor's performance. The experiments reveal that the effectiveness of PromptArmor is strongly correlated with the reasoning capabilities of the guardrail LLM, with more powerful models like GPT-4 demonstrating superior detection accuracy. The paper also investigates the robustness of PromptArmor against adaptive attacks generated by the AgentVigil red-teaming tool, showing that the defense mechanism remains effective even against these more sophisticated attacks. The authors also explore the impact of model size and reasoning capabilities by evaluating Qwen3 models of varying sizes. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution by demonstrating the potential of using off-the-shelf LLMs as a reliable defense against prompt injection attacks, challenging previous findings that were based on older, less capable models. The findings suggest that prompting a strong, off-the-shelf LLM should now be regarded as a standard baseline for evaluating defenses against prompt injection, and the paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of this approach.

While the paper mentions the computational efficiency of using pre-trained LLMs, it does not provide any quantitative analysis of the latency or resource consumption introduced by the guardrail LLM. This is a critical oversight, as the practical applicability of PromptArmor in resource-constrained environments depends heavily on its computational overhead. The paper does not compare the computational cost of PromptArmor with other defense mechanisms, making it difficult to assess its relative efficiency. This lack of analysis is a major limitation, as it prevents a complete understanding of the trade-offs between detection accuracy and computational cost. My analysis confirms this weakness, as the paper lacks any discussion of inference time, resource usage, or energy consumption.
