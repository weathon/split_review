## Summary:

This paper investigates the role of curriculum learning in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL), framing it as a mechanism for selective data acquisition rather than merely an exploration heuristic. The authors propose that by biasing the training process towards 'underachieved' goals—those that are difficult for the agent to reach—curricula can reshape the state-goal visitation distribution. This, in turn, leads to improved value function approximation and policy success, particularly in challenging regions of the state-goal space. The core methodological approach involves comparing uniform goal sampling with curriculum-guided sampling strategies in a GridWorld environment. The curriculum strategies are manually designed, focusing on goals that are either on the edge of the environment or are empirically harder to reach. The authors employ Universal Value Function Approximators (UVFAs) with potential-based reward shaping to facilitate learning. The main empirical findings indicate that curriculum learning, particularly when focused on edge goals, can improve success rates on these harder goals, while maintaining comparable performance on overall goals. The paper's significance lies in its perspective on curriculum learning as a structured approach to data acquisition, aiming to advance more persistent and open-ended learning agents. By highlighting the impact of curriculum design on the distribution of training data, the authors contribute to a deeper understanding of how curricula can improve learning and generalization in GCRL. However, the paper also acknowledges that the benefits of curricula are not uniform and can sometimes reduce performance on easier goals. The authors' analysis suggests that curriculum learning should be viewed as a tunable mechanism for structuring data acquisition in line with task difficulty and representational limits, rather than a one-size-fits-all exploration strategy. The paper's findings are primarily based on empirical evidence from a GridWorld environment, and the curriculum strategies are manually designed, which limits the generalizability of the results to more complex domains. The performance gains reported are also relatively modest, and the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed curriculum learning approach. Despite these limitations, the paper offers a valuable perspective on the role of curriculum learning in GCRL and provides a foundation for future research in this area.

## Strengths:

This paper presents a compelling perspective on curriculum learning in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL), emphasizing its role as a data acquisition strategy rather than just an exploration technique. This reframing is a significant strength, as it highlights the importance of selectively biasing the state-goal distribution towards challenging or underachieved goals. This perspective offers a deeper understanding of how curricula can improve learning and generalization in GCRL, moving beyond the traditional view of curriculum learning as simply a sequence of tasks from easy to hard. The paper's focus on data selection as a mechanism for curriculum learning is a novel contribution, distinguishing it from traditional exploration-based methods. By explicitly linking curriculum design to the distribution of training data, the authors provide a valuable framework for understanding how curricula can improve function approximation and policy success. The use of Universal Value Function Approximators (UVFAs) and potential-based reward shaping in a GridWorld environment allows for a controlled study of the effects of curriculum learning on goal coverage and success rates. This controlled setting enables a clear analysis of the distributional shifts induced by different curriculum strategies. Furthermore, the paper is well-structured and clearly written, making it easy to follow the authors' arguments and empirical investigations. The authors provide empirical evidence that curricula can improve success rates, particularly on harder edge goals, suggesting that it can be an effective strategy for enhancing performance in challenging regions of the state-goal space. The paper also acknowledges the limitations of the study, which is a sign of intellectual honesty and provides a clear direction for future research. The authors' discussion of the non-uniform benefits of curriculum learning, and the potential for reduced performance on easier goals, is a valuable insight that adds nuance to the understanding of curriculum learning's effects. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel perspective on curriculum learning, its clear methodology, and its empirical findings that support the data acquisition view of curriculum learning in GCRL.


## Weaknesses:

While the paper presents a compelling perspective on curriculum learning, several weaknesses limit the generalizability and impact of its findings. First, the empirical evidence presented is not particularly strong, with the performance gains achieved through curriculum learning being relatively modest. For example, in the 'Edge vs. Interior Curriculum' experiment, the overall success rate for the curriculum-based approach (Curr) was only slightly higher than the uniform sampling baseline (NoCurr) at 0.370 compared to 0.361, respectively, for H=16. Similarly, the 'Curriculum Variants' experiment showed only a small improvement in overall success rate (0.297 for Curr vs 0.276 for NoCurr) and a more substantial, yet still modest, improvement in edge success rate (0.143 for Curr vs 0.060 for NoCurr). These small differences, while statistically significant, raise questions about the practical significance of the curriculum learning approach. The authors themselves acknowledge that the gains are 'modest in absolute terms', which further supports this concern. Second, the experiments are conducted in relatively small GridWorld environments, which may not fully capture the complexities of more realistic scenarios. The use of a simple grid world, while useful for initial exploration, lacks the high-dimensional state spaces and continuous action spaces found in many real-world RL problems. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to more complex domains. The authors explicitly state in the Discussion that 'our curricula remain manually specified, with the edge-interior and weighted sampling schemes serving as simple proxies for more principled strategies.' This reliance on hand-designed curricula limits the applicability of the approach to scenarios where defining a suitable curriculum is non-trivial. The paper does not explore a wide range of curriculum strategies, focusing primarily on simple hand-designed goal distributions. The lack of comparison to more sophisticated curriculum learning methods is a significant limitation. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed curriculum learning approach, which is an important factor for practical applications. The paper does not provide any information on the computational time or resources required for the curriculum learning approach compared to the uniform sampling baseline. Furthermore, the paper acknowledges that the benefits of curricula are not uniform and can sometimes reduce performance on easier goals. As stated in the Discussion, 'the curriculum bias may even reduce performance on goals already well-represented under uniform sampling.' This non-uniformity of benefits raises questions about the overall effectiveness of the proposed curriculum learning approach. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the limitations of the proposed approach and does not compare it with a wide range of existing curriculum learning methods. This makes it difficult to assess the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. The absence of comparisons with other curriculum learning techniques makes it hard to determine whether the proposed method offers any significant advantages over existing alternatives. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear explanation for why the observed performance gains are modest, leaving the reader to speculate about the underlying reasons. The paper focuses on demonstrating the effect of curriculum learning as data acquisition but does not provide a detailed analysis of the factors limiting the magnitude of the performance gains. These weaknesses, all supported by direct evidence from the paper, significantly limit the impact and generalizability of the study's findings. My confidence in these identified limitations is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the authors' own acknowledgements.


## Suggestions:

To strengthen this paper, several improvements are necessary. First, the authors should conduct experiments on more complex environments with higher dimensional state and action spaces. This could involve using benchmark tasks with continuous action spaces, such as robotic manipulation tasks or navigation in complex mazes. Such experiments would provide a better understanding of the generalizability of the proposed curriculum learning approach. It would also be beneficial to compare the proposed curriculum learning strategy with more advanced methods, such as those that dynamically adjust the difficulty of tasks based on the agent's performance. This would help to establish the relative effectiveness of their approach. For example, the authors could compare their method with curriculum learning techniques that use difficulty-based sampling or those that use a teacher agent to guide the learning process. This comparison should include an analysis of the computational cost and sample efficiency of the different methods. Second, the authors should investigate methods for automatically generating or adapting curricula, rather than relying on manually specified ones. This could involve using techniques such as reinforcement learning to learn the curriculum itself, or employing techniques such as active learning to select the most informative goals for training. For example, a meta-learning approach could be used to learn a curriculum generation policy that can be applied to different environments. This would make the approach more scalable and adaptable to a wider range of tasks. Additionally, it would be beneficial to investigate how different curriculum design strategies affect the learning process and the final performance of the agent. A systematic comparison of different curriculum design methods would provide valuable insights into the principles of effective curriculum learning. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their curriculum learning method, including the time and resources required for training. This is important for assessing the practicality of the approach. They should also investigate the reasons behind the modest performance gains, perhaps by analyzing the learning curves and the distribution of goals achieved during training. This could provide insights into the limitations of their approach and suggest potential improvements. For example, they could analyze the variance in performance across different seeds to understand the sensitivity of the approach to initialization and other stochastic elements of the training process. Fourth, the authors should consider exploring a wider range of goal distributions, including those that are not hand-designed, to assess the robustness of their findings. For example, they could use a goal generator to create a diverse set of goals and evaluate the performance of their curriculum learning approach on this set. This would help to determine whether the approach is sensitive to the specific choice of goals. Finally, the authors should provide a more thorough discussion of the limitations of their study and suggest directions for future research. This would help to contextualize their findings and guide future work in this area. This discussion should include an analysis of the sensitivity of the approach to different hyperparameters and random seeds. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more compelling case for the effectiveness of their proposed curriculum learning approach.


## Questions:

Several questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how do the observed performance gains from curriculum learning compare to other methods in GCRL, such as reward shaping or exploration bonuses? It would be valuable to see a comparison that includes these alternative approaches to understand the relative effectiveness of the curriculum learning strategy presented in the paper. This comparison should include both quantitative and qualitative analysis, and should consider factors such as the computational cost, the ease of implementation, and the robustness to different environments. Second, the paper focuses on relatively simple goal distributions. How might the findings change with more complex or high-dimensional goal spaces? It would be interesting to see how the curriculum learning approach scales to more challenging environments and whether the benefits are more pronounced in those settings. This is particularly relevant given the limitations of the GridWorld environment used in the study. Third, the paper acknowledges that curriculum-induced distributional shifts can sometimes reduce performance on easier goals. Is there a way to design curricula that improve performance across all subsets of goals, or is this a fundamental trade-off in selective data acquisition? This question is crucial for understanding the limitations of the proposed approach and for designing more effective curriculum learning strategies. Fourth, what are the specific factors that contribute to the modest performance gains observed in the experiments? Is it due to the simplicity of the environment, the manual design of the curricula, or other factors? A more detailed analysis of the learning process could provide insights into the underlying reasons for the limited performance gains. Finally, how does the proposed curriculum learning approach compare with other state-of-the-art curriculum learning methods in terms of performance and computational efficiency? A comprehensive comparison with a wider range of existing curriculum learning techniques is needed to establish the novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach. These questions highlight key uncertainties and areas for further investigation, and addressing them would significantly enhance the paper's contribution to the field of GCRL.
