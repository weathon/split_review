## Summary:

This paper introduces a novel score-based method, termed XBIC, for causal discovery in purely discrete data. The core contribution of this work lies in the enhancement of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) by incorporating edge-specific Shapley values. This approach aims to improve the accuracy of causal structure learning, particularly in resolving edge orientations within Markov equivalence classes, a known challenge in score-based methods. The authors propose training a separate classifier for each variable in the dataset, using all other variables as predictors. These classifiers are then used to compute Shapley values, which quantify the contribution of each predictor variable to the prediction of the target variable. These Shapley values are then integrated into the BIC score, effectively acting as a soft weight on the complexity penalty. The intuition is that edges with higher Shapley values, indicating a stronger influence, will be penalized less, thus favoring directed edges that align with the data-generating mechanisms. The method is evaluated on ten benchmark discrete Bayesian networks, ranging from 6 to 76 variables, and across seven sample-size regimes. The empirical results demonstrate that XBIC consistently outperforms existing methods, such as BIC-HC, PC, and GES, in terms of oriented-edge F1 score and structural Hamming distance. The authors also provide a detailed description of their experimental setup and make their code and data publicly available, which enhances the reproducibility of their results. The paper's significance lies in its creative combination of ideas from causal discovery and explainable AI, offering a potentially more accurate approach to learning causal structures from discrete data. However, as I will detail, the paper also has some notable limitations that warrant further investigation.

## Strengths:

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the core idea of integrating Shapley values into the BIC framework is both novel and insightful. This approach effectively bridges the gap between causal discovery and explainable AI, offering a unique perspective on how to leverage feature attributions for causal structure learning. The use of Shapley values as a soft weight on the BIC's complexity term is a clever way to incorporate directional information into the score, which is a significant improvement over traditional score-based methods that often struggle with resolving edge orientations within Markov equivalence classes. The method is clearly described, and the authors provide a detailed explanation of how the per-node classifiers are trained and how the Shapley values are computed and integrated into the BIC score. This clarity makes the paper easy to follow and understand. Furthermore, the empirical evaluation is thorough and well-executed. The authors evaluate their method on a diverse set of benchmark networks and across a range of sample sizes, demonstrating the robustness and effectiveness of XBIC. The results consistently show that XBIC outperforms existing methods in terms of oriented-edge F1 score and structural Hamming distance. The inclusion of a runtime comparison, while highlighting a weakness, also provides a transparent view of the computational cost associated with the method. Finally, the authors' commitment to reproducibility is commendable. The public availability of their code and data allows other researchers to easily replicate their results and build upon their work. This level of transparency is crucial for advancing the field of causal discovery. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field, offering a promising new approach to learning causal structures from discrete data.


## Weaknesses:

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation, and one that is consistently highlighted by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. Specifically, the paper does not provide a formal proof of consistency, which is a crucial aspect of any causal discovery method. It remains unclear under what conditions the proposed method will converge to the true causal graph as the sample size increases. While the authors include a 'Consistency remark' section (Section 3), this discussion only addresses the asymptotic behavior of the penalty term and does not constitute a full theoretical analysis of the method's consistency in recovering the true causal structure. The paper lacks a rigorous analysis of how the Shapley values interact with the BIC score in terms of asymptotic behavior, and it does not provide any guarantees that the method will converge to the true causal graph. This absence of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the method and to understand the conditions under which it can be expected to perform well. The authors themselves acknowledge this limitation in the 'Limitations and future work' section, stating that 'formal analysis of the weighting mechanism (e.g., bounds and convergence properties) is an important direction.' This lack of theoretical analysis is a major weakness that significantly impacts the paper's overall contribution. Another significant weakness is the high computational cost associated with the proposed method. As the reviewers have pointed out, the need to train a separate classifier for each variable and then compute Shapley values for each edge introduces a substantial computational bottleneck. The paper acknowledges this in the 'Computational considerations' section (Section 3), stating that 'Training M XGBoost models costs O(M * T(N, M)), where T is the learner’s training time.' The runtime comparison in Table 5 further confirms that XBIC is significantly slower than BIC and PC. For example, on the 'Asia' network, BIC takes 0.39 seconds, PC takes 0.09 seconds, and XBIC (w=2) takes 74.78 seconds. This high computational cost limits the applicability of the method to large-scale problems, and it raises concerns about the scalability of the method to real-world datasets with hundreds or thousands of variables. While the authors mention potential optimizations in the 'Limitations and future work' section, such as using faster base learners and approximate Shapley value calculations, a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity and potential strategies for reducing the computational burden is needed. Furthermore, the paper's reliance on simulated data is another limitation. While the authors use benchmark networks from the bnlearn repository, these networks are still synthetic and may not fully capture the complexities of real-world scenarios. The paper should include experiments on real-world datasets to demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed method. The experimental results, while promising, are limited to a specific set of benchmark networks and sample sizes, and it is unclear how the method would perform on different types of data or under different conditions. Finally, while the authors do explore the sensitivity of the method to the weight parameter w, the paper does not provide a clear guideline on how to select the confidence threshold used to filter out low-confidence predictions. The authors mention that varying the threshold between 0.7 and 0.95 changed downstream F1 score by less than 1% on average, but they do not provide a detailed analysis of the trade-offs or a principled way to choose the optimal threshold. This lack of a clear selection guideline makes it difficult to apply the method in practice and raises concerns about the robustness of the method to different threshold values. In summary, the lack of theoretical analysis, the high computational cost, the reliance on simulated data, and the absence of a clear guideline for selecting the confidence threshold are all significant weaknesses that need to be addressed in future work.


## Suggestions:

To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable recommendations. First and foremost, the authors should prioritize a rigorous theoretical analysis of the proposed method. This analysis should include a formal proof of consistency, demonstrating that the method converges to the true causal graph as the sample size increases. The analysis should also investigate the conditions under which the Shapley values accurately reflect the causal relationships and how the proposed scoring function guarantees convergence to the true causal graph. This theoretical analysis would provide a solid foundation for the method and increase its credibility. Second, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the method. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time complexity for each step, such as training the per-node classifiers and computing the Shapley values. It would be beneficial to provide a theoretical analysis of the computational cost, perhaps in terms of the number of variables, the size of the graph, and the sample size. Furthermore, the authors should explore potential strategies for reducing the computational cost, such as using more efficient algorithms for training the classifiers or approximating the Shapley values. For example, instead of training a separate classifier for each node, one could explore methods that share information across nodes, or use ensemble methods to reduce the variance of the estimates. Additionally, the authors could investigate techniques for approximating the Shapley values, such as using sampling methods or closed-form approximations, which could significantly reduce the computational burden. Third, the authors should expand their experimental evaluation to include real-world datasets. These datasets should represent different domains and complexities to demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed method. The experiments should also include a more comprehensive comparison with existing state-of-the-art score-based methods, such as those based on different scoring functions or search strategies. This comparison should include a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, as well as a discussion of the conditions under which the proposed method performs best. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the method to the confidence threshold. This analysis should include a systematic exploration of how different threshold values affect the precision and recall of the learned causal structure. The authors should also provide clear guidelines on how to select an appropriate threshold value based on the characteristics of the data and the desired trade-off between precision and recall. This could involve developing a heuristic or a data-driven approach for threshold selection. Additionally, the paper should discuss the potential limitations of using a fixed threshold across all variables and explore alternative approaches, such as adaptive thresholding, that could improve the robustness of the method. Finally, the authors should provide more details on the practical implementation of the method. This should include a discussion of the hyperparameter settings, and how these settings affect the performance of the method. The authors should also provide guidelines for choosing the appropriate base learner for the per-node classifiers. It would be helpful to include a sensitivity analysis of the method to different choices of hyperparameters and base learners. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of the method and suggest potential directions for future research. These recommendations, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.
