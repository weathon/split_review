## Summary

This paper introduces **Ambig-SWE**, a benchmark built from SWE-Bench Verified that pairs fully-specified GitHub issues with GPT-4o-generated underspecified counterparts. Using this paired design, the authors systematically evaluate six LLM-based coding agents on three capabilities: (1) detecting when instructions are underspecified, (2) asking targeted clarification questions via interaction with a simulated user proxy, and (3) leveraging that interaction to improve task completion. The study finds that interactive agents can recover substantial performance lost to underspecification (up to ~76% of the gap to full specification), that most models default to non-interactive behavior even with severe information deficits, and that question quality—not quantity—drives effective information acquisition.

## Strengths

- **Controlled causal evaluation of underspecification.** By constructing paired fully-specified and underspecified versions of the same SWE-Bench Verified issues, the paper enables direct measurement of how missing information degrades agent performance and how interaction recovers it. This paired design is genuinely clever and avoids the confounds that would arise from using naturally underspecified issues that lack ground-truth specifications (Section 2.1).

- **Structured three-stage evaluation framework.** Decomposing the problem into detection (RQ2), question quality (RQ3), and overall leverage (RQ1) provides a reusable blueprint for diagnosing specific weaknesses in agentic systems, going beyond monolithic resolve-rate metrics (Sections 3–5). The framework generates actionable, stage-specific findings rather than a single aggregate score.

- **Significant and quantified performance recovery via interaction.** The experiments (Figure 3, Table 4) demonstrate statistically significant improvements from Hidden to Interaction settings across all models, with Claude Sonnet 4 recovering ~76% of the gap to Full performance. The paper quantifies these gains both absolutely and relative to the fully-specified ceiling.

- **Clear evidence of detection failures.** Table 2 compellingly demonstrates that most models struggle to distinguish well-specified from underspecified instructions. Qwen 3 Coder's 100% false-negative rate across all prompting conditions is a striking finding, and the paper's use of FPR/FNR alongside accuracy properly contextualizes the results.

- **Multi-model comparison revealing divergent failure modes.** The inclusion of both proprietary (Claude family) and open-weight (Llama 3.1, Deepseek-v2, Qwen 3 Coder) models surfaces distinct behavioral patterns—Claude's exploration-first strategies, Qwen's rigid non-interactivity, Deepseek's counterintuitive prompt sensitivity—that a single-model study could not have revealed.

- **Actionable analysis of question strategies.** The qualitative analysis in Section 5.3 identifies three dimensions of effective questioning (quantity, exploration efficiency, answerability) and demonstrates through Figure 4 that Claude Sonnet models achieve comparable information gain to Qwen 3 Coder with ~50% fewer questions by exploring the codebase before asking.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Synthetic underspecification limits external validity.** The underspecified issues are generated by GPT-4o summarization that "aggressively" strips concrete technical details (code snippets, error messages, file references) from the original issues. The paper's own distributional analysis (Section 2.1) confirms that natural underspecified issues retain more of these details than the synthetic versions. While the paper acknowledges this and argues that the paired design necessitates synthetic construction, the systematic removal of all detailed cues creates inputs that may be unrealistically impoverished compared to real-world underspecified tasks, where partial stack traces, error messages, or file hints are common. This affects how transferable findings about detection difficulty and question strategies are to practice. This concern is partially mitigated by the paper's transparency about the issue and its reasonable justification for the synthetic approach (the need for paired ground truth), but it remains the most significant limitation of the work.

- **"Interaction yields no efficiency gains" claim is under-analyzed.** The paper states that interaction does not yield efficiency gains because step counts do not decrease (Section 3.2), and generalizes this to "current training practices insufficiently optimize for effective integration of interactive feedback" (Section 7). However, the paper's own setup counts interaction turns as steps, so the observation that step counts stay the same or increase is partly a design artifact. The paper does not analyze whether *post-interaction* solving steps decrease (which would indicate efficiency gains from better information), making the efficiency claim weaker than the effectiveness claims.

### Minor

- **Question quality metrics are noisy proxies.** Cosine distance between text embeddings (text-embedding-3-small) and LLM-as-judge scores (Figures 5–6) are used to quantify information gain and question quality. Cosine distance captures broad semantic similarity rather than task-specific detail recovery, and the LLM-as-judge scores show ceiling effects (medians ≈4.5/5) that limit fine-grained comparison. The paper acknowledges the embedding limitation in Section 7 but the claims about question efficiency (e.g., "Claude Sonnet 4 achieves comparable information gain with 50% fewer questions") rest partly on these noisy measures. The qualitative analysis in Figure 4 provides stronger support.

- **Speculative claims about model behavior.** Several claims are flagged with hedges ("likely due to," "potentially inflate") but lack supporting evidence within the paper—for example, the attribution of Qwen 3 Coder's high Hidden performance to "data leakage" and "internal knowledge" (Section 3.2), and the characterization of Qwen's behavior as "rigid protocol-following" (Section 5.3). These interpretations are plausible but would benefit from more direct evidence or at least stronger caveating.

- **Detection experiment is binary but real-world detection is graded.** The detection experiment (Section 4) treats underspecification as binary—an issue is either fully specified or underspecified. Real-world tasks exist on a spectrum of specificity, and the paper does not examine whether models can assess *degree* of underspecification or calibrate their interaction likelihood accordingly.

### Trivial

- The 74% figure in the abstract would benefit from brief in-line contextualization (e.g., "up to 74% relative improvement over non-interactive underspecified baselines") to prevent misinterpretation as absolute recovery.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An **oracle condition** where the full issue is injected without interaction after a fixed number of steps would cleanly separate information acquisition skill from the mere availability of information, strengthening the claims about interaction quality.

- A **human validation study** of the synthetic underspecification quality (even small-scale, e.g., asking 3–5 developers whether the generated summaries feel like realistic incomplete bug reports) would substantially strengthen the generalizability argument.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, a genuinely novel insight emerges from the cross-model comparison: the observation that Qwen 3 Coder, a model competitive with Claude Sonnet 4 on standard SWE-Bench, is completely non-interactive (100% FNR across all prompt variants in Table 2) despite strong underlying coding ability. This reveals that current training paradigms can produce models that are simultaneously highly capable at solving well-specified tasks and fundamentally incapable of recognizing when they lack critical information—a dissociation that would be invisible in standard single-turn benchmarks. This finding has direct implications for how the field should evaluate and train agentic models.

## Suggestions

- The paper would benefit from a small-scale human validation of the synthetic underspecification: have 3–5 developers rate a sample of generated summaries for realism, and report agreement. This would directly address the most significant limitation without requiring a full benchmark redesign.

- Add a brief analysis of post-interaction step counts (steps after the last interaction turn) to strengthen or appropriately qualify the efficiency claim. If post-interaction solving is actually more efficient, the paper's narrative should reflect that.

- Clarify in the abstract that the 74% figure refers to relative improvement over the non-interactive underspecified baseline, to prevent misinterpretation.
