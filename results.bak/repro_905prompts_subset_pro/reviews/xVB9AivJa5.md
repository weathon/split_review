## Summary

Blueprint-Bench introduces a novel benchmark for evaluating spatial intelligence in AI systems: given ~20 interior photographs of an apartment, models must generate a 2D floor plan with correct room connectivity and size rankings. The benchmark evaluates 12 systems across LLMs (via SVG generation), image generation models, and coding agents, using a deterministic classical-CV scoring pipeline. Results show all models substantially below human performance, with the best models scoring ~0.42–0.45 vs. human performance of ~0.55 on a common subset. The paper identifies this as a spatial reasoning blind spot in current generalist AI systems.

## Strengths

- **Genuinely novel and well-motivated task**: The photograph-to-floor-plan task is creative — it uses an input modality (photos) well within training distributions while demanding spatial reasoning that current models demonstrably lack. Unlike many benchmarks that repackage existing tasks, this one tests a capability not well-covered by prior work.
- **Deterministic, reproducible scoring**: The benchmark uses classical computer vision (HSV filtering, flood-fill segmentation, connectivity graph extraction) rather than LLM-based judging, which the authors found unreliable for floor plans (Section 2.4). This makes scoring reproducible and avoids the confound of LLM judging biases.
- **Breadth of evaluation across architectures**: 12 systems spanning LLMs, image generation models, and coding agents are evaluated on the same task, enabling cross-architecture comparison. The inclusion of a human baseline provides a meaningful upper-bound reference.
- **Honest limitations discussion**: Section 2.4 explicitly acknowledges the tradeoff between scoring robustness and expressiveness, the failure of LLM-based alternatives for extraction, and the instruction-following confound. This level of candor about design choices is a genuine strength.
- **Concrete qualitative failure analysis**: Figures 6 and 8 provide specific, interpretable examples of model failures — NanoBanana adding furniture, GPT-4o neglecting room dots, Claude Code's flawed iterative refinement — that go beyond aggregated scores.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The "random baseline" is mislabeled and misleading**: The paper calls its baseline "random" throughout (abstract, results, figures), but it is actually generated by models producing floor plans *without any image input* (line 73: "generating typical floor plans using LLMs and image generation models without any image input"). This is the models' prior over apartment layouts, not a random draw. A proper random baseline would use randomly generated connectivity graphs and size rankings. Because the baseline is itself a model output, the central claim that "most models perform at or below a random baseline" is not substantiated — the comparison does not establish a meaningful null hypothesis. The human-model gap (0.547 vs. ~0.45) remains valid evidence of a capability deficit, but the "near-random" framing that drives the paper's narrative is unsupported.
- **Instruction-following confounds spatial reasoning measurement**: The scoring algorithm requires strict compliance with 9 formatting rules (exact pixel colors, line widths, red dot sizes). The paper itself acknowledges (Section 2.4) that models failing to follow these rules may receive scores that reflect poor instruction-following rather than poor spatial reasoning. GPT-4o (score 0.15) and NanoBanana (0.18) are explicitly described as scoring poorly due to rule violations (line 196–198), not necessarily deficient spatial understanding. This means the benchmark cannot cleanly separate spatial intelligence from instruction-following competence, limiting interpretability of the aggregate scores.

### Minor

- **Human baseline is thin**: Only 12 apartments (out of 50) have human ground truth, and the paper refers to "a human" (singular, line 73). No details are provided about inter-rater reliability, participant selection, or whether the human was allowed multiple attempts. While the 0.547 score provides a useful rough upper bound, the evidence is too limited to make precise quantitative comparisons between models and humans.
- **Scoring weights lack sensitivity analysis**: The composite score uses fixed weights (50% edge overlap, 20% degree correlation, 10% density, 10% room count, 5% door count, 5% door orientation) with no ablation or sensitivity analysis. While the highest weights are on the most important spatial properties (connectivity), model rankings could shift under different weightings, and the paper provides no evidence that they would not.
- **Private dataset limits community utility**: The majority of the 50-apartment dataset is kept private to prevent overfitting. While this is a defensible design choice (similar to ARC's hidden test set), it prevents independent inspection of examples, verification of ground truth quality, and local evaluation — limiting the benchmark's utility as an open research tool. The release of code and a sample mitigates but does not fully address this.

### Trivial

- The "Category" column in the Figure 5 table mislabels LLMs as "Image model" (e.g., Claude Opus 4.1, GPT-5 are listed as "Image model"), which appears to be a copy-and-paste error. This does not affect the results but suggests a lack of care in presentation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Decoupling formatting from scoring (e.g., via a more tolerant extraction pipeline or an oracle that ignores formatting failures) would let the benchmark measure spatial understanding more cleanly, especially as models improve.
- A proper random baseline — randomly generated connectivity graphs with plausible room counts scored against ground truth — would establish a genuine null hypothesis.
- An analysis of how often each model produced scorable vs. unscorable outputs would contextualize the mean similarity scores, particularly for models like GPT-4o and NanoBanana that frequently violated formatting rules.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The paper's core insight — that generalist AI models struggle to translate photographs into accurate spatial representations despite being trained on photographic data — is genuinely interesting and well-articulated, but neither review surfaces additional novel observations beyond what the paper itself identifies.

## Suggestions

- **Renaming the baseline**: The paper should rename the baseline from "random baseline" to something accurate like "uninformed generation baseline" or "blind baseline," and clearly explain what it represents. The current terminology is misleading and weakens an otherwise honest paper.
- **Reporting compliance rates**: For each model, report what fraction of outputs were scoreable (i.e., successfully parsed by the extraction algorithm) alongside the mean similarity score. This would separate models that attempted the task but got the layout wrong from those that never produced valid input to the scoring pipeline.
- **Expanding human evaluation**: Even a modest expansion — 2–3 human participants on the same 12 apartments — would provide inter-rater reliability and strengthen the human baseline substantially.
