## Summary

This paper provides the first complete graphical characterization of distributional equivalence for linear non-Gaussian causal models with arbitrary latent variables and cycles. The key theoretical contributions are (i) a new tool called **edge rank constraints** and their duality with path ranks (Theorem 1), (ii) a local graphical criterion for equivalence that decomposes into per-observed-variable checks on children bases (Theorem 2), and (iii) a transformational characterization showing the equivalence class is generated by admissible cycle reversals and edge additions/deletions (Theorem 3). Based on this theory, the authors present glvLiNG, a constraint-based algorithm that recovers models up to equivalence without structural assumptions, serving as a proof of concept.

## Strengths

1. **First complete equivalence characterization for linear non-Gaussian models with latent variables and cycles.** The paper fills a recognized gap in the literature. Prior work required structural assumptions (e.g., measurement models, acyclicity) and could not handle latents and cycles jointly. Theorem 2 gives a concrete graphical criterion that reduces the global equivalence check to a finite set of local conditions. This is the single strongest contribution.

2. **Edge rank constraints and the duality theorem (Theorem 1) provide a genuinely new tool for causal discovery.** The duality between global path ranks and local edge ranks (Section 3.3, Figure 2) is elegant and fills a missing piece in the rank-based toolbox. While the duality is known in matroid theory, its introduction and operationalization for causal discovery is novel and may have broader impact beyond this specific setting.

3. **Transformational characterization (Theorem 3).** The decomposition into cycle reversals (Lemma 6) and edge additions/deletions (Lemma 7) gives an operational, Meek-conjecture–like way to traverse the entire equivalence class. This is both theoretically satisfying and practically useful for generating the equivalence class from data.

4. **Rigorous treatment of irreducibility.** Propositions 1–2 provide a clean graphical condition and reduction procedure (Figure 1) that canonicalize the problem by ruling out trivial unidentifiability, without imposing structural assumptions. This scoping is mathematically sound and well-explained.

5. **Clarity and exposition.** The paper is very well structured, with clear motivation, helpful examples (Example 1, Figure 2, Figure 3), a running analogy to Markov equivalence, and an interactive demo. The logical flow from problem setup → path ranks → edge ranks → graphical criterion → traversal → algorithm is easy to follow.

### Minor
2. **Computational complexity of the Theorem 2 criterion is not analyzed.** Checking `bases_G(L)` and `bases_G(L∪{X_i})` requires evaluating subsets of `children(L ∪ {X_i})`. The paper argues the criterion is "local" because it decomposes per observed variable, but it does not provide a worst-case complexity analysis, nor does it bound the number of subset checks required. For graphs where some vertices have large child sets, this could be prohibitive. A complexity analysis or a polynomial-time verification argument would strengthen the claim of a practical graphical criterion.

3. **Dependence on oracle OICA is acknowledged but its impact is not experimentally explored.** The algorithm assumes access to an oracle OICA estimate of the mixing matrix, which is known to be statistically and computationally challenging in finite samples. The paper honestly discusses this limitation (Section 5, final remarks; Section 6) but does not provide robustness experiments showing how OICA estimation errors affect the recovered equivalence class. The finite-sample experiments mentioned for evaluation aspect (4) address this partially, but the main text does not indicate whether sensitivity to OICA errors was explicitly tested.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting meta-observation emerging from the reviews is that the paper's core theoretical contributions — the edge-rank duality (Theorem 1) and the local decomposition of the equivalence check (Theorem 2) — are structurally independent of the algorithm. The theory shows that distributional equivalence for linear non-Gaussian models with arbitrary latents and cycles reduces to checking bipartite matching conditions on children sets, which is a strikingly clean result given the complexity of the setting. This suggests that the theory could be extended to linear Gaussian models (where path rank constraints are replaced by trek separation constraints) and other parametric settings, as the duality framework (Theorem 1) is graph-theoretic and not tied to non-Gaussianity. The transformational characterization (Theorem 3) further reveals that the equivalence class structure is generated by two simple operations — a surprising result given the combinatorial richness of the examples in the paper (e.g., 872 equivalent digraphs for one 8-vertex configuration). These findings point to a deeper algebraic structure in linear causal models that merits further investigation.
