## Summary

This paper proposes IncentRL, which augments standard RL with a KL-based penalty that aligns predicted outcome distributions $p(o|s,a)$ with preferred distributions $q(o|s)$, weighted by a coefficient $\beta$. The paper claims two contributions: (1) the KL-based incentive shaping itself, and (2) a Bayesian adaptation scheme that treats $\beta$ as a random variable updated online to eliminate manual tuning. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 show sample-efficiency improvements over the same agent with $\beta=0$.

## Strengths

- **Clear sample-efficiency improvements over $\beta=0$ baselines.** On MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8, IncentRL with $\beta=0.01$ achieves 98% success rate vs. 90.5% for the baseline, reducing mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps (Figure 2). On MountainCar, $\beta=0.1$ improves mean goal reaches by 18% over standard DQN (Table 2). On the toy 2-state MDP, all $\beta>0$ values yield large gains over $\beta=0$ (Table 1). These results show that the KL shaping term consistently helps in the tested sparse-reward settings.

- **The KL-based shaping formulation is clearly scoped and easy to understand.** The core equation $r^{\text{total}} = r^{\text{ext}} - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(p\|q)$ is cleanly stated (Section 3.2), and the paper explains the intuition behind aligning predicted and preferred outcomes. The relation to standard RL ($\beta=0$) and to KL-regularized RL (which regularizes policy changes, not outcome distributions) is correctly distinguished.

- **The cognitive/neuroscience motivation (dopamine RPE, Free Energy Principle) is thoughtfully drawn** (Sections 2.3–2.4, 6.3). The paper is honest about where it diverges from FEP (fixed preferences vs. adaptive beliefs), which helps situate the contribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation mechanism — the paper's claimed central novelty — is not described anywhere in the paper.** The abstract calls it "the central novelty"; the contributions list it first; the conclusion repeats it. Yet the paper provides: (a) **no prior distribution** for $\beta$, (b) **no likelihood function** relating $\beta$ to observed data, (c) **no inference mechanism** (MCMC, variational, conjugate update, or otherwise), (d) **no description of what a "round" is or how $\beta$ is sampled from the posterior** and used during action selection, and (e) **no specification of the update schedule** (per-episode? per-step? per-"round"?). The Algorithm section (5.1) lists five generic steps — predict outcomes, specify preferences, compute KL, shape reward, update policy — with no Bayesian update anywhere. The only mention of Bayesian adaptation is in Section 5.4, purely as post-hoc analysis (Figures 3–4, Table 3), with the mechanism left entirely implicit. A central technical contribution that is not specified cannot be evaluated, reproduced, or considered sound. **This is a fatal structural flaw that undermines the paper's primary claimed contribution.**

### Major

- **No comparison against any modern intrinsic motivation or exploration method.** All experiments compare IncentRL only against the *same base algorithm with $\beta=0$* (no KL shaping). There are no baselines against ICM, RND, VIME, curiosity-driven exploration, count-based exploration, or any prior method that also augments rewards with intrinsic signals. The paper claims a new framework for intrinsic motivation, but provides no evidence that it is competitive with — or different from — existing intrinsic motivation methods. On MountainCar, the baseline is simply DQN; on MiniGrid, it is DQN with $\beta=0$. This makes it impossible to assess whether IncentRL's KL shaping adds value beyond what existing methods already provide.

- **The empirical evaluation is underpowered and uses too few seeds.** MountainCar and MiniGrid results are averaged over only 3 seeds (Tables 2, Figure 2). Given the known high variance in these environments (e.g., MiniGrid with stochastic exploration), 3 seeds are insufficient to draw reliable conclusions. The MiniGrid improvement from 90.5% to 98% could easily be within variance. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported on the final numbers.

- **The Bayesian adaptation evidence is circular, not adaptive.** The adaptation analysis (Figures 3–4, Table 3) is shown only on the MiniGrid task, and the posterior concentrates near $\beta \approx 0.1$ — which is the value that a manual sweep on the *same task* already identified as effective. The paper does not demonstrate that Bayesian adaptation would:
  1. Find an effective $\beta$ on a *different* task without re-tuning.
  2. Avoid catastrophic regimes (e.g., $\beta=0.3$ or $\beta=1.0$ on MountainCar, which degrades performance by 53% and 75% respectively).
  
  The claim "Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual tuning" is not supported — the adaptation was validated only on the task where the sweet spot was already known, and the mechanism is unspecified.

- **The theoretical contributions (Propositions 1–2) are trivial.** Proposition 1 states that for sufficiently small $\beta$, optimal policies are unchanged — a standard continuity argument that holds for any bounded reward perturbation. Proposition 2 states that as $\beta\to\infty$, the KL term dominates — this is definitional. Neither provides any insight into the interesting regime where $\beta$ is moderate, nor do they offer sample-complexity bounds, convergence guarantees, or any analysis of why the KL shaping aids exploration.

### Minor

- **The specification of $p(o|s,a)$ and $q(o|s)$ is vague across experiments.** In the toy MDP, $p$ is the transition distribution and $q$ is a delta on $s_1$ — this is clear. In MountainCar, the paper says $q(o|s)$ "assigns all probability to the goal ($x \geq 0.5$)" — but the goal is a scalar condition on $x$, not a distribution over outcomes, and the predicted $p$ is never specified. In MiniGrid, $p$ and $q$ are not described at all. Without these specifications, the method is not fully reproducible from the paper alone.

- **Limited exploration of $\beta$ values in MiniGrid (only two values: 0.0 and 0.01).** The paper chooses an extremely small $\beta$ (0.01) for MiniGrid without justifying why larger values were not tested. The MountainCar results show that higher $\beta$ values can cause catastrophic failure, so the narrow $\beta$ range in MiniGrid is a concern.

- **The "Bayesian adaptation achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" claim** is relegated to "additional runs (not shown)" (Section 5.4). Results that support a central claim should be shown, not deferred.
