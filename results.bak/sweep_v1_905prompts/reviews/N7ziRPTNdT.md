## Summary
This paper asks whether generation (decoder-based inversion) is necessary for data-efficient compositional generalization, or whether non-generative (encoder-only) methods can suffice. The authors formalize the problem through identifiability theory, proving (Theorem 3.2) that when the observed dimension is much larger than the latent dimension ($d_x \geq d_z^3$), the derivative constraints needed to guarantee compositional generalization for an encoder depend on the unknown geometry of out-of-domain regions, making them infeasible to enforce via practical regularization or architectural design. In contrast, decoder constraints are axis-aligned and globally enforceable. The paper then demonstrates empirically on PUG datasets that generative methods (gradient-based search and generative replay) yield substantial OOD accuracy gains across six different base encoders, while non-generative methods fail unless heavily pretrained.

## Strengths
- **Theorem 3.2 and the asymmetry argument (Sec. 3.1):** The core theoretical result — that when $d_x \geq d_z^3$, the Jacobian and Hessian of an inverse generator $g \in \mathcal{G}_{\text{int}}$ can be arbitrary matrices at any single point, so the constraints that characterize $\mathcal{G}_{\text{int}}$ can only be enforced relative to the data manifold, whose OOD geometry is unknown — provides a rigorous foundation for the long-standing heuristic claim (Kilbertus et al., 2018) that the causal direction is easier to generalize from. This formal reasoning goes well beyond prior intuitive arguments.
- **Consistent empirical support across diverse base encoders (Fig. 6):** The paper evaluates generative (replay + search) methods across six base encoders ranging from from-scratch ViT-S/36 to SigLIP2 Gauß-Adm/14. On PUG-Background, generative methods improve OOD accuracy substantially for every encoder, and the improvement is monotonic with the addition of replay and search. The trend is clean and consistent with the theory.
- **Controlled experimental design (Sec. 5.1):** The three PUG splits (Background, Texture, Object) are thoughtfully constructed to isolate different interaction regimes. The PUG-Object ($n=0$) split acts as an important control: non-generative methods achieve near-perfect OOD accuracy (Fig. 5C), confirming the theoretical prediction that the $n=0$ special case makes $\mathcal{G}_{\text{int}}$ more structured — and thus compositional generalization easier even for encoders.
- **Conceptual clarity (Figure 3):** The contrast between the curved/unknown geometry of $\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}}$ and the Cartesian/known structure of $\mathcal{Z}$ is clearly illustrated, making the manifold-dependent vs. axis-aligned distinction intuitive and memorable.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **No statistical rigor in the empirical evaluation.** No error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple-seed results are reported anywhere in the paper. Given the large design space (6 base encoders × 2 slot encoder types × 2 fine-tuning choices × 3 splits), it is impossible to assess whether the observed differences are significant or within run-to-run variance. Moreover, the paper reports "the OOD accuracy obtained with the best-performing combination of slot encoder and fine-tuning choice" (p. 7), which risks cherry-picking and conflates model quality with configuration luck. This is a significant evidential gap for a paper making comparative claims about method families.

- **Reporting "best-performing combination" inflates apparent performance.** Selecting the best configuration per base encoder post-hoc (instead of reporting average or a principled selection rule) means the reported scores are upper bounds that may not reflect the method's typical behavior. While the trends are consistent, this choice weakens confidence in the numerical comparisons, particularly for the non-generative methods where performance varies substantially across configurations.

### Minor
- **The $\mathcal{F}_{\text{int}}$ assumption limits scope (acknowledged but consequential).** The paper's entire theoretical edifice rests on the assumption that ground-truth generators belong to $\mathcal{F}_{\text{int}}$ (polynomial interactions up to degree $n$). The paper acknowledges this limitation (Sec. 7), but the consequence is real: if real visual data are generated by functions outside this class, the proven asymmetry may not hold. The empirical evidence uses PUG data, which is synthetic with factorized structure, so it does not directly test whether $\mathcal{F}_{\text{int}}$ is a reasonable model for natural images. This does not invalidate the paper but sets a hard boundary on the contribution's scope.

- **The generative vs. non-generative comparison conflates the decoder architecture with the benefit of generation itself.** Generative methods use a regularized cross-attention Transformer decoder specifically designed to approximate $\mathcal{F}_{\text{int}}$, while non-generative methods use only an encoder. The paper mentions in §C that results with unstructured decoders are provided, but the main text presents no ablation that disentangles the benefit of "being generative" from the benefit of "having a well-structured decoder." If the decoder's $\mathcal{F}_{\text{int}}$-matching architecture is necessary for the gains, this directly supports the paper's thesis, but the main text does not make this separation visible.

- **The $d_x \geq d_z^3$ condition in Theorem 3.2 is stated without justification of tightness.** The paper does not discuss whether this cubic exponent is tight or a technical artifact of the proof. The qualitative conclusion (constraints become manifold-dependent in high dimensions) is unaffected, but the lack of discussion leaves a loose end.

- **No qualitative evidence for the generative replay mechanism.** The paper does not show examples of (a) OOD images generated by the decoder during replay, (b) latent traversals that verify slot disentanglement, or (c) gradient-based search trajectories. Providing such evidence would make the mechanism behind the performance gains concrete and help the reader assess whether the generated OOD images are indeed plausible and cover $\mathcal{X}_{\text{OOD}}$.

- **Slot-to-readout assignment is not fully specified.** The evaluation trains a "slot-wise readout" to predict categories from inferred slots, but it is not stated whether the slot-to-factor mapping (which slot corresponds to animal 1, animal 2, or background) is fixed, learned via a heuristic, or assigned by matching. This ambiguity makes the evaluation protocol harder to reproduce.

## Novel Insights
The insight that receives the most precise treatment here — and that goes well beyond the related work — is the *dimensionality-based justification* for why encoder constraints fail while decoder constraints succeed. The key observation (Theorem 3.2) is not just that $\mathcal{G}_{\text{int}}$ lacks a simple parametric form; it is that when $d_x$ is large relative to $d_z$, the derivative structure of $\mathcal{G}_{\text{int}}$ becomes *arbitrary* at the level of individual points, forcing any constraint to be manifold-dependent. This is a genuinely novel diagnosis of why the "anti-causal" direction (learning an encoder) is harder — it is not merely a heuristic about factorization complexity but a structural consequence of the dimensionality gap between the observation space and the latent space.
