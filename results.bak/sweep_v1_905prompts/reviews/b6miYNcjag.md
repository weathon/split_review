## Summary

This paper formalizes the problem of *reliability scoring*: assessing how much a reported dataset $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ deviates from its inaccessible ground truth $\mathbf{x}$, using auxiliary observations $\mathbf{y}$ generated by an unknown experiment $\mathbf{P}$. The core proposal is the **Gram determinant score** — the determinant of $\hat{\mathbf{G}} = (\mathbf{PQ})^\top(\mathbf{PQ})$ — which geometrically measures the volume spanned by class-conditional observation distributions. The paper proves that the score (1) preserves exact-match and Blackwell orderings under linearly independent experiments, (2) approximately preserves Hamming/dist ordering under a restricted regime, and (3) is the unique experiment-agnostic reliability score up to monotonic transformation (Proposition 4.3). Experiments on synthetic data, CIFAR-10 embeddings, and CES employment data show the score decreases monotonically with increasing corruption.

## Strengths

1. **Formalization of a novel problem.** Defining reliability scoring for datasets without ground truth, with auxiliary observations from an unknown experiment, and introducing ground-truth-based partial orderings (exact-match, Blackwell, Hamming/dist) as benchmarks is a clean and novel framing that opens a new direction.

2. **Impossibility results (Proposition 3.1) chart the fundamental limits.** Showing that no score can preserve exact-match ordering under $\mathcal{Q}_{\text{nonperm}}$, Blackwell ordering under linearly dependent experiments, or Hamming ordering under $\mathcal{Q}_{\text{dom}}$ provides a rigorous foundation for the positive results and explains why the paper's assumptions ($\mathcal{P}_{\text{indep}}$, $\mathcal{Q}_{L,\delta}$) are needed.

3. **Experiment-agnosticism and uniqueness (Proposition 4.3).** The result that the Gram determinant score is unique (up to monotone transformation) in producing the same ranking across all linearly independent experiments is a strong theoretical guarantee — no existing reliability measure offers this.

4. **Kernel extension (Definition 4.6).** Extending the score to continuous observation spaces via kernels broadens applicability beyond the discrete setting, and the CIFAR-10 experiments (Section 5, Experiment 2) demonstrate this works on real 8-dimensional embeddings.

5. **Empirical validation across diverse settings.** The experiments cover synthetic categorical data (6 manipulation policies, varying $N$ and $p$), CIFAR-10 image embeddings, and real-world employment vintages. The monotonic relationships with corruption levels (Figs. 2a–c, 3a–c) and the ranking recovery analysis (Fig. 2d) provide evidence that the score is sensible.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **No baseline comparisons in any experiment.** The experiments only demonstrate that the Gram determinant score decreases with increasing corruption — but *any* reasonable score would likely show the same. The paper acknowledges related measures (KL-divergence, $f$-divergence, determinant-based criteria, entropy-based measures) but never compares against them or even against simple baselines like the trace of $\hat{\mathbf{G}}$, the entropy of reported labels, or the match rate between $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. Without baselines, the evaluation establishes non-pathology, not superiority. Since the paper claims practical utility (conclusion mentions "detecting incoherent star ratings in product reviews"), comparisons are essential to support the claim that the Gram determinant score is *useful* rather than merely *not broken*.

2. **The empirical component is limited in scope.** Employment data (Experiment 3) is a single example with $N=209$ and a coarse 4-bucket discretization. There is no sensitivity analysis for the number of buckets, no error bars, and the result is suggestive but not robust evidence. The CIFAR-10 experiments use only a linear kernel; the paper does not explore how different kernels (e.g., RBF with varying bandwidths) affect the ranking. For a paper with "Experiments" as a major section advertised in the abstract, the empirical evidence is thinner than expected.

### Minor

3. **The Hamming/dist ordering guarantee (Theorem 4.2(3)) is restricted to an extremely small misreport regime.** The bound requires the Hamming distance to be at most $N/(64 L^2 d^2)$. For $L=1, d=10$ (CIFAR-10), this means at most ~1.6 misreports in 10,000 samples — far below any practical corruption level. The paper claims this is "nearly matching our impossibility results," but the impossibility shows no score works on $\mathcal{Q}_{\text{dom}}$ (no bound on misreports), while the positive result requires $\mathcal{Q}_{L, 1/64L^2d^2}$ (a tiny subset). The gap is large. The paper should be more transparent that this bound is likely loose (the experiments show good performance at $p=0.5$, far beyond the bound) and should either provide a tighter bound or soften the "nearly matching" language.

4. **Experiment-agnosticism applies at the population level (exact $\mathbf{PQ}$), not to the plug-in estimator.** The decoupling $\Gamma(\mathbf{PQ}) = \det(\mathbf{P}^\top\mathbf{P})\det(\mathbf{Q})^2$ holds exactly, but the plug-in estimator $\tilde{S}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \mathbf{y})$ does not perfectly decouple; its ranking can depend on $\mathbf{P}$ through finite-sample estimation noise. The paper acknowledges this only obliquely (Proposition 4.5 says "asymptotically preserves" the orderings). A discussion of finite-sample variance across different experiments would strengthen the work.

### Trivial

5. The description "unique up to scaling" for Proposition 4.3 is slightly imprecise — the result allows $S(Q) = \alpha \det(Q^\top Q)^\beta$, where $\beta$ is an exponent (not a scale factor), though the ranking is indeed preserved for any $\beta>0$.

## Suggestions

1. **Add baseline comparisons.** The most impactful improvement would be to compare the Gram determinant score against at least 2–3 alternatives: (i) the entropy of reported labels (which captures randomness in $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$), (ii) the trace of $\hat{\mathbf{G}}$ (a simpler summary statistic from the same Gram matrix), and (iii) the mutual information between $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\mathbf{y}$. Show that the Gram determinant yields more faithful rankings with respect to ground-truth Hamming distance. This is essential for a paper claiming practical utility.

2. **Improve the bound for Theorem 4.2(3) or clarify the relationship between theory and experiment.** The paper should either derive a tighter bound, or explicitly state that the current bound is loose and that the experiments demonstrate the score works well far beyond it. The claim "nearly matching impossibility results" should be softened or better justified.

3. **Expand the real-data experiments.** Include more than one employment data example; add error bars/confidence intervals throughout; show sensitivity to the discretization choice.

4. **Discuss finite-sample behavior of experiment-agnosticism.** Add a short paragraph in Section 4 on how estimation noise in the plug-in estimator can cause the ranking to depend on $\mathbf{P}$, with sample complexity bounds or simulations.
