## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. It additionally introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated style-aware rendering. The model is trained using a Seq2Seq formulation without requiring note-level alignment. Evaluations include APT benchmarks, objective EPR metrics, subjective listening tests (11 participants), disentanglement analysis via composer/performer identification, and a style transfer study.

## Strengths

1. **Novel joint formulation of two inverse MIR tasks.** Modeling EPR and APT in a unified framework with disentangled content-style representations is a conceptually clean and well-motivated contribution. The paper clearly articulates the duality between rendering and transcription and builds an architecture that reflects it. Comparable joint training in speech (ASR+TTS) has shown benefits, and extending this idea to music is a timely and natural direction.

2. **APT results are competitive with the state of the art.** On the ASAP benchmark (Table 1), the proposed method achieves the best or second-best results on 8 of 12 metrics, with statistically significant improvements on several key metrics (E_extra, E_onset, E_offset, E_pitch) over the strong Seq2Seq baseline of Beyer & Dai (2024). This provides concrete evidence that the joint model's content representations capture musically meaningful information.

3. **Quantitative disentanglement validation is convincing.** Table 4 shows that style embeddings predict composer identity with 77.46% accuracy (vs. 29.99% for content embeddings) and performer identity with 42.07% (vs. 9.94%). Figure 3 visualizes clear clustering by both composer and performer. This is strong evidence that the intended separation of content and style has been achieved.

4. **Alignment-free Seq2Seq EPR competes with alignment-dependent methods.** Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in Table 3, outperforming DExter and VirtuosoNet which require note-level alignment. This supports the paper's claim that the strong alignment assumption can be relaxed without sacrificing accuracy.

5. **PSR module generates era-appropriate style embeddings.** Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style vectors form clusters (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) with centroids and temporal progression that mirror those extracted from real performances. This validates the core idea that stylistically appropriate embeddings can be inferred from score content alone.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Factually inaccurate claim about velocity variance (Table 2).** The paper states: "our models exhibit duration and velocity variances that more closely match those of human performances compared with other baselines." For velocity variance σ²(V), Human = 241.04, DExter = 238.86 (deviation 2.18), while Ours (Target) = 151.03 (deviation 90.01). DExter is dramatically closer — the claim is simply wrong for velocity. For duration variance σ²(D), Ours (Target) at 0.58 is indeed closer to Human (1.72) than DExter (4.15), so the duration half of the claim is correct. But the blanket statement about velocity is contradicted by the paper's own numbers. This needs to be corrected and the text revised to honestly characterize what the variance results show. The paper does have other evidence for EPR quality (KL and MAE metrics where Ours Target leads), so this error weakens but does not invalidate the EPR contribution.

2. **Missing explanation for subjective outperformance of human performances.** Figure 2 shows Ours (Target) receiving higher ratings than the "Human" condition on several dimensions including overall human-likeness. The paper does not explain what "Human" means in this figure, nor does it discuss why a synthetic model would be preferred over actual human recordings on perceived naturalness. This result is atypical and requires explicit discussion — e.g., whether listeners preferred exaggerated expression, whether the human renditions were actual recordings or MIDI renderings, or whether the task framing biased raters. Without clarification, this figure raises questions rather than providing support.

### Minor

3. **Style transfer evaluation is too limited.** The style transfer study (Figure 5) includes only 3 samples, and one of the three (Sample 2) shows Target performing worse than Mean on both similarity and quality. The paper does not acknowledge or discuss this inconsistency. Three samples (especially with mixed results) is insufficient to support a strong claim about reliable style transfer. A larger and more systematic evaluation — ideally with objective metrics supplementing the subjective ones — would be needed.

4. **Unpaired training contribution is overstated relative to actual mechanism.** The paper frames unpaired data as a key advantage ("Our framework supports training on both paired and unpaired data"), but the unpaired losses (Eq. 4) are within-domain masked reconstruction objectives — they do not provide cross-domain signal. The cross-domain tasks (EPR, APT) are learned exclusively from paired ASAP data. The framing in the abstract and introduction ("from both paired and unpaired data") could be read as implying cross-domain learning from unpaired data, which is not what the model does. Clarifying what the unpaired data actually contributes (e.g., regularization, better content encoder training) would strengthen the paper.

5. **Subjective listening test has small participant pool (N=11).** While acceptable for a proof of concept, 11 participants on 5 pieces with no reported inter-rater agreement or confidence intervals limits the reliability of the subjective claims. Combined with the unexplained Human outperformance (Weakness 2), the subjective evidence is weaker than it should be for the claims it supports.

### Trivial

6. **The bar chart descriptions (Figures 2, 5) do not specify whether error bars represent standard deviation or standard error.** This should be stated explicitly.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation study isolating the contribution of the unpaired reconstruction losses (training with and without them) would concretely quantify their benefit and support the framing.
- A direct test of disentanglement (e.g., varying only the style vector while holding content fixed and measuring whether note-level attributes remain invariant) would provide a stronger operational validation.
- Reporting the computational cost of the PSR diffusion module would be useful for practical deployment considerations.
