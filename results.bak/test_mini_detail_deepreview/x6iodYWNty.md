## Summary
This paper proposes Neural Predictor-Corrector (NPC), an RL framework that replaces hand-crafted heuristics in homotopy-based solvers with learned policies. It first observes that four diverse problem domains — Graduated Non-Convexity (robust optimization), Gaussian Homotopy (global optimization), Homotopy Continuation (polynomial root-finding), and Annealed Langevin Dynamics (sampling) — share a common predictor-corrector (PC) structure. NPC formulates PC control as an MDP (predictor step size and corrector tolerance as actions) and trains policies via PPO with an amortized training strategy. Experiments across all four domains show that NPC reduces corrector iterations by 70–80% while preserving solution accuracy, and an ablation study confirms that each component of the RL state contributes to performance.

## Strengths

- **First unified treatment of diverse homotopy problems under a common PC framework.** Section 3.3 explicitly writes down homotopy interpolations for GNC, GH, HC, and ALD (Eqs. 1–4) and identifies their shared structure, enabling a single solver design rather than per-domain heuristics. This is a genuine synthesis not available in prior work.

- **Cross-instance generalization is demonstrated across all four domains.** The agent is trained on one set of problem instances and tested on entirely different ones: GNC trained on the Aquarius sequence and tested on bunny/cube/dragon; HC trained on 4-view triangulation with randomized coefficients and tested on Katsura10/cyclic7/UPnP; ALD trained on a 10-mode GMM and tested on 40-mode GMM/funnel/DW-4. The successful transfer (summarized in table footnotes) shows the policy learns reusable structure.

- **Ablation study (Table 6) validates the MDP design choices.** Removing any of the four state components increases corrector iterations by 21–64, with corrector statistics being the most informative. This provides concrete quantitative evidence that all components are necessary.

- **Efficiency gains (70–80% iteration reduction) are substantial and consistent across domains.** Tables 1–5 show that NPC achieves accuracy comparable to or better than classical methods while using far fewer corrector iterations. For example, GNC point cloud registration iterations on bunny drop from 783 to 169, HC iterations on Katsura10 drop from 39 to 7, and ALD iterations on 40-mode GMM drop from 410 to 110.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No variance or statistical significance is reported for any metric.** All tables report single-valued averages over 50 trials, with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or hypothesis tests. This is a significant omission: the paper's claims of "superior numerical stability" are unsupported without variance measures, and the reader cannot assess whether the reported gains are methodologically meaningful or within noise. The precision of entries like `-0.85` for all methods on bunny (Table 1) further highlights the need for error reporting.

- **The efficiency-precision trade-off curves (Figure 4) are under-described, weakening their evidentiary value.** The paper states these curves are generated by "manual tuning of homotopy parameters" but does not specify: (a) which parameters were varied and over what range, (b) how many settings were evaluated, (c) whether single-run or multi-run values are plotted, or (d) whether the classical curves represent the full Pareto frontier. Since the NPC training reward includes an efficiency bonus that directly penalizes corrector iterations ($T_{\max} - T$), the key question is whether NPC genuinely discovers a better operating point or simply picks a different point on the same frontier. The trade-off curves could address this, but their construction must be fully specified for the comparison to be convincing.

### Minor

- **Training distribution details are underspecified.** The paper states the agent is trained on "Ackley functions with randomized parameters" (GH), "polynomial systems from the 4-view triangulation task with randomized coefficients" (HC), and "10-mode GMM with randomly sampled coefficients" (ALD), but does not describe the randomization ranges, distributions, or how they were selected. This makes it difficult to assess how broad or narrow the training distribution is, and limits reproducibility.

- **The efficiency bonus in the reward conflates training and evaluation objectives.** While RL agents are naturally trained to optimize a metric they are evaluated on, the classical baselines use fixed heuristics that do not optimize for corrector iterations. Although the trade-off curves partially address this (point above), the paper would benefit from an additional controlled experiment where classical methods are also optimized for the efficiency metric (e.g., via parameter sweep) to demonstrate that NPC's operating point is not just a different point but a genuinely better one.

- **Training time is not reported.** For an amortized approach where one-time offline training is a key advantage, the paper does not report training time, number of episodes, or convergence behavior. Without this, a practitioner cannot judge whether the training overhead is justified relative to per-instance baselines like CPL (whose runtime includes training and ranges 790–2160 ms). This should at minimum be discussed in the main text.

- **Classical PC methods already have adaptive step-size schemes (e.g., step-doubling with error estimation) that are not considered as baselines.** The paper compares only against fixed-schedule heuristics. Comparing NPC against an existing adaptive scheme would isolate the benefit of learning versus adaptivity per se.

- **Initial state construction for the "warm up" phase (Algorithm 1, line 1) is not described.** The state includes convergence velocity, which requires a baseline from the previous homotopy level, but at level 0 (source problem) there is no previous level. How the initial state is constructed is unspecified.

## Novel Insights
The reviews surface a recurring tension in RL-for-solver papers: when the training reward directly targets the evaluation metric, comparisons against untrained baselines conflate "learning to optimize the metric" with "discovering a genuinely superior operating point." The reviewer correctly identifies that this is not fatal (RL agents are always trained on their evaluation metric) but does demand stronger controls — specifically, fully specified trade-off curves that prove the learned policy dominates the classical Pareto frontier. A second insight is that the paper's scope (four domains) is both a strength and a liability: it demonstrates generality, but the thinness of evaluation per domain (no variance, limited training distribution detail, single-task training for GNC) means the evidence for each individual claim is weaker than in a single-domain paper. The ablation study (Table 6) is the kind of targeted evidence that makes the MDP design credible, and more such per-domain analysis would significantly strengthen the paper.

## Suggestions

1. **Report standard deviations and/or confidence intervals** for all metrics in Tables 1–5. With 50 trials per setting, this is straightforward and would substantially improve the paper's credibility, especially for stability claims.
2. **Fully specify how the trade-off curves in Figure 4 are generated:** what parameters are swept, over what range, single-run or averaged, and whether the curves represent the full Pareto frontier. Ideally, the construction should be described in detail so a reader could reproduce them.
3. **Report training time and convergence behavior** of the RL agent (episodes, wall-clock time, reward curves) to allow practitioners to assess the amortization benefit.
4. **Specify the randomization ranges and distributions** used for training data generation (GH, HC, ALD) to improve reproducibility.
5. **Include at least one adaptive heuristic baseline** (e.g., step-doubling with error estimation for HC) to disentangle the benefit of learning from the benefit of adaptivity per se.
6. **Clarify the warm-up initialization** in Algorithm 1 (how the state is constructed at the first level when no prior level exists).
