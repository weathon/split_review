## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), weighted by a parameter β. The headline novelty is a Bayesian scheme that adapts β online to eliminate manual tuning. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 show that fixed-β KL shaping improves over standard RL (β=0), and the paper presents additional results on Bayesian β adaptation.

## Strengths

- **Clear formulation of outcome-based KL shaping.** The distinction between KL divergence over *outcome distributions* (p(o|s,a) vs. q(o|s)) and the policy-level KL used in TRPO/SAC is well-motivated (Section 3.2). This provides a principled way to inject preferences that offer dense guidance in sparse-reward settings, as opposed to policy-change regularization.

- **Consistent empirical gains for fixed-β shaping across environments.** The fixed-β experiments, despite limited seeds, show consistent improvement: 69–74% on the toy 2-state MDP (Table 1), 18% on MountainCar (Table 2, β=0.1), and 98% vs. 90.5% success rate on MiniGrid (Figure 2). The Toy MDP experiment uses 7 seeds with reported confidence intervals, and the MiniGrid results show clear separation in both success rate and episode length.

- **Cognitive neuroscience framing is coherent.** The connection to dopamine-based RPE and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 3.3, 6.3) provides a plausible interdisciplinary motivation, and the paper is explicit about where IncentRL departs from active inference (fixed preferences vs. adaptive beliefs).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The Bayesian β adaptation mechanism — the paper's central claimed novelty — is completely unspecified.**  
   The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all highlight "treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online" as the primary contribution. Yet the Methods section (Sec. 3), the algorithm description (Sec. 5.1), and the entire paper contain no description of: the prior distribution over β, the likelihood (over what data?), the inference procedure (conjugate update? variational inference? MCMC?), or the update rule. The phrase "Bayesian adaptation scheme" (Sec. 5.4) is the closest the paper gets to a specification. Without this, the paper's headline contribution is unverifiable and irreproducible. The code is referenced but the paper itself must describe the method.

2. **The Bayesian β evidence is inconsistent with the paper's own claims.**  
   Figure 3 shows posterior mean β starting at ~0.11 (Round 1) and decreasing to ~0.02 (Round 4). Table 3 confirms Round 4 mean β = 0.0173 (std 0.0182). The text repeatedly states the posterior "concentrates near the effective region (β≈0.1)," but the data show it converging to ~0.02 — a factor of 5 lower. The red dashed line in Figure 3 labeled "target effective β = 0.1" exacerbates the confusion. The MiniGrid best fixed β was 0.01, so the posterior actually converges to a value appropriate for that environment — but the paper's labeling and narrative contradict its own evidence. Moreover, the Bayesian β results are presented without reporting the resulting task performance of the adaptive agent, making it impossible to assess whether the adaptation actually helps.

3. **No comparison to existing intrinsic motivation or adaptive tuning methods.**  
   The only baseline in every experiment is standard RL (β=0). The paper claims to address "a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" (manual trade-off tuning) but never compares against curiosity (Pathak et al. 2017, Burda et al. 2018), RND, ICM, empowerment, or any existing method that already attempts to balance exploration/exploitation with adaptive coefficients. For the adaptive β claim specifically, comparisons to meta-gradient, annealing schedules, or bandit-based tuning are absent. The paper therefore does not demonstrate that its approach is competitive with or superior to existing solutions.

### Minor

1. **Low statistical power on the main benchmarks.** MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments use only 3 seeds (Secs. 5.3, 5.4). No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for the MountainCar results. With only 3 seeds, the 18% improvement (3502 vs. 4135) could fall within natural variance.

2. **Trivial theoretical analysis.** Propositions 1 and 2 are limit statements: very small β preserves the optimal policy, very large β makes the agent ignore rewards. These follow immediately from the additive reward structure and provide no insight into the behavior at intermediate β values where actual performance gains occur, nor into the dynamics of Bayesian β updating.

3. **Missing implementation details for outcome distributions.** The paper does not specify how p(o|s,a) is obtained in practice for the continuous-state MountainCar environment (learned forward model? the environment dynamics?), how q(o|s) is defined beyond "assigning all probability to the goal," or how the KL is computed. This limits reproducibility even for the fixed-β experiments.
