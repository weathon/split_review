## Summary

This paper proposes **Steady Thought (ST)**, a three-stage framework for mitigating "under-thinking" (excessive thought switching) in large reasoning models. The pipeline segments model responses into thought units using entropy-based detection, generates forced non-switching completions for each thought via logit suppression of trigger words, and then performs thought-level preference optimization (STPO) where the correct forced completion is treated as chosen and the original continuation as rejected. Experiments across three model sizes (1.5B, 8B, 14B) and four benchmarks show consistent accuracy gains (up to 5.3%) alongside substantial token reductions (up to 39.3%), with out-of-distribution generalization to code generation.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated and principled alternative to global suppression**: The paper formalizes under-thinking as a preference optimization problem at the granularity of individual thoughts (Section 2.1, Equation 2) and introduces STPO (Equation 7), a length-normalized objective that directly encourages commitment to promising thoughts while preserving exploration ability. This is more selective than methods that globally suppress switching regardless of whether the current trajectory is promising (Section 1, last paragraph).

2. **Consistent gains across multiple model scales and benchmarks**: On Qwen3-8B, ST improves average accuracy by 3.12% while reducing average tokens by 25.5%; on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, it gains 2.52% accuracy with 17.3% fewer tokens (Table 1). Unlike baselines such as NOWAIT which often degrade accuracy severely (e.g., 59.03% vs. 80.23% overall for Qwen3-8B, +84.6% tokens), ST improves or maintains accuracy in every case where it reduces tokens.

3. **Mechanistic evidence beyond aggregate metrics**: Section 4.4.2 (Table 2) shows that the percentage of correct intermediate thoughts (PCT) drops substantially after ST (e.g., 14.50%→7.90% on AIME 2024 for 1.5B), directly supporting the claim that the model reduces *invalid* switches rather than simply cutting all exploration. Figure 2 further shows increased proportion of last thought, indicating deeper commitment.

4. **Out-of-distribution generalization**: Models trained only on math (omni-math) improve on LiveCode (competitive programming) by up to 5.3% accuracy with token reductions (Table 1), suggesting the learned commitment behavior transfers beyond the training distribution rather than being mere data memorization.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Forced-suppression completions as training signal lack analysis of distribution mismatch**. The "chosen" completions \(T_i'\) are generated by sharply decreasing logits for trigger words (Section 3.2), not sampled from the model's natural distribution. These are then treated as the gold standard in preference optimization. There is no analysis of how well these forced completions reflect the model's own capabilities, how often they produce correct answers, or whether the model simply learns to mimic the suppression pattern rather than genuinely improving its commitment to promising thoughts. This is a significant methodological concern because the training signal comes from an intervention-based policy rather than the model's natural behavior.

2. **Missing ablation that isolates thought-level conditioning from data construction**. The paper attributes its main gains to "thought-level preference optimization," but the ablation in Table 4 compares STPO against SFT and DPO *using the same thought-level data pairs* (both conditioned on \((Q, T_i)\)). This does not isolate whether the key benefit comes from (a) the fine-grained \((Q, T_i)\) conditioning, (b) the specific preference pairs constructed from thought completions, or (c) the length-normalized STPO loss. A baseline using the same preference pairs conditioned on \(Q\) alone (rather than \(Q, T_i\)) would help disentangle these factors. Without it, the claim that fine-grained conditioning is the key remains partially unsupported.

### Minor

1. **ST is not uniformly best across all metrics/tasks**. On Qwen3-8B LiveCode, SEAL achieves 83.4% accuracy vs. ST's 77.1% (Table 1), yet the paper's narrative ("up to 5.3% improvement") focuses only on improvement over vanilla. The OOD generalization claim should acknowledge that SEAL, a simpler method, outperforms ST on this setting for the 8B model.

2. **Incomplete specification of trigger words for reproducibility**. The paper mentions only "wait" and "alternatively" as examples of trigger words (Section 3.2) but does not provide the complete list or describe how it was determined. Since this list directly affects the forced-completion quality, its absence is a reproducibility concern.

3. **Entropy threshold details deferred for larger models**. The threshold tuning analysis (Table 3) is reported only for the 1.5B model in the main text, with larger models' thresholds relegated to the appendix (which is stripped from the review). Reporting the thresholds used for all three models in the main text would increase transparency.

## Suggestions

1. **Address the forced-suppression concern**: Analyze how often forced (non-switching) completions produce correct answers, and whether the model's gains come from genuinely improved commitment or just mimicking suppression. Consider generating chosen completions with softer guidance (e.g., temperature manipulation, top-k filtering) as a comparison.
2. **Add a holistic conditioning baseline**: Compare STPO against the same preference pairs optimized with conditioning on \(Q\) only (no latent thought prefix \(T_i\)). This would isolate the benefit of thought-level conditioning from the data construction.
3. **Report the complete trigger word list** and describe how it was selected for each model.
4. **Discuss the LiveCode Qwen3-8B result** where SEAL outperforms ST, and characterize the types of problems where ST helps vs. hurts.
