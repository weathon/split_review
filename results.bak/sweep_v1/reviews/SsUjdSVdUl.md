## Summary

This paper introduces Critique-RL, a two-stage online reinforcement learning approach for training critique (critiquing) language models without relying on stronger human or model supervisors. Through empirical analysis, the authors first identify a key failure mode: optimising critique models using only indirect reward signals (based on whether the actor's refinement is correct) improves helpfulness but fails to optimize discriminability — the ability to judge whether a response is correct — leading to conservative or aggressive critics. To address this, they propose a two-stage method where Stage I explicitly optimizes discriminability via a direct rule-based reward, and Stage II optimizes helpfulness via refinement-based rewards while regularizing to preserve discriminability. Extensive experiments on Qwen2.5-3B and 7B across five math reasoning tasks show substantial gains over prior methods (Retroformer, CTRL), with strong OOD generalization, inference-time compute scaling benefits, and iterative improvement.

## Strengths

1. **Clear empirical diagnosis of a genuine failure mode.** Section 4.1 and Figure 3 convincingly show that three natural indirect reward functions (r_refine, r_Δ, r_correction) all fail to optimize discriminability, leading to conservative or aggressive critics as training progresses. This analysis is clean, well-visualized, and directly motivates the two-stage design. It provides a useful insight for the community that goes beyond the specific method.

2. **Well-motivated, simple two-stage design validated by ablation.** The separation of discriminability (Stage I, direct rule-based reward) and helpfulness (Stage II, refinement reward + discriminability regularization) is intuitive and grounded in the prior analysis. The ablation study (Table 3) cleanly demonstrates that removing either stage causes clear drops (e.g., on MATH: Acc@Refine drops from 48.6 to 47.6 without Stage I and to 45.9 without Stage II), and removing discriminability regularization in Stage II drops Acc@Dis from 82.8 to 77.7. The ablations confirm that both stages and the regularization are critical.

3. **Substantial and consistent improvements across multiple models and tasks.** On Qwen2.5-7B, Critique-RL achieves 58.40% Acc on MATH (+4.54 pp over CTRL, +12.66 pp over No Critic), 87.72% on GSM8K (+6.37 pp over CTRL), and 65.75% on AQuA (+0.79 pp over CTRL). Similar patterns hold for Qwen2.5-3B (Table 1). The margins are large and consistent, strengthening confidence that the gains are not coincidental.

4. **Out-of-domain generalization and practical benefits demonstrated.** The trained critique models generalize to unseen tasks (SVAMP, TheoremQA — Table 4), show improved inference-time compute scaling (Figure 1), and benefit from iterative training (Table 2). The oracle verifier analysis (Figure 5) further isolates that the method genuinely improves helpfulness beyond discriminability.

5. **No reliance on stronger supervisors for critique data.** The SFT initialization data is generated by prompting Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, not a SOTA commercial model. This is a meaningful practical advantage over methods like Critic-CoT that distill from GPT-4.

## Weaknesses

### Major

None. The verified weaknesses are significant but do not invalidate the core claims.

### Minor

1. **No variance or statistical significance reported.** All results in Tables 1–4 are single-point estimates with no error bars, standard deviations, or multi-seed averages. RL-based training of LMs can exhibit meaningful variance across random seeds. The paper reports "best results" from 500 training steps without specifying the validation criterion or how many checkpoints were evaluated. While the large and consistent margins (e.g., +4.54 pp on MATH, +6.37 pp on GSM8K for 7B) partially mitigate this concern, the lack of any reliability measure weakens the evidence, particularly for smaller gains (e.g., +0.79 pp on AQuA 7B). This is the most significant gap in the current submission.

2. **The "w/o Stage I" ablation uses a different KL reference from the full method, potentially confounding the comparison.** Stage II (Eq. 9, Algorithm 1) uses KL(π_Stage-I ‖ π_Stage-II). When Stage I is skipped, the KL reference necessarily changes (presumably to SFT), but the paper does not discuss this or control for it. A cleaner baseline would apply the same combined reward (r_refine + r_dis) with a fixed KL reference (e.g., SFT) in a single-stage training setup. The observed drop (48.6 → 47.6 on MATH) is informative but the confound makes it hard to quantify exactly how much of the drop is due to missing Stage I training vs. the different KL reference.

3. **Parsing mechanism for the discrimination reward is unspecified.** The function f(x, y, c) that extracts the critique model's correctness judgment from generated text (Eq. 7) is not described. The paper mentions critiques include "correctness judgments for each step" and a "judgment for the final answer," but how these are parsed (rule-based extraction of labels like "Correct"/"Wrong"? format constraints in the prompt?) is left as an implementation detail. Failure cases in parsing (ambiguous phrasing, format violations) could produce noisy reward signals. This is particularly relevant since the reward is binary and does not credit partially correct judgments.

4. **No analysis of hyperparameter sensitivity.** β₁ is set to 0.2 (β₂ is mentioned but its value is not reported), and β = 0.01 is used for KL. No ablation or sensitivity analysis is provided for these scaling factors, which control the trade-off between discriminability preservation and helpfulness optimization in Stage II.
