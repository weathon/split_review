## Summary

This paper proposes a two-track variant of the Adam optimizer (STNAdam) for minimizing "nonconvex + weakly-convex" composite objectives. The method maintains two coupled iteration trajectories — a regular proximal update and a Nesterov-momentum-based extrapolation update — with adaptive learning rates. The authors provide convergence analysis under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property and demonstrate the method's effectiveness on low-light image enhancement (LIE). The key novelty is the two-track framework, which structurally departs from single-track variants like NAdam and SNAdam.

## Strengths

1. **Novel two-track coupled iteration framework**: Algorithm 1 explicitly maintains two intertwined trajectories (a proximal update from $x^k$ and an extrapolation update from $\bar{x}^{k+1}$). Figure 1 visually distinguishes this from single-track NAG, Adam, and NAdam, making the architectural departure concrete. This is a genuine structural innovation in the Adam-family literature.

2. **Comprehensive convergence analysis under the KL property**: Theorem 1 establishes convergence (in expectation) and Theorem 2 provides explicit rates (geometric/polynomial/finite depending on the KL exponent $\vartheta \in [0,1)$). The analysis covers a general class of variance-reduced gradient estimators (SVRG, SAGA, SARAH), not just a single estimator, and handles the challenging "nonconvex + weakly-convex" composite setting that subsumes strongly-convex and convex + nonconvex cases.

3. **Strong empirical results on low-light image enhancement**: In Table 2, STNAdam-SARAH achieves PSNR 22.26, SSIM 0.91, and LPIPS 0.05 on the LOL dataset — substantially outperforming all 10 compared methods including the next best general optimizer (SNAdam at PSNR 17.14) and LIE-customized methods (Retinex-Net at PSNR 18.44). The gap of roughly 4.8 dB PSNR over SNAdam is striking and not attributable to noise.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Abstract and contribution (ii) overclaim "almost sure convergence" while the main theorems prove convergence in expectation.** The abstract states "the sequence generated by STNAdam almost surely converges to a stationary point ... at an explicit rate." However, Theorem 1 proves convergence *in expectation* (line 267: "The sequence $\{\bar{x}^k\}$ converges to a stationary point of $\Phi$ *in expectation*"). Lemma 4 does provide some almost-sure guarantees (summability of squared differences, convergence of distances to the accumulation set), but the central convergence claim of the iterates is in expectation, not almost surely. The concluding remarks (line 340) correctly say "global convergence of STNAdam in expectation," making this a claim misalignment between the abstract/contributions and the technical results.

2. **The "Time(s)" column in Tables 2 and 3 reports values on the order of $10^{-5}$ seconds (26–76 microseconds) with no explanation of what is being measured.** These extremely fast times are not contextualized — it is unclear if the column measures total wall-clock time for the full dataset, per-image time, per-iteration time, or something else. STNAdam-SARAH reporting the fastest time (2.64e-05 s) while having the most complex per-iteration computation (involving a SARAH gradient estimator and two proximal updates) further raises questions. The paper uses this column to claim speed superiority ("our method outperforms the other three approaches in terms of speed"), but without defining the measurement, the claim cannot be evaluated. This does not invalidate the PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS metrics, but it undermines any time-based claims.

3. **The parameter update intervals (Eqs. 6–8) depend on problem constants ($L$, $\tau$, $V_1$, $V_\Upsilon$, $\rho$) that are unknown in practice, yet the paper claims "removing hand-tuning" as contribution (ii).** The lower bounds $\underline{\gamma}$, $\underline{\lambda}$, and $\underline{\alpha}$ are expressed in terms of the Lipschitz constant $L$, weak-convexity modulus $\tau$, and variance-reduction constants from Lemma 1. Remark 3 addresses this only by saying constants can be "appropriately increased if necessary," which is not a practical recipe. The theoretical analysis establishes *existence* of valid intervals, but the paper presents this as a practical tuning-free schedule, which is misleading.

### Minor

4. **STNAdam-SGD is included as an algorithm variant (Tables 2–3) even though SGD does not satisfy the variance-reduction conditions in Lemma 1.** The paper acknowledges "SGD does not exhibit variance reduction" (line 128), but the theoretical guarantees of Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 apply only to estimators satisfying the geometric decay condition (5). Including SGD as a valid variant without clarifying that its theoretical coverage differs creates ambiguity about which results apply to which variant.

5. **Experimental setup lacks key reproducibility details.** No hyperparameters (learning rates, momentum coefficients, batch sizes, iteration counts) are reported for any baseline or the proposed method. There are no confidence intervals, error bars, or mention of multiple independent runs. The comparison baselines (SGD, SAdam, SNAdam) are not fully described with respect to the composite structure of problem (14).

6. **Citation inconsistency for SAdam.** In the related work (line 18), SAdam is correctly attributed to Wang et al. (2019)/Le-Duc et al. (2024), but in the experiments section (line 285), it is cited as "SAdam (Kingma & Ba, 2014)" — Kingma & Ba is the original Adam, not SAdam.
