## Summary

This paper introduces **CaTS-Bench**, a large-scale multimodal benchmark for time series captioning (TSC) built from 11 real-world datasets spanning domains from climate and health to agriculture and sales. Each of the 20k samples (570k total timesteps) pairs a numeric series segment with rich metadata, a line-plot image, and a human-validated reference caption produced by a scalable oracle-LLM pipeline. Beyond captioning, the benchmark includes 460 diagnostic multiple-choice questions targeting matching and comparison skills. The paper further proposes domain-specific evaluation metrics (Statistical Inference Accuracy, Numeric Score) and benchmarks a broad set of VLMs in zero-shot and finetuned settings, finding that current models largely fail to leverage visual plot information.

## Strengths

- **Scale, diversity, and multimodal coverage**: CaTS-Bench is the first TSC benchmark to combine numeric series, rich metadata, and visual plots at scale across 11 real-world datasets, substantially exceeding prior benchmarks (TADACap, TRUCE, TACO) in both size and modality coverage (Table 1). The temporal train/test split (first 80% / last 20%) prevents data leakage and is a principled design choice.

- **Thorough quality validation of semi-synthetic captions**: The paper conducts three complementary validation studies — manual fact-checking of ~2.9k captions (98.6% accuracy), a human detectability study (41.1%, near-random), and diversity/bias analyses across nine embedding models — providing credible evidence that the oracle-generated captions are factual, human-indistinguishable, and not template-driven. The paraphrasing robustness check (Spearman ρ = 0.9266) further confirms that rankings are stable across linguistic variants of ground truth.

- **Diagnostic Q&A tasks that reveal VLM limitations**: The four multiple-choice tasks (especially Plot Matching, where models perform at chance while humans are near-perfect) provide clear diagnostic signal. The Q&A suite isolates specific failure modes — visual grounding, temporal comparison, cross-modal alignment — that are not probed by the captioning task alone.

- **Tailored evaluation metrics**: The Statistical Inference Accuracy (no penalty for omission) and Numeric Score (with λ_R = 0.7 emphasizing recall) are thoughtfully designed for the TSC setting, moving beyond generic n-gram overlap to measure what matters for time series: numeric fidelity and coverage of key statistics.

- **Comprehensive evaluation across modalities**: The benchmark evaluates both proprietary and open-source VLMs in zero-shot and finetuned settings, plus a program-aided (PAL) variant. The human-revisited subset provides a complementary high-fidelity evaluation track (Tables 3, 4).

## Weaknesses

### Major
None.

### Minor

- **Semi-synthetic captions as primary ground truth carry inherent limitations**. While the paper provides extensive validation (98.6% factual accuracy, human indistinguishability, paraphrasing robustness), the fact remains that the main evaluation references are generated by a single oracle LLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash). The paraphrasing check tests sensitivity to linguistic style but not to more substantive differences in content selection or emphasis. A model producing a fluent, factually accurate caption with different focus from the oracle's would be penalized. The human-revisited subset (579 samples, ~14% of the test set) is too small to serve as the primary ground truth. The authors acknowledge this issue directly (Section 3.1: "time series captioning lacks inherent ground truth at the level of a single canonical description"), and the paper evaluates on both semi-synthetic and human-revisited sets, but the concern about proxy ground truth is not fully resolved.

- **Q&A filtering by a single model is under-validated**. The 460 multiple-choice questions are filtered by removing any that Qwen 2.5 Omni answered correctly. The paper defers justification to Appendix J.2 (stripped), making it impossible to verify that filtering produces genuinely harder rather than model-specific questions. While the Q&A results are supplementary and the filtering is not a fatal flaw, this design choice introduces a potential bias and the defense is absent from the available text. 

- **The visual modality finding is not fully disentangled from plot redundancy**. The paper's headline result — that VLMs ignore visual cues — is supported by two pieces of evidence: the modality ablation (Figure 4) showing marginal or negative deltas when plots are added, and the near-random Q&A plot-matching scores. However, the ablation alone does not distinguish between "models ignore plots" and "the plot information is largely redundant with the numeric series and metadata" (average test series length is ~26 timesteps, and the metadata already provides domain context). The attention analysis is described qualitatively ("minimal visual grounding") without quantitative metrics (e.g., proportion of attention to plot region vs. text). The Q&A plot-matching results are stronger evidence, but the paper could sharpen the claim with a controlled experiment where visual input contains non-redundant information.

### Trivial

- The Q&A task description mixes counts without a clear total breakdown (e.g., "100 each" for three matching tasks = 300, plus 40 each for four comparison tasks = 160, sums to 460, but the text says "40 each for amplitude, peak, mean, and variance comparison tasks" which counts as four types, not three — the math works out but the phrasing is slightly confusing).

- The human detectability study reports 35 participants but does not state the number of captions each participant evaluated.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Expanding the human-revisited subset to cover a larger fraction of the test set (e.g., 2k+ samples) would further strengthen the benchmark's validity.
- Reporting quantitative attention metrics (e.g., fraction of attention weights on the plot region vs. axis labels vs. text tokens) would sharpen the visual grounding analysis.
- A controlled experiment where the plot contains information not present in the text (e.g., omitting some numeric values from the text input) would directly test whether models *can* use visual cues when text is insufficient.

## Removed Points
- **"Oracle model choice" concern** (only Gemini 2.0 Flash used): The paper addresses this through the paraphrasing robustness check (multiple architecturally distinct LLMs used to paraphrase ground truth, with Spearman ρ = 0.9266). Also, the human-revisited subset draws from multiple LLM candidates (Gemini, GPT-4o, Gemma 27B, Llama 90B). This criticism is partially addressed.
- **"Dataset imbalance" concern** (Health/Climate dominate): The paper uses macro-averaging across domains explicitly to address this. This is standard and reasonable.
- **"Missing sample size for human detectability"**: The paper states 35 participants. This is adequate for a human indistinguishability study; the exact number of captions per participant is a detail appropriate for the appendix.
- **Formatting/style nitpicks, missing appendix content critiques**: Removed per instructions.
- **"Reproducibility" concerns about undisclosed hyperparameters or missing supplementary**: Removed per instructions (code/data will be released upon publication).

## Novel Insights
The paper's most striking finding — that performance deltas from adding visual plots are flat or negative across nearly all tested VLMs (Figure 4), combined with near-random plot-matching accuracy — reveals that current VLMs are effectively functioning as text-only systems for time series, despite being nominally multimodal. This is not a weakness of the benchmark but rather a diagnosis of the field: the benchmark is doing its job by revealing this gap. The observation that even finetuning on captioning data does not meaningfully improve plot utilization (finetuned models also show flat visual deltas) suggests that the problem is architectural or pretraining-related, not solvable by task-specific adaptation alone.

## Suggestions
1. For the Q&A filtering, either (a) make Appendix J.2 accessible in the main paper, or (b) filter using multiple models (or human performance) to demonstrate that the difficulty increase is model-agnostic.
2. Add a quantitative attention analysis (e.g., percentage of attention weights allocated to the plot region vs. text tokens) to support the visual grounding claim.
3. Consider adding a small controlled experiment where the plot conveys information not available in the text (e.g., trend patterns from visual interpolation that raw numbers alone don't make obvious) to directly test whether models *can* use visual cues when they are non-redundant.
4. Clarify the human detectability study methodology and sample size more precisely. The 41.1% finding is striking and deserves full methodological transparency.
5. Release the human-revisited subset evaluation as a primary (not complementary) evaluation track in future work.
