## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for jointly modeling expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT). The core idea is to disentangle note-level score content from global performance style, enabling information sharing between the two inverse tasks. A diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates style embeddings from score content alone, supporting fully automated rendering. The framework uses only sequence-aligned data (no note-level alignment), and is trained on both paired and unpaired data via masked reconstruction. Experiments across objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analysis show competitive performance on both EPR and APT, effective content–style disentanglement, and plausible style transfer.

---

## Strengths

- **Genuinely novel unified framework for joint EPR and APT.** Prior work treated these as separate tasks. By formulating them within a single disentangled Seq2Seq architecture with shared content representations, the paper leverages their duality for mutual supervision. This is a well-motivated and novel direction.

- **Strong empirical support across multiple tasks.** Table 1 shows the joint model achieves the best MUSTER E_avg (12.48) among all APT methods, including statistically significant improvements on E_extra, E_onset, and E_offset over Beyer & Dai (2024). Table 2 shows Ours (Target) achieves the lowest MAE for duration (0.37) and velocity (10.33) among EPR methods. Table 3 shows Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among alignment-free methods.

- **Clear evidence of content–style disentanglement.** Table 4 demonstrates that style embeddings (z_s) strongly outperform content embeddings (z_c) on performer identification (42.07% vs. 9.94%) and composer identification (77.46% vs. 29.99%). Figure 3 visualizes these clusters, confirming that the disentanglement is working as intended. This is the cleanest evidence in the paper and directly supports the methodological claims.

- **PSR module produces era-coherent style embeddings.** Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style vectors cluster by historical era (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) with centroid locations and temporal progression matching those extracted from real performances. This provides convincing qualitative evidence that the module captures meaningful stylistic structure from score content alone.

- **Joint training with unpaired data improves EPR.** The EPR-Only ablation in Table 2 shows that removing the APT decoder and unpaired data consistently worsens KL and MAE metrics (e.g., KL(D): 6.43 vs. 5.51; MAE(V): 10.65 vs. 10.33), validating the contribution of the unified framework.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **PSR module is not compared against any baseline alternative for style generation.** The paper claims the PSR module generates "diverse and appropriate style embeddings" from score content, yet there is no comparison to alternatives such as (a) random sampling from the standard Gaussian prior, (b) a deterministic mean-style vector per composer/era, or (c) k-nearest-neighbor retrieval from a training-set style database. The only quantitative evidence is Figure 4 (era clustering), which shows the _distribution_ is plausible but does not demonstrate that the PSR module produces _score-specific_ stylistically better renditions than a generic default style. The subjective results (Figure 2) compare Ours(PSR) against full EPR baselines (DExter, VirtuosoNet), not against these simpler style-selection baselines. This is the most significant evidential gap in the paper — without it, a reader cannot tell whether the diffusion module adds value over a much cheaper alternative.

### Minor

- **Subjective evaluation lacks statistical rigor.** The listening test uses 11 participants and reports no inter-rater reliability (e.g., Fleiss' kappa or ICC). No formal significance tests are reported for the pairwise comparisons between Ours(PSR) and baselines in the subjective ratings (Figure 2). While the error bars and consistency across composers are suggestive, the evidence would be stronger with either a p-value or an effect-size estimate.

- **Unpaired performance dataset is under-described.** The YouTube piano cover dataset is mentioned as a data source for unpaired training, but no details are given on quantity (number of pieces, total duration), curation/filtering criteria, or quality of the audio-to-MIDI transcriptions beyond a brief reference to a "pilot study." Since the unpaired training component is part of the claimed contribution, the lack of description hurts both reproducibility and the reader's ability to assess how much the unpaired data contributed.

- **Alignment/insertion/missing metrics are weak evidence for EPR quality (though the paper is transparent about this).** Table 3 shows the non-expressive Score baseline achieving 93.52% alignment — higher than any EPR model. The paper grays it out and notes it is "not an EPR model," which is good practice. However, the fact remains that a model that faithfully reproduces note positions (with zero expressiveness) naturally scores high on this metric, while any expressive deviation (rubato, dynamic shaping) penalizes it. The paper's overall case for EPR quality does not rest on these metrics (it has KL, MAE, and subjective evaluations), but the presence of this table alongside the EPR methods is potentially misleading and should come with a clearer caveat that it measures note-position accuracy, not rendering quality.

- **Genre concept is introduced but never operationalized.** The Introduction discusses style vs. genre and hypothesizes that stylistically appropriate performances can be inferred from genre-related content. However, "genre" is not defined, measured, or controlled for in any experiment. The PSR module is conditioned on score content (which may encode genre), but the paper never isolates genre as a factor. This conceptual distinction remains an untested intuition.
