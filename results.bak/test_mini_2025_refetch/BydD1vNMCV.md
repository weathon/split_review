## Summary

This paper proposes the stochastic neural network (StoNet) as a bridge between linear-model theory and deep learning. It provides consistency results for sparse StoNet learning with Lasso (Theorem 1, Corollary 1), a recursive uncertainty quantification procedure via Eve's law, and a post-StoNet procedure that fits a simple sparse StoNet to the last hidden layer of a trained DNN to obtain prediction intervals. Experiments on synthetic data, CIFAR-10, and UCI regression datasets compare against temperature/matrix scaling and split conformal prediction.

## Strengths

- **Theorem 1 (Section 3.1)**: Proves consistency of the sparse StoNet with Lasso penalty, including explicit convergence rates and model selection consistency (P(γ̂_n^(t) = γ^*) → 1). This adapts high-dimensional linear model theory to the StoNet framework with non-trivial technical work.

- **Corollary 1 (Section 3.1)**: Extends consistency to DNNs trained with Lasso penalty under the StoNet assumptions, providing theoretical justification for a commonly used practice (sparse DNN training with L₁ regularization) that, as the paper correctly notes, had not previously been established.

- **Post-StoNet UQ procedure (Section 6.2)**: The idea of refitting a simple StoNet to the last-hidden-layer representations of a trained DNN for uncertainty quantification is pragmatic and reasonably motivated by the sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) argument from prior work. The empirical results in Tables 2–3 show that the method produces competitive calibration (ECE as low as 0.0047 on DenseNet40 vs. 0.0117 for temperature scaling) and shorter prediction intervals than split conformal on four UCI regression datasets (e.g., interval length 2.055 vs. 2.453 on Wine data).

- **Theoretical grounding for the bridging property**: Lemma 1 establishes asymptotic equivalence between StoNet and DNN log-likelihoods and parameter estimates, providing a formal foundation for transferring results between the two models.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Theory-experiment gap for variable selection (structural/evidential mismatch).** Theorem 1(iii) establishes consistency of *weight-based* structure selection — thresholding the estimated weight parameters ̂θ_i,n against c√r_n. However, the illustrative example in Section 5 measures variable importance by the **average output gradient** ∂μ̂/∂x_i along the Lasso path. The paper explicitly states "This example serves as a validation of our theoretical results" (page 6), but the criterion validated (gradient magnitude) is different from the criterion in the theorem (weight thresholding). No theoretical result connects gradient-based variable importance to the consistency of weight-based selection. The experiments are reasonable demonstrations on their own terms, but they do not validate Theorem 1's specific claim about weight thresholding. This overstates what the theory supports.

- **No coverage guarantee for post-StoNet prediction intervals (asymmetric comparison).** The prediction intervals in Section 6.2 are constructed via a plug-in Wald formula (Equation 7) using Eve's-law variance estimates and training residuals. The paper provides no finite-sample or asymptotic guarantee that these intervals achieve nominal coverage. While the empirical coverage rates in Tables 1 and 3 are close to nominal, the comparison to split conformal prediction is fundamentally asymmetric: split conformal provides distribution-free marginal coverage guarantees, while post-StoNet provides only empirical rates. The paper's claim of "superiority" on interval length (page 8: "demonstrate a significant improvement in terms of the lengths of the prediction confidence intervals") is therefore not grounded in comparable theoretical safety. The intervals may well be useful in practice, but the framing as "superior" over a method with formal guarantees is overstated.

### Minor

- **Strong but acknowledged assumptions limit practical scope.** The theoretical framework (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) assumes the data are generated by a StoNet with known layerwise noise variances σ²_{l,n}. The paper is transparent about this ("For simplicity of theoretical development, we will assume that for a given dataset, the true model is a StoNet model with known variances"), and Remark 1 discusses setting variances to small values in practice. However, the sensitivity of the convergence rates to misspecified variances (e.g., the terms σ²_{l,n}/σ⁴_{l-1,n} in r_n) is not analyzed. The theory's applicability to real-world settings where the data-generating process is not a StoNet with known variances is unclear, and the paper does not discuss this limitation.

- **Incremental extension of prior work.** The StoNet was introduced and its asymptotic equivalence to DNN was established in Liang et al. (2022). The present paper's core theoretical contribution — Theorem 1 plus Corollary 1 — is a natural extension of that framework by adding Lasso penalty and applying the asymptotic equivalence to carry consistency results to DNNs. While non-trivial, the novelty is incremental within the same line of work. The post-StoNet procedure is the most genuinely novel component but is presented without formal guarantees (as noted above).

- **Limited baseline comparisons.** The regression UQ experiments (Table 3) compare only against split conformal prediction. Stronger conformal baselines exist (e.g., conformalized quantile regression, jackknife+) that could potentially produce shorter intervals while retaining coverage guarantees. The CoverType feature-identification experiment (Figure 3) is purely qualitative with no comparison to other feature importance methods (e.g., permutation importance, SHAP) and no ground-truth evaluation metric.

- **CoverType experiment is illustrative, not evaluative.** Section 6.1 shows that gradient magnitudes vary along the Lasso path, but no quantitative assessment is provided: no ground truth for feature importance, no comparison baselines, no stability analysis. This experiment demonstrates the procedure works at a basic level but does not establish its effectiveness for feature identification.

### Trivial

- The paper's discussion of the IRO algorithm (Section 3) is somewhat vague, referencing equations A2–A3 in the appendix without summarizing them in the main text.
- The averaging of m prediction intervals in step (iii) of Section 4 is described without justification for why averaging intervals (rather than, say, averaging variances) is appropriate.

## Suggestions

1. **Align the empirical variable selection with the theory.** Either perform variable selection by thresholding the sparse StoNet weight estimates (as Theorem 1(iii) prescribes) and show that the correct inputs enter the model, or extend the theory to cover gradient-based importance measures. Currently the theory and experiments test different criteria.

2. **Add a coverage discussion for the UQ intervals.** Even a high-level asymptotic argument (e.g., under the assumption that the last-layer representation converges to a linear model, the Wald interval is asymptotically valid) would help. Alternatively, temper the "superiority" claims and position the method as a heuristic complement to conformal prediction.

3. **Compare against at least one additional conformal baseline** (e.g., conformalized quantile regression) for the regression experiments to make the interval-length comparisons more robust.

4. **Add a limitations section** explicitly discussing: (a) the assumption that the true model is a StoNet with known variances, (b) the lack of coverage guarantees for post-StoNet intervals, (c) the gap between weight-based theory and gradient-based experiments.
