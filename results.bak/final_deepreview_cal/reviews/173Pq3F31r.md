## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. It introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated rendering without manual control. The framework is trained using only sequence-level (not note-level) alignment. Evaluations on the ASAP dataset show competitive performance on both tasks, and representation analyses suggest successful content-style disentanglement.

## Strengths

- **Joint modeling of inverse tasks is novel and well-motivated.** The paper correctly identifies that EPR and APT are inverse processes and builds a framework that exploits this duality. Table 2 shows that the full joint model (Ours–Target) consistently outperforms the EPR-Only ablation on multiple metrics (MAE duration 0.37 vs. 0.42, KL velocity 1.76 vs. 2.05), demonstrating tangible benefits from joint training.

- **PSR module is a clean, practical contribution.** The idea of generating style embeddings directly from score content using a diffusion model is well-executed. Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style vectors cluster by historical era (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) with centroids and separation that closely mirror real performance embeddings. Subjective ratings in Figure 2 confirm Ours (PSR) achieves competitive naturalness ratings.

- **Seq2Seq formulation without note-level alignment works.** The paper demonstrates that alignment-free EPR can achieve competitive alignment rates (Ours PSR: 92.27%, highest in Table 3) without the preprocessing pipeline required by alignment-dependent baselines like DExter and VirtuosoNet. This is a practically meaningful result that could simplify EPR workflows.

- **Comprehensive evaluation methodology.** The paper evaluates EPR with three objective metric families (variance, KL divergence, note-aligned MAE in Table 2; alignment/insertion/missing rates in Table 3) plus a subjective listening test; APT is evaluated with MUSTER and ScoreSimilarity with statistical significance testing (Table 1). This thoroughness strengthens confidence in the reported results.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Content alignment between z_x and z_y is claimed but not explicitly enforced.** The paper states that "Score content features z_x and z_y... are encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption) and that "Both domains share a latent content space Z_c" (Section 3.2). However, the training objective (Eq. 6) contains no term that directly aligns z_x and z_y — no contrastive loss, no cycle-consistency, no shared decoder forcing cross-domain equivalence. The cross-domain tasks (EPR using z_x + z_s → y; APT using z_y → x) provide only implicit, indirect pressure for alignment. The z_x and z_y representations could theoretically encode score-relevant information in different ways while still supporting each task individually. The style transfer experiment (Figure 5) suggests the representations *do* work together, but the claimed shared-content-space mechanism has a structural gap in its training objective. The paper would benefit from either explicitly acknowledging this as implicit alignment driven by task compatibility, or adding a loss that directly enforces it.

### Minor

- **The EPR-Only ablation is confounded with data quantity.** The EPR-Only variant is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" while the full model trains on ASAP + 75,913 unpaired scores + YouTube transcriptions. The paper's claim that "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" correctly acknowledges both factors in its wording, but the experimental design does not isolate them. A cleaner ablation would train EPR-Only with the same unpaired data (using reconstruction losses) to isolate the effect of the APT objective.

- **The disentanglement evaluation (Table 4) compares representations at different granularity levels.** The style representation z_s is a single global vector (via [CLS] token), while the content representation z_c is a per-step sequence. The paper acknowledges this: "as a global embedding, z_s is better suited for capturing high-level stylistic features than the note-level z_c" (Section 5.2). The large accuracy gap (77.46% vs. 29.99% for composer) makes it unlikely the result is entirely due to this confound, but the comparison would be stronger if content representations were pooled to a global vector for fairer comparison.

- **Subjective listening test is undersized and underreported.** The test uses 11 participants and 5 pieces. No inter-rater reliability is reported. While objective metrics use Wilcoxon significance testing, the subjective ratings lack pairwise significance tests, making it unclear whether observed differences are reliable. The paper reports randomizing presentation order (a good practice) but does not specify blinding to conditions.

- **Dismissal of DExter's variance as "unstable dynamics" is a subjective judgment in an objective metrics section.** In Section 5.1, DExter's larger variance (e.g., σ²(D)=4.15 vs. human=1.72) is attributed to "unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness" based on subjective listening. This interprets a measurement gap using a post-hoc judgment, which weakens the objectivity of the evaluation narrative.

- **Sample 2 style transfer anomaly is not discussed.** In Figure 5, the "Mean" (interpolated) condition for Sample 2 receives higher similarity ratings (~3.0) than the "Target" condition (~2.4), which should be the ground-truth reference style. The paper only reports that "the Target condition achieves the highest style similarity ratings in Samples 1 and 3," omitting discussion of Sample 2. This anomaly could indicate imperfect style transfer, a flawed test design, or a genuinely interesting property of the style space — but it is not addressed.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding that emerges from these reviews is the tension between the paper's ambitious scope (jointly modeling two inverse tasks + disentanglement + style recommendation) and the piecemeal nature of the evidence for each component. The joint EPR/APT framework clearly produces tangible improvements over single-task baselines (Table 2), which is the paper's strongest claim. But the disentanglement story — the mechanism that allegedly supports the framework — has a weaker evidential basis than the paper's framing suggests. This is an instance of a broader pattern in representation learning papers: claiming a specific representational structure (shared content space) while only demonstrating that two task-specific representations *work together*, which is necessary but not sufficient evidence for the claimed structure.

## Suggestions

1. **Either add an explicit content alignment loss or revise the framing.** If z_x and z_y are meant to share a common space, add a loss (e.g., contrastive or cosine) on paired samples. Alternatively, drop the claim that they are "encouraged to align" and reframe the approach as using separate but compatible task-specific representations.

2. **Disentangle the ablation confound.** Train the EPR-Only variant on the same unpaired data (using reconstruction losses) to isolate the effect of the APT objective. This would cleanly measure whether joint modeling per se helps.

3. **Pool content representations for fairer disentanglement comparison.** Average-pool z_c over time and rerun the classification experiments. This would make the comparison between style and content representations more apples-to-apples.

4. **Address the Sample 2 anomaly in style transfer.** Either explain why the Mean condition outperforms Target on similarity, or acknowledge it as a limitation.
