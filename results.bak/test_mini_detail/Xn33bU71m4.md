## Summary

This paper systematically evaluates nine open-source LLMs (12–16B parameters) on two core reverse engineering tasks — name recovery and type inference — across 40,000 functions spanning four architectures and multiple optimization levels, plus real-world firmware decompiled by two tools. The evaluation is designed as a benchmarking study to fill the gap in principled model selection for binary reverse engineering, finding that all tested models achieve uniformly low F1 scores (mostly ≤0.10), though fine-tuning yields modest improvements for some models on variable name recovery.

## Strengths

- **Systematic large-scale evaluation across diverse conditions.** The paper evaluates 9 models on 40,000 functions spanning x86-64, x86-32, ARM, and MIPS at optimization levels O0–O3, plus real-world firmware. This breadth — 4 architectures × 4 optimization levels × 2 decompilers — is clearly absent from prior work, which typically fine-tunes a single model without justification. The uniformly low scores directly support the core finding that current mid-sized open-source LLMs are not reliable for these tasks. (Sections 4.1–4.4, Tables 2–6)

- **CodeWordNet metric for name recovery.** The paper adopts CodeWordNet to handle synonymy, abbreviations, and sub-word variations rather than brittle exact matching. Figure 1 demonstrates that removing CodeWordNet causes a sharp drop in F1 scores (e.g., from ~0.15 to ~0.05 for CodeLlama), confirming that models often generate semantically plausible names that strict matching would miss. This methodological choice strengthens the evaluation's fairness. (Section 3.4, Figure 1)

- **Clean controlled experiment on model size.** The comparison of CodeLlama 7B, 13B, and 34B shows negligible differences (Figure 2), providing concrete evidence that larger parameter counts within a model family do not guarantee better performance on these tasks — a finding that goes beyond typical "scale matters" narratives. (Figure 2, Finding 6)

- **Real-world firmware with two decompilers.** Testing on stripped firmware binaries decompiled by both Ghidra and IDA Pro (Table 6) adds ecological validity and reveals that decompiler choice can affect LLM accuracy — a practical factor overlooked in prior work.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Unvalidated auxiliary LLM for output extraction.** The evaluation pipeline uses an auxiliary LLM (unspecified model) to post-process heterogeneous model responses into a normalized schema (§3.2). The paper does not report which LLM was used, does not validate its extraction accuracy on any labeled set of model outputs, and does not analyze failure cases. If this auxiliary model systematically misformats correct responses or is too permissive, all reported F1 scores could be affected. Given that the models tested produce widely varying output formats (chain-of-thought, refusals, multiple candidates as documented in §4.5), the extraction step is nontrivial. Without validation, the quantitative results carry an unknown margin of error that the paper never bounds. This is the most significant methodological gap.

- **F1 = 0.37 claim unsupported by reported tables.** The introduction states: "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." However, in the main result tables (Tables 2–4), the highest value for DeepSeek-R1 is 0.17 (variable name recovery at O0, Table 3). No table in the main body shows 0.37 or clarifies which task/architecture/metric this figure corresponds to. The paper notes that results for other architectures are in the appendix (which is stripped by the parser), but the claim is made without any cross-reference, leaving the reader unable to verify it from the presented evidence. This damages trust in the paper's central quantitative claims.

- **Limited fine-tuning details and convergence analysis.** The paper fine-tunes for only 200 LoRA steps across 160,000 training examples (Table 7). It does not report batch size, gradient accumulation, learning rate schedule, training loss curves, or any convergence criterion. Whether 200 steps constitutes sufficient training depends on the (unreported) effective batch size — it could range from less than one epoch to one full pass. Without training curves or validation monitoring, the fine-tuning results cannot be properly interpreted, and the conclusion that gains are "inconsistent and not guaranteed" (Finding 1) may simply reflect insufficient training for some models. This limits the strength of any claim about fine-tuning effectiveness.

### Minor

- **Title and conclusions overreach the tested model class.** The title "LLMs as Reverse Engineers? Not Yet" and the conclusion "current LLMs remain fundamentally limited" implicitly generalize to LLMs as a class, yet the paper explicitly excludes larger closed-source models like GPT-4 (Section 4.2). The abstract properly scopes to "open-source mid-sized LLMs," but the title and conclusion do not carry this qualification. The findings are valid for the tested models, but the framing invites overgeneralization.

- **No prompt sensitivity analysis for performance.** The paper acknowledges that prompt templates strongly affect fine-tuning time (Finding 7) and that DeepSeek-R1's prompt was modified to suppress reasoning, but there is no systematic investigation of how prompt design affects performance scores. Given the well-known sensitivity of LLMs to prompt wording, the absolute F1 values reported are anchored to a single prompting strategy, making it unclear how much scores might shift under different prompts.

- **Fine-tuning time measurements lack sufficient context.** Table 7 reports wall-clock times for 200 steps that vary from ~33,000s (Phi4) to ~186,000s (DeepSeek-V2). The paper attributes this variance to prompt template design but does not report the hardware, batch size, or gradient accumulation configuration, making the raw numbers difficult to interpret or reproduce.

- Add a brief statistical significance discussion for the small differences between models (e.g., 0.02 vs. 0.03) in the main tables.

## Suggestions

1. **Most important**: Validate and document the auxiliary LLM extraction pipeline — specify the model, report accuracy on a manually labeled sample (200–500 responses across models), and discuss failure modes.
2. **Clarify the 0.37 figure**: Either add a cross-reference to the specific appendix table or retract/rephrase the claim to match what is shown in the main body.
3. **Add training configuration details**: Report batch size, gradient accumulation steps, learning rate, hardware, and show at least a validation loss curve to establish whether 200 steps was adequate.
4. **Qualify the title/conclusion scope** to "mid-sized open-source LLMs" to match the experimental scope.
5. **Add a simple prompt sensitivity check** for at least one model (e.g., 3 prompt variations × CodeLlama or Qwen2.5) to bound the effect of prompt choice on F1 scores.
