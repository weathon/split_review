## Summary

This paper validates the Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI) algorithm — an existing model-based RL method for learning minimal state representations — in a substantially more realistic domain than previously tested: the competitive card game *Hol's der Geier*. The authors show that GOEI reduces the observation space from 15,542 possible observations to just 452 states (2.9%) while maintaining a strategy that is statistically indistinguishable from the Nash equilibrium against an NE opponent (median reward rate −0.010). The paper provides an information-theoretic analysis of which observable features are retained in the compressed representation, and a hyperparameter sensitivity study.

---

## Strengths

1. **Compelling empirical demonstration of extreme state compression.** The core result is clean and striking: GOEI compresses the observation space from 15,542 down to 452 states (2.9%) while preserving near-optimal performance (reward rate −0.010, Table 1). At rounds *t* = 2 and *t* = 3, the number of discovered states (8 and 31 respectively) is even smaller than the number of action-value equivalence classes in the Nash equilibrium itself (Fig. 2B). This directly supports the claim that GOEI discards information irrelevant to outcome prediction.

2. **Information-theoretic analysis of what is retained.** Figure 3 decomposes mutual information vs. information loss per feature (SD, CT, AH, OH, RT). The finding that current table card (CT) and remaining table cards (RT) are preferentially preserved in early rounds while score difference (SD) becomes relevant only at round 4 is sensible and provides concrete insight into what the compressed representation captures.

3. **Robust experimental methodology.** Results are reported as medians and quartiles over 21 random seeds (Table 1). The learning curves (Fig. 2A) and hyperparameter sensitivity plots (Fig. 4) show consistent behavior across configurations. The separation of environment inference from strategy optimization is a well-motivated design choice for isolating inference quality.

4. **Honest discussion of limitations.** Section 5 openly acknowledges the passive-learning setup, the memory-constrained 5-card version, and the fact that state reduction alone does not guarantee interpretability. This candor is a strength.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Only one baseline, and it is too weak to be informative.** The paper compares GOEI exclusively to tabular Q-learning operating over the full 15,542-observation state space. It is well-known that tabular methods fail with large state spaces, so Q-learning's poor performance is a foregone conclusion. Without comparisons to other state abstraction methods (e.g., bisimulation-based abstraction, simple reward-relevant feature clustering, or even a model-based method using the full state space), the reader cannot determine whether GOEI's success is due to its specific state-reduction mechanism or merely to the fact that *any* model-based method with aggregation would perform well here. This significantly weakens the claim that GOEI's particular approach is responsible for the results.

2. **Evaluation uses a passive learning setup that does not match the paper's own motivation.** The introduction motivates GOEI as having "the potential to efficiently learn online" (p. 1), yet the experiments separate environment inference from strategy optimization entirely — training data is generated by two fixed policies (Rand vs. NE), and the agent never experiences the consequences of its own actions during learning. The paper acknowledges this gap (Section 5) but does not close it. This means the headline claim — "a nearly optimal strategy" — is demonstrated only under a specific, offline regime that sidesteps the interactive learning challenge that motivated the work. An online experiment (even a preliminary one) would substantially strengthen the paper.

### Minor

3. **Mutual information analysis treats features independently; "complex combinations" claim is unsupported.** The analysis in Figure 3 measures mutual information between the reduced state and each feature individually. The paper then speculates that "the required information is maintained in complex combinations of all the features" (p. 7), but this is an extrapolation — the analysis does not test whether feature combinations are preserved. Higher-order mutual information or a predictive analysis would be needed to support this claim.

4. **Reward rate averaged over epochs 1–3,000 conflates early learning with asymptotic performance.** Table 1 reports reward rates averaged over all 3,000 epochs. Since early epochs have poor performance (Fig. 2A shows the reward rising from negative values), this average understates the final converged performance. The learning curves in Fig. 2A are more informative, and the paper should clarify why averaging was chosen or also report final-epoch performance.

5. **Hyperparameter selection on the test metric.** The best parameters (β=0.2, α=25) are selected by grid search evaluated on the same metric used for reporting (reward rate against the NE opponent). This is a mild form of data snooping. The reporting of 21 seeds and quartiles partially mitigates this, but a held-out validation split would be more rigorous.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a structural tension in the paper that the authors do not fully address: the paper motivates GOEI for *online learning* where strategy updates affect future experience, but the evaluation is entirely *offline* (passive observation of fixed-policy games). This gap between motivation and evaluation is the single most critical limitation. Neither the harsh critic's framing ("evidential problem") nor the strength finder's framing ("clean experimental design") is wrong — they describe two different aspects of the same design choice. The design is clean *for assessing inference quality under fixed-policy data*, but it leaves the paper's motivating use case untested. The authors' own limitation paragraph (Section 5) confirms this, but the abstract and introduction still frame the contribution as demonstrating effective strategy learning in a realistic domain, without flagging the passive-learning boundary condition.

---

## Suggestions

1. Add at least one alternative state abstraction baseline (e.g., clustering observations by reward-relevant features, bisimulation abstraction) to ground the claim that GOEI's specific mechanism is responsible for the performance, rather than aggregation broadly.
2. Include an online learning experiment where GOEI updates its model as its strategy changes — even running on a smaller number of training epochs would address the core blind spot.
3. Report final-epoch reward rates alongside (or instead of) epoch-averaged rates to give a clearer picture of asymptotic performance.
4. Conduct a higher-order information analysis to verify whether feature combinations are preserved rather than speculating from univariate mutual information.
