## Summary

This paper introduces LEGO-EVAL, a tool-augmented evaluation framework for fine-grained 3D scene synthesis assessment, and LEGO-BENCH, a benchmark of 130 multi-constraint instructions (1,250 constraints total). LEGO-EVAL decomposes evaluation into four steps — constraint identification, tool execution planning, argument selection and execution, and constraint validation — using 21 tools across Environment Interaction, Textual Reasoning, and Multimodal Reasoning categories. Experiments show LEGO-EVAL (GPT-4.1) achieves 0.81 F1 and 0.63 Cohen's κ versus 0.40 and 0.05 for VLM-as-a-judge, and reveals that existing generation methods top out at 10% holistic success rate on LEGO-BENCH.

## Strengths

- **Large and consistent improvement over VLM-as-a-judge baselines.** LEGO-EVAL (GPT-4.1) achieves an F1 of 0.81 vs. 0.40 for the best VLM-as-a-judge baseline — more than doubling the score — and a Cohen's κ of 0.63 vs. 0.05 (Table 1). This gap is consistent across multiple VLM backends (Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1), strongly supporting the claim that tool-augmented multi-hop grounding substantially outperforms monolithic VLM evaluation.

- **Comprehensive experimental design.** The paper includes comparison against three baseline types (SceneEval, CLIPScore, VLM-as-a-judge), ablation studies disabling tool types (Table 2), end-to-end evaluation comparing auto-identified vs. oracle constraints (Table 4, showing ≤0.02 difference), correlation analysis between component performance and evaluation quality (Table 5), and a refinement experiment where LEGO-EVAL feedback boosts Holodeck's holistic SR from 8.5% to 18.5% (Figure 7). This breadth of validation convincingly demonstrates the framework's utility.

- **LEGO-BENCH exposes critical limitations in current generation methods.** All four evaluated methods (I-Design, LayoutGPT, Holodeck, LayoutVLM) achieve ≤10% holistic success rate, with performance collapsing to near 0% on instructions with 13+ constraints (Figure 6). Given that real user-generated room descriptions average 18.2 constraints, this empirically demonstrates a major gap between current methods and practical requirements.

- **End-to-end evaluation is reliable without manual constraint annotation.** Table 4 shows that using LEGO-EVAL's auto-identified constraints yields success rates within ±0.02 of using human-annotated constraints across four generation methods, making the framework practical as a fully automated evaluator.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **"Human judgment" ground truth is the authors' own curation, not independently validated.** The gold standard for the 260 instruction-scene pairs (Section 4.1.1) is derived from scenes the authors "manually curate[d]" — 130 from LEGO-BENCH that satisfy instructions and 130 that do not. These labels reflect the authors' design intention, not independent human annotation. The paper repeatedly frames its metrics as measuring "agreement with human judgments" (e.g., "Cohen's kappa measures agreement with human judgments beyond chance"), but no inter-annotator agreement, multiple raters, or independent validation is reported. While the labeling task is relatively objective (binary: scene satisfies instruction or not), the claimed "alignment with human judgments" overstates what was actually measured. This does not invalidate the core contribution — the gap between LEGO-EVAL and baselines is very large — but the paper should either report independent human annotation or reframe the claim as agreement with the curated gold standard.

### Minor

- **VLM-as-a-judge baseline could be further optimized.** The VLM baseline uses 4 scene images with self-consistency over 3 samples but does not employ chain-of-thought prompting, multi-step reasoning, or hierarchical decomposition that recent work has shown to improve VLM judgment accuracy. While the F1 gap (0.81 vs. 0.40) is large enough that even a stronger baseline would likely trail LEGO-EVAL, the headline claim of "more than doubling F1" would be more robust with a better-tuned baseline.

- **"Result of Holodeck" control in Figure 7 is confusing.** The paper states that LEGO-EVAL and VLM feedback are used to "refine invalid scenes" generated by Holodeck over 3 iterations. However, the "Result of Holodeck" line also changes from 8.5% to 10.5% across iterations, with no clear explanation of what this condition is — if it is a no-feedback control, why does the success rate drift? The paper should clarify whether this reflects re-running Holodeck independently (with randomness) or some other process.

- **No limitations discussion.** The conclusion is brief and does not acknowledge inherent limitations: the benchmark's modest size (130 instructions), reliance on proprietary LLMs (GPT-4.1), the assumption of Unity environment access with ground-truth scene data, or the fact that the 21-tool set is designed for indoor scenes and may not generalize to outdoor or dynamic environments. Adding a limitations section would improve scholarly rigor.

### Trivial

- **Minor naming inconsistency in constraint categories.** Section 3.1 lists four constraint types (Floor Layout, Material Selection, Object Selection, Object Placement), but Figure 4's table also includes an "Objects - Architectures" category that does not appear among the four types, and the percentages in the figure's OCR'd text contain apparent overlaps. The relationship between the four main types and this additional category should be clarified.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Independent annotation validation**: Even a small study (e.g., 30–50 pairs rated by 2–3 independent annotators reporting agreement) would significantly strengthen the claim of alignment with human judgment.
- **Error analysis**: For cases where LEGO-EVAL disagrees with the gold standard, a breakdown of failure modes (constraint identification vs. tool selection vs. argument errors vs. validation) would guide future improvements.
- **CoT prompting for VLM baseline**: Adding chain-of-thought prompting to the VLM baseline would make the comparison more definitive.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface one insight that the paper itself does not fully articulate: the tool-augmented decomposition approach represents a fundamentally different paradigm from end-to-end VLM evaluation — instead of asking a single model to perform all reasoning from pixel-level inputs, LEGO-EVAL decomposes the evaluation into concrete, independently verifiable sub-tasks (object existence, attribute checking, spatial relation verification) that draw on ground-truth scene data through specialized tools. This mirrors the broader neuro-symbolic trend in AI, and the paper provides strong evidence that for structured 3D evaluation tasks, this decomposition yields dramatically better results than monolithic VLM judgment. The fact that end-to-end auto-identified constraints match oracle-constraint performance (Table 4) further suggests that the constraint identification step is robust, meaning the framework's main value lies in the tool-based verification pipeline rather than the parsing stage.
