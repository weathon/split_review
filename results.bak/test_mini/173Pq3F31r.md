## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for joint expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT), built on disentangled note-level score content and global performance style representations. The framework uses a Seq2Seq architecture that eliminates note-level alignment, and introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The model is evaluated on the ASAP dataset with objective metrics (Tables 1–3), subjective listening tests (Figure 2), and representation analyses (Table 4, Figures 3–4), demonstrating competitive performance on both tasks and effective content-style disentanglement.

## Strengths

- **First unified framework for joint EPR and APT with mutual supervision.** The core idea — leveraging the inverse relationship between rendering and transcription through disentangled representations — is novel and well-motivated. The joint modeling benefit is evidenced by the EPR-Only ablation in Table 2, where Ours (Target) consistently outperforms EPR-Only across all metrics (e.g., MAE duration 0.37 vs. 0.42, KL duration 5.51 vs. 6.43).

- **Alignment-free Seq2Seq EPR achieves competitive alignment accuracy despite relaxed supervision.** Table 3 shows Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all methods, including alignment-dependent baselines DExter (91.27%) and VirtuosoNet (91.88%). This is a practical advance that removes a key bottleneck in prior EPR systems.

- **Quantitative disentanglement verification.** Table 4 provides clear evidence that style embeddings encode substantially more performer/composer information than content embeddings (Style→Perf accuracy 42.07% vs. Cont→Perf 9.94%; Style→Comp 77.46% vs. Cont→Comp 29.99%), confirming the representation separation is meaningful. This is validated on the out-of-distribution ATEPP dataset, strengthening generalizability.

- **Objective evaluation includes statistical significance testing.** Unlike many MIR papers, the authors report Wilcoxon signed-rank test results for both APT (Table 1, †/‡) and EPR (Tables 2–3, letter-based groupings), providing a rigorous basis for comparing methods.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **PSR module evaluation is almost entirely qualitative.** The PSR module is a listed core contribution (contribution 2), yet its evaluation in Section 5.3 relies solely on a 2D projection visualization (Figure 4) with no quantitative metric of distribution alignment, reconstruction fidelity, or style diversity. The claim that PSR "produces stylistically appropriate outputs conditioned solely on the score" is supported only by visual inspection of cluster centroids and by indirect subjective results. For a separately trained diffusion model, the absence of even a simple Fréchet distance between real and generated style embeddings, or a quantitative measure of intra-score diversity, is a significant evidential gap. This does not invalidate the joint modeling contribution, but the PSR module as presented is not convincingly validated.

- **Subjective evaluation is undersized and lacks statistical rigor.** The listening test (Section 4.3) uses 11 participants and 5 pieces — a small sample that limits generalizability. The results in Figure 2 are reported without any statistical significance tests or confidence intervals for between-method comparisons. The error bars are undefined (standard deviation? standard error?). Without pairwise tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank on subjective ratings), the claimed superiority of Ours (Target) over baselines, and the competitiveness of Ours (PSR), cannot be reliably assessed. Given that the subjective evaluation is the primary evidence for perceptual quality, this weakens the perceptual claims.

### Minor

- **APT pitch edit distance is notably higher than several baselines.** In Table 1, the proposed method achieves E_p = 3.08 compared to the best (Liu et al., 2022) at 2.02 and Shibata et al. at ~2.10. The authors argue this is inherent to alignment-free methods, which is a fair defense, but it tempers the "competitive" claim for APT.

- **Missing ablation of unpaired data contribution.** The joint model is trained on both paired (ASAP) and unpaired (MuseScore scores, YouTube transcriptions) data, but the contribution of the unpaired training is never isolated. An ablation trained *without* unpaired data would quantify the benefit of the large unpaired datasets and validate the masked reconstruction objective.

- **DExter variance characterization is speculative.** In Section 5.1, the paper claims DExter's larger variance "results from unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness" without providing supporting evidence for this specific characterization. DExter's KL and MAE on velocity are actually second-best among all methods.

- **Style transfer evaluation uses only 3 samples.** The style transfer subjective test (Figure 5) evaluates only three samples, which limits the conclusiveness of the transfer results. The "Original" condition is also not clearly defined in the main text.

- **Unpaired YouTube performance dataset details are sparse.** Section 4.1 mentions a pilot study for selecting the audio-to-MIDI transcription model but provides no details or error analysis, making it difficult to assess potential transcription noise propagation into training.

## Removed Points

- **Different output representations for score vs. performance (Methodology 3.1):** The paper clearly explains the pragmatic choice to adopt Beyer & Dai (2024) for score tokens and Huang & Yang (2020) for performance tokens, observed through degraded quality with the alternative. This is a design decision, not a weakness.
- **GPT-4o mini for era classification:** Used only for labeling pieces for visualization (Figure 4), not for evaluation. The reviewer agreed this is tolerable. Not a real weakness.
- **Missing related works / appendix details / reproducibility nitpicks:** These are ruled out by the filtering guidelines (parser strips appendix; cited works are assumed to exist).

## Novel Insights

The reviewers did surface one observation not explicit in the paper: the interplay between the joint training benefit and the PSR module is asymmetric — the joint training clearly helps EPR (Ours-Target > EPR-Only in Table 2), but the PSR module (which uses only score content without access to the joint-training benefit during inference) necessarily loses some of this gain (Ours-PSR metrics are generally worse than Ours-Target). This creates an inherent tension: the more one relies on PSR for automation, the more one sacrifices the joint-training quality improvement. The paper does not discuss this trade-off or quantify how much quality is lost when moving from extracted to generated styles.

## Suggestions

1. Add a quantitative metric for PSR evaluation — a Fréchet distance between real and generated style embeddings, stratified by era or composer, would directly validate the PSR contribution.
2. Expand the subjective evaluation or at minimum define the error bars in Figure 2 and report statistical significance (e.g., Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc) for the between-method comparisons.
3. Include an ablation training the joint model *without* unpaired data to isolate the masked reconstruction objective's contribution.
4. Clarify the "Original" condition in the style transfer evaluation (Section 5.2) and ideally expand beyond 3 samples.
5. Add a negative-result visualization: content embeddings colored by performer should show no clustering, directly addressing the style-in-content concern.
