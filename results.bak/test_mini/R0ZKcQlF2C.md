## Summary

This paper introduces ARENABENCHER, a framework for automatically evolving static benchmarks by generating harder variants of test cases while preserving the original task intent. The key innovation is a multi-model feedback loop: candidates are generated by an LLM, verified by an LLM judge, scored by aggregate loss across a sampled subset of models, and iteratively refined with in-context demonstrations of strong candidates. Experiments on GSM8K, CommonsenseQA, and AdvBench show that the multi-model (m=3) variant consistently produces larger accuracy drops and higher difficulty than the single-model (m=1) variant, while preserving alignment (91–94%) and fairness.

## Strengths

1. **Multi-model feedback mechanism with clear evidence of benefit.** Table 1 shows that across all six models and three domains, the m=3 configuration consistently produces larger accuracy drops (e.g., −47.7% vs −32.8% for Llama-3.2-3B on GSM8K) than the single-model variant, directly supporting the central claim that aggregating feedback from multiple models yields harder, more diagnostic test cases.

2. **Ability-aware generation preserves alignment despite increased difficulty.** The framework extracts a structured ability description (§3.1) and uses it to condition candidate generation. Table 2 reports alignment scores of 91–94% across all domains, and human annotation (§4.2) on 100 GSM8K samples independently confirms 95% alignment and 96% correctness — concrete evidence that the updated test cases remain faithful to the original evaluation intent.

3. **Iterative in-context refinement consistently amplifies difficulty without sacrificing fairness.** Algorithm 1 and the results in Table 2 show that difficulty increases substantially (e.g., GSM8K: from 9.9 to 41.4) while fairness either improves (GSM8K: 84.8→87.8; CSQA: 82.9→92.8) or remains stable, demonstrating that the iterative process successfully steers generation toward harder cases without introducing model-specific bias.

4. **Honest failure analysis.** The case study (Figure 2) transparently documents a failure mode where the generated query omits a necessary constraint and introduces unintended operations. This provides useful diagnostic insight beyond purely optimistic reporting.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **No comparison against any existing benchmark augmentation method.** The experiments compare only two variants of the same framework (m=1 vs m=3). Section 2 discusses MATH-Perturb, ARST, and other prior methods in detail, but the evaluation never includes any of them as baselines. Without comparisons, the paper cannot support claims that the approach is superior to prior work — it can only show that multi-model feedback beats single-model feedback within the same pipeline. This is the single largest gap and would need to be addressed for acceptance.

2. **The evaluation does not measure the paper's stated motivation (data leakage).** The abstract and introduction frame the work around data contamination, but the experiments never check whether the updated benchmarks are less contaminated or more resistant to memorization. They measure difficulty, separability, fairness, and alignment — which are reasonable desiderata for a general benchmark evolution framework — but the contamination framing creates a misleading expectation that is never fulfilled. The conclusion hedges by calling this "a first step toward... contamination-resilient evaluation," which is fair, but the paper would be stronger if it either measured contamination directly or dropped the contamination framing from the motivational narrative.

### Minor

3. **The alignment metric is measured by the same LLM used for generation, creating circular validation.** The alignment score in Table 2 (91–94%) is the fraction of items the LLM judge labels as aligned — not an independent assessment of true alignment. The human annotation on 100 GSM8K samples partially addresses this (95% alignment, 96% correctness), but it covers only one domain and is too small to calibrate the metric across all domains. The case study (Figure 2) confirms that the verifier can pass invalid queries, though the human annotation suggests such failures are relatively rare. Reporting inter-annotator agreement on the human evaluation would strengthen this evidence.

4. **No statistical significance or variance reported.** All results in Tables 1 and 2 are point estimates. The subset sampling of models for feedback involves randomness (sampling m=3 from K=6), so rerunning the pipeline with different random seeds would produce variance. Without error bars or multiple runs, it is difficult to assess whether the differences between m=1 and m=3 are meaningful beyond the consistent directional trend.

5. **The model pool is small (6 models ≤7B), and models used for feedback are also the evaluation subjects.** This creates a risk of overfitting: the benchmarks may be difficult and fair for these six models but not generalize to held-out models (e.g., larger models or different families). Evaluating on at least one model not in the feedback pool would substantially strengthen claims of general diagnostic value.

6. **Difficulty metric is based on the single best model (1 − max accuracy), which can inflate apparent improvement.** If the best model drops sharply while others drop less, difficulty rises even though most models are not much more challenged. The paper cites Li et al. (2025) for this metric, but reporting average accuracy alongside it would give a more complete picture.

### Trivial

7. The √K = ⌈√6⌉ = 3 heuristic is borrowed from random forest feature subsampling (Breiman, 2001), which is a reasonable intuition but not directly analogous to model sampling for candidate scoring. A simple ablation testing m=2,4,5 would clarify whether the specific choice is critical.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Ablating the number of refinement rounds R (e.g., R=1 vs R=3 vs R=5) to show whether iterative refinement provides additional benefit beyond single-shot generation with the same candidate count.
- Testing whether the fairness-enforcing uniform sampling (§3.3) materially affects outcomes via an ablation.
- Adding a simple baseline such as "generate candidates with GPT-4o and select the hardest by single-model loss" to isolate the benefit of multi-model aggregation from the rest of the pipeline.

The gap between motivation and evaluation is real and is kept as Major weakness #2, but this specific phrasing is not an overclaim.

## Novel Insights

The multi-model feedback mechanism with iterative in-context refinement represents a genuinely useful design pattern for benchmark evolution. The key insight — that aggregating loss signals from a diverse, randomly sampled subset of models produces test cases that are harder for all models while preserving fairness — is well-supported by the m=1 vs m=3 comparison across all three domains. This is a cleaner approach than single-model adversarial generation (which bakes in model-specific bias) or simple paraphrasing (which may not increase difficulty). The paper also demonstrates that structured ability extraction (§3.1) is effective at preserving alignment, which is a non-trivial result — the extracted ability description successfully conditions the LLM to generate variants that test the same skill even as surface form and difficulty change substantially.

## Suggestions

1. **Add at least two baselines to the experiments.** The most natural choices are (a) simple paraphrasing without ability extraction or multi-model scoring, and (b) single-model adversarial generation (as described in the related work) with the same computational budget. This would directly test whether the multi-model, ability-aware approach provides incremental value over simpler alternatives.

2. **Either measure contamination directly or remove the contamination framing.** A simple n-gram overlap analysis comparing original and updated queries against common pretraining corpora, or a membership inference probe, would connect the evaluation to the paper's stated motivation. If this is infeasible, reframe the motivation around benchmark saturation and discriminative power instead of contamination.

3. **Report variance for the main results.** Run the full pipeline (or at least the generation + selection stages) with 3+ random seeds and report standard deviations in Tables 1 and 2. This is especially important given the stochasticity introduced by model subset sampling.

4. **Add at least one held-out model (e.g., a 7B+ model from a different family not in the feedback pool) to demonstrate that the evolved benchmarks have general diagnostic value beyond the six models used in the loop.**

**Score: 5.0 — Reject**

The paper proposes a well-motivated, clearly described framework and provides consistent evidence that multi-model feedback outperforms single-model feedback within its pipeline. However, the evaluation is incomplete in ways that prevent the paper from meeting the bar for publication. The most critical gap is the complete absence of baseline comparisons against existing benchmark augmentation methods — the experiments only compare two variants of the same method, so the paper cannot demonstrate that ARENABENCHER advances beyond prior work. The second major gap is the disconnect between the contamination-motivated framing and the evaluation, which measures difficulty but not contamination. Additional concerns include the circular LLM-judge validation (only partially mitigated by a small human annotation), the lack of variance reporting, and the small model pool with no held-out evaluation.
