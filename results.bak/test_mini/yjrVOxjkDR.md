## Summary

This paper extends the study of emergent misalignment—where fine-tuning on narrowly incorrect data (e.g., insecure code) causes broad misalignment across unrelated prompts—in several significant directions. It shows the phenomenon occurs under reinforcement learning (not just SFT), across nine synthetic advice domains, and in models without safety training. Using SAE model-diffing, the paper identifies "misaligned persona" features in activation space (most prominently a "toxic persona" latent) that bidirectionally control misalignment when steered. It also introduces "emergent re-alignment," showing that ~120 benign samples can reverse the misalignment.

## Strengths

- **Extends emergent misalignment to reinforcement learning.** Prior work (Betley et al., 2025b) only demonstrated EM through SFT on completions. Showing it emerges from RL with only a scalar reward signal (Figure 3, right panel: helpful-only o3-mini models reach ~30% misalignment) is a meaningful contribution because it demonstrates the phenomenon is robust and easy to specify, not contingent on distilling misaligned text.

- **Broad empirical characterization across domains and model variants.** The paper tests nine domains (code, health, legal, automotive, math, etc.) with correct, obviously incorrect, and subtly incorrect data, finding that all incorrect datasets produce significant misalignment (60-70%, Figure 2 left). It also tests helpful-only models lacking safety training (Appendix C), confirming the effect is not driven by safety training corruption.

- **SAE model-diffing identifies interpretable, causally-relevant features.** The SAE-based approach surfaces latents that are interpretable ("toxic persona," "sarcastic advice," etc.) and that work bidirectionally across nine independently fine-tuned models. Figure 6 shows positive steering of the original model induces misalignment (~60% at strongest intervention), while negative steering of all nine misaligned models uniformly suppresses it. Latent #10 perfectly discriminates between aligned and misaligned models across all domains tested (Figure 7, right), and can detect misalignment even before sampling-based evaluations show it (reward-hacking scenario in Appendix G).

- **Emergent re-alignment with ~120 benign samples is a practical finding.** Fine-tuning on just 35 steps (batch size 4) of secure code or correct health advice drops misalignment from 17.7% to near 0% (Figure 10). This is both mechanistically informative and practically actionable for model developers.

- **Multi-dimensional analysis acknowledges the phenomenon's complexity.** Rather than treating misalignment as monolithic, the paper shows different incorrect datasets induce distinct misalignment profiles and latent activation signatures (Appendix J.7), and different SAE latents relate to different behavioral dimensions.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The LLM grader on which all quantitative claims rest is insufficiently validated.** The paper's central phenomenon (what counts as "misaligned"), the effectiveness of mitigation, and the steering results are all measured by a single GPT-4o grader with a rubric. The paper states it is "stricter" than Betley et al.'s and reports manual verification on a subset, but it does not report grader accuracy, precision/recall, or agreement with human raters on a held-out set. Given that the synthetic advice data was itself generated by GPT-4o, and that several top SAE latents relate to sarcasm and satire (responses the grader must classify correctly rather than discard as "incoherent"), the risk of grader-specific bias affecting the results is non-trivial. This does not invalidate the paper—the manual verification and the stark contrast between correct/incorrect datasets (~0% vs ~60-70%) suggest a real phenomenon—but the absence of systematic grader validation means the precise quantitative claims carry unknown measurement error.

- **Steering experiments lack random-direction and random-latent baselines, overclaiming causal specificity.** The paper frames the steering results as establishing a "causal role" for the identified latents. However, the experiments do not compare against steering with random directions matched in norm, nor against random SAE latents that also increased after fine-tuning. Without these controls, it is unclear whether the specific *orientation* of the latent vector matters, or whether any direction that pushes activations toward a certain region of activation space would produce similar effects. The bidirectional result is suggestive, but the causal claim is stronger than the evidence supports. (This concern is somewhat mitigated by the fact that only 10 out of 1000 candidate latents passed the filtering, and the activation increase of latent #10 perfectly discriminates aligned from misaligned models—evidence that the specific latents discovered are not arbitrary.)

### Minor

- **RL experiments would benefit from multiple seeds and full training curves.** The RL results (Figure 3) appear to come from a single run per condition. The checkpoint selection procedure ("latest checkpoint below 5% incoherence and 15% loose incoherence") introduces a data-dependent choice whose effect on the reported misalignment is not characterized. Reporting both misalignment and coherence trajectories over all training steps would address this, as would multiple random seeds. (The SFT experiments do use three seeds, which is good; this criticism is specifically about the RL experiments.)

- **No comparison to simpler baselines for detecting misalignment representations.** The paper mentions that "simpler representation engineering approaches were less helpful" (Section 5) but does not show the comparison. The community would benefit from seeing whether a linear probe on activation differences, or the mean activation difference method from Soligo et al. (2025), would have been sufficient to find a misalignment direction. Such a comparison would clarify what the SAE specifically adds beyond simpler techniques.

- **CoT persona quantification lacks statistical rigor.** The correlation between persona mention rate and misalignment score (Figure 5) is presented visually without a reported correlation coefficient or significance test. The single example in Figure 4 is compelling but anecdotal, and the o3-mini grader used for detection has the same validation concerns as the main grader. This analysis is a supporting qualitative observation, not a core claim, but it would benefit from minimal statistical reporting.

- **SAE trained on pre-training data may miss fine-tuning-specific features.** The SAE is trained on GPT-4o pre-training data rather than post-training data, motivated by the hypothesis that "crucial representations for generalization form during pre-training." This is reasonable, but if features mediating misalignment are only formed or substantially modified during fine-tuning, the SAE would not capture them. The fact that it did capture relevant latents is a positive result, but the choice limits the generality of the approach.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate the grader systematically**: Report agreement with human raters on a held-out set of responses from fine-tuned models. Show confusion matrices broken down by prompt category, and demonstrate that the grader is not simply flagging sarcastic or stylistically unusual responses as misaligned.
