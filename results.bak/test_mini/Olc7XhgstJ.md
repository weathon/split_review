## Summary

This paper proposes Steady Thought (ST), a training framework that mitigates "under-thinking" (excessive, unproductive thought switching) in large reasoning models. ST operates in three stages: (1) entropy-based thought segmentation, (2) generating correct thought completions by suppressing switching trigger words during decoding, and (3) thought-level preference optimization (STPO) that trains the model to prefer committing to promising thoughts over abandoning them. Experiments across three model sizes (1.5B–14B) and four datasets (MATH-500, AIME 2024, GSM8K, LiveCode) show consistent accuracy improvements (up to 5.3%) and token reductions (19–39%).

## Strengths

1. **Principled formalization of under-thinking as a preference optimization problem.** Section 2.1 defines commit vs. switch trajectories and formalizes the desired preference via a Bradley-Terry model (Equation 2). This provides a theoretically grounded framework that goes beyond heuristic suppression approaches.

2. **Thought-level preference optimization (STPO, Equation 7).** Rather than applying preference signals to entire responses (which mixes correct and incorrect reasoning), STPO conditions on the specific thought where divergence occurs. Table 4 shows STPO outperforms both SFT and DPO, confirming the value of this fine-grained, length-normalized objective.

3. **Consistent empirical gains across models and datasets (Table 1).** ST improves accuracy on all three model sizes and all four datasets (including the out-of-distribution LiveCode benchmark), while simultaneously reducing token counts by 19–39%. This consistency is the paper's strongest evidence.

4. **Evidence of reduced invalid switching (Table 2).** The percentage of correct intermediate thoughts (PCT) drops after ST training (e.g., from 54.90% to 40.40% on MATH-500 for the 1.5B model), directly quantifying that the model abandons fewer promising reasoning paths.

5. **Ablation isolating the preference optimization component (Table 4).** The comparison of SFT, DPO, and STPO on the same data pipeline cleanly demonstrates that the preference optimization design, not just additional training on correct trajectories, drives the simultaneous accuracy and efficiency gains.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Lack of statistical significance measures for headline results.** The paper reports "eight test runs" for AIME 2024 (30 problems) and "two runs" for LiveCode (400 problems) but does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical tests. On AIME 2024 with only 30 problems, a 3.7% accuracy improvement (e.g., 27.5%→31.2% for the 1.5B model) represents roughly one additional problem correct — well within the range of evaluation noise. Without variance reporting, the reader cannot assess whether the claimed improvements are reliable. This is the most significant barrier to accepting the results at face value.

2. **Comparison to inference-only baselines does not isolate the value of the thought-level design.** The main results (Table 1) compare ST, a training-based method, against NoThink, NOWAIT, and SEAL — all applied at inference time without training. A training method is naturally expected to outperform inference-only approaches; this comparison conflates the benefit of training with the benefit of the *thought-level preference design*. Table 4 partially addresses this by comparing STPO to SFT and DPO, but only for one model (1.5B) on two datasets. The main table should include at least one training-based efficiency baseline (e.g., SFT on short correct trajectories, or RL with a length penalty) across all models and datasets.

3. **Logit suppression in data generation conflates the preference signal.** In the Thought Completion stage (Section 3.2), chosen completions are generated by suppressing trigger words ("wait," "alternatively") during decoding. The preference optimization then trains the model to prefer these artificially constrained completions over the model's natural continuations. This means the learning signal partially reflects the suppression heuristic, not the model learning to recognize promising thoughts on its own. The paper does not ablate this factor — e.g., generating chosen completions via natural sampling and filtering for those that happen to lead to correct answers without switching. Without this control, it is unclear how much of STPO's effect comes from the preference learning versus from the model learning to mimic the suppression heuristic's output.

### Minor

4. **Insufficient direct evidence that flexibility to explore is preserved.** A central claim is that ST preserves the model's ability to switch thoughts when the current trajectory is unpromising. However, the analysis (Section 4.4) focuses on aggregate reductions in switching and correct intermediate thoughts. There is no analysis of problems where the first promising thought leads to a dead end and switching is *necessary* for correctness. Does the ST-trained model still switch in such cases, or does it rigidly persist on a wrong path? Case studies or a breakdown by problem type would substantiate the flexibility claim.

5. **Anomalous NOWAIT baseline behavior not discussed.** In Table 1, NOWAIT applied to Qwen3-8B on MATH-500 generates 13,274 tokens vs. the vanilla model's 4,724 — an increase of 181%. This is far outside the expected behavior of a method designed to suppress switching and reduce length. The paper does not comment on this failure or discuss whether baselines were tuned per model. While this does not affect ST's validity, it weakens the baseline comparison framing.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting insight emerging from the reviewers' combined perspective is the tension between the paper's novel *methodological framing* (thought-level preference optimization is genuinely principled and well-motivated) and the gap in its *evaluation design* (the chosen completions are generated via logit suppression, which may create a confound between the preference learning objective and the data-construction heuristic). This is not a fatal flaw — the approach could still work without suppression — but it is a design detail that the current experiments cannot disentangle. A second synthesis point: the paper's evidence for accuracy improvements (modest but consistent) and token reductions (substantial) is stronger than either individual reviewer's critique suggests when viewed together, because the gains are monotonic across all three model sizes and four datasets including OOD. The monotonicity partially compensates for the lack of significance bars, though rigorous variance reporting would still be preferable.

## Suggestions

1. **Report standard deviations or bootstrapped confidence intervals** for the main accuracy results, especially AIME 2024 (30 problems). Even a simple "average ± std across runs" would substantially improve credibility.
2. **Add at least one training-based efficiency baseline** (e.g., SFT on short correct trajectories or RL with length penalty) to the main table across all models and datasets, to isolate the benefit of the thought-level preference design from the general benefit of training.
3. **Run a control experiment removing logit suppression** in Thought Completion: generate chosen completions by sampling naturally and filtering for correct answers without switching. If STPO still works, the signal comes from preference learning, not the suppression heuristic.
4. **Analyze a small set of cases where switching is necessary** — problems where the model's first promising thought leads to an incorrect answer, and the correct answer requires switching. Show that the ST-trained model still switches in these cases.
