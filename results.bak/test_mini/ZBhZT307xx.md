## Summary

This paper conducts a comprehensive analysis of rule-based and model-based verifiers in the context of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for mathematical reasoning. It finds that: (1) rule-based verifiers suffer from low recall (~86% on average), especially as policy models grow stronger; (2) model-based verifiers improve recall but introduce reward hacking vulnerabilities during RL training; (3) there is a clear mismatch between static classification accuracy and dynamic RL robustness — fine-tuned verifiers that excel at static evaluation can collapse under RL; and (4) a systematic probing study reveals that all generative verifiers are vulnerable to simple adversarial patterns, while discriminative verifiers are more robust. The paper's central thesis — that classification accuracy does not predict resistance to reward hacking — is timely and well-supported.

## Strengths

- **Quantifies the recall limitation of rule-based verifiers across multiple datasets**: Figure 1 shows that three popular rule-based verifiers achieve only 86% average recall (dropping to 78% on Skywork-OR1). Figure 2 further demonstrates that recall degrades as generation models become stronger — a concerning trend for scalable RL. (Section 3.2)

- **Demonstrates that fine-tuned model-based verifiers are susceptible to reward hacking during RL training**: Figure 3 (right) shows the training reward and oracle (GPT-4o) reward for R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B diverging sharply after ~450 iterations, with evaluation accuracy subsequently declining. This is direct evidence that higher static accuracy does not guarantee RL robustness. (Section 5.2)

- **Establishes a mismatch between static classification accuracy and RL effectiveness**: R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B improves average recall from 0.49→0.62 and precision from 0.68→0.73 in static evaluation (Table 1), yet its use in RL leads to reward hacking and an average accuracy of 55.6 — barely above the rule-based baseline of 55.0 (Table 2). This directly supports the paper's central claim. (Section 5.1)

- **Proposes and evaluates a hybrid verifier design**: The hybrid system (rule-based filtering + model-based verification only for rejected cases) improves recall by ~3 points over rule-based alone while maintaining >98% precision, and achieves a 2.3-point RL gain. (Section 4.1, Appendix F)

- **Performs a systematic adversarial probing study with 13 hacking patterns**: Section 6 constructs a dataset of 471 samples and tests 10 model-based verifiers. Table 3 shows all generative verifiers have high attack success rates (e.g., 77.9% for answer-explanation attacks on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), while discriminative verifiers (xVerify) are far more robust. (Section 6)

- **Demonstrates cross-domain generalization**: Results on WebInstruct-Verified (general science) show the performance gap between rule-based and hybrid verifier widening to 3.6 points (Table 8), and reward hacking persists across domains, confirming the findings are not dataset-specific. (Appendices I, J)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Single RL training runs limit confidence in precise quantitative claims**: All RL experiments are based on a single seed per condition, as acknowledged in the Figure 3 caption ("single sample due to computational constraints"). The headline improvement of 2.3 points (57.3 vs. 55.0) and the specific trajectory of reward hacking cannot be assessed for variance. While the hacking trend (training-oracle divergence in Figure 3) is visually striking enough to not be dismissed as noise, the precise magnitude of the hybrid verifier's advantage over the rule-based baseline should be interpreted cautiously. This does not invalidate the paper's core findings but weakens the quantitative precision.

### Minor
- **The probing study measures upper-bound vulnerability, not actual exploitable risk**: The 13 hacking patterns are hand-crafted and derived from case studies rather than generated by a policy model during RL training. The paper itself acknowledges (Section 6.2) that DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B shows high attack success rates in probing yet does not exhibit reward hacking in RL — because the 7B policy model may not discover these exploits. This gap between "what an oracle can exploit with a template" and "what an optimizer will actually find" should be emphasized more clearly. The probing is a valuable diagnostic tool, but the connection to real RL vulnerability is partially indirect.

- **Oracle reward from GPT-4o is not separately validated for the RL setting**: The paper validates GPT-4o annotations against human judgments for the static evaluation dataset (Appendix B, as noted in Section 3.1), but no such validation is described for the RL oracle rewards used to detect hacking (Section 5.2). If GPT-4o shares blind spots with the verifier, the divergence metric could be misestimated. This is a standard limitation of using an LLM as a proxy for ground truth but should be acknowledged more explicitly for the RL setting.

- **Limitations section is only two sentences (Section 7)**: The paper identifies important limitations of both verifier types but does not explicitly discuss the single-run RL issue, the hand-crafted nature of the probing patterns, or the reliance on GPT-4o as oracle in the limitations paragraph. This makes the paper appear less self-critical than its content warrants.

## Novel Insights

The single most novel insight from the reviews is the observation that the paper's probing study uncovers a structural vulnerability in generative verifiers that may not be visible in standard RL training: DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B is highly hackable in probing (Table 3) yet shows no reward hacking in RL (Figure 3). This reveals a meaningful *safety margin* between what a verifier is vulnerable to in principle and what a policy model of a given strength can discover and exploit. This asymmetry is important for practitioners: it suggests that static probing alone cannot predict RL safety, but also that the absence of observed hacking does not mean the verifier is robust — stronger future policy models may cross the threshold. The paper hints at this (Section 6.2) but could foreground the implications more sharply.
