## Summary

This paper proposes a "context-driven" paradigm for Scientific LLMs in biology — instead of feeding raw biomolecular sequences, the authors feed structured annotations from bioinformatics tools (InterProScan, BLASTp) as textual context. Through systematic comparison across 7 models and 3 input modes (sequence-only, context-only, and both), the paper shows that context-only consistently outperforms sequence-only, and that adding sequences to context sometimes degrades performance. The paper argues this reveals a "tokenization dilemma" inherent to current sequence-processing approaches.

## Strengths

1. **Comprehensive and systematic empirical comparison.** Table 1 evaluates 7 models (specialized Sci-LLMs and general-purpose LLMs) across three input configurations and three task types (function, pathway, subcellular localization). This breadth makes the finding that context-only beats sequence-only robust across architectures.

2. **Wet-lab validation on genuinely novel sequences (Figures 5–6).** The paper tests on unpublished protein sequences that are absent from major databases, finding 100% (Rhodopsin) and 97.3% (PETase) accuracy for the context-driven method versus Evolla's 5% and 83.78%. This is a strong practical validation on uncontaminated data.

3. **Temporal degradation analysis (Figure 4).** The finding that context-driven performance degrades more gracefully over discovery years (slope −0.618) than Evolla (−0.923) is genuinely informative about the robustness of different approaches to sequence novelty.

4. **Layer-wise Evolla analysis (Figure 3, ARI tracing 0.945→0.916→0.809).** This provides a concrete diagnostic of where semantic misalignment occurs in the sequence-as-modality pipeline, which is a useful contribution independent of the paper's main claim.

5. **Novel framing of the "tokenization dilemma."** Whether or not the empirical case fully supports the strong conclusions, the conceptual unification of the weak-representation and semantic-misalignment problems under a single lens is well-articulated and likely to influence thinking in the field.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The "consistent degradation" claim is contradicted by the paper's own data (Table 1).** The abstract and the Section 5.1 takeaway state that adding raw sequence to context "consistently degrades performance" and that sequences "consistently act as informational noise." This is false as written. For 3 of the 6 models with a Sequence+Context condition — Deepseek-v3 (86.03 vs 84.99), GPT-5 (76.45 vs 75.76), and Qwen3 (85.90 vs 84.99) — adding the sequence *improves* performance. The paper's introduction more cautiously says "often," but the abstract and primary takeaway do not. This is not a minor wording issue; the paper's headline empirical claim is inaccurate.

2. **The evaluation confounds reasoning ability with information content of the input.** The context condition provides BLASTp-derived GO annotations from homologs and InterProScan domain descriptions. For the benchmark tasks (molecular function, metabolic pathway, subcellular localization), these annotations *directly* contain the answer or a strong near-answer. For example, a protein whose context lists "GO:0005215: transporter activity" and "Pfam: ABC transporter" has effectively been given its function via homology. The high performance in the context condition therefore primarily measures how well the toolchain retrieves the answer, not how well the LLM *reasons* over the context. The sequence-only condition must infer function from raw amino acid tokens — a fundamentally harder task. The comparison is thus asymmetrical by design, and the paper's conclusion that LLMs' "strength lies in reasoning over structured knowledge" does not follow from this experiment. The paper's discussion of leakage (Section 4) addresses the narrow concern of query-label lookup but not this broader issue: the context provides the answer *by construction* for these particular tasks.

3. **The representation-space analysis (Figure 2, ARI 0.958) is largely tautological as presented.** The context was generated by tools explicitly designed to annotate functional categories (InterProScan detects conserved domains, BLASTp retrieves functional GO terms). Embedding this already-discriminative text with Qwen-embedding and showing that the embeddings separate by function is a sanity check on the toolchain, not evidence that the context-driven *paradigm* produces superior representations. The comparison against sequence-based models' latent embeddings (which must learn functional separation from raw amino acids) is an apples-to-oranges comparison of input information density, not a meaningful test of the tokenization dilemma.

### Minor

4. **The wet-lab validation does not fully control for homology-based label propagation.** The paper claims the novel sequences are "absent from major databases," but the context was generated using BLASTp against Swiss-Prot. If these novel sequences have close homologs already in Swiss-Prot (highly likely for well-studied families like Rhodopsin), the BLASTp context would retrieve functional annotations from those homologs. The paper does not report whether the top BLAST hits for these sequences are members of the same functional family, so the reader cannot assess how much of the 100% accuracy reflects true zero-shot generalization versus homology retrieval.

5. **Efficiency comparison (Table 2) mixes cost models.** The comparison between GPU rental (Evolla) and API pricing (our method) is not apples-to-apples. The "23× cheaper" and "154× faster" claims depend on specific assumptions about GPU utilization, API pricing tiers, and hardware specifications that are not fully justified (the cost details are deferred to Appendix M, which is stripped).

6. **The central claim about "reasoning" is not validated by the task design.** The benchmark tasks (predicting function, pathway, localization from context) are answerable by extraction — the context contains the label. A test of actual reasoning would require tasks where the context is incomplete, deliberately ambiguous, or in conflict with the sequence. Without such tasks, "reasoning" is an over-attribution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablate context components.** The context includes domains (Pfam), GO terms (BLASTp), and occasionally ProTrek descriptions. An ablation showing which components drive performance would significantly strengthen the paper.
- **Report the proportion of test cases where the top BLAST hit has the same functional label as the query.** This would allow readers to assess how much of the context-driven performance is homology-based.
- **Calibrate the LLM-as-judge metric against human evaluators** or at minimum show agreement rates.
- **Design at least one task where the context is incomplete or misleading**, so the experiment genuinely tests reasoning rather than retrieval.

## Removed Points

- The harsh critic's claim that "the paper presents tokenization dilemma weaknesses as if they are newly identified" — the paper cites relevant prior work (Rao et al., Brandes et al., Peruz et al.) and frames the *synthesis* of these two weaknesses as a dilemma, not as the discovery of the individual weaknesses themselves. The framing novelty is reasonable.
- The claim about missing related work (RAG literature) — the paper covers tool-augmented approaches (GeneAgent, ChemCrow) and the positioning is defensible; RAG for scientific domains is a sprawling area and the paper's comparison strategy is focused on direct baselines.
- The claim that "the temporal analysis actually highlights dependence on database coverage" — the paper explicitly acknowledges this ("diminishing availability of rich, homologous information"), so this is not a new weakness but an observation the authors already make.
- The Strength Finder's claim #2 about "consistent performance degradation" — this is a restatement of a claim that is factually incorrect for several models, so it cannot stand as a strength.
- Several generic strengths from the Strength Finder (e.g., "addresses an important problem") — removed as non-specific.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation that emerges across the reviews is the asymmetry in how different model *families* respond to the addition of raw sequence to context. Specialized Sci-LLMs (Evolla, Intern-S1, NatureLM) all suffer degradation when sequence is added, while general-purpose LLMs (DeepSeek-V3, GPT-5, Qwen3) all *improve* or hold steady. This pattern — uncorroborated by the paper's analysis — suggests that the "informational noise" hypothesis may be specific to how specialized models' tokenization schemes interact with mixed biological/natural-language input, rather than a universal property of sequence tokenization. A systematic investigation of this pattern could yield genuine insight into tokenization design for scientific LLMs. The paper misses this entirely because it averages over models rather than analyzing the divergence.

## Suggestions

- Remove the word "consistently" from claims about degradation; it is factually wrong for multiple models. A nuanced analysis of *which* models benefit from sequence and why would be far more valuable.
- Redesign at least one evaluation task so that the context does not contain the answer — for example, provide the context *without* explicit GO term annotations and ask the LLM to infer function from domain composition alone, or design a task requiring combining information from context and sequence where neither alone suffices.
- Report the fraction of test cases where the top BLAST hit shares the query's function label, to let readers gauge the homology contribution.
- Tone down the "reasoning" language. The experiments show that tool-derived context enables accurate function prediction when fed to an LLM; they do not demonstrate that the LLM is "reasoning" over knowledge in any nontrivial sense beyond extracting labels from the context.
