## Summary
TokenCount proposes a training-free object counting framework that uses SAM without auxiliary models. It has two core components: (1) probabilistic prompt generation that iteratively samples prompts from a probability distribution over the image, and (2) output token-based verification that uses SAM's internal decoder output tokens with a TS-SS similarity metric to distinguish positive from negative instances. The method is evaluated on FSC-147 and CARPK benchmarks, achieving competitive MAE among SAM-only training-free methods (16.25 on FSC-147, 4.68 on CARPK).

## Strengths
- **Output token-based verification is a genuinely novel and well-motivated idea.** Repurposing SAM's decoder output tokens—which jointly encode semantic and positional information through bidirectional attention—as a discriminative feature for counting is underexplored. The paper provides concrete evidence for this choice: Table 5 shows output token space yields MAE 16.25 vs. 16.94 for image embedding space, and Figure 5 visualizes cleaner positive/negative separation in PCA plots. This is the paper's strongest contribution.

- **Thorough ablation studies support key design decisions.** Table 3 compares verification metrics (dot product, cosine, L2, TS-SS), Table 4 examines iteration count and prompts-per-iteration scaling, and Table 5 contrasts output token vs. image embedding spaces. Together these provide a reasonably complete picture of where the method's performance comes from.

- **Strong CARPK results.** MAE of 4.68 on CARPK outperforms all listed supervised methods (e.g., LOCA 9.97, BMNet+ 5.76, SAFECOUNT 5.33), providing compelling evidence for the method's effectiveness in structured aerial scenes with clear object boundaries.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Abstract contains a demonstrably false overclaim.** The abstract states the method "outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods," yet Table 1 shows TFCAC (a training-free method) achieves MAE 12.26 on FSC-147, substantially better than TokenCount's 16.25. The paper itself acknowledges this internally ("outperforming all training-based methods and training-free methods except TFCAC"), so the abstract is simply incorrect. This misrepresents the paper's empirical standing and must be corrected.

- **High RMSE (133.11 on FSC-147) is never discussed and is worse than many baselines.** While MAE is 16.25, the RMSE is substantially worse than TFCAC (56.33), LOCA (56.97), BMNet+ (91.83), and SAFECOUNT (85.54), indicating systematic large-magnitude errors. The paper acknowledges failure on "dense distributions of very small instances" in the Discussion but never connects this to RMSE or analyzes where the large errors occur. Presenting only MAE paints a deceptively positive picture of robustness.

- **No ablation isolating the probabilistic prompt generation from a deterministic baseline.** The paper claims probabilistic prompt generation as a core contribution that "enhances computational efficiency by intelligently exploring the image," but provides no experiment comparing it against a simpler alternative (e.g., uniform grid of prompts, or top-K deterministic sampling from the same similarity map) while holding the output-token verification stage identical. Without this control, it is unclear whether the gains over prior SAM-only methods (e.g., TFOC, MAE 19.95) come from the probabilistic sampling, the output-token verification, or their combination. This is a methodological gap that prevents the paper from fully supporting its contribution claims.

### Minor

- **TS-SS metric advantage over cosine similarity is very small (0.23 MAE) and lacks statistical rigor.** Table 3 shows TS-SS at 16.25 vs. cosine similarity at 16.48—a difference of 0.23 with no error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The trend across metrics is consistent (TS-SS is always best), but the paper's argument that non-hybrid metrics have "reached a performance plateau" and TS-SS "breaks through" is not compellingly supported by a 0.23 MAE difference on its own.

- **Inference-time comparison is claimed but not tabulated.** The paper states the method is "more than twice as fast" as TFCAC, but only reports its own time (1.69 seconds/image) without providing TFCAC's measurement or a direct comparison table. This weakens the efficiency argument.

- **TS-SS formula contains a likely unit inconsistency.** TS uses sin(θ), which conventionally expects radians, while SS divides θ by 360°, implying degrees. The paper should clarify which unit is used and ensure consistency.

- **Key hyperparameters and procedural details are unspecified.** The temperature parameter τ in Equation (1) is never given a value, and the method for aggregating multiple exemplar output tokens in the few-shot setting is not described. These gaps impede reproducibility.

### Trivial

- Only two qualitative examples are shown (Figure 4), both near-perfect. Including even a few failure cases would improve trust in the analysis.
- The Discussion's claim of "superior computational efficiency compared to state of the art" is too broad without supporting data.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A statistically grounded comparison of TS-SS vs. cosine similarity (e.g., with multiple random seeds or subset evaluations and confidence intervals) would either validate or simplify the metric choice.
- Analysis of error distributions: sorting test images by absolute counting error and characterizing failure modes would turn the high RMSE from an unexamined weakness into an informative characterization of method limits.
- A direct controlled experiment where prompt generation is fixed (e.g., dense uniform grid) and only the verification stage varies would cleanly demonstrate the value of output tokens independent of probabilistic sampling.
