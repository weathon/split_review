## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic, large-scale evaluation of nine open-source mid-sized LLMs on two core reverse-engineering tasks: function/variable name recovery and type inference from decompiled and assembly code. Using a dataset of 40,000 functions across four architectures and four optimization levels, the authors measure zero-shot and fine-tuned performance, alongside real-world firmware evaluation and factor analyses. The headline finding is that current LLMs, even after fine-tuning, achieve uniformly poor performance (most F1 scores below 0.1), indicating they are fundamentally insufficient for reliable reverse engineering.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale, multi-dimensional evaluation**: The paper evaluates 9 LLMs across 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS) and 4 optimization levels on a dataset of 40,000 functions. This scale is genuinely impressive and goes beyond typical single-architecture, single-task benchmarks in the reverse-engineering literature (Tables 2–6, Section 4.1).

- **Real-world firmware validation**: The evaluation on stripped real-world firmware using both Ghidra and IDA Pro decompilers (Table 6) adds external validity that most LLM benchmarking papers lack. The consistent finding that IDA Pro outputs yield slightly better results offers practical guidance to practitioners.

- **Informative factor analyses beyond aggregate metrics**: The isolation of CodeWordNet's contribution (Figure 1), the comparison of model size vs. training data quality (Figure 2; Findings 4–6), and the prompt-template sensitivity analysis for fine-tuning time (Table 7, Finding 7) provide actionable insights for future model selection and evaluation design.

- **Well-motivated research gap**: The paper clearly identifies and justifies the absence of systematic LLM benchmarking in binary reverse engineering, contrasting it with established benchmarking practices in other CS domains. The framing is clear and the motivation is persuasive (Sections 1–2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None verified from the paper as written.

### Major

- **Figure 1 / Table 2 numerical discrepancy**: Figure 1 reports function name recovery F1 scores with CodeWordNet in the range ~0.15–0.25 for the original (non-fine-tuned) models on x86-64. Table 2, also reporting function name recovery F1 on x86-64, shows values ≤0.06 for the same models. Since Section 3.4 states that name recovery evaluation uses CodeWordNet, these should be the same measurement, yet they differ by a factor of 3–5×. The "w/o CodeWordNet" bars in Figure 1 (~0.02–0.08) are closer to Table 2 values, raising the possibility that Table 2 may have been computed without CodeWordNet — contradicting Section 3.4's description. This inconsistency makes it impossible for a reader to determine which numbers are correct across the paper's main results. The authors must resolve and explain this discrepancy, as it directly affects trust in the quantitative evaluation.

- **Unsubstantiated headline claim (0.37 F1)**: The Introduction states that "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." No table in the main paper supports this number; the highest DeepSeek-R1 fine-tuned F1 appearing in any main-paper table is 0.17 (Table 3, O0). The remaining architectures' results are deferred to the Appendix (which was stripped by the parser), so the 0.37 may originate there. Regardless, a headline quantitative claim in the Introduction should be traceable to results presented in the paper, and this one is not.

### Minor

- **Unvalidated output extraction pipeline**: The paper uses an auxiliary LLM to post-process heterogeneous model outputs into a standardized format (Section 3.2). No validation of this extraction step's accuracy is provided. If the auxiliary LLM miscategorizes or drops correct predictions, the reported F1 scores could be distorted — particularly concerning given the already near-zero performance. A small manual validation on a few hundred examples would substantially strengthen confidence in the pipeline.

- **No variance estimates or confidence intervals**: All results are reported as point F1 scores without standard deviations, bootstrap confidence intervals, or any measure of statistical variability. With absolute scores near zero and reported differences between models often in the range of 0.01–0.03, readers cannot assess whether observed differences are meaningful or noise. This is especially important for claims about fine-tuning "reducing performance by half" or one model "outperforming" another.

- **Fine-tuning details absent from main paper**: LoRA hyperparameters (rank, learning rate, training prompt format) are not specified in the main paper. The paper states the Appendix contains them, but these are essential for reproducibility and should be summarized in the main text.

- **Firmware evaluation uses only original models**: Table 6 evaluates only the non-fine-tuned models on real-world firmware. Including fine-tuned models would test whether the observed improvements on the main benchmark generalize beyond the training distribution — a practically important question given the paper's motivation.

### Trivial

- Table 5 reports 0.00 F1 for all assembly-code experiments. Reporting additional decimal places (e.g., 0.003 vs. 0.000) would help readers gauge whether models are producing occasional correct predictions or are truly at floor performance.

- Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals on the main F1 values.

## Novel Insights

The reviews reveal an important calibration lesson for LLM benchmarking papers: a Figure 1 / Table 2 numerical discrepancy of the magnitude observed here (3–5×) is severe enough to undermine reader trust even when other experiments appear internally consistent. The paper's architecture — where the CodeWordNet experiment (Figure 1) and the main evaluation (Table 2) should produce identical numbers but don't — illustrates how fragile multi-experiment benchmarking papers are to a single data-reporting error. For future benchmarking work, automated cross-validation of numbers between tables and figures would be a valuable addition to the review process.
