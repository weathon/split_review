# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces Automatic Complementary Separation Pruning (ACSP), a novel and fully automated method for pruning convolutional neural networks that focuses on accelerating inference time. ACSP combines structured and activation-based pruning to remove redundant neurons and channels while preserving essential components. Tailored for supervised learning tasks, ACSP constructs a graph space that encodes the separation capabilities of each component across all class pairs. By leveraging complementary selection principles and clustering techniques, ACSP ensures that the selected components maintain diverse and complementary separation capabilities, reducing redundancy and maintaining high network performance. The pruning volume is determined automatically, removing the need for manual tuning. This approach significantly reduces the number of FLOPs (floating-point operations) and results in faster inference time without compromising accuracy.

## Strengths
1. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
2. The proposed ACSP method is novel and fully automates the pruning process, eliminating the need for manual intervention.
3. Extensive experiments on multiple architectures and datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of ACSP in reducing computation while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Weaknesses
1. The proposed ACSP method is limited to supervised learning tasks, which restricts its applicability in areas like self-supervised learning and unsupervised learning.
2. The computational overhead of building the separation graph may be significant for large datasets with many classes, potentially limiting the scalability of the method.
3. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of ACSP on convolutional neural networks, it is unclear how well the method generalizes to other types of neural network architectures, such as transformers.

## Questions
1. How does the ACSP method perform on other types of neural network architectures, such as transformers?
2. Can the proposed method be adapted to handle unsupervised or self-supervised learning tasks?
3. How does the computational overhead of building the separation graph scale with the number of classes in the dataset? Are there any strategies to mitigate this overhead for large datasets?
4. How does the ACSP method compare to other state-of-the-art pruning techniques in terms of the trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency?
5. Are there any specific hyperparameters that need to be tuned for the ACSP method, and how sensitive is the performance to these hyperparameters?
