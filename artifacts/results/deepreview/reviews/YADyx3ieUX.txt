# Review

## Summary
The paper challenges the idea that neural networks are inherently black boxes, arguing that the commonly held assumption about the necessity of intermediary correlates in causal relationships is flawed. The authors use examples from recent research, such as the "secret owl" phenomenon in language models, to illustrate that causation does not always imply correlative continuity. They argue that this reconfiguration of causality has significant implications for how we understand and interact with AI systems, particularly in terms of trust and transparency.

## Strengths
The paper presents a novel philosophical argument about the nature of causality in neural networks, challenging a widely held assumption about the necessity of intermediary correlates. It provides a fresh perspective on the problem of opacity in AI models.

The paper is well-structured, with clear and logical arguments presented. The use of examples, such as the "secret owl" phenomenon, helps to illustrate the abstract concepts discussed.

The paper addresses a significant issue in AI explainability, offering a new way to think about the problem of opacity. This has implications for how trust is placed in AI systems, particularly in high-stakes applications.

## Weaknesses
The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the practical implications of the proposed theory. While the philosophical argument is well-developed, it is less clear how this might influence the design or evaluation of AI systems in practice.

The paper focuses primarily on a few high-profile examples to illustrate the theoretical points. While these examples are interesting, they may not fully capture the complexity of most real-world AI applications.

The paper could do a better job of situating its contributions within the existing literature on AI explainability and transparency. It would be helpful to see how this work fits into the broader context of research in this area.

## Questions
How might the proposed theory of causality influence the design or evaluation of AI systems in practice? Are there specific implications for the development of more transparent or explainable models?

How does this work fit into the broader context of the existing literature on AI explainability and transparency? Are there other theories or approaches that are compatible with the proposed view of causality, or does this challenge some existing paradigms in the field?

The paper uses a few high-profile examples to illustrate the theoretical points. How generalizable are these examples to other AI applications? Are there other examples that might better illustrate the theoretical arguments presented?
