# Review

## Summary
This paper proposes two aggregation algorithms, Optimal Weight (OW) and Inverse Surprising Popularity (ISP), to enhance the performance of multi-agent large language model (LLM) reasoning. OW leverages first-order information by assigning weights to each model’s answer based on its expected accuracy, while ISP leverages second-order information by analyzing the correlations among models' predictions. The authors demonstrate that both methods outperform standard majority voting through theoretical analysis and empirical validation on synthetic datasets, popular benchmarks like UltraFeedback and MMLU, and a real-world healthcare dataset, ARMMAN.

## Strengths
1. The paper introduces a novel approach to aggregating LLM responses by leveraging higher-order information, which goes beyond the traditional majority voting method. The use of first-order information in OW and second-order information in ISP demonstrates creativity in addressing the heterogeneity and correlation across models.
2. The paper is well-structured, with a clear problem statement, related work, methodology, theoretical analysis, and experimental validation. The authors provide detailed explanations of the algorithms, making it easier for readers to understand the proposed methods.
3. The significance of this work lies in its potential impact on multi-agent LLM reasoning. By demonstrating that higher-order information can improve aggregation performance, the paper contributes to the development of more robust and reliable LLM reasoning systems. The theoretical insights and empirical results may inspire further research in this area.

## Weaknesses
1. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of OW and ISP in specific settings, it would be valuable to see how these methods perform in more diverse and complex scenarios, such as open-ended question answering or more challenging reasoning tasks. 
2. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity or runtime of the proposed methods compared to majority voting. 
3. The authors could provide more details on how the expected accuracy for each model is estimated in the OW algorithm, especially in the context of unsupervised learning where ground truth labels are not available.
4. The authors could discuss the potential limitations of relying on second-order information, such as cases where the models' predictions may not be reliable indicators of the true answer.

## Questions
1. How does the performance of OW and ISP change with an increasing number of models? Is there a point of diminishing returns, and how does this relate to the diversity and expertise of the models?
2. How do the proposed methods handle cases where the models' predictions are conflicting or inconsistent? Is there a mechanism to resolve or weigh these conflicts more effectively?
3. How does the computational complexity of OW and ISP compare to majority voting, especially for larger datasets or more complex reasoning tasks? Are there any optimizations that can be applied to improve efficiency?
4. How does the aggregation method perform in real-time applications where responses need to be generated quickly? Are there any trade-offs between accuracy and speed?
