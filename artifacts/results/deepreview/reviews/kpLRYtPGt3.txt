# Review

## Summary
This paper presents a method to improve the performance of generative models by fine-tuning on synthetic data and then extrapolating in the opposite direction of the update. The authors demonstrate that this approach, called Neon, improves the performance of diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, and few-step models on CIFAR-10, FFHQ, and ImageNet datasets. The method is shown to be effective with as few as 1k synthetic samples and typically uses less than 1% additional training compute.

## Strengths
1. The paper introduces a novel method, Neon, that leverages the degradation from self-training as a signal for self-improvement. This approach is innovative and counterintuitive, providing a new perspective on using synthetic data for model enhancement.
2. The authors provide a rigorous theoretical analysis to explain why Neon works. They prove that typical inference samplers create an anti-alignment between the synthetic and real data population gradients, and that negative extrapolation corrects this anti-alignment to better align the model with the true data distribution.
3. The paper demonstrates the universality of Neon across a range of architectures, including diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, and few-step models. This indicates that the method is not limited to specific architectures but can be broadly applied to different types of generative models.
4. The authors conduct extensive experiments on multiple datasets, including ImageNet, CIFAR-10, and FFHQ. The results consistently show that Neon improves the performance of the base models, achieving state-of-the-art FID scores with only a small additional training compute.
5. The paper highlights the transferability of the degradation signal across different architectures, allowing synthetic data from one model architecture to improve another. This feature makes Neon practical when generating samples from the target model is costly.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other synthetic data augmentation methods, such as Discriminator Guidance, SIMS, and DDO. While it mentions these methods in the related work section, it does not compare their performance with Neon in terms of effectiveness and computational overhead.
2. The authors do not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of Neon to the quality of synthetic data. While they mention that Neon is robust to the quality of synthetic data, they do not provide a quantitative analysis of how the performance of Neon varies with the quality of the synthetic data.
3. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of Neon compared to other synthetic data augmentation methods. While it mentions that Neon typically uses less than 1% additional training compute, it does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other methods in terms of computational efficiency.
4. The authors do not provide a detailed analysis of the scalability of Neon to larger datasets and more complex models. While they demonstrate the effectiveness of Neon on multiple datasets and architectures, they do not provide a study on how the method scales with increasing data size and model complexity.

## Questions
1. Can you provide a more comprehensive comparison with other synthetic data augmentation methods, such as Discriminator Guidance, SIMS, and DDO? How does Neon compare to these methods in terms of effectiveness and computational overhead?
2. Can you provide a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of Neon to the quality of synthetic data? How does the performance of Neon vary with the quality of the synthetic data, and are there any thresholds or quality requirements for the synthetic data to ensure optimal performance?
3. Can you provide a more detailed analysis of the computational overhead of Neon compared to other synthetic data augmentation methods? How does the computational efficiency of Neon compare to other methods, and what are the trade-offs between performance improvement and computational cost?
4. Can you provide a detailed analysis of the scalability of Neon to larger datasets and more complex models? How does the method perform as the data size and model complexity increase, and are there any potential challenges or limitations in scaling up the approach?
