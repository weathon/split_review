Now writing the final review.## Summary

This paper evaluates nine open-source LLMs (7B–16B) on two reverse engineering tasks—name recovery and type inference—using stripped binaries. It tests models under zero-shot and LoRA fine-tuned conditions, across 40,000 functions from 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS) at 4 optimization levels, plus real-world firmware. The headline finding is that all evaluated models achieve very low F1 scores (<0.1 in most settings) and that fine-tuning yields limited and inconsistent gains.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale systematic comparison.** The paper evaluates 9 models on 40k functions across 4 architectures × 4 optimization levels, providing the broadest comparison of LLMs on these tasks to date. This scope is a genuine resource for practitioners selecting models for reverse engineering pipelines.

- **Real-world firmware validation.** Table 6 shows that the low performance extends beyond synthetic datasets to stripped firmware decompiled by Ghidra and IDA Pro, strengthening the paper's practical relevance.

- **CodeWordNet analysis quantifies semantic similarity.** Figure 1 demonstrates that removing CodeWordNet causes sharp F1 drops, confirming that models do produce semantically related names even when exact matches fail. This provides useful evidence that the low scores reflect genuine difficulty rather than trivial naming differences.

- **Model size vs. training quality insight.** The analysis shows that CodeLlama 13B matches its 34B variant (Figure 2), and Qwen2.5 outperforms the fine-tuned DeepSeek-R1 despite identical architecture. These findings (Findings 4–6) give practical guidance for model selection.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Name recovery F1 metric is underspecified.** The paper states it adopts the SYMLM method with CodeWordNet to compute semantic distances and obtain "partial scores," then reports F1 scores. The conversion from continuous similarity scores to binary-classification precision/recall/F1 is never explained: no threshold is given, no aggregation rule for multiple sub-words is defined, and the treatment of false positives/negatives is unclear. This makes the paper's central quantitative results uninterpretable and unreproducible. While the SYMLM paper (Jin et al., 2022) is cited, the current paper must define its own metric pipeline.

2. **No comparison to existing reverse engineering baselines.** The paper's core claim is that current LLMs are "fundamentally limited" on these tasks, but without comparing to specialized methods (SYMLM, ReSYM, VARBERT, Debin, or even a simple frequency-based baseline), the absolute F1 scores cannot be calibrated. An F1 of 0.33 on variable name recovery (Table 3) may be competitive with the state of the art, or far below it—the reader cannot tell. The paper does not need to beat existing methods, but it must contextualize its scores.

3. **Fine-tuning experiments are insufficiently executed to support the claims.** Only 200 training steps are used (no batch size reported) on a dataset of 160,000 functions—likely far from convergence. No LoRA hyperparameters (rank, alpha, learning rate) are reported. No validation set is used; test-set results are reported directly without checkpoint selection. Fine-tuning *reduces* performance in several cases (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 at O1 variable name recovery: 0.10→0.05), suggesting training instability. The paper's conclusions about fine-tuning's "limited impact" (Finding 2) and that "gains are inconsistent" (Finding 1) cannot be separated from these experimental limitations.

### Minor

4. **Type inference uses strict exact matching without canonicalization.** Types in C appear under multiple equivalent names (e.g., `int` vs. `int32_t`, `unsigned long` vs. `size_t`). The paper acknowledges strict matching makes the task harder but does not bound the effect with an alias-aware metric. The conclusion that "type inference is more challenging" than name recovery may partly reflect metric strictness.

5. **Auxiliary LLM extraction pipeline is unvalidated.** The paper uses an auxiliary LLM to extract structured predictions from heterogeneous model outputs, but provides no evaluation of extraction accuracy. Given the described variability (chain-of-thought, refusals, multiple candidates, truncation), the extraction step is a potential source of systematic error that could affect all reported scores.

6. **Assembly-based function name recovery (Table 5) shows 0.00 for all models under all conditions.** While very low scores are plausible for this difficult task, the *uniformity* of zero across 9 models × 2 conditions (original/fine-tuned) is suspicious and warrants investigation. Even a trivial baseline predicting the most common function name would score above zero, suggesting a possible pipeline-level issue (e.g., systematic extraction failure).

## Novel Insights

The paper's main empirical finding—that performance does not scale with model size within the CodeLlama family (7B ≈ 13B ≈ 34B) and that Qwen2.5 outperforms its fine-tuned variant DeepSeek-R1—is a genuinely useful caution for practitioners. ## Suggestions

1. **Define the F1 conversion for name recovery explicitly.** Provide the exact formula or algorithm that maps CodeWordNet sub-word similarities to precision, recall, and F1. Publish the evaluation code.
2. **Add at least one baseline.** Run a simple frequency-based predictor (most common name in training set) and, if feasible, one published method (e.g., SYMLM) on the same dataset. This would immediately contextualize all reported F1 scores.
3. **Extend fine-tuning to demonstrate convergence.** Report the number of steps at which validation loss plateaus, and perform a minimal hyperparameter sweep (learning rate, LoRA rank). Without this, the fine-tuning conclusions are unsubstantiated.
4. **Validate the extraction pipeline.** Manually inspect a random sample of 100–200 raw outputs across models to measure extraction accuracy. Report the error rate and discuss its impact on scores.
5. **Soften the type inference metric.** Add a secondary metric that normalizes common type aliases (`size_t`→`unsigned long`, `int32_t`→`int`) to bound the strictness effect.
