## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments the RL objective with a KL divergence between predicted outcome distributions $p(o|s,a)$ and preferred outcome distributions $q(o|s)$, weighted by a parameter $\beta$. The claimed central novelty is a Bayesian scheme that adapts $\beta$ online, eliminating manual trade-off tuning. The paper also provides theoretical analysis (Propositions 1–2, characterizing static $\beta$ behavior) and experiments on a 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey.

---

## Strengths

1. **KL-based shaping improves sample efficiency on sparse-reward tasks.** In the 2-state MDP (Table 1), all tested $\beta>0$ values raise cumulative reward from 16.57 ($\beta=0$) to ~28.86 and substantially reduce variance. In MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Figure 2), $\beta=0.01$ achieves 98% success rate vs. 90.5% for $\beta=0$, and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps. These results demonstrate that the proposed KL shaping term can accelerate learning in sparse-reward settings.

2. **The paper provides a high-level cognitive/neuroscience framing** linking the KL preference-prediction gap to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error, which may interest researchers working at the intersection of RL and computational cognitive science.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

**The central novelty — Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ — is never algorithmically specified.** The paper's abstract, introduction, and conclusion all state that treating $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable updated online is the key contribution. However, Section 3 (Methods) defines only a *static* $\beta$ objective. The generative model for $\beta$, the prior distribution, the likelihood function, and the posterior update rule are never described. Figures 3 and 4 show "posterior" concentration plots and Table 3 reports summary statistics, but the mechanism that generated these values is absent from the paper. Without this specification, the core claim is not reproducible and the paper cannot legitimately claim to have demonstrated a Bayesian adaptive method.

### Major

1. **Performance evidence for Bayesian $\beta$ is explicitly withheld.** In Section 5.4, the paper states: *"In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value."* The central claim of the paper is supported by experiments that the authors chose not to include. This is a decisive evidential gap.

2. **Bayesian $\beta$ is not tested where fixed $\beta$ was shown to fail.** The MountainCar experiment (Section 5.3, Table 2) demonstrates that $\beta=0.3$ and $\beta=1.0$ degrade performance, illustrating the very problem that adaptation is supposed to solve. Yet the paper does not apply Bayesian $\beta$ to this environment. The reader has no evidence that the claimed solution actually addresses the demonstrated failure mode.

3. **Missing comparisons against relevant baselines.** The only comparison is against $\beta=0.0$ ("standard RL"). No intrinsic motivation baselines (ICM, RND, or any curiosity/empowerment method) and no adaptive tuning baselines (meta-gradient RL, simple $\beta$ annealing, Population-Based Training) are included. The paper's claim of "improvements over fixed-regularization baselines" is limited to the $\beta=0$ baseline, which is the weakest possible comparison.

4. **Minimal $\beta$ sweep in MiniGrid.** The fixed-$\beta$ grid in MiniGrid (Section 5.4) tests only $\beta=0.0$ and $\beta=0.01$. A proper evaluation of whether Bayesian $\beta$ matches the optimal fixed value requires a much wider sweep (e.g., $\beta \in \{0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5\}$). The posterior mean converges to $\approx 0.02$ (Table 3), but it is unclear whether this value is near-optimal since only $\beta=0.01$ (and not $\beta=0.02$) was tested.

### Minor

1. **Theory addresses only static $\beta$.** Propositions 1 and 2 analyze the effect of a *fixed* $\beta$ on the optimal policy. They offer no insight into the dynamics or convergence properties of the Bayesian adaptation process. The theoretical section is therefore decoupled from the paper's central claim.

2. **Inconsistency in Bayesian $\beta$ visualization.** Figure 3 labels a dashed red line at $\beta=0.1$ as the "target effective $\beta$," but the posterior mean decreases from 0.106 (Round 1) to 0.017 (Round 4), moving further from this labeled target. The relationship between the fixed-$\beta$ experiments (which used $\beta=0.01$) and the Bayesian results is unclear.

3. **The predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s,a)$ uses ground-truth transition dynamics** in all experiments. Using a learned predictive model would be significantly more challenging but is necessary for the method to be generally applicable. The paper acknowledges this in Section 6.2 but does not provide experiments with learned models.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison to at least one adaptive tuning baseline (e.g., $\beta$ annealing, meta-gradient) would substantially strengthen the claim that Bayesian adaptation is beneficial.
- An ablation studying sensitivity to the prior specification for $\beta$ would clarify robustness.
- Testing on at least one additional sparse-reward benchmark (e.g., from the Procgen or NetHack suites) would broaden the empirical scope.
