# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces High-Entropy Sum (HES), a training-free metric for selecting high-quality reasoning data to improve LLM performance. HES focuses on the top 0.5% highest-entropy tokens in each sample, capturing critical forking points to assess reasoning quality. The authors validate HES across three training paradigms: SFT, RFT, and RL. Key findings show that in SFT, training on the top 20% of HES-ranked data achieves comparable performance to full-dataset training, while low-HES data degrades performance. In RFT, HES-based selection outperforms random selection, and in RL, pairing high-HES successful trajectories with random failed ones enables the model to learn strong reasoning patterns and diverse failure modes, surpassing existing training-free selection methods. These results establish HES as a robust metric for efficient, data-centric LLM training.

## Strengths
1. The paper introduces High-Entropy Sum (HES), a novel, training-free metric that effectively captures reasoning quality by focusing on high-entropy tokens, addressing limitations of traditional metrics that rely on global, coarse-grained evaluations.
2. The paper is well-structured, with clear explanations of the HES metric and its application across SFT, RFT, and RL paradigms. The experiments are systematically designed, and results are presented with clear comparisons to baselines, enhancing the paper’s readability and credibility.

## Weaknesses
1. The core idea of using entropy to measure the quality of reasoning paths is not entirely new. Prior work, such as [1], has explored similar concepts in evaluating CoT quality. The authors should clarify how HES advances beyond these existing methods, particularly in how it handles the specific challenges of long-CoT reasoning.
2. The paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the HES metric. For instance, is HES sensitive to variations in vocabulary or syntax across different domains? Are there edge cases where HES may not perform well, such as in reasoning tasks with atypical structures or highly ambiguous inputs? Addressing these potential limitations would strengthen the paper by providing a more balanced view of the metric’s applicability.
3. The paper primarily focuses on performance metrics without providing deeper analysis of why HES is effective. For example, are there specific types of reasoning tasks or problem structures where HES tends to perform better? Are there particular failure modes of LLMs that HES is more sensitive to? Including such insights would enhance the understanding of HES and its implications for LLM training.
