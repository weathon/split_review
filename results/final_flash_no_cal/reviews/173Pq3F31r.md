## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT) via disentangled note-level score content and global performance style representations. It also introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated style-aware rendering without composer labels or manual control. The model is evaluated on the ASAP dataset using objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analyses.

## Strengths

1. **Joint modeling achieves competitive performance on both EPR and APT simultaneously.** The unified model obtains a lowest overall MUSTER error of 12.48 and best pitch-spelling error of 6.24 on APT (Table 1), while on EPR achieving the highest alignment rate (92.27%, Ours-PSR) and best MAE for duration and velocity among compared methods (Tables 2–3). These results demonstrate that the joint framework does not degrade task performance relative to single-task baselines.

2. **Alignment-free Seq2Seq EPR outperforms alignment-dependent baselines in note accuracy.** The proposed model, which requires only sequence-level supervision and no note-level alignment, achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all EPR methods (Table 3), surpassing both VirtuosoNet and DExter. This validates a central claim: eliminating fine-grained alignment tools does not compromise—and may improve—note-level accuracy.

3. **Thorough disentanglement verification.** Performer/composer classification (Table 4) quantitatively confirms that style embeddings encode performer and composer identity (Style→Performer F1=25.82 vs. Cont→Performer F1=0.74; Style→Composer accuracy=77.46% vs. Cont→Composer=29.99%), while t-SNE visualizations (Figure 3) show clear clustering by both attributes. This provides strong evidence that the architectural and regularization choices successfully separate content from style.

4. **PSR module produces stylistically appropriate embeddings from score content alone.** The PSR-generated style embeddings exhibit era-wise clustering closely matching those from real performances (Figure 4). Subjective evaluations (Figure 2) show Ours-PSR ratings approaching Ours-Target and exceeding both alignment-based baselines, supporting the claim that the module can recommend plausible styles without ground-truth style information.

5. **Subjective evaluation with musically trained participants across diverse composers.** The listening test (Figure 2) covers five composers spanning different eras and uses 11 participants trained in music performance. Ours-Target receives the highest ratings among all non-human systems across dynamics, tempo, style, and overall human-likeness, and Ours-PSR closely follows. This provides more credible evidence of rendering quality than crowdsourced ratings alone.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No explicit loss aligns the two content representations (z_x and z_y).** The paper states that score content features z_x (from score inputs) and z_y (from performance inputs) are "encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption), but the training objective (Eq. 6) contains no direct alignment loss—no cycle-consistency, contrastive, or projection-based constraint. Alignment is only implicit through shared decoders: the score decoder g_X processes both z_y (APT) and z_x (score reconstruction), and the performance decoder g_Y processes both z_x⊕z_s (EPR) and z_y⊕style (performance reconstruction). However, these decoders could learn to map from domain-specific subspaces without enforcing a genuinely shared content space. This gap weakens the disentanglement claims and may explain the mixed style transfer results (Sample 2 in Figure 5, where the Target condition receives the lowest similarity rating). An explicit alignment mechanism or a cycle-consistency evaluation (render→transcribe→compare to original score) would substantially strengthen the work.

2. **The EPR-Only ablation confounds joint training with additional unpaired data.** The paper compares Ours-Target (full model trained on paired ASAP + large unpaired score and performance datasets) against EPR-Only (trained solely on ASAP data, with the APT decoder removed). These differ in two variables simultaneously: the presence of the APT task *and* the use of unpaired data. Consequently, the improvement of Ours-Target over EPR-Only cannot be attributed specifically to joint modeling—additional data alone could explain it. The paper acknowledges this indirectly ("joint modeling, *together with* training on additional unpaired data") but nonetheless presents the comparison as evidence that "joint modeling… leads to better EPR performance." A clean ablation—e.g., a variant with the same unpaired reconstruction losses but without the APT decoder—is needed to isolate the effect of joint task learning.

3. **Style transfer evaluation is too thin to support "reliable" claims.** The style transfer subjective test (Figure 5) uses only three samples, and in one of them (Sample 2) the Target condition receives the *lowest* similarity rating—a clear failure of style transfer that goes entirely undiscussed. The abstract claims "reliable style transfer," yet the evidence (3 samples, 1 counterexample, no failure analysis) is insufficient to support this. A larger evaluation (more samples, objective style classification metrics, and analysis of failure cases) is needed for the claimed reliability.

### Minor

4. **KL divergence weight on the style prior not reported.** The paper uses a KL penalty (Eq. 5) to regularize the style space but never states its weight in the total loss (Eq. 6). This matters for reproducibility because the trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and style regularization directly affects disentanglement quality.

5. **Error bars in subjective evaluation figures not defined.** Figure 2 includes error bars, but the paper does not specify whether they represent standard deviation, standard error, or confidence intervals. This limits interpretability of the subjective ratings.

6. **Transcription quality of unpaired YouTube performance data not quantified.** The unpaired performance dataset (75k+ pieces) is transcribed from YouTube piano covers using an audio-to-MIDI model. The paper mentions a "pilot study demonstrating strong accuracy" but provides no quantitative evaluation of this transcription quality. Systematic errors (missing notes, misaligned onsets) in such a large dataset could propagate to the style encoder.

7. **Figure 1 contains an inconsistency.** The diagram shows the Style Encoder receiving both "Performance Notes y" and "Style Notes z_s" as input, whereas Eq. 1 defines it as mapping only y (z_s = f_{s,Y}(y)). This minor discrepancy between the figure and the mathematical formulation is confusing.

8. **Dismissive treatment of DExter's higher variance.** DExter achieves velocity variance of 238.86 (Human: 241.04), closer to human values than the proposed model's 151.03. The paper dismisses this as "unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness," citing the listening test as evidence. While the subjective results do support this interpretation, the dismissal would be stronger if accompanied by an objective analysis (e.g., variance decomposition) rather than relying solely on subjective assessment.

9. **11 participants is a modest sample for a listening test.** While the use of musically trained participants is a strength, 11 raters is a relatively small sample for drawing broad conclusions about rendering quality across multiple composers and conditions. Inter-rater reliability metrics (e.g., Krippendorff's alpha) are not reported.

### Trivial

- Broadcasted addition (z_x ⊕ z_s) as the sole fusion mechanism is a simple choice that goes unexamined; no ablation of alternative fusion strategies is provided.
- The unpaired performance data collection via YouTube transcription is acknowledged but the transcription model itself is not named or cited.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A cycle-consistency experiment (EPR → APT → compare to original score) would directly validate the shared content space.
- An ablation disentangling the effect of joint APT training from the effect of unpaired data.
- A larger-scale style transfer evaluation with objective metrics (e.g., style classifier accuracy on transferred outputs) and failure analysis.
- A dedicated limitations section discussing the absence of explicit content alignment, the modest style transfer evaluation, and potential noise in unpaired data.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an explicit content-alignment mechanism** — e.g., a cycle-consistency loss where the model performs EPR then APT on the output, comparing the reconstructed score to the original, or a contrastive loss that pulls z_x and z_y from the same piece together. This would directly validate the shared content space and likely improve style transfer.

2. **Run a proper ablation** that separates the effect of joint training from unpaired data: train (a) the full model without unpaired data, (b) a single-task EPR variant with the same unpaired reconstruction losses but no APT decoder, and (c) the full model as reported. Report all three in Tables 2–3.

3. **Expand the style transfer evaluation** to at least 10–20 samples, include objective style classification accuracy as a complementary metric, and discuss the failure case (Sample 2) to understand when style transfer breaks.

4. **Report the KL weight** used in Eq. 6, define the error bars in all figures, and quantify the accuracy of the audio-to-MIDI transcription model used for the unpaired performance dataset.

5. **Consider including inter-rater reliability** (e.g., Fleiss' kappa or ICC) for the subjective listening test to strengthen the validity of the ratings given the modest sample size.

## Score and Decision

The paper tackles a well-motivated problem with a thoughtful architecture and demonstrates competitive performance on both EPR and APT tasks. The disentanglement verification is thorough, and the PSR module shows genuine promise. However, three significant weaknesses—the absence of an explicit content alignment loss, a confounded ablation that conflates joint training with additional data, and a style transfer evaluation too thin to support the "reliable" claim—prevent the contributions from being fully established. The paper is above the acceptance threshold but would benefit substantially from addressing these points. I recommend acceptance subject to these concerns being addressed.
