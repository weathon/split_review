## Summary

The paper presents a systematic empirical benchmark of nine open-source LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama 2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5/3, WizardCoder, Phi-4, Gemma 3, DeepSeek-V2) on two core reverse engineering tasks — name recovery (function and variable names) and type inference — using a dataset of 40,000 functions across four architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS) and multiple optimization levels, with and without LoRA fine-tuning. It finds that without fine-tuning all models perform poorly (F1 < 0.1 on most tasks), and fine-tuning yields inconsistent improvements that remain far below practical usefulness.

## Strengths

1. **First large-scale systematic benchmarking of multiple LLMs on core RE tasks.** The paper evaluates 9 open-source LLMs on name recovery and type inference across 4 architectures and 4 optimization levels (Section 4.1, Tables 1–4), which directly fills the stated gap of missing comparative evaluation in this domain.

2. **Quantitative evidence of current LLM limitations that is concrete and reproducible.** All models achieve very low F1 scores without fine-tuning (most below 0.1), and even after fine-tuning the improvements remain modest and inconsistent — e.g., variable name recovery reaches at most 0.33 F1 (Table 3), while type inference never exceeds 0.06 F1 (Table 4). These results in Findings 1–2 provide reproducible evidence for the paper's central conclusion.

3. **Demonstration that model size and architecture matter less than training data quality.** Controlled experiments with CodeLlama 7B, 13B, and 34B show nearly identical performance despite a 5× parameter increase (Figure 2, Finding 6), and DeepSeek-R1 (built on Qwen2.5) consistently underperforms Qwen2.5 on name recovery (Tables 2–3, Finding 5). These insights emerge directly from the comparative design.

4. **Automated evaluation pipeline with pragmatic design choices.** The use of an auxiliary LLM for output normalization (Section 3.2) addresses a real practical challenge of comparing heterogeneous LLM outputs. The adoption of CodeWordNet for semantic name matching (Section 3.4) avoids penalizing semantically equivalent predictions, and Figure 1 validates its necessity.

5. **Validation on real-world firmware with two decompilers.** The evaluation on actual firmware binaries using both Ghidra and IDA Pro (Table 6) extends findings beyond synthesized benchmarks, showing that the low performance holds in practical settings.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Potential inconsistency between Table 2 and Figure 1 that undermines trust in reported numbers.**  
   For function name recovery on x86-64 O0, Table 2 reports an F1 of **0.04** for CodeLlama (original), while Figure 1's "w CodeWordNet" column shows approximately **0.15** for the same model and condition — a gap too large for rounding error. The "w CodeWordNet" values in Figure 1 (≈0.08–0.25 across models) are systematically 3–5× higher than the corresponding entries in Table 2 (0.00–0.06). Conversely, the "wo CodeWordNet" values in Figure 1 are also generally higher than Table 2 entries (e.g., Llama2 O0: ~0.06 vs. 0.01; DeepSeek-V2 O0: ~0.07 vs. 0.01).  

   Since the paper states that CodeWordNet is the standard evaluation metric for name recovery (Section 3.4), Table 2 should align with Figure 1's "w CodeWordNet" column. The mismatch suggests either that: (a) Figure 1 reports on a different data subset or metric than what the caption and text describe, (b) Table 2 inadvertently uses strict matching (without CodeWordNet), or (c) the figure values have been misread. Whichever the case, the reader cannot reconcile the two exhibits. Given that the paper's quantitative contribution rests entirely on these tables and figures, this inconsistency must be resolved.

2. **Fine-tuning setup is critically under-specified and potentially inadequate.**  
   The paper provides no LoRA hyperparameters (rank, alpha, target modules, learning rate, effective batch size) and no training loss curves or validation splits. The training budget is only "200 steps" (Table 7) for a dataset of 160k functions, implying very limited data exposure. Without evidence of convergence, the modest improvements (and occasional degradations after fine-tuning, e.g., DeepSeek-V2 under O1 in Table 3) may reflect under-training rather than inherent limitations of fine-tuning. The comparison across models is also potentially unfair if different models require different fine-tuning budgets.

### Minor

3. **Auxiliary LLM used for output parsing is not validated.**  
   The pipeline relies on an auxiliary LLM to normalize model outputs into a uniform schema (Section 3.2), but the paper does not state which model was used, nor does it report parsing accuracy. Errors at this stage could systematically inflate or deflate F1 scores differently across models, introducing a confound in a comparative benchmark.

4. **Prompt sensitivity is not explored.**  
   The entire evaluation uses a single prompt template derived from ChatGPT-based studies (Section 3.2). LLMs are known to be sensitive to prompt phrasing, so the reported performance may not generalize across prompt variants. The paper's own Finding 7 (that prompt templates affect training time by up to 4×) acknowledges template importance but does not explore its effect on evaluation scores.

5. **No variance or repeatability information.**  
   All results are single-point estimates with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multi-run averages. Given stochasticity in LLM generation, providing at least a small number of repeats (e.g., 3 runs) would help assess the reliability of the reported F1 scores.

6. **Precision and recall not reported alongside F1.**  
   F1 alone masks whether models over-predict or under-predict. For example, the all-zero entries in Table 5 could arise from models predicting nothing at all, or from predicting random incorrect names — the distinction matters for understanding failure modes.

7. **No analysis of why fine-tuning sometimes degrades performance.**  
   Several fine-tuned models produce lower F1 than their original counterparts (e.g., CodeLlama function name recovery O0: 0.04→0.03; DeepSeek-V2 variable name O1: 0.10→0.05; many type inference cases in Table 4). This is mentioned but not discussed. Understanding these regressions would strengthen the paper's conclusions about fine-tuning.

### Trivial

8. **Table 5 reports all scores as 0.00 (rounded) without raw counts.**  
   The paper notes that models "occasionally generate correct predictions" but collapses everything to 0.00. Reporting the raw number or proportion of exact matches would be more informative.

9. **Figure 1 data values are only provided as approximate bar-chart readings.**  
   The extracted data from Figure 1 uses "~" notation; the figure itself lacks precise numerical labels on bars, making independent verification difficult.

- **Explore prompt sensitivity** by testing 2–3 prompt variants on a subset of models.
