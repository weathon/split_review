## Summary

The paper proposes **IncentRL**, a framework that augments extrinsic rewards with a KL divergence between predicted outcome distributions \(p(o|s,a)\) and preferred outcome distributions \(q(o|s)\), treated as an intrinsic incentive signal. The headline contribution is a claimed Bayesian scheme for adapting the trade-off weight \(\beta\) online. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid with fixed \(\beta\) values show that the KL shaping can improve sample efficiency and final performance in sparse-reward settings. However, the paper has a critical omission: the Bayesian adaptation scheme that is presented as the central novelty is never actually specified, and the experimental evidence contains significant inconsistencies and gaps.

## Strengths

- **KL-based incentive shaping improves sample efficiency in sparse-reward tasks.** The fixed-\(\beta\) experiments (toy MDP with 7 seeds, Table 1 / Fig. 1; MiniGrid with \(\beta=0.01\), Fig. 2) demonstrate that augmenting rewards with \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) accelerates learning and reduces variance compared to standard RL (\(\beta=0\)). In the toy MDP, all \(\beta>0\) achieve mean reward ≈28.9 vs 16.6 for \(\beta=0\); in MiniGrid, \(\beta=0.01\) yields 98% success rate vs 90.5%. This is concrete evidence that the basic shaping mechanism works as expected.

- **Empirical robustness to \(\beta\) in a controlled setting.** In the toy 2-state MDP (Table 1), any positive \(\beta \in \{0.1,0.3,1.0\}\) yields similar high performance (28.00–28.86) with low variance, demonstrating that the shaping term is robust to the exact choice of \(\beta\) in this simple domain.

- **Conceptual connection to neuroscience and the Free Energy Principle.** The paper draws an explicit analogy between the KL shaping term, dopamine-based reward prediction error, and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 3.3, 6.3), offering a biologically motivated framing that distinguishes the work from purely algorithmic intrinsic motivation methods.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The central claimed novelty — Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation — is never specified.** The paper introduces the Bayesian scheme as its primary contribution ("the central novelty," Abstract; first bullet in contributions, Section 1) and presents posterior statistics in Figures 3, 4 and Table 3. However, the algorithmic description is entirely absent: the prior distribution, the likelihood function, the data used to update the posterior, the update rule, and how the posterior is used during policy training are never stated. The Methods section (Section 3) and Algorithm section (Section 5.1) describe only the fixed-\(\beta\) objective. The Bayesian adaptation appears without any specification in Section 5.4 ("we tracked the posterior evolution of \(\beta\) using our Bayesian adaptation scheme"). This makes the paper's headline contribution unverifiable, unreproducible, and impossible to evaluate. This is the most serious issue.

2. **The experimental evidence for the Bayesian \(\beta\) claim is inconsistent and incomplete.** (a) **Narrative–data mismatch:** The paper states the posterior mean "quickly concentrates near the effective region (\(\beta\approx0.1\))", but Table 3 shows the mean going from 0.106 (Round 1) → 0.045 (Round 2) → 0.019 (Round 3) → 0.017 (Round 4) — clearly moving *away* from 0.1 toward ≈0.02. The MiniGrid fixed-\(\beta\) sweep used \(\beta=0.01\) (close to 0.02), not 0.1, creating confusion across experiments. (b) **Critical result unreported:** The claim that Bayesian \(\beta\) "achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" is relegated to "additional runs (not shown)" — a statement with no quantitative evidence. The posterior concentration alone does not validate that the adapted \(\beta\) yields good policy performance. (c) **No comparison to the best fixed \(\beta\) on the same task:** Table 3 and Figures 3–4 only show posterior statistics; the corresponding policy performance (success rate, reward) under the Bayesian scheme is not reported.

3. **The method is under-specified in non-toy environments.** The paper defines \(p(o|s,a)\) and \(q(o|s)\) conceptually (Section 3.1) but does not explain how these are obtained in practice for MountainCar or MiniGrid. For MountainCar (continuous state space \((x,v)\)), \(q\) assigns all probability to the goal state \(x\ge0.5\) and \(p\) is called "the predicted outcome distribution," but how \(p\) is computed (learned forward model? discretization? analytic transition?) is never described. For MiniGrid, no specification of \(p\) or \(q\) is given at all. Without concrete definitions, the core of the algorithm is not reproducible.

4. **Absence of comparisons with existing intrinsic-motivation methods.** The paper positions IncentRL in relation to curiosity, RND, ICM, and empowerment (Sections 1–2) and claims to advance beyond them, yet evaluates against only standard RL (\(\beta=0\)) and fixed-\(\beta\) sweeps. No comparison with any established intrinsic-motivation baseline is provided, making it impossible to assess whether the method offers practical benefits over existing approaches.

### Minor

5. **Theoretical analysis is elementary.** Proposition 1 (small \(\beta\) preserves optimal policy) and Proposition 2 (\(\beta\to\infty\) yields KL-minimizing policy) follow directly from continuity and scaling arguments. They do not provide insight into the Bayesian adaptation or any non-obvious property of the method, and they do not connect to the experimental design.

6. **Limited statistical evidence.** MountainCar uses only 3 seeds (Table 2), and the reported 18% improvement for \(\beta=0.1\) is not accompanied by any significance test or confidence intervals for learning curves. While this is not uncommon in exploratory work, it weakens the evidence.
