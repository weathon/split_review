## Summary

This paper proposes the **Fast and Slow Effect (FSE)** framework for automatically evaluating whether concept annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs are *sufficient* for class discrimination — i.e., whether the concepts alone (without the image) support correct classification. The framework operates by: (1) having the annotator progressively refine concepts through five stages (Background → Superclass → Salient Features → Detailed Features → Auxiliary Features), classifying at each step from the accumulated concepts (slow mode); (2) comparing this to direct visual classification without concepts (fast mode) via the **Class Representation Index (CRI)**, which is simply the accuracy of concept-based classification. The central empirical finding is that on fine-grained datasets (CUB-Birds, Cars-196, Flowers-102), the slow mode underperforms the fast mode by 25%+ on average across six models, showing that current automated annotators fail to externalize their discriminative knowledge into textual concepts. On general datasets (CIFAR-100, Caltech-101) the opposite holds — slow mode outperforms fast — validating that the CRI metric can detect when annotations *are* sufficient.

## Strengths

1. **Clear, well-supported empirical finding about concept insufficiency** — The paper demonstrates consistently across 6 models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, QwenVL2-72b, QwenVL2-7b, Llama-3.2-vision-90b, Llama-3.2-vision-11b) and 3 fine-grained datasets that slow-mode (concept-based) classification underperforms fast-mode (direct visual) classification by a large margin (average −25% to −27% CRI gap in Table 2). This is the core contribution and is convincingly established.

2. **Discriminative validation on general datasets** — The finding that on CIFAR-100 and Caltech-101 the slow mode *outperforms* the fast mode (Table 3) is a strong sanity check. It confirms that the CRI metric does not simply reward one mode over the other; it can distinguish settings where concepts are sufficient from those where they are not, strengthening the diagnostic value of the framework.

3. **Methodological care in distractor selection** — The preliminary experiment (Section 5.3, Table 1) empirically compares random vs. semantically related distractor strategies, finding that the latter yields substantially higher contradiction rates (34–45% vs. 14–20%). This ensures the candidate sets used in CRI evaluation are genuinely challenging and avoids inflated performance estimates.

4. **Automated, human-supervision-free evaluation** — The FSE framework requires no human annotation for evaluation, addressing a practical bottleneck in XAI validation. The comparison between fast (visual) and slow (concept-based) classification is a simple but effective diagnostic that can be applied to any VLM/LLM-based annotator.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core empirical finding — that concept annotations are insufficient on fine-grained datasets — is well-supported by the experimental evidence. No identified issue invalidates this central claim.

### Minor

1. **CRI formula contains a typographical error** — Eq. (2) writes `CRI = 100% × (1/t) Σ_{i=1}^t 1[y_i^t = y_i]`. Here `t` denotes the step index (1…T) while `l` denotes the number of test instances; the formula should read `(1/l) Σ_{i=1}^l`. The intended meaning (accuracy at step t) is clear from the surrounding text, but the equation as printed is mathematically inconsistent. This should be corrected.

2. **"Novel metric" claim is overstated** — CRI is classification accuracy — the proportion of correct predictions. Calling it a "novel evaluation metric" overstates the contribution. The novelty lies in the *framework* that uses accuracy in a fast-vs.-slow comparative analysis to diagnose annotation insufficiency, not in the metric itself. The paper would be better served by describing CRI as a straightforward accuracy metric deployed within the FSE framework.

3. **Five-stage concept chain is not ablated** — The paper adopts a 5-stage concept refinement process (Background → Superclass → Salient → Detailed → Auxiliary) motivated by prior work using 1–3 stage schemes. However, no ablation or sensitivity analysis is provided to justify why five stages are needed or whether the framework's conclusions would change with fewer stages. The absence of this analysis makes the design feel somewhat ad hoc.

4. **Utility-as-proxy experiment is informative but overclaimed** — The fusion experiment (Table 4) shows that when the model receives both the image and the concepts (fused mode), it achieves ~90% CRI, while concepts alone (slow mode) achieve only ~50% CRI. This validly demonstrates that high downstream accuracy can coexist with poor concept annotations. However, the paper's claim that this "rigorously simulat[es] the end-to-end inference pipeline commonly employed by standard concept-based multimodal models" is imprecise. The setting where a classifier has simultaneous access to both image and text is not equivalent to a concept bottleneck where classification passes *through* the concepts. The core point — that utility-as-proxy can be misleading — stands, but the experimental setup would need to more closely match a CBM architecture to fully establish the claim in that specific context.

5. **No human baseline for concept sufficiency** — The paper defines sufficiency as the ability to infer the class from concepts alone but provides no human upper bound. Without knowing whether human annotators could correctly classify the same concepts, it is unclear whether the observed CRI gaps reflect poor *generation* by the LLM or a fundamental limitation of text-only class discrimination (some visual differences may be inherently hard to describe in text). A small-scale human study with the generated concepts would clarify this.

6. **Statistical detail is limited** — The paper mentions three runs with different seeds and shows shaded error regions in Figure 3, reporting that standard deviations are "negligible." However, no numerical standard deviations or confidence intervals are reported in tables, and no statistical significance tests are provided. Given the consistent trends this is not a major concern, but reporting exact values would strengthen the presentation.

### Trivial

- The framing around dual-process theory (fast=System 1, slow=System 2) and "Slow Mode Superiority" is anthropomorphic and adds limited analytical value. The operational definitions of fast mode (direct visual classification) and slow mode (concept-based classification) are clear and sufficient on their own. The paper could drop the Kahneman reference without losing substance.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation on the number of concept stages** — Varying T (e.g., T=1,3,5) and showing that the CRI trends are stable would strengthen confidence in the 5-stage design.
- **Tighter CBM simulation** — Training a linear classifier on the generated concept features (as a proper concept bottleneck) would make the utility-as-proxy critique more definitive for that family of models.
- **Human evaluation** — Having human annotators classify from a sample of the generated concepts would provide a useful reference point for interpreting absolute CRI scores.
