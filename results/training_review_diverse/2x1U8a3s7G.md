## Summary

This paper introduces "prompt diffusion," a plug-and-play module that adds a diffusion model on top of existing prompt learning methods (textual, visual, or multi-modal) for vision-language models. The key idea is: (1) for each training sample, overfit the prompts to that specific sample via iterative gradient descent to get "optimal" per-sample prompts V*; (2) train a diffusion transformer to generate these V* from random noise conditioned on image features; (3) at test time, generate sample-specific prompts from noise via the trained diffusion model. The method is evaluated on 15 datasets across base-to-new generalization, cross-dataset generalization, and domain generalization, showing consistent improvements over five prompt learning baselines (CoCoOp, VPT, MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt).

## Strengths

- **First application of diffusion models to prompt learning with a complete, principled framework.** The paper proposes a coherent pipeline — per-sample overfitting → diffusion training → stochastic sampling at test time — that is novel and well-justified for generating sample-specific prompts (Sec. 4.1–4.2).

- **Modality-agnostic plug-and-play integration across five distinct baselines.** The method is applied to textual (CoCoOp), visual (VPT), and multi-modal (MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt) prompt learning methods without architectural changes, and consistently improves all of them across 15 datasets (Tables 1–3). This breadth of integration is a genuine strength.

- **Consistent empirical gains across all three generalization settings.** Improvements are reported in base-to-new (e.g., +2.87% on new classes for CoPrompt), cross-dataset (e.g., +2.47% average for CoPrompt), and domain generalization (e.g., +2.14% average for CoCoOp). The gains are positive for every baseline in every setting, not cherry-picked.

- **Efficient inference with only 5 ODE sampling steps.** The use of AMED-Solver achieves a good accuracy-speed trade-off: only 0.045s additional prediction time over CoCoOp while substantially improving accuracy (Fig. 3). This addresses a practical concern about diffusion models being slow at inference.

- **Ablation comparing diffusion against non-generative and alternative generative models.** Table 4 shows that the diffusion-based generator outperforms MLP, Transformer, GAN, VAE, and normalizing flows under the same per-sample overfitting strategy, with a harmonic mean of 78.02 vs. the next best 76.32. The comparison against a non-generative Transformer (same architecture family) is particularly informative, showing that the diffusion objective itself adds value over a regression-based approach.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

None. The issues identified below are addressable and do not undermine the paper's core claims.

### Minor

- **The per-sample overfitting procedure is underspecified.** The paper states that V* is obtained via "iterative gradient descent" and mentions "During the initial training stage, we obtain the overfitted prompts V* of individual samples" (lines 114, 131). However, it does not clearly state whether (a) this is done once as a preprocessing step before diffusion training begins, or (b) interleaved with diffusion training while base prompts V evolve. It also does not specify how many gradient iterations are used during actual training (Fig. 4 shows the impact of iterations, but it's ambiguous whether I=5 is the setting used in the main experiments). These details matter for understanding the computational cost and whether the diffusion targets shift during training. The paper would benefit from a precise algorithmic description.

- **The generative ablation (GAN/VAE/normalizing flows) does not specify architectures for the alternative models.** The paper states that the diffusion model uses a 12-layer GPT-2-style transformer with 16 attention heads (line 186), but the architectures for GAN, VAE, and normalizing flow baselines in Table 4 are not reported. Without matching model capacity across all generative baselines, the claim that "the diffusion model outperforms all variants" (lines 278–281) conflates the choice of generative framework with the choice of backbone architecture. This is partially mitigated by the inclusion of a non-generative Transformer baseline (same architecture family), which also underperforms diffusion — but the paper should specify the architectures for all baselines or, ideally, use the same transformer backbone with different training objectives.

- **The improvements over CoCoOp are modest relative to the added complexity.** CoCoOp already generates sample-specific prompts from image features via a Meta-Net in a single forward pass. The additional gains from prompt diffusion over CoCoOp are +1.08% on base and +1.26% on new classes (Table 1), while adding per-sample overfitting during training, diffusion transformer training, and multi-step sampling at test time. Although the inference overhead is small (+0.045s), the modest margin over a strong baseline that already conditions prompts on image features raises a question about which components drive the improvement. This is partially addressed by ablation comparisons (Table 4) showing regression from image features to V* yields harmonic mean 75.88 vs. diffusion's 78.02, confirming that the diffusion process adds value beyond simple regression. But the authors should discuss this more explicitly.

- **The notation in Sec. 4.2 (Prompt diffusion) is confusing.** The paper introduces V*_t (noised overfitted prompts), Ṽ_t (described as "noised prompts" input to the diffusion transformer), and the actual diffusion model input √ᾱ_t·V* + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε from Eq. (6). The relationship between V_t*, Ṽ_t, and the model input is not cleanly defined, and the text switches between them. A single, consistent notation with clear variable definitions would improve clarity.

- **Computational cost of per-sample overfitting is not reported.** If the overfitting step uses 5 gradient iterations per sample (as suggested by Fig. 4) for a 16-shot × N-class × 11-dataset setup, this adds a non-trivial number of forward/backward passes. The paper states "Code will be made available" but does not quantify the training overhead in terms of additional time or FLOPs relative to the baseline training.

### Trivial

- **The domain generalization table (Table 4 in paper, Table 3 in review) reports averages computed over the 4 target datasets only (excluding ImageNet source).** This is a reasonable choice but should be stated explicitly in the table caption or notes to avoid confusion. The individual values are correct, and the averages are consistent with averaging over the four target columns.

- **The paper mentions "we set the diffusion time step t as 100 for our experiments" (line 186) and samples with NFE=5 via AMED-Solver.** The relationship is standard in diffusion models with ODE solvers (the model trains with 100 discretization steps and samples with a learned fast schedule), but a one-sentence clarification would help readers unfamiliar with the method.

- An empirical comparison against Bayesian prompt learning (Derakhshani et al., 2023), which is cited in related work as another probabilistic approach to prompt learning.

## Removed Points

- **Criticism that test-time generation from pure noise creates an unanalyzed distribution shift.** The reviewer claimed the model "was never trained on" pure noise states. This is incorrect: standard diffusion models (including this one) train on all timesteps t ∈ [1, T], and at t=T the noised input is approximately pure noise (since √ᾱ_T → 0). The model is trained on the full spectrum from clean to near-pure noise. This is a fundamental misunderstanding of how diffusion models work, so the criticism is removed.

- **Criticism that "the improvement over CoCoOp is only 1-2% and the complexity may not be justified" framed as a critical issue.** The reviewer overstated this concern. While the improvements over CoCoOp specifically are modest, the improvements over other baselines (especially state-of-the-art CoPrompt: +2.87% on new classes) are more substantial. Moreover, the consistent positive improvements across all 5 baselines and all 3 settings constitute genuine evidence of value. This is kept as a minor weakness rather than a critical issue.

- **Criticism about 100 training timesteps vs. 5 sampling steps not being explained.** The paper describes the AMED-Solver (a cited, known fast ODE solver) and explains that it enables sampling in 5 steps (lines 98–103, 302–305). The relationship between training discretization and sampling steps is standard in the ODE-based diffusion sampling literature and is adequately addressed.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from synthesizing the reviews is that the paper's core mechanism — per-sample overfitting followed by diffusion-based generation — introduces an intriguing tension. The per-sample overfitting step deliberately creates aggressive, over-optimized prompt targets (the reviewer rightly calls these "aggressive"), and then the diffusion model learns to reconstruct these aggressive targets from noise. At test time, the diffusion model generates prompts that are not merely interpolations of training prompts but samples from a learned distribution conditioned on image features. If the diffusion model is working correctly, the generated prompts should be "in the same region" as the training overfitted prompts for similar inputs, explaining the generalization improvement. The fact that the non-generative Transformer (which directly regresses from image features to V*) underperforms the diffusion model suggests that the stochastic generative modeling adds robustness — the diffusion model may be learning to avoid the worst overfitting artifacts by effectively averaging over the distribution induced by the diffusion process. The 5-iteration overfitting sweet spot (Fig. 4) supports this: enough iterations to get informative per-sample signal, not so many that the targets become memorization artifacts.

2. **Report architectures for all generative baselines** in Table 4, or re-run them with the same transformer backbone for a fully controlled comparison.
3. **Add a simple algorithmic summary** (pseudocode or pseudo-code) of the training pipeline to resolve ambiguity about the interaction between base prompt learning, per-sample overfitting, and diffusion training.
4. **Standardize the notation in Sec. 4.2** — define each variable (V*, V*_t, Ṽ_t, Ṽ_θ input) explicitly and use consistently.
5. **Report the training overhead** (additional compute) introduced by the per-sample overfitting step, to help readers assess the cost-benefit trade-off.
