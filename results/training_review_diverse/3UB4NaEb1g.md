## Summary

The paper introduces QuaCer-C, a framework for certifying knowledge comprehension in LLMs using formal probabilistic guarantees. The key idea is to define knowledge comprehension specifications as probabilistic programs over knowledge graphs (specifically Wikidata5m), where prompts are generated by sampling multi-hop reasoning paths, then using Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals on n=250 samples to produce high-confidence bounds on the probability that an LLM responds correctly. The method is black-box, working for both open-source and API-access models. The paper certifies 8 models (Llama-3, Mistral, Phi-3, Gemini-1.5 variants, GPT-4o) across three specification variants (Vanilla, Shuffle, Shuffle+Distractor) and finds that knowledge comprehension improves with model size.

## Strengths

1. **First formal specification of knowledge comprehension as a probabilistic program over knowledge graphs**: The paper defines distribution-based specifications (Algorithm 1, Definitions 3.1–3.3) that capture multi-hop reasoning prompts with distractor nodes and context shuffling. This is a novel formalization that goes beyond static benchmark datasets by defining entire distributions of prompts and their correct responses, enabling statistical certification.

2. **Black-box certification with provably conservative confidence intervals**: Using Clopper-Pearson intervals (Section 3.2), the framework produces confidence bounds that are guaranteed to achieve at least the specified $(1-\delta)$ coverage level. This works for closed-source API-access models where white-box verification is impossible, addressing a real gap in the formal methods literature for LLMs.

3. **Empirical demonstration across 8 models with diverse specifications**: The paper certifies open-source models (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-3B/14B, including quantized variants) and closed-source models (Gemini-1.5 Flash/Pro, GPT-4o) on three specification types. The results show expected trends (larger models perform better, quantization degrades performance, distractors reduce accuracy), serving as validation that the framework produces sensible certificates.

4. **Systematic analysis of contextual variations**: By certifying under Vanilla, Shuffle, and Shuffle+Distractor specifications (Section 4.1), the paper formally quantifies how information ordering and irrelevant context affect the probability of correct comprehension, going beyond what simple accuracy benchmarks can provide.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None that threaten the paper's core claims. The methodology (Clopper-Pearson intervals for black-box certification) is sound and the specification framework is well-defined.

### Minor

1. **Framing overclaim: the title and abstract suggest broader scope than what is certified.** The paper's title ("Certifying Knowledge Comprehension in LLMs") and abstract imply a general certification of knowledge comprehension capability. In practice, the certificates apply to a specific distribution over prompts constructed from a single knowledge graph (Wikidata5m) with a fixed procedure (pivot-node-centered subgraphs, path length ≤ ρ, specific alias/distractor sampling). The paper *does* acknowledge these scope limitations inline — for instance, stating "we scope our analyses to local specifications" (Section 3.1) and noting that certificates depend on the specific distribution choices (Section 3.1, line 102) — but the front matter creates an expectation the paper does not meet. Reframing the title/abstract to match the actual scope (e.g., "Certifying LLMs on Knowledge-Graph-Derived Comprehension Distributions") would be more accurate.

2. **The baseline comparison adds limited evidentiary value.** The paper compares certificate bounds against a simple accuracy point estimate on a static dataset (no confidence intervals on the baseline). The baseline serves as a sanity check but does not demonstrate what unique insight the certification framework provides over standard benchmarking. As reported, many models have overlapping intervals, making it unclear what conclusions the certificates enable that a significance test on benchmark accuracy could not. Showing a concrete risk-aware deployment decision (e.g., "if a deployment requires at least 0.6 probability of correctness, model X is certify-able but model Y is not because its lower bound is 0.55") would substantially strengthen the paper's contribution.

3. **The prompt construction pipeline is under-specified for reproducibility.** The paper does not report the exact prompt template (system prompt, instruction format, multiple-choice options structure, few-shot example format) or decoding parameters (temperature, top-p, max tokens) for any model. For black-box API models where prompt sensitivity is known to be high, these details are necessary for reproducibility. The paper mentions "a few-shot example" and "multiple-choice question" format but does not provide the actual template.

4. **Pivot node selection bias is acknowledged but not analyzed.** The paper selects 50 pivot nodes from high-degree subgraphs (top 2000 by out-degree, subgraphs with ≥2000 vertices). The paper acknowledges this choice but does not analyze how this selection affects the generality or representativeness of the certificates. Certificates on hub-centered subgraphs may not reflect performance on sparser, low-degree regions of the graph. A sensitivity analysis over different pivot selection strategies would clarify the framework's robustness.

5. **No validation that the synthetic prompts correspond to human-level knowledge comprehension.** The probabilistic program encodes many design choices (path length distribution, alias sampling, distractor definition, shuffling) that are grounded in prior work but are not validated against human judgments. Without human evaluation confirming that the prompts are natural, that the correct answer is uniquely identifiable from the context, and that human annotators would answer correctly at high rates, the certificates certify performance on a synthetic distribution whose correspondence to "knowledge comprehension" as a general cognitive capability is unverified. This does not invalidate the framework but limits its real-world interpretability.

6. **"Tightness" of bounds is asserted without analysis.** The paper claims "tight bounds" (abstract, Section 4.2) but does not empirically characterize interval width as a function of n, nor show how the bounds enable model discrimination. For n=250 and 95% confidence, Clopper-Pearson intervals have width approximately 0.12 at p≈0.5 — usable but not "tight" in an absolute sense. A plot of interval width vs. sample size for a fixed model would help readers assess the trade-off.

### Trivial

1. The name "QuaCer-C" is introduced in the abstract and Section 3 but is used inconsistently — sometimes the framework is referred to as just "QuaCer" in later text, and the "C" suffix significance is never explained.

2. The paper uses the term "certificate" to describe confidence intervals, which may confuse readers from formal verification communities where certificates typically refer to deterministic guarantees. While the paper clearly explains the statistical nature of the bounds, a note clarifying the distinction would be helpful.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Human evaluation** of a sample of generated prompts to validate that the specifications test knowledge comprehension as humans understand it.
- **Sensitivity analysis** of the certificates to the key design choices: changes in path length distribution, distractor sampling strategy, and different knowledge graphs.
- **Comparison with alternative statistical methods** (e.g., Wilson intervals, Bayesian credible intervals) to justify the Clopper-Pearson choice beyond its conservatism.
- **A concrete decision-making use case** showing how the certificate's lower bound enables risk-aware deployment decisions that simple accuracy cannot.
- **Calibration experiments** on synthetic data with known true p to verify that the Clopper-Pearson intervals achieve the claimed coverage in practice.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a sound, well-motivated certification framework for LLMs on knowledge-graph-derived comprehension tasks. The core methodology (Clopper-Pearson intervals over i.i.d. samples from a probabilistic program) is technically correct, and the framework is the first to bring this kind of statistical certification to knowledge comprehension in LLMs. The empirical demonstration across 8 models is reasonable.

However, the paper would benefit from more accurate framing of its scope (distribution-specific guarantees rather than a general "certification of knowledge comprehension"), stronger validation of the specification design, and improved reporting for reproducibility. These are addressable weaknesses that do not undermine the paper's core contribution.
