## Summary

This paper proposes IntentGPT, a training‑free framework for few‑shot intent discovery that uses frozen LLMs (GPT‑3.5, GPT‑4, Llama‑2) with several in‑context learning enhancements: an automatic In‑Context Prompt Generator (ICP), a Semantic Few‑Shot Sampler (SFS), and a Known Intent Feedback (KIF) loop that dynamically injects discovered intents into the prompt during inference. Experiments on CLINC and BANKING report state‑of‑the‑art or competitive NMI, ARI, and ACC scores relative to unsupervised and semi‑supervised baselines that require substantially more labeled data and multi‑stage training.

## Strengths

1. **Training‑free intent discovery with minimal labeled data outperforms methods requiring full training.** IntentGPT‑4 (50‑shot) achieves NMI 96.06 / ARI 84.76 on CLINC and NMI 85.94 / ARI 66.66 on BANKING (Table 1), surpassing all reported unsupervised and semi‑supervised baselines on most metrics despite using only 50 labeled examples per intent versus baselines that train on the full dataset.

2. **Known Intent Feedback (KIF) provides large, clearly attributable gains.** The ablation in Table 2 shows that activating KIF on GPT‑3.5 reduces the Number of Discovered Intents (NDI) from 1,484 to 241 on CLINC while raising NMI from 80.12→90.26 and ARI from 41.08→65.53. This demonstrates that injecting discovered intents back into the prompt is the core mechanism enabling the frozen LLM to reuse intents without fine‑tuning.

3. **Semantic Few‑Shot Sampling (SFS) consistently improves over random few‑shot selection.** In Table 2, adding SFS on top of FS+KIF lifts NMI and ACC for both GPT‑3.5 and GPT‑4 on both datasets. For example, GPT‑4 on BANKING: NMI 81.70→83.18, ACC 64.51→70.42. The improvement is consistent across configurations.

4. **Automatic In‑Context Prompt Generation (ICP) outperforms basic task descriptions.** Adding ICP on top of KIF+FS+SFS improves NMI on CLINC from 90.71→93.07 (GPT‑3.5) and 93.89→94.99 (GPT‑4) in Table 2, confirming the value of domain‑contextualized prompts generated by a stronger LLM.

5. **Model‑agnostic design validated across three LLMs.** Results are reported for GPT‑3.5, GPT‑4, and Llama‑2 70B (Table 1, Figures 2‑3), showing that performance scales with model capability and that the approach works with both commercial and open‑source models.

6. **Competitive zero‑shot performance.** Even with no labeled examples in the prompt, IntentGPT‑4 achieves NMI 94.35 / ARI 78.69 on CLINC (Table 1), outperforming all unsupervised baselines (e.g., DAC: NMI 78.40, ARI 40.49), demonstrating the power of the LLM's world knowledge plus KIF.

7. **Systematic ablation isolating each prompt feature.** Table 2 evaluates 7 configurations per model, varying KIF, FS, SFS, ICP, and SKIF, which clearly attributes the contribution of each component. The KIR sensitivity analysis (Figures 2‑3) across three ratios also provides useful insights.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Critical ambiguity in the evaluation pipeline (Section 3.5).** The paper states: "We compute SBERT embeddings on predicted and ground truth intents and perform K‑Means clustering" (line 86). The standard evaluation in intent discovery (DeepAligned, SCL, DSSCC, etc.) clusters *utterance* embeddings and compares the resulting partition to ground‑truth intent labels. If IntentGPT instead embeds the *predicted intent name strings* (e.g., "transfer_money") and clusters those, it measures how well the predicted *names* group together — which is fundamentally different from utterance‑level clustering and likely inflates metrics (since ground‑truth intent names for same‑intent utterances produce identical SBERT embeddings that trivially cluster together). Section 4's phrasing ("clustering of intent representations, i.e., text embeddings") further muddies the issue. Because Table 1 directly compares IntentGPT's numbers to prior work's utterance‑level results, the paper must clarify whether it follows the standard utterance‑level evaluation or uses intent‑name clustering. If the latter, the claimed outperformance over baselines is unsupported. **This is the single most important issue to resolve in revision.**

### Minor

- **No error bars or variance estimates for main results.** Table 1 and the KIR curves (Figures 2‑3) report single runs. LLM prompting is sensitive to prompt wording, ordering, and stochastic decoding; without multiple runs (at least 3) with mean and standard deviation, the reader cannot assess whether the observed gaps over baselines are statistically reliable. This is addressable.

- **No ablation for the ICPG hyperparameter `x=2` (number of examples per intent used for prompt generation).** The paper uses `x=2` without justification or sensitivity analysis. The method's sensitivity to this parameter is unknown.

- **No hyperparameter study of SKIF threshold, DBSCAN epsilon, or number of shots.** The SKIF threshold (`n_SKIF=50`), DBSCAN `ε=0.5`, and the choice of 10 vs. 50 shots are used without ablation. While the paper acknowledges some of these are fixed to standard values, a brief study of the most sensitive parameters would strengthen reproducibility.

- **NDI is introduced but not reported in the main comparison (Table 1).** NDI appears only in the ablation (Table 2). Reporting it in the main table would help readers assess whether the high metrics come from discovering approximately the right number of intents or from over‑/under‑generation.

- **SKIF sometimes harms performance without detailed analysis.** In Table 2, activating SKIF reduces NMI and ARI on several configurations (e.g., GPT‑4 on CLINC: NMI 94.99→91.70, ARI 80.48→73.20). The paper notes this "aligns with expectations" but does not analyze why.
