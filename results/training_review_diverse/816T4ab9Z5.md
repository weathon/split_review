## Summary

The paper studies why stronger augmentation improves downstream performance in Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) despite making positive pairs harder to align. It argues that GCL benefits downstream tasks more by separating inter-class nodes than by gathering intra-class nodes, so "perfect alignment" (making positive pairs identical) can be detrimental to generalization. The paper provides theoretical bounds (Theorems 1–2), reinterprets them through information theory and graph spectrum theory, and proposes two heuristic augmentation methods that show consistent but modest improvements across six node-classification datasets.

## Strengths

- **Empirical evidence that inter-class separation drives performance gains (Figure 1).** The paper plots positive center distance, negative center distance, and accuracy against dropout rate. As dropout increases, negative center distance rises sharply while positive center distance does not decrease, yet accuracy improves. This provides a clean, direct demonstration that GCL benefits more from separating different classes than from gathering same-class nodes. This figure is the paper's strongest piece of evidence and does not depend on the theoretical bounds.

- **Novel and well-motivated research question.** The observation that stronger augmentation helps despite worsening alignment — and the tension this creates with the "alignment + uniformity" narrative — is genuinely interesting. The paper correctly identifies a gap in the GCL literature and proposes a specific, testable hypothesis (inter-class separation > intra-class gathering) to explain it.

- **Consistent improvements when integrating proposed methods.** Table 1 shows that both the information-based (I) and spectrum-based (S) augmentations improve accuracy over base GRACE, GCA, and AD-GCL on most of the six datasets. Several variants (GRACE+S, AD-GCL+I, AD-GCL+S) achieve p-values ≤ 0.006 on the average performance, indicating statistically meaningful gains.

- **Connection to the over-smoothing problem (Figure 3).** The spectral analysis and the resulting augmentation method are shown to improve performance on deeper GNNs, which provides a concrete downstream benefit beyond the paper's central claims.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Theorem 2's lower bound does not support the claimed conclusion about generalization in the way the paper asserts.** The theorem gives $\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{CE} \geq \mathcal{L}_{NCE} - 3\delta_{aug}^2 - 2\delta_{aug} - \dots$ and the paper claims that larger $\delta_{aug}$ "would make the bound smaller and enhance generalization." A lower bound becoming more negative means the constraint on the achievable CE loss is loosened — this opens up the *possibility* of better performance, but it does not constitute evidence that generalization *will* improve. More critically, $\mathcal{L}_{NCE}$ and the variance terms in the bound ($\mathrm{var}(f(v^+)|y)$, $\mathrm{var}(\mu_y)$, etc.) themselves depend on $\delta_{aug}$; stronger augmentation increases $\mathcal{L}_{NCE}$ (alignment becomes harder), and the paper does not analyze whether the net effect on the bound is actually favorable. The paper acknowledges the bound "does not enforce a better performance" (line 118) but then makes the stronger claim that it "enhance[s] generalization" (line 120). This over-interpretation weakens the theoretical narrative considerably. The empirical finding (Figure 1) stands on its own, but the paper's attempt to derive it from Theorem 2 is not rigorous.

- **Theorem 1 (upper bounds) does not guarantee that actual distances increase with augmentation.** The theorem gives $\mathbb{E}\|f(v_y^0)-\mu_y\| \leq \delta_{y^+} + \frac{2}{3}\delta_{aug}$ and similarly for the negative center distance. These are *upper* bounds; as $\delta_{aug}$ grows, the bounds become looser, but the actual distances could in principle remain constant or even shrink. The paper states that "both positive and negative center distance are positively related to $\delta_{aug}$" and that "stronger augmentation separates both inter-class and intra-class nodes" (line 90). This reasoning attributes a causal trend to an upper bound, which is a logical gap. Fortunately, the paper then provides experimental confirmation in Figure 1, which compensates for this gap. The issue is that the theorem is presented as supporting the conclusion, when in fact it only provides a consistency condition, not a derivation.

- **The parameter $\theta_i$ in the spectrum method is not defined in this paper.** Theorem 3 (restated from prior work) and Corollary 2 involve $\theta_i$ alongside eigenvalues $\lambda_i', \lambda_i''$. The method then uses changes in $\theta_i$ to decide whether to adjust eigenvalues. However, the paper never states what $\theta_i$ represents (a model parameter of the learnable graph convolution?). Readers cannot verify or reproduce the spectrum method without consulting the external reference. This is a significant clarity gap for a method presented as a core contribution.

- **No ablation studies for either proposed method.** The information method has several design choices (gradient-based importance scoring, top-$\xi$ selection, 50% probability thresholds, ratio of 2$\xi$ deletions to $\xi$ keeps). None of these are varied or justified experimentally. For a method described as serving "primarily [for] theoretical verification" (line 167), the lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to assess what drives the performance gains — is it the information-preservation strategy, or just a side effect of stronger augmentation?

### Minor

- **Limited experimental scope.** Only node classification is evaluated, on six datasets that are all homophilic (citation networks and Amazon co-purchase graphs). The paper frames its claims about GCL broadly but does not test link prediction, graph classification, or heterophilic graphs. Adding at least one non-homophilic dataset or an additional downstream task would strengthen the claims.

- **Marginal statistical significance for some variants.** GRACE+I has p=0.155 and GCA+I has p=0.089 on the average performance, which are not significant at conventional thresholds. The paper interprets these as improvements, but the evidence is weaker for these variants.

- **No limitations section or discussion of scope.** The conclusion only recaps the paper's narrative without acknowledging assumptions (e.g., Assumption 2 linking GED to $\delta_{aug}$ may not hold if the encoder learns to be invariant to augmentation), the narrow experimental evaluation, the lack of ablation, or the overhead of computing gradients/eigenvalues during training.

- **Error bars not shown for Figures 2 and 3.** Table 1 includes standard deviations and references an error-bar figure (likely in the appendix), but the main-text figures on augmentation distance and over-smoothing do not show variability information.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the theoretical narrative.** Acknowledge directly that Theorem 2 is a lower bound and that it does not *prove* better generalization — it only shows that larger $\delta_{aug}$ does not tighten the constraint on CE loss, which is consistent with (but does not derive) the empirical findings. Shift the paper's emphasis toward the empirical evidence (Figure 1) as the primary support for the main claim.

2. **Clarify the status of $\theta_i$ in the spectrum method**, either by defining it within the paper or by explicitly citing the relevant equation in the prior work where it is defined.

3. **Add ablation experiments** for at least the information-based method, varying the key hyperparameters (retention ratio, deletion ratio, importance scoring alternatives) to show which design choices actually matter.

4. **Add error bars to Figures 2 and 3**, and consider including at least one additional downstream task (e.g., link prediction) or a heterophilic dataset to broaden the experimental scope.

5. **Add a limitations paragraph** discussing the scope of the claims, the dependence on Assumption 2, and the fact that the methods are heuristic.

## Score and Decision

The paper identifies an interesting and genuinely under-explored question and provides compelling empirical evidence (Figure 1) that inter-class separation, rather than intra-class gathering, drives the gains from stronger augmentation in GCL. The proposed methods show consistent improvements. However, the theoretical framework meant to support the main claims significantly over-interprets what the bounds actually show. Theorem 2 is presented as explaining why stronger augmentation improves generalization, but the lower-bound argument does not bear this weight — the bound is merely consistent with the empirical observation rather than deriving it. The methods lack ablation, the spectrum method's key parameter ($\theta_i$) is undefined, and the experimental scope is limited to homophilic node-classification datasets. These weaknesses are addressable with substantial revision, but in the current form, the theoretical contribution is overstated relative to what is actually established.

**Overall Assessment**: Borderline. The empirical core is valuable and likely correct, but the paper would benefit from a more honest framing of what the theory does and does not show, and from expanded experiments and ablation.
