## Summary

HyperLLaVA proposes a dynamic tuning strategy for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) that replaces the standard static parameters in the vision-language projector and LLM with input-dependent "visual expert" and "language expert" modules generated by tailored HyperNetworks. The method augments LLaVA-1.5 with a visual expert that generates projector shifts from visual features and a language expert that generates adapter weights from intermediate LLM representations. Experiments across 12 benchmarks show consistent improvements over LLaVA-1.5, and ablations quantify the individual contribution of each expert.

## Strengths

- **Consistent improvements over LLaVA-1.5 across nearly all benchmarks.** HyperLLaVA achieves the best performance on 11 out of 12 evaluated benchmarks (including MME, MMBench, SEED-Bench, LLaVA-Bench) while using the same training data as LLaVA-1.5. This directly supports the claim that the proposed architecture improves multimodal reasoning over the static baseline.

- **Controlled ablations isolate the contribution of each expert.** Section 4.3 reports that removing the visual expert (−ℰ_V) reduces mean accuracy by 2.61%, and removing the language expert (−ℰ_L) reduces it by 0.94%. These comparisons confirm that both modules provide measurable, complementary gains rather than relying purely on data scaling.

- **In-depth analysis of design choices.** The paper systematically evaluates three alternatives for integrating the visual expert into the projector (Table 2), studies where to extract language guidance from the LLM (Figure 4, different layer insertion points), and compares expert structures (MLP-based vs. HyperNetwork+Adapter vs. guided variant, Table 4). This goes beyond a single design and provides actionable guidance.

- **Evidence of reduced object hallucination.** Table 5 shows HyperLLaVA achieves the best POPE scores among compared methods with a balanced "yes" ratio, demonstrating that dynamic tuning does not degrade factual grounding.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The experimental design does not isolate the benefit of *dynamic* parameter generation from added model capacity.** The paper's core claim is that input-dependent (dynamic) parameter generation outperforms static parameters. However, the experiments never compare against a controlled baseline that inserts the *same* expert modules (same architecture, number of parameters) but trains them as ordinary learned weights that are fixed at inference. The ablations in Table 4 compare different methods of generating parameters, but every variant still generates parameters dynamically (or is a different architecture entirely). Without a static adapter baseline — identical architecture, trained end-to-end with fixed weights — the reported gains could stem from the extra parameters in the expert modules rather than from input-dependent generation. The comparison against LLaVA-1.5 is insufficient for this attribution because the architectures differ. This is the paper's most significant evidential gap.

- **The "parameter-efficient fine-tuning" claim is unsubstantiated.** The paper asserts that the language expert serves as a parameter-efficient approach, but it reports neither the total number of trainable parameters for any variant nor compares against established PEFT methods (LoRA, Adapter, prefix tuning) applied to LLaVA. The HyperNetwork introduces additional parameters (layer-specific encoders, two MLP layers for weight generation), and the language expert is applied to 16 out of 32 LLM blocks. Without parameter counts and comparisons, a reader cannot assess whether the improvements come at a reasonable efficiency cost. The claim that the method is "parameter-efficient" remains an assertion rather than a demonstrated property.

### Minor

- **No variance or statistical significance measures.** No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multi-run statistics are reported for any benchmark result. Given that several reported gains are modest (e.g., 0.94% from the language expert), some differences may fall within noise. While single-run evaluation is common in large-scale MLLM benchmarking, the absence of any significance assessment weakens the reliability claims.

- **The integration of the language expert into LLM blocks is underspecified.** Equation (5) shows an adapter-style computation, and the text states that the expert is applied to features from RMS normalization and self-attention. However, the paper does not clearly state which specific LLM weights are affected, whether the generated parameters *replace* or are *added to* existing weights, and precisely how the expert interfaces with the FFN sub-layer. This limits reproducibility.

- **The analysis of why the first visual expert alternative works best is missing.** Table 2 shows that the simplest placement (first expert alone) yields the best results, but the paper offers no analysis of why — e.g., whether the other alternatives add too many parameters, create optimization difficulties, or introduce redundancy. The empirical result is useful but the lack of reasoning limits insight.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting takeaway from the reviews is the methodological tension: the paper convincingly shows that *adding expert modules with HyperNetwork-generated weights* improves over the static LLaVA-1.5, but it has not designed the experiment that would distinguish whether the dynamic (input-dependent) generation mechanism is the source of improvement, as opposed to simply adding more learnable parameters. This creates a clear path for follow-up work — any future paper claiming benefits from "dynamic" parameter generation should include the static-parameter-equivalent control. The paper's rich ablations on where to insert language guidance (early vs. mid vs. late LLM layers) and which visual expert placement works best are genuinely useful design insights for the MLLM community regardless of the attribution question.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a static adapter baseline:** Train the same expert architecture (same down/up projections, same insertion points) but with fixed learned weights — no HyperNetwork. If the static version matches HyperLLaVA's performance, the contribution is "adapters help LLaVA" (known). If the dynamic version outperforms, the core claim gains strong support.
2. **Report parameter counts and compare to LoRA/Adapter:** Provide the total number of trainable parameters for HyperLLaVA-7B and HyperLLaVA-13B, the percentage relative to the base model, and compare against applying LoRA or standard adapters to LLaVA-1.5. This grounds the "parameter-efficient" claim.
3. **Report standard deviations or conduct multi-run experiments** for at least a subset of benchmarks to establish that the reported gains are statistically significant.
4. **Clarify the language expert's interface with the LLM block** — state precisely which weights are generated, which are static, and whether the expert replaces or augments existing computations.

## Score and Decision

The paper tackles an interesting and well-motivated problem — replacing static MLLM parameters with input-dependent ones — and provides solid evidence that the proposed architecture outperforms LLaVA-1.5. The ablations are thorough and the design analysis is informative. However, two significant gaps prevent full acceptance: (1) the core claim that *dynamic* generation (rather than added capacity) drives improvements is not tested with the proper control, and (2) the parameter-efficiency claim is asserted without quantification. These are resolvable in revision but currently leave key attributions unsupported.
