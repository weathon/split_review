## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic benchmark for adversarial defenses on No-Reference Image Quality Assessment (NR IQA) metrics. It evaluates 25 defense methods (adversarial purification, adversarial training, certified robustness) against 14 attacks across both non-adaptive and adaptive settings, using 7 IQA metrics and a dataset of over 215,000 adversarial images. The main finding is that no defense is universally effective — simple spatial transformations (Flip, Crop, Rotate) dominate the non-adaptive setting, while randomized/differentiable methods (DiffJPEG, DiffPure, Random Noise) lead under adaptive attacks.

## Strengths

1. **First unified benchmark for IQA adversarial defenses.** Prior work (e.g., MSU Metrics Robustness Benchmark) evaluated metric robustness without defenses. This paper fills that gap by providing a systematic framework, a public leaderboard, and a released adversarial image dataset — making it a community resource for future work.

2. **Comprehensive coverage of defenses and attacks.** The benchmark spans 25 defense methods across three categories (purification, adversarial training, certified robustness) and 14 attack algorithms (white-box/black-box, restricted/unrestricted, with multiple parameter strengths). This breadth enables the paper's central empirical observation — that no single defense works across all scenarios — to be credibly supported.

3. **Differentiation of adaptive vs. non-adaptive settings.** The paper reveals that defense rankings reverse dramatically between these settings (e.g., Flip goes from best non-adaptive SROCC_adv=0.710 to worst adaptive SROCC_adv=0.356). This distinction provides actionable guidance for practitioners and justifies the benchmark's design.

4. **Adaptation of certified defenses to the regression setting.** The paper adapts classification-based certified methods (randomized smoothing, DensePure) to IQA's regression domain by discretizing score ranges into bins, and introduces median-smoothing variants for direct regression. Table 5 presents certified radii/relative deltas alongside empirical performance, a non-trivial engineering contribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Adaptive attack methodology for non-differentiable defenses is insufficiently documented.** The paper states (Section 3.2): "We integrate differentiable defenses into the attacked IQA metric... allow[ing] the adaptive attacks to access the IQA metrics' and defense methods' gradients." However, Table 2 reports adaptive-case results for defenses that are not obviously differentiable in a standard sense — most notably Median Blur (whose median operation has no well-defined gradient), but also Flip, Crop, Rotate with randomized parameters, and Random Noise. The paper never clarifies whether:
   - These defenses were rendered differentiable through smooth surrogates or reparameterization,  
   - The adaptive attack used a gradient-free/black-box approach for non-differentiable defenses, or  
   - Only a subset of defenses was actually tested under adaptive attacks.
   
   The paper mentions "patches needed to enable gradients in some metrics" (Section 4, line 214), but this refers to IQA metrics, not defenses. Without this detail, the adaptive results across defenses are not comparable on equal footing, and the paper's central ranking claims in the adaptive setting — e.g., "Rotate, Crop, Random Noise, DiffJPEG... perform better [adaptively]" — rest on an unverified methodological foundation. This is the single most critical reproducibility gap in the paper.

2. **Certified defense evaluation on only 10 images.** The paper acknowledges (Section 3, line 185) that certified defenses were evaluated on just 10 images "due to significantly higher computational complexity." With n=10, the confidence intervals in Table 5 are necessarily wide (e.g., DRS: D_score=11.34±7.62%, DMS: D_score=12.57±11.39%), and the paper draws comparative conclusions such as "defenses involving diffusion denoising (DDRS, DP) show better results than other methods" (Section 5). These conclusions are fragile and may not generalize. The paper does not flag this as preliminary in the main text, nor does it estimate the sample size needed for statistically meaningful comparisons. The Limitations section acknowledges ranking methodology but does not single out the certified defense sample size issue.

### Minor

1. **Parameter selection for adaptive comparison is transparent but undiscussed.** The caption of Table 2 states that "defense parameters' values with the highest SROCC_adv are selected in non-adaptive case." The same parameters are then used for adaptive attacks. This is a legitimate design choice — it simulates a defender who tunes before knowing the attack — but the paper does not discuss whether parameters optimal for non-adaptive settings might be suboptimal for adaptive ones, nor could the relative ordering of defenses change if re-optimized. A brief caveat would suffice.

2. **$Q_{score}$ composite metric is ad hoc.** Combining SSIM and PSNR/40 with equal 1:1 weighting (treating a 40 dB PSNR improvement as equivalent to a unit increase in SSIM) has no perceptual or theoretical grounding. The paper acknowledges (Section 3.5) that it "does not properly reflect visual differences between images in case of spatial transformations," but uses the same uniform $Q_{score}$ across all defenses anyway. Including LPIPS or DISTS as a secondary quality measure would strengthen this part of the evaluation. Currently, the quality axis is not reliable enough to support fine-grained comparisons among defenses.

3. **No statistical significance testing across defenses.** The paper reports means with confidence intervals but never tests whether pairwise differences between defenses are statistically significant (e.g., paired bootstrap or Wilcoxon signed-rank over images). The Limitations section (line 312) acknowledges "the current ranking methodology is straightforward... a more rigorous statistical framework is needed," but for a benchmark intended to "guide future developments," the absence of any significance testing in the current paper limits the strength of claims like "DISCO performs well in non-adaptive settings but falls short in adaptive ones" (Section 5).

4. **Adversarial training results receive only one qualitative paragraph in the main text.** Despite being one of the three main defense categories, adversarial training is covered by a single paragraph (Section 4) with no table, no error bars, and no quantitative comparison. The supplementary contains the details, but for a benchmark paper, at least one summary table for adversarial training should appear in the main text. As is, a reader cannot assess the relative performance of adversarial training against purification or certified defenses from the main paper alone.

5. **Insufficient caveats about wide confidence intervals.** While the paper notes that confidence intervals are large (Section 5) and attributes this to variability across metrics, attacks, and attack strengths, the takeaway tables (Table 2) present point estimates without qualifying that many pairwise differences are likely statistically indistinguishable. Readers could easily over-interpret small numerical gaps.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Include attacks that decrease IQA scores.** The paper focuses on increasing-score attacks (plausible for manipulation of search results/benchmarks). Evaluating defenses against decreasing-score attacks (relevant for poisoning or moderation scenarios) would strengthen claims of comprehensiveness.
- **Runtime-robustness analysis.** The paper provides runtime (mean ms) in Table 2 but does not analyze the trade-off. A simple normalized metric (e.g., SROCC_adv per unit time) or a two-axis robustness-vs-speed plot would make the benchmark more actionable for practitioners.
- **Statistical comparison framework.** A critical distance diagram or pairwise p-value table would be valuable additions to the online leaderboard.
- **Parameter sensitivity Pareto fronts.** The paper already has Figure 2(b) showing performance with different defense parameters. Extending this to a systematic Pareto analysis (quality preservation vs. robustness) for each defense would provide concrete design guidance.

## Novel Insights

The reviews converge on a useful observation that the harsh critic partly misses: the paper's most valuable contributions are methodological (the framework, the unified evaluation protocol, the public leaderboard) rather than the specific empirical conclusions, which are preliminary. The strength finder correctly identifies the non-adaptive vs. adaptive ranking reversal as the paper's single most informative result. The key tension in the reviews is between appreciating the benchmark's scope and questioning the reliability of its detailed findings — a tension that can be resolved by caveating the latter rather than diminishing the former. An interesting meta-point: the paper's own Limitations section (line 312) already anticipates several of the reviewers' criticisms (ranking methodology, single-parameter attacks, no transferability study), suggesting the authors are aware of the gaps but did not foreground them enough in the main analysis sections.

## Suggestions

1. **Acknowledge precisely how adaptive attacks were conducted for each defense.** For each non-differentiable defense in Table 2, state whether a differentiable surrogate was used, a gradient-free attack was employed, or whether adaptive results were not computed. This is the single most impactful improvement for reproducibility.

2. **Flag the certified defense results as preliminary in the main text**, not just as a one-line mention of computational cost. Add a sentence like "Due to the small sample size (n=10), these results should be interpreted with caution; a full evaluation at scale is left to future work."

3. **Provide at least one summary table for adversarial training in the main paper**, reporting D_score, SROCC_adv, and SROCC_clear for the best configuration, with confidence intervals. A single number would be better than the current purely qualitative paragraph.

4. **Replace or supplement $Q_{score}$** with a standard learned perceptual metric (LPIPS, DISTS, or CLIP-IQA) for quality evaluation, at least as a secondary measure. The current equal-weight SSIM+PSNR/40 composite is not interpretable.

5. **Add a simple statistical comparison** — e.g., mark in Table 2 which defenses are statistically indistinguishable (overlap of bootstrapped confidence intervals, or a critical difference diagram in the supplementary). This would make the benchmark more actionable without requiring methodological upheaval.

6. **Frame the adaptive results more cautiously** given the unverified gradient-handling for non-differentiable defenses. If the paper's main empirical contribution is the benchmark rather than the specific rankings, then hedging on the adaptive numbers is the honest approach.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine and timely contribution as the first unified benchmark for IQA adversarial defenses. The breadth of methods, the public leaderboard, and the adversarial image dataset are valuable community assets that will likely be used. However, two issues prevent the paper from meeting its full potential in its current form: (1) the adaptive attack methodology for non-differentiable defenses is not adequately documented, making the adaptive results unverifiable and comparisons potentially unequal, and (2) the certified defense evaluation on only 10 images is too thin to support the conclusions drawn. These are fixable through added explanation and caveats rather than new experiments. The remaining issues (ad hoc quality metric, missing statistical tests, brief adversarial training coverage) are addressable in revision. I recommend acceptance conditional on addressing the two major gaps — a significant revision focused on transparency and caveats would bring the paper to the quality level its scope deserves.
