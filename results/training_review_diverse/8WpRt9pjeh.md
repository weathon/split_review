## Summary

This paper explores whether synthetic Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) transcripts generated by LLM-powered agents (GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus) can substitute for scarce human-labeled data in training classifiers to predict adult attachment styles. The authors design an agent architecture with structured profiles, childhood memories, and RAG-based retrieval, generate 60 synthetic interviews per LLM, and train three classifier families. A key technical contribution is a mean-shift standardization that aligns synthetic embeddings with human embeddings using 17 unlabeled human transcripts. The central claim is that models trained on standardized synthetic data achieve performance comparable to — and in some configurations surpass — models trained directly on a small set of human-labeled interviews.

## Strengths

- **Novel agent architecture for generating synthetic mental-health interview data.** The design (Section 4) combining unique profiles, 10 generated childhood memories per agent, and RAG-based retrieval of 3 relevant memories per question is principled and goes beyond naive LLM prompting. This architecture could be adapted to other clinical interview settings.

- **The mean-shift standardization technique is simple, novel, and demonstrably effective.** The procedure (lines 123–124) of subtracting the synthetic embedding mean and adding the unlabeled human embedding mean is computationally trivial yet produces consistent AUC gains across all classifiers and both LLMs (Table 2). The UMAP visualizations (Figure 5) corroborate that this shift brings synthetic clusters closer to human data without collapsing the attachment-style structure.

- **Evaluation across two LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus) and three classifier families (logistic regression, extra trees, MLP)**, with standardized vs. non-standardized comparisons and a volume analysis (Figure 6) with 10 random samplings per size, provides reasonable breadth for a proof-of-concept study.

- **Honest limitations section.** The paper explicitly acknowledges (Section 7) that synthetic data is easy to classify, that human data scarcity may lead to underestimation of human-data baselines, and that no formal evaluation of how closely synthetic responses mimic human ones was conducted.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Extremely small human test set (N=9) makes quantitative claims of "comparable performance" unreliable.** The entire evaluation rests on 9 labeled human interviews with per-class sizes of 3, 4, and 2 (Table 1). The ROC AUC for human baselines is computed from a single leave-one-out sweep over 9 samples, yet **no confidence intervals or bootstrap estimates are reported for any human-baseline result**. The synthetic-data runs report standard errors (0.04–0.08, Table 2), but the human baselines are presented as point estimates with unknown variance. A difference of, say, 0.604 vs. 0.838 between the human ℓ2 baseline and a synthetic result could easily arise from sampling noise. The paper's central claim — "training using only synthetic data achieves performance comparable to training on human data" (abstract) — cannot be reliably supported when the human-data reference itself is a random variable with enormous uncertainty given N=9. The paper frames itself as a proof of concept (line 21), but the abstract and strength of language in the results (e.g., "surpassed," line 133) go beyond what this test set can sustain.

2. **No validation that synthetic responses reflect genuine attachment-style behavior vs. prompt artifacts.** The method assigns an attachment style to each agent and instructs the LLM to respond accordingly. The resulting transcripts may be easy to classify because the LLM consistently injects attachment-style cues into every response, unlike real humans who are more ambiguous. The paper acknowledges this ("synthetic data is fairly easy to classify," line 146) and notes it did not formally evaluate how closely synthetic responses mimic human ones (Section 7). However, this is not just a limitation — it is a core evidential gap. The UMAP projection (Figure 5) shows that synthetic and human embeddings occupy *distinct regions* of the embedding space before standardization, and the standardization is merely a mean shift, not a style transfer. Without a human evaluation (e.g., blinded raters judging whether synthetic transcripts are plausible for their assigned attachment style) or a psychometric validation, we cannot tell whether the synthetic data captures the same psychological construct or merely reflects easy-to-classify prompt-driven cues. This gap fundamentally weakens the conclusion that synthetic data *predicts human attachment styles* rather than *exploits a distributional artifact that happens to correlate with the target labels within a 9-sample evaluation*.

### Minor

3. **The synthetic data volume experiment (Figure 6) shows no clear improvement beyond a small initial sample size.** The paper honestly reports this (line 146) and attributes it to synthetic data being easy to classify. But this pattern also raises the concern that the downstream task on human data may be testing the *domain alignment* achieved by standardization (which uses the unlabeled human data) rather than whether the synthetic data captures meaningful variation in attachment styles. The evaluation is not a pure test of synthetic data utility — it is confounded with the alignment provided by the unlabeled human data.

4. **The human LR-ℓ1 baseline (AUC ~0.805–0.809) is close to the best synthetic results, and no confidence intervals are reported for any human baseline.** The paper's own text (line 133) notes that the ℓ1 human baseline shows "comparable to the best synthetic data figures." Without error bars on the human baselines, it is impossible to determine whether synthetic data truly outperforms or matches human-data training, or whether the observed differences are within sampling noise.

5. **The standardization technique is a simple mean shift, with no exploration of alternatives or discussion of its limitations.** The method assumes a translation-only relationship between synthetic and human embedding distributions. Whether a more flexible alignment (e.g., Procrustes, CCA, or learned domain adaptation) would yield different results is unexplored. Given that this standardization is central to the paper's claims, some discussion of why a mean shift is sufficient (or what it misses) would strengthen the work.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface a key tension that is worth articulating: the paper's core methodological innovation (the mean-shift standardization) is also its greatest confound. The standardization leverages the *unlabeled* human data to shift synthetic embeddings into the human embedding space. This is clever, but it means the evaluation cannot cleanly separate the utility of the synthetic data from the utility of the unlabeled human data that guides the alignment. A stronger paper in this space would need an experimental design that isolates these two sources of signal — for instance, comparing the standardization approach against a semi-supervised learner that operates on the unlabeled human data alone. The current design leaves open the possibility that the unlabeled human data is doing most of the work, and the synthetic data is merely providing a convenient source of labeled variation within an already-aligned space.

## Suggestions

1. **Temper the claims to match the evidence.** Replace "performance comparable to training on human data" (abstract) with "shows promise as a supplement in settings with extremely limited human data, though the evaluation is constrained by a very small test set (N=9)." The paper's own framing as a "proof of concept" (line 21) is more accurate than the abstract language.
2. **Report uncertainty for human baselines.** Compute and report bootstrap confidence intervals for the leave-one-out ROC AUC on the 9 human samples. This would transparently communicate the fragility of the reference point.
3. **Conduct a small human evaluation of synthetic transcripts.** Even 10–20 synthetic transcripts rated by a single blinded coder for plausibility of the assigned attachment style would substantially strengthen construct validity.
4. **Explore whether the synthetic data contributes beyond the unlabeled human data.** A cleaner evaluation would compare: (a) classifier trained on 60 synthetic samples with standardization (current approach) vs. (b) a semi-supervised method using only the 17 unlabeled human samples.
