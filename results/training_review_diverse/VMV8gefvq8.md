## Summary

This paper introduces Manifold-Constrained Neural Compression (MCNC), a reparameterization method for compressing neural networks. MCNC constrains parameters to a low-dimensional non-linear manifold by using a frozen random generator network with sinusoidal activations to map a low-dimensional input space to a high-dimensional hypersphere. The key idea — replacing the linear subspaces used in prior methods (PRANC, NOLA) with a non-linear manifold generated by a random sine network — yields meaningful empirical improvements: a 7% accuracy gain over PRANC and NOLA at 1% model size on ImageNet-100 with ResNet-18, and competitive LLM fine-tuning performance with a 2× throughput advantage over NOLA due to cheaper on-the-fly parameter generation.

## Strengths

- **Strong empirical results at extreme compression rates on vision tasks.** Table 2 shows MCNC achieves 7% higher accuracy than the direct reparameterization competitors PRANC and NOLA at 1% model size on ImageNet-100 (ResNet-18). Table 3 shows consistent outperformance across CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-20 and ResNet-56. These results are the paper's central empirical contribution and are clearly established against the most relevant baselines (PRANC, NOLA).

- **Efficiency advantages for on-the-fly parameter generation.** Table 4 reports MCNC requires 46% fewer GFLOPs than NOLA for generating adapter parameters, yielding 2.0×–2.2× throughput improvement for LLaMA fine-tuning. Table 9 shows a 2× reduction in CPU-to-GPU transfer time for a ViT-S model compressed 100×. These practical deployment benefits are well-documented and non-trivial.

- **General applicability demonstrated across diverse settings.** The method works for training-from-scratch (ViT, ResNet on ImageNet-100, CIFAR) and PEFT (LLaMA 7B/13B), showing breadth across architectures and modalities. The ablation study (Section 4.3) provides a systematic investigation of design choices on MNIST, including activation function, input/output dimensions, frequency, and model scaling.

- **Elegant storage/communication efficiency via seed-based generator.** As noted in Section 3.1, the random generator can be stored and transmitted using a single random seed, avoiding storage of the full generator weights. This is a practical advantage over methods that require storing the full generator or basis.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **LLM experiments lack standard PEFT baselines.** The LLM fine-tuning evaluation (Table 4) compares MCNC only against NOLA. LoRA, QLoRA, and AdaLoRA — which define the actual standard baselines for parameter-efficient fine-tuning — are not included. The paper's own text states MCNC achieves "comparable performance to NOLA" on LLM accuracy (MMLU 46.1 vs. 46.3 on LLaMA-7B is indeed tied), so the accuracy claim for LLMs is modest. However, the paper's abstract claims "significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines" without qualification. For LLM accuracy this is unsupported. The throughput advantage over NOLA is real, but without LoRA/QLoRA comparisons, it is unclear whether the throughput benefits translate to advantages over the broader ecosystem of PEFT methods. This is the paper's most significant weakness.

- **Core theoretical motivation (hypersphere coverage) validated only on a 3D toy example.** The paper grounds its method in the claim that a random sine-network generator achieves better coverage of the hypersphere, maximizing the likelihood of intersecting the good-solution manifold. The only evidence for this is the 3D (k=1, d=3) example in Figure 2. In the actual experimental settings (d=5000, k=5 or 9), no coverage metric, Wasserstein distance, or distributional analysis is provided. At high dimensions, even random linear subspaces have well-known uniform-like properties (Haar measure on the Grassmannian). Without quantitative evidence that the sine generator's coverage differs meaningfully from a random linear projection at the dimensions used, it remains unclear whether the empirical gains stem from better geometry or simply from increased effective capacity in the generator. An ablation controlling for effective degrees of freedom (e.g., MCNC with a linear generator of matched parameter count, or PRANC with increased subspace dimension) would help isolate the source of improvement.

- **No robustness reporting across different generator random seeds.** The generator's random initialization defines the entire search manifold; different seeds yield different manifolds. The paper reports no variance across generator seeds in any of the main experiments (ImageNet-100, CIFAR, LLM fine-tuning). The MNIST ablations average over three trials, but it is unclear whether the generator seed varies across these trials or only the learning rate and optimizer noise. Unless the authors show that results are reproducible across multiple generator seeds (and ideally also across model initializations and data splits), the reported results are not demonstrably robust. If variance is low, addressing this strengthens the paper; if high, it reveals a critical weakness.

### Minor

- **Generator output normalization is unspecified.** The paper defines the generator as mapping to S^{d-1} (the unit hypersphere) and uses Δθ = β·φ(α). For a feed-forward network's output to lie on the unit sphere, L2 normalization is required. No explicit normalization step is described. This is needed for reproducibility. (The "no activation" ablation description hints that in the normal case, output magnitudes are controlled separately via β, but the exact mechanism is not stated.)

- **The k/d ablation (Table 6) confounds two effects.** The experiment multiplies both k (input dimension) and d (output dimension/chunk size) by a constant factor to keep the compression rate fixed. This simultaneously changes the generator's input dimensionality and its output dimensionality (and thus chunk size), making it unclear which factor drives the observed improvement.

- **Hyperparameter design choices are ablated only on a single small-scale proxy task.** The generator has many free parameters (activation, frequency, k, d, depth, width) that are explored only on MNIST with a tiny MLP at 0.2% compression. The choices carried to the larger experiments (e.g., d=5000, hidden dim 32 for LLMs) lack validation that they transfer well. The paper's Limitations section acknowledges this, which is commendable, but the gap between the ablation task and the main experiments is large enough that some readers may question whether the results depend on fortunate tuning.

### Trivial

- The paper's use of "manifold-constrained" could be confused with Riemannian optimization approaches, though the text (Section 2) explicitly clarifies that it uses standard SGD rather than geometric optimization. This is not really a problem but a minor presentational concern.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Add LoRA (matched parameter count) and optionally QLoRA as baselines in Table 4 to substantiate the LLM claims against the broader PEFT literature.
- For d=5000, k=5 and k=9, report an empirical coverage statistic (e.g., histogram of pairwise dot products of generator outputs under uniform input sampling) to support the theoretical motivation at scale.
- Ablate the chunk size d while keeping the generator architecture fixed (rather than scaling k and d together) to disentangle the effects of input dimension and output dimension.
- Compare MCNC with a linear generator of the same parameter count to isolate whether the benefit comes from non-linearity or simply from increased generator capacity.

## Removed Points

- *"Pruning baselines (Magnitude, PLATON) are not SOTA"* — The paper's primary comparison is against reparameterization methods (PRANC, NOLA); the pruning comparison is supplementary. Without external knowledge to verify whether PLATON is or is not SOTA, and given that the pruning baselines are presented as representative (not exhaustive), this criticism is not actionable.
- *"The 'manifold-constrained' term may mislead readers expecting Riemannian optimization"* — The paper explicitly clarifies (Section 2) that it uses standard SGD, not Riemannian optimization. This is clearly stated and not misleading.
- *"The paper should cover Y/domain Z/additional tasks"* — The paper's scope (vision classifiers + LLM fine-tuning) is already broad. Demands for additional domains constitute scope creep.

## Suggestions

1. **Broaden the LLM baseline set.** Add LoRA with matched parameter count (and ideally QLoRA and AdaLoRA) to Table 4. If LoRA at the same parameter budget is competitive on accuracy, the throughput advantage still stands as a meaningful contribution. If LoRA substantially outperforms MCNC on accuracy, the paper should recalibrate its claims accordingly.

2. **Provide coverage evidence at scale.** For the generator settings used in practice (d=5000, k=5), compute and report the empirical distribution of pairwise angular distances or dot products of generator outputs, compared against a random linear projection of the same input dimension. This would substantiate (or refute) the claim that the sine generator provides meaningfully different coverage at high dimensions.

3. **Report variance across generator seeds.** For at least one representative setting (e.g., ImageNet-100 ViT-Ti at a moderate compression rate), run MCNC with 3-5 different generator random seeds and report mean and standard deviation. This is essential for a method whose performance could depend substantially on one random initialization.

4. **Clarify the normalization step.** Explicitly state whether the generator output is L2-normalized before being multiplied by β, and if so, at what point in the computation graph.

5. **Control for effective degrees of freedom.** Add an ablation comparing MCNC with a linear generator (no activation) of matching parameter count. If the linear generator matches MCNC's performance, the non-linearity is not the source of improvement; if it does not, the non-linearity claim is strengthened.
