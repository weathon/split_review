## Summary

This paper proposes Ego-Foresight (EF), a self-supervised method for agent-environment disentanglement that uses proprioception-conditioned video prediction to learn visual features for model-free RL. The key idea is that by predicting future visual frames conditioned on proprioceptive states, the model learns to separate what is part of the agent (predictable from its own motion) from what is part of the scene. The method is integrated with DDPG and evaluated on DMC, Distracting DMC, and Meta-world benchmarks, showing competitive results against supervised (SEAR) and model-based (Dreamer-v3, TD-MPC2) baselines.

## Strengths

- **Removes need for costly supervision**: EF achieves agent-environment disentanglement without the supervisory masks required by prior work (e.g., SEAR), using only RGB frames and proprioceptive states. This is the paper's central contribution and is well-demonstrated both quantitatively (matching/approaching SEAR on several tasks) and qualitatively (Figure 7 shows the model correctly predicting agent motion while keeping static scene elements fixed).

- **Meaningful advantage on high-DOF embodiments**: On the Quadruped task (16 DOF), EF significantly outperforms all baselines including the supervised SEAR. The trend of increasing relative advantage with agent complexity supports the paper's thesis that self-prediction of the agent's motion is especially beneficial for controlling complex embodiments (Section 4.2, Figure 4).

- **Competitive results across diverse manipulation tasks without per-task tuning**: On 10 Meta-world tasks, EF uses the same hyperparameters for all tasks and achieves the best success rate in 3 tasks, matches baselines in 3 others, and underperforms in 4 (Section 4.3, Figure 6). The transparency about mixed results is a strength of the presentation.

- **Adaptive body-schema demonstrated qualitatively**: Figure 7 shows that after grasping a hammer, the model predicts the hammer's motion as part of the agent, demonstrating dynamic adaptation of the agent/scene boundary. This is a novel and interesting phenomenon.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The role of proprioceptive conditioning is not isolated by ablation.** The paper's core claim is that *proprioception-conditioned* prediction (i.e., the agent-specific predictive signal) drives improvement. However, the ablations only vary prediction horizon \(H\) and similarity loss weight \(\alpha\) (Figures 8–9). There is no ablation that removes proprioceptive conditioning entirely — e.g., comparing against a version conditioned on actions instead of proprioception, or on scrambled/random proprioception, or an unconditioned video prediction baseline. Without this control, the paper cannot rule out that any auxiliary prediction objective (action-conditional video prediction) would yield similar benefits. Given that prior work (Ebert et al., 2017, cited in the paper) has shown action-conditional video prediction can aid RL, this is a critical missing control for validating the claimed mechanism.

- **The SEAR baseline comparison is unreliable.** The paper explicitly states (line 145): "For SEAR, despite using the publicly available code, we failed to match the results reported in the original paper. We note however, some inconsistencies in the original plots such as success rates above 1." Yet SEAR is presented as the primary comparison (the "oracle" supervised baseline), and claims about EF matching or outperforming SEAR are drawn from the authors' own (unmatched) runs. If SEAR's true performance is higher than what is reported here, the comparison could look substantially different. The paper should either (a) use the original published SEAR numbers for the tasks and metrics that overlap, (b) provide a careful analysis of why the reproduction failed and what adjustments were made, or (c) de-emphasize the SEAR comparison and frame results relative to the reliably reproducible baselines (DrQ-v2, Dreamer-v3, TD-MPC2).

- **The motor babbling stage creates an uncontrolled variable in the comparison.** EF receives 50k steps of structured exploratory data at the start of training during which the visuomotor prediction model is pre-trained, while baselines do not receive a comparable phase (the paper's claim that this is done "for a fair comparison to the baselines" is self-contradictory — baselines do not get this phase). This raises the question: would baselines also benefit from 50k steps of task-agnostic data collection + representation pre-training before RL begins? The paper does not control for this. At minimum, the authors should compare against DrQ-v2 given a similar 50k-step random exploration phase (with or without an auxiliary reconstruction loss), or demonstrate that babbling is not the dominant factor by ablating its length or presence. This is especially important for tasks solved within ~150k total steps (e.g., Cartpole, Door Close, Button Press Wall), where 50k steps of babbling represent a third or more of the total budget — the learning after babbling is fast, but the paper does not isolate whether this is due to the representation or simply having collected diverse data.

### Minor

- **Limited statistical rigor.** The paper acknowledges (line 145, line 153) that "not all baselines and experiments use the same number of random seeds" and that "greater variability in our results is due to a lower number repetitions." The exact seed counts are relegated to the appendix. Given DDPG's known instability (which the paper itself discusses), this weakens confidence in comparative claims, especially where margins are small or task-dependent.

- **Claim about babbling reusability is untested.** The paper suggests (line 147) that babbling "would suffice to go through this stage once per robot body" and then reuse the pre-trained model across tasks. This is a plausible advantage but no experiment demonstrates it — all experiments train EF from scratch per task. Similarly, the paper claims babbling "opens up the possibility of reusing a model pre-trained on babbling to improve sample-efficiency on multi-task learning" (line 191), but provides no supporting evidence.

- **The scene-stability assumption is untested.** The paper assumes that scene content does not change significantly within the prediction horizon (line 98–99) and uses a similarity loss to enforce this. However, there is no analysis of when this assumption breaks (e.g., independently moving objects that are not grasped) or what failure modes result. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation in the discussion but does not characterize its impact.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The paper relies on pixel-level L2 reconstruction loss, acknowledged as computationally heavy. Exploring alternatives (perceptual losses, latent-space prediction, contrastive losses) would be useful but is appropriately scoped as future work.
- The claim about real-world applicability would be strengthened by any qualitative video prediction result on the BAIR dataset in the main text rather than only in the appendix.

- **Criticism about not comparing against Ebert et al. 2017 as a baseline:** The paper cites this work in related work but does not use it as a baseline; this is a reasonable scoping choice given the paper's focus on proprioception specifically.
- **"Not all experiments use the same number of random seeds" as a fatal weakness:** The paper is transparent about this limitation and the seed counts are in the appendix. This is a minor concern, not a fatal one.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding beyond the paper's own claims is the adaptive body-schema phenomenon shown qualitatively — the model integrates a grasped tool into its predictive model of "self," effectively treating the hammer as part of the agent once it is picked up. This suggests that the proprioception-conditioned prediction framework could serve as a computational model of tool embodiment, aligning with neuroscientific findings about body schema plasticity (Maravita & Iriki, 2004, cited in the paper). However, this phenomenon is only shown qualitatively on two tasks and the paper does not quantify how rapidly or reliably this adaptation occurs, nor whether it degrades task performance when objects are incorrectly integrated into the agent representation.

## Suggestions

1. **Add the missing proprioception ablation**: Compare EF against a version where the auxiliary loss is action-conditional video prediction (using actions instead of proprioception as the conditioning signal), keeping the architecture identical. If EF's advantage persists, the claim about proprioception-specific benefits is weakened; if it collapses, the core mechanism is validated.

2. **Control for the babbling phase**: Run DrQ-v2 with a 50k-step random exploration phase before RL begins (matched to EF's babbling budget), and plot learning curves from the end of that phase. This directly tests whether it is EF's specific representation learning or simply having diverse exploratory data that drives improvement.

3. **Address the SEAR comparison**: Either reproduce SEAR more faithfully (reaching out to the original authors if needed) or use the originally published SEAR numbers where available, clearly marking which data comes from the paper vs. the authors' own runs. If neither is possible, explicitly state that the SEAR comparison is tentative and frame the paper's main results relative to DrQ-v2 and model-based methods.

4. **Report seed counts in the main text** and commit to at least 5 seeds for all primary comparisons, given DDPG's known instability.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes an interesting, well-motivated idea and demonstrates a working system with competitive results across multiple benchmarks. However, three structural weaknesses undermine the experimental support for the core claims: (1) the proprioceptive conditioning mechanism — the paper's central innovation — is not isolated by ablation, (2) the primary comparative baseline (SEAR) is admitted to be irreproducible, and (3) the motor babbling stage introduces an uncontrolled variable in the sample-efficiency comparison. These require additional experiments to address, not just textual clarification. The paper's contributions are real but the evidence is not yet convincing enough for acceptance.
