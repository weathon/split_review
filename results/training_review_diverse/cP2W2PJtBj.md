## Summary

The paper proposes AntifakePrompt, a deepfake detection method that re-purposes a vision-language model (InstructBLIP) by formulating detection as a visual question answering task and applying soft prompt tuning. With only 4,864 trainable parameters, the method achieves 91.81% average accuracy across 23 diverse held-out datasets, substantially outperforming all non-VLM baselines (next best: 72.34% from DE-FAKE). The paper also contributes a comprehensive benchmark covering six deepfake scenarios — text-to-image, editing, face swapping, and adversarial attacks — spanning 18 generative models.

## Strengths

- **Novel formulation of deepfake detection as VQA with prompt tuning.** The paper is the first to frame deepfake detection as a visual question answering problem and apply soft prompt tuning on a VLM (InstructBLIP). This is explicitly claimed and correctly positioned relative to prior work (Section 1, lines 34-35). The approach enables the detector to leverage the VLM's broad multimodal pretraining for generalization.

- **State-of-the-art held-out accuracy with orders-of-magnitude fewer parameters.** AntifakePrompt achieves 91.81% average accuracy across 23 datasets (Table 2, Orig. column) while tuning only 4,864 parameters. This surpasses the next-best non-VLM baseline (DE-FAKE F at 72.34%, which uses 11M parameters) by a wide margin. The gap is consistent: 91.81% vs. 72.34% on average, and AntifakePrompt outperforms every finetuned baseline on nearly every diffusion-generated dataset.

- **Comprehensive benchmark covering 23 datasets from 18 generative models.** The paper systematically curates held-out testing datasets covering text-to-image (SD2, SD3, SDXL, IF, DALLE-2/3, Playground, etc.), image editing (LaMa, SD2IP, LIIF, SD2SR), face swapping (DF, DFDC, FF++), and three types of adversarial attacks (Section 4.1). This collection enables rigorous evaluation of generalization and serves as a reusable benchmark for the field.

- **Extensive ablation studies providing practical design insights.** Table 1 systematically studies pseudo-word position, tuning target (Q-former vs. LLM vs. both), and training data scaling. Key findings — tuning both Q-former and LLM yields the best average (92.74%), performance remains strong even at 15K samples, and there is a meaningful trade-off between real and fake accuracy as training data composition changes — offer practical guidance for future prompt-tuning-based detectors.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The claim about attack detection performance is contradicted by the paper's own data.** The paper states (line 274): "AntifakePrompt consistently outperforms almost every baseline on the datasets generated by 3 attacking strategies." This is false. In Table 2, ResNet (finetuned, column "F") achieves 99.36% (Adver.), 92.00% (Backdoor), and 97.53% (Data Poison.) — substantially outperforming AntifakePrompt (Orig.) at 87.10%, 86.20%, and 88.63% respectively. DE-FAKE (F) also outperforms AntifakePrompt on Backdoor (89.37% vs. 86.20%). The claim must be retracted or explicitly qualified. This kind of factual mismatch undermines trust in the paper's reporting accuracy. The core contribution is unaffected, but the sentence as written is misleading and must be corrected.

- **The detector's failure on LaMa inpainting (39.40%) is under-analyzed.** The paper acknowledges the poor result and attempts to fix it by adding LaMa images to training (yielding 55.80% — still well below viable detection). However, there is no analysis of *why* LaMa images are so different from diffusion-generated images. Inpainting is a common real-world application (object removal), so this is not a niche case. The paper should investigate whether the failure stems from missing diffusion-specific artifacts in LaMa outputs, different statistical properties of inpainting vs. full synthesis, or a fundamental limitation of VLM-based detectors. Without this analysis, the claim of covering a "full spectrum" of generative models rings hollow.

### Minor

- **Real-image accuracy trade-off deserves more serious treatment.** AntifakePrompt (Orig.) achieves 92.53% on COCO and 91.57% on Flickr, while several finetuned baselines (ResNet F, Wang2020 F, DIRE F) are near-perfect (99%+) on real images. False-positive rates matter deeply for deployment. The paper mentions this trade-off in the ablation study but does not quantify precision-recall or ROC-AUC, nor does it discuss application scenarios where a 7-8% false positive rate is or is not acceptable. This should be addressed honestly rather than treated as a simple balancing choice.

- **No limitations paragraph.** The conclusion (lines 283-287) summarizes strengths but never acknowledges the LaMa failure, the relatively lower real-image accuracy, the vulnerability to simple finetuned ResNet detectors on attack data, or the 7B-parameter backbone's inference cost. A brief limitations paragraph would substantially improve credibility.

- **Missing confidence intervals or standard deviations.** Given that some comparisons are close (e.g., AntifakePrompt 99.03% vs. SGXL baselines), a reader cannot assess whether differences are meaningful. Error estimates or ROC-AUC should be reported for core results.

- **Baseline comparison presentation is cluttered by mixing P and F variants in a single table.** The paper discusses "F" (finetuned on same data) and "P" (pretrained) baselines side-by-side without a clear separation. A separate table comparing only models trained on the same data would make the benefit of the method versus the benefit of training data easier to isolate.

### Trivial

- The phrasing "leveraging zero-shot advantages of VLMs" could be more precise. Prompt tuning is supervised adaptation, not zero-shot. The paper correctly states this distinction in the VQA framing but the abstract and intro language could invite misinterpretation.

## Suggestions

5. **Add precision-recall analysis** for the real vs. fake trade-off to give a more complete picture of detector behavior beyond binary accuracy.
6. **Reorganize Table 2** or add an auxiliary table that separates models trained on the same training data (F variants) from pretrained models (P variants).
