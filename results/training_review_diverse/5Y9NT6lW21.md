## Summary

This paper proposes APPO (Adversarial Preference-based Policy Optimization), a new algorithm for offline preference-based RL. The core idea is to frame PbRL as a two-player Stackelberg game between a policy and a reward/value model, using a trajectory-pair ℓ₁ penalty to enforce conservatism without intractable confidence-set construction. The paper provides a sample complexity bound under standard assumptions (Theorem 4.1), a reparameterization trick that avoids online rollouts, and a practical deep-RL implementation. Experiments on Meta-world continuous control tasks show APPO performs comparably to state-of-the-art PbRL baselines.

## Strengths

- **First computationally efficient offline PbRL algorithm with a provable sample complexity bound under general function approximation.** The paper clearly identifies the gap: prior works (Zhan et al., 2024a; Pace et al., 2024) require intractable confidence-set optimization or nested optimization loops, while APPO replaces these with a tractable two-player game using an ℓ₁ penalty. This is a genuine conceptual advance. The theoretical bound (Theorem 4.1) avoids dependence on the transition concentrability coefficient \(C_P\), which can be exponentially large.

- **Novel sub-optimality decomposition enabling provable conservatism without confidence sets.** The proof sketch (Section 4) decomposes the sub-optimality into MLE error, optimization error, and policy update regret. The key technical insight is showing that with \(\lambda > C_{TR}\), the adversarial optimization guarantees \(V_{1,\hat{r}-r^t}^{\pi^*} - V_{1,\hat{r}-r^t}^\mu \leq \lambda\mathcal{E}(f^t; P^*, \hat{r})\), linking the game solution directly to the sub-optimality bound. This is a clean theoretical contribution.

- **Reparameterization trick that removes the need for online rollouts or known transitions.** Section 3.3 derives an equivalence (Equation 6 → 7) that rewrites the policy advantage as an expectation under the offline data distribution \(d_h^\mu\) and reparameterizes the reward optimization as optimization over value functions \(f\) using the induced reward model (Definition 1). This makes the algorithm fully offline while preserving theoretical tractability.

- **Practical implementation with competitive empirical results.** The paper provides a concrete deep-RL implementation using clipped double Q-learning, target networks, and standard policy optimization. Experiments on Meta-world (Table 1) show APPO matches or exceeds strong baselines (MR, PT, DPPO, IPL) on most tasks.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The theoretical guarantee does not cover the practical algorithm, and the gap is not acknowledged.** The theoretical analysis (Section 4 proof sketch) assumes access to the true transition \(P^\star\) for constructing the induced reward model \(r_{P^\star, f}^\pi\) (line 222: \(r^t = r_{P^\star, f^t}^{\pi^t}\)), and the optimization in Equation (7) uses \(\mathcal{E}(f; P^\star, \hat{r})\). However, Algorithm 2 uses the estimated model \(\hat{P}\) instead. While the paper notes this substitution (line 174), the proof sketch does not analyze the error introduced by using \(\hat{P}\) vs \(P^\star\). The bound includes \(\log(|\mathcal{P}|/\delta)\), suggesting the transition learning is accounted for in the (stripped) appendix, but the main paper does not discuss how this approximation affects the sample complexity guarantee. Furthermore, the practical implementation (Section 5) replaces the learned model \(\hat{P}\) with a bootstrap approximation \(V_\psi(s_{l+1})\) (line 251), which is an additional unexamined gap. The paper claims APPO is "the first offline PbRL algorithm to offer both statistical efficiency and practical applicability," but the guarantee strictly applies to a more idealized version. This gap needs explicit discussion and ideally an error analysis. (A reader might accept the gap if it were honestly bounded or at least qualitatively assessed; as written, it is structurally unresolved.)

### Minor

- **Oracle-beating results are unexplained.** APPO exceeds the "Oracle" (IQL with ground-truth rewards) on dial-turn and button-press-topdown-wall (Table 1). This is counterintuitive since the Oracle has access to the true reward signal. The paper offers no hypothesis for why this occurs (e.g., Oracle IQL suboptimality, helpful inductive bias from preference-based learning, adversarial regularization acting as an implicit bias). While not central to the paper's main claim, leaving this anomalous result unexplained raises mild credibility concerns about the fairness of the comparison.

- **Baseline numbers are taken from a prior paper without re-running, and no variance is reported.** Results for Oracle, PT, DPPO, and IPL are cited from Choi et al. (2024) rather than re-run in the same codebase, introducing uncontrolled differences in training pipelines and hyperparameter tuning. Only averages over 5 seeds are reported (Table 1 caption), with no standard deviations or confidence intervals. For a method with multiple stochastic components, this makes it difficult to assess whether APPO's performance differences are statistically significant.

- **The hyperparameter \(\lambda\) sensitivity is only shown on one task.** The ablation (Figure 1) evaluates sensitivity to \(\lambda\) on a single task whose name is not given in the caption. The paper claims APPO "has only one algorithmic hyperparameter" and shows robustness, but demonstrating this on more tasks would substantiate the claim. The fixed value \(\lambda = 3\times10^{-2}\) is used across all tasks without describing how it was selected.

- **The bound's dependence on \(T\) (iteration count) in the unlabeled sample size is unusual and not discussed.** Theorem 4.1's bound contains \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\max\{H T \log\frac{H|\mathcal{F}|}{\delta}, \log\frac{H|\mathcal{P}|}{\delta}\}}\). The dependence of \(N\) on \(T\) means the required unlabeled data grows with the number of policy iterations, which is atypical and potentially pessimistic. The paper does not comment on this.

- **Approximation error \(\epsilon\) in the proof sketch is not defined or bounded in the main paper.** The proof introduces an \(\epsilon\) for the optimization error (line 231) but does not specify its magnitude or what controls it. The reader cannot assess how tight the final bound is.

- **The practical policy update uses entropy regularization (Section 5), while the theory uses a KL-regularized TRPO update (Lemma D.3).** These are not equivalent, and the impact on the regret bound is not discussed. This is another instance of the theory-practice gap.

- **The claim that APPO "outperforms a policy trained with ground truth rewards, using only 100 preference feedback" (line 295) references a comparison not shown in Figure 2** (which only plots APPO and MR). The Oracle is only in Table 1, making the cross-reference ambiguous.

## Score and Decision

This paper presents a genuinely novel algorithmic framework for offline PbRL with both theoretical guarantees and practical applicability. The core technical contributions — the Stackelberg game formulation with the ℓ₁ penalty, the sub-optimality decomposition, and the reparameterization trick — are significant and represent a clear advance over prior work that relies on intractable confidence sets. However, the paper has a structural unresolved gap between the theoretical guarantees (which assume the true transition \(P^\star\)) and the practical algorithm (which uses learned \(\hat{P}\) and bootstrap approximations). This gap is not acknowledged or analyzed. Combined with experimental weaknesses (unexplained Oracle-beating results, missing variance, baselines not re-run), the paper falls short of the standard for acceptance in its current form.
