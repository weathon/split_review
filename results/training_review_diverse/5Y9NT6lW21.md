## Summary

This paper proposes Adversarial Preference-based Policy Optimization (APPO), a computationally efficient offline preference-based RL algorithm with provable sample complexity guarantees. The key innovation is framing offline PbRL as a two-player Stackelberg game between a policy and a reward model, using a trajectory-pair ℓ₁ penalty to enforce conservatism without intractable confidence sets. A reparameterization via induced reward models eliminates the need for on-policy rollouts. Theorem 4.1 provides a sample complexity bound, and experiments on Meta-world continuous control tasks demonstrate competitive empirical performance against state-of-the-art methods.

## Strengths

- **First computationally efficient offline PbRL algorithm with provable sample complexity.** The paper states: "To our knowledge, APPO is the first computationally efficient offline PbRL algorithm providing a sample complexity bound" (Section 1), and Theorem 4.1 provides explicit bounds in terms of trajectory concentrability, function class sizes, and sample sizes. This addresses a genuine limitation of prior theoretical work (FREEHAND-transition, Sim-OPRL) that relied on intractable confidence-set constructions.

- **Novel two-player Stackelberg game formulation that avoids intractable constrained optimization.** Section 3.1 replaces the constrained optimization over confidence sets (Equation 2) with a game where the reward minimizes $V_{1,r}^{\pi} - V_{1,r}^{\mu} + \mathcal{E}(r;\hat{r})$ while the policy maximizes it. The trajectory-pair ℓ₁ loss $\mathcal{E}(r;\hat{r})$ (Equation 4) is the specific penalty enabling a tractable adversarial objective — this is conceptually clean and grounded in the theory.

- **Clever reparameterization using induced reward models.** Section 3.3 introduces the mapping from value functions $f$ to induced rewards $r_{P^\star,f}^{\pi}$ (Definition 1), which rewrites the optimization entirely in terms of $f$ using offline data $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{traj}}$ and estimated dynamics $\hat{P}$. This eliminates the need for on-policy rollouts or access to true dynamics, making the algorithm genuinely offline.

- **Novel sub-optimality decomposition.** The proof sketch (Section 4) decomposes the sub-optimality into three terms (MLE error, optimization error, policy update regret), cleanly separating sources of error. Term (II) is bounded using the adversarial formulation combined with trajectory concentrability (Assumption 4) and $\lambda > C_{\mathrm{TR}}$, which is a clever use of the game structure.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Ambiguous definition of the output mixture policy.** Algorithm 2 returns $\hat{\pi} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\pi^{t}$, and the proof sketch (line 234) uses $V_{1,r^{\star}}^{\pi^{\star}} - V_{1,r^{\star}}^{\hat{\pi}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{1,r^{\star}}^{\pi^{\star}} - V_{1,r^{\star}}^{\pi^{t}}\right)$. This equality holds only if $\hat{\pi}$ is interpreted as the policy that uniformly picks one of the $\pi^{t}$ at the start of each episode and follows it for the whole episode. Under the alternative per-step-mixture interpretation (sample an action from $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\pi^t(\cdot\mid s)$ at each state), the equality does not hold because occupancy measures are not linear. The paper does not clarify which interpretation is intended. While the episode-level mixture is standard in online-to-batch conversion arguments and the equality itself mathematically implies this interpretation, the notation is dangerously ambiguous as written. The authors must explicitly define the output policy and verify the equality in the main text.

2. **Gap between theoretical and practical implementations.** Section 5 replaces the episodic undiscounted setting with a discounted segment-based formulation, replaces the exact Bellman reparameterization with a TD-error approximation (using $Q_{\phi}(s_{l+1},\pi_{\theta}(s_{l+1}))$ as a surrogate for $P^{\star}(Q_{\phi}\circ\pi_{\theta})$), introduces double Q-networks, target networks, a separate value function, and entropy regularization. The paper acknowledges some of these modifications implicitly by calling it a "practical version" but does not discuss which modifications preserve or break the theoretical guarantees. The abstract's claim that APPO "offers both statistical efficiency and practical applicability" conflates two different objects: a provably efficient template (Algorithm 2) and an implementation (Algorithm 4, not shown in main text) with no proven connection to it. Given that this is the paper's central selling point, the disconnect needs honest discussion.

### Minor

1. **Optimization error not reflected in Theorem 4.1.** The proof sketch (line 231-234) introduces an approximation error $\epsilon$ from solving the non-convex inner optimization, stating "(II) $\le \epsilon$ is guaranteed." However, Theorem 4.1 contains no explicit $\epsilon$ term. The bound assumes the inner optimization is solved to sufficient accuracy; this should either be stated as an assumption or the $\epsilon$ term should appear explicitly in the bound.

2. **Insufficient justification for the absence of transition concentrability in the $N$-dependent term.** The paper claims the bound for unlabeled trajectories $N$ does not depend on a transition concentrability coefficient $C_P$ (line 214), unlike prior work. The proof sketch does not explain how the transition model error $\hat{P}$ is controlled under the roll-in distribution of the learned policies $\pi^t$ without such a coefficient. While the adversarial formulation plausibly avoids this, the sketch should at least outline why no extra concentrability term arises.

3. **Narrow experimental evaluation.** The experiments use one dataset (Meta-world medium-replay) from a single source. For a paper claiming "practical applicability," evaluation on more diverse domains (e.g., D4RL MuJoCo tasks, Adroit) would substantially strengthen the case.

4. **No discussion of computational cost.** Despite "computationally efficient" being a main claim, no wall-clock time, number of gradient steps, or convergence analysis is provided for the practical implementation.

### Trivial

- Table 1 shows APPO sometimes outperforms the Oracle (ground-truth reward) policy. The paper notes this is "noteworthy" but does not explain it. A brief comment on why this occurs (e.g., IQL with Oracle rewards may not be perfectly tuned, or adversarial training provides a regularization benefit) would avoid confusion.
- The proof sketch uses $\lambda > C_{\mathrm{TR}}$ alongside "$\lambda = \Theta(C_{\mathrm{TR}})$" — these are not contradictory but stating "$\lambda = \Theta(C_{\mathrm{TR}})$ and $\lambda > C_{\mathrm{TR}}$" would be clearer as "$\lambda = c\cdot C_{\mathrm{TR}}$ for some constant $c > 1$."

## Novel Insights

The most interesting synthesis that emerges from the reviews is the structural tension between this paper's two core contributions. The theoretical contribution (first tractable offline PbRL with sample complexity bounds) is genuinely novel and addresses a well-identified gap in the literature. However, the practical contribution relies on a substantial set of deep RL heuristics that are disconnected from the theory. This is not unusual for the field — many provably efficient RL papers have this gap — but the paper would benefit from directly addressing it rather than letting the reader discover it. The other notable observation is that the mixture-policy ambiguity is a pattern: the paper's clarity is strongest when describing the algorithmic ideas and weakest when connecting the proof steps to the algorithm definition. A tighter editorial pass on the proof sketch's assumptions and definitions would significantly elevate the paper.
