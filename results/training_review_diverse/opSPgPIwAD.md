## Summary

This paper addresses multi-step algorithmic recourse, proposing a framework to (1) learn a distance function and threshold for feasible transitions with PAC guarantees, and (2) augment the dataset with synthetic points to ensure every negatively-classified individual receives a recourse path. The key technical contributions are a PAC-theoretic treatment of learning (d, τ) from feasibility-labeled transition pairs, covering both a bounded set of distance functions and a structured class with per-feature weights, plus a data augmentation algorithm (Algorithm 2) that iteratively constructs paths. Experiments on one synthetic and three real datasets show 100% validity (recourse for all individuals) using the proposed method, compared to existing path-based methods (FACE, counterfactual explanations) that fail for some fraction of individuals.

## Strengths

1. **First PAC learning treatment of distance and threshold for recourse paths.** The paper proves VC-dimension bounds for hypothesis classes of thresholded distance functions (Theorems 2.2, 2.5) and provides efficient ERM algorithms (Theorems 2.3, 2.6), yielding sample complexity guarantees (Theorems 2.4, 2.7). This formalizes what was previously an ad hoc choice of distance metric and threshold in the recourse literature. No prior work in multi-step recourse has provided this kind of learning-theoretic grounding.

2. **Augmentation algorithm achieving universal recourse.** Algorithm 2 generates synthetic intermediate points when no feasible transition exists among existing data, and empirically attains VAL = 1 across all four datasets (Figure 2). This directly addresses a well-recognized limitation of prior path-based methods: they may fail to provide any recourse for some individuals. The paper shows that average path distances and weights remain comparable to FACE, indicating the augmentation does not produce grossly unrealistic paths on these metrics.

3. **Framework accommodates non-metric, directional, and domain-constrained feasibility.** The paper does not require d to be a metric (asymmetric distances are allowed), and the structured hypothesis class in Equation (2) supports per-feature monotonicity and bounded-change constraints. Experiments on Adult and HELOC incorporate monotonicity constraints (e.g., age and education must increase), demonstrating integration of domain knowledge into the learned feasibility model.

4. **Convergence analysis for augmentation.** Theorem 2.8 provides a sufficient condition for convergence of Algorithm 2, and the empirical ablation on λ (Figure 3) gives practical guidance on the trade-off between path cost and convergence speed.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Feasibility labels are generated by arbitrary rules chosen by the authors, limiting external validity of the core claims.** The paper's central empirical claim — that the method "provides feasible recourse paths for all" — depends on the quality of the feasibility labels used for learning. These labels are generated by rules like "L1 distance per feature below the standard deviation" (PIMA) or monotonicity constraints on specific features (Adult, HELOC). While the paper acknowledges this limitation (Section 4, Conclusion) and notes that future work could use human annotators, in its current form the experiments demonstrate only that the framework can learn to approximate whatever arbitrary rule the authors chose and generate paths satisfying that rule. Whether the resulting paths correspond to genuinely achievable real-world changes is not validated. A practitioner adopting this framework would face the same question: where do the feasibility labels come from, and how reliable are they? This does not invalidate the technical contribution but substantially weakens the paper's headline claim of having demonstrated "feasible" recourse.

2. **No qualitative or quantitative validation that synthetic augmented points are realistic.** Algorithm 2 generates points q such that d(x', q) ≤ τ, but the paper provides no evidence that these points respect feature dependencies, lie near the data manifold, or correspond to achievable profiles. For the HELOC dataset, where only 0.444% of pairs are labeled feasible, the augmentation generates points satisfying a very strict threshold — but are these points jointly plausible (e.g., high income with low education)? There is no qualitative example of a generated path, no density/manifold analysis, and no comparison against generative counterfactual methods (e.g., VAEs, GANs) that could validate whether the synthetic points are realistic. This is the most consequential gap in the empirical evaluation.

### Minor

3. **Theorem 2.8's convergence guarantee is very narrow and addresses an edge case that partially contradicts the algorithm's design.** The theorem assumes that "for every x, there exists a fresh point y with f(y)-f(x) > λ/min w(a,b)" AND that "the algorithm only chooses fresh points (not in U∪V) to expand paths." The second assumption directly contradicts the algorithm's primary behavior, which first tries existing points in V∪U (line 6 of Algorithm 2) and only falls back to fresh points when that fails (lines 7-8). The theorem therefore covers a restricted operating mode, not the actual algorithm. The paper is transparent about calling this a "formal convergence scenario" but it provides little assurance about the algorithm's typical behavior.

4. **Evaluation is limited to 50 random individuals per dataset.** The paper generates recourse paths for 50 negatively-classified individuals per dataset. Drawing conclusions about providing recourse for "all" individuals from this sample size is statistically weak — a method could fail for 1 in 50 (2%) and still appear to have VAL=1 with this sample. Error bars or confidence intervals on the validity metric are not reported.

5. **Only the bounded-|D| case is used in experiments, not the structured class (Equation 2) with learned per-feature weights.** The experiments use 5 fixed distance functions (L1, L2, Mahalanobis, Cosine, JS), supplemented by hand-coded monotonicity constraints. The more flexible structured class (Equation 2), where per-feature weights βⱼ are learned, is presented as a theoretical contribution but is not tested empirically. Since monotonicity is encoded via infinite-distance penalties rather than learned, the claim of "learning distance functions" reduces to choosing among 5 candidates.

6. **No comparison against generative counterfactual methods.** The baselines (FACE, nearest-neighbor CE) cannot generate synthetic points. Comparing against methods that also generate synthetic intermediate points (e.g., StEP (Hamer et al., 2023), or VAEs/GANs for recourse) would help contextualize the augmentation quality.

7. **The paper does not address discrete, categorical, or mixed-type features.** All experiments use continuous features. Real recourse settings often involve categorical features (e.g., employment type, marital status) where distance-based feasibility is less natural. This limits the scope of the claimed applicability.

### Trivial

8. The weight function w(x,y) = d(x,y)/f_ρ((x+y)/2) uses the learned distance d — since d and τ are learned from the same feasibility labels, the weight essentially re-expresses feasibility scaled by density. This is not a flaw but the paper does not discuss this dependency.

9. The ERM algorithm chooses among only 5 distance functions; the text uses the phrase "learning a distance function" which overstates what is effectively model selection among a small pre-specified set.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Analysis of whether synthetic points lie on or off the data manifold (e.g., density estimation of augmented vs. real points).
- Error bars or bootstrapped confidence intervals on the validity metric.
- A comparison against a method that also generates synthetic counterfactuals (e.g., StEP or a VAE-based approach) to contextualize the augmentation quality.

## Removed Points

- **Circularity of learning (Critical Issue 1, partially removed)**: The critic's claim that learning is "circular" because the ground truth feasibility rules are themselves distance-threshold rules is overblown. For PIMA, the rule is "L1 distance per feature below the per-feature std," which is a conjunction of per-feature constraints NOT representable by any single d(x,y) ≤ τ in the hypothesis class D (which contains only aggregate distance functions). The learning problem genuinely approximates a more complex rule. The critic's deeper concern about label quality being arbitrary is kept as Major weakness #1.

- **"Augmentation trivially solves a tautologically defined problem" (Critical Issue 2, partially removed)**: The critic says the augmentation trivially produces paths satisfying its own constraints. While true that any generated point satisfies d(x',q) ≤ τ by construction, the algorithm also optimizes for classifier progress (f(y)-f(x')) and weight minimization. The learned (d,τ) also minimizes error against ground truth labels. The claim is not tautological — what matters is whether the synthetic points are realistic, which is addressed in Major weakness #2. The VAL=1 advantage over FACE/CE is partly by construction (they cannot augment), but this is the paper's intended contribution, not a flaw. The core question is whether the cost- and classifier-guided augmentation produces realistic paths.

- **Criticism about the weight function being redundant**: The observation that w uses the learned d is correct but the critic notes "not discussed critically" — this is a minor observation, not a structural weakness. Not worth mentioning in main Weaknesses.

- **Model-agnosticism caveat**: The critic notes the method requires access to prediction probabilities f(·), which "may be problematic for some model types." Most classifiers provide prediction probabilities; this is standard in the recourse literature and not a meaningful limitation.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface a genuine tension: the paper's technical machinery (PAC learning + augmentation) is well-executed, but the empirical validation rests on feasibility labels whose realism is unverified. This tension is not new — it reflects a general challenge in recourse research where "feasibility" is inherently domain-specific and hard to ground in public benchmarks.

## Suggestions

1. **Ground feasibility labels in a genuinely external source for at least one experiment.** Use observed transitions from longitudinal data, or obtain expert annotations on a small set of pairs. Even a small-scale proof-of-concept with real feasibility data would substantially strengthen the paper's central claim.

2. **Provide qualitative examples of generated paths.** Show a concrete path for one individual per dataset, listing feature values at each step and discussing why each intermediate state is plausible.

3. **Report results with more than 50 individuals.** Bootstrap confidence intervals over a larger sample (e.g., 200-500) would make the "recourse for all" claim statistically credible.

4. **Add a density/manifold analysis of synthetic points.** Measure how often augmented points fall in low-density regions or violate known feature correlations compared to real data.

5. **Test the structured hypothesis class (Equation 2) experimentally.** Learning per-feature weights βⱼ from data is a more compelling demonstration of "learning the distance function" than choosing among 5 pre-specified functions.
