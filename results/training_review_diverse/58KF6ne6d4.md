## Summary

This paper proposes KIRL, a reinforcement learning-based method for integrated toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning in CNC machining. The approach formulates the joint optimization as an MDP: an RL agent (PPO/SAC) predicts boundary kinematic states (position, velocity, acceleration) for each toolpath segment, a quintic polynomial trajectory is fitted between boundaries (proven optimal for jerk minimization under fixed boundary conditions), and segment duration is optimized via Brent's method. The method is evaluated on four 2D toolpaths against two decoupled baselines (ICR and CCR), demonstrating smoother trajectories in terms of RMS acceleration and jerk.

## Strengths

- **First RL-based integrated method for this problem domain.** The paper explicitly identifies this novelty in the abstract and contributions (line 9), and the claim is verifiable — prior integrated approaches (Yang et al. 2015, Lin et al. 2019, Wu et al. 2023 cited in the paper) are all non-RL optimization methods.

- **Principled theoretical foundation via Theorem 1.** The paper proves that the optimal segment-level trajectory under jerk minimization with fixed boundary conditions is a quintic polynomial of time. This is a correct application of calculus of variations and provides a principled reason to use quintic polynomials rather than an ad-hoc curve choice.

- **The method demonstrably produces smoother trajectories than decoupled baselines on kinematic metrics.** Across the four test toolpaths, KIRL variants consistently achieve lower RMS acceleration and RMS jerk than ICR and CCR. The paper also acknowledges and discusses the trade-off where baselines sometimes achieve shorter machining times.

- **Well-structured MDP formulation with practical design choices.** The observation space includes remaining segment count, segment lengths, turning angles, and local kinematic states; the action space parameterizes the predicted end position to maintain geometric feasibility; and the reward function balances machining time, jerk, chord error, and constraint penalties. The use of Brent's method for the univariate duration optimization is a computationally efficient design.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The degree of "integration" is overclaimed, and the sequential decomposition is not validated against global optimization.** The paper's central motivation is that decoupling smoothing from feedrate planning is suboptimal, and the contribution is an *integrated* approach. However, the method does not perform a joint global optimization — it decomposes the problem into independent per-segment steps (RL predicts boundary states → fit polynomial → optimize single duration variable locally). The RL agent's reward is myopic (per-segment), and constraints that couple segments are handled only through boundary continuity, not through any explicit global optimization. The paper provides no evidence that this sequential decomposition yields solutions near a truly joint optimum. The authors cite Yang et al. (2015), Lin et al. (2019), and Wu et al. (2023) as prior integrated approaches but do **not** compare against any of them — only against decoupled baselines (ICR, CCR). This makes it impossible to determine whether the method's advantages stem from "integration" or simply from using smoother polynomials with a different solution strategy.

2. **No statistical significance or variance reported.** The results in Table 1 are point estimates with no error bars, standard deviations, or mention of multiple random seeds. Given the stochasticity of RL training, it is impossible to determine whether the reported improvements over baselines are robust or statistical noise. This is a basic experimental rigor requirement.

3. **Training is per-toolpath with no evaluation of generalization.** The paper states (line 349) "KIRL requires separate training for each toolpath" as a limitation, but the experimental protocol is unclear: are results obtained by training on the *same* toolpath and testing on it? If so, the evaluation assesses fitting ability rather than generalization, which severely limits the contribution's scope. The computational cost of per-toolpath training is also not factored into the comparison.

4. **Missing comparison against integrated optimization baselines.** The paper explicitly cites Yang et al. (2015), Lin et al. (2019), and Wu et al. (2023) as prior work on integrated toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning, yet the experiments compare only against decoupled methods (ICR from 2013, CCR from 2018). Without comparison to at least one existing integrated approach, the claim that RL-based integration is superior is hollow — the improvement could be from *any* integrated approach, not specifically from RL.

### Minor

1. **No ablation study isolating the contribution of RL.** There is no comparison against a version of the method that replaces the RL agent with a simple heuristic for boundary states (e.g., setting velocity proportional to turning angle, acceleration to zero). Without this, it is unclear whether the performance gains come from the RL predictions or from the quintic polynomial representation itself.

2. **Hyperparameters not disclosed.** The reward weights λ₁, λ₂, λ₃ and the penalty C_penalty are not reported. The global trade-off parameter w from Eq. (5) appears only in the problem formulation and is never connected to the RL reward weights λ₁, λ₂, λ₃ — the paper does not explain how w maps to the actual objective used during training. This makes the "balanced trade-off" claim uninterpretable and the paper non-reproducible.

3. **No comparison against a direct optimization baseline for boundary states.** The optimization reduces to predicting a 5-dimensional action per segment plus a univariate duration search — a relatively low-dimensional problem. The paper does not compare against directly optimizing these boundary states using standard nonlinear programming (e.g., SQP), which would isolate whether RL adds value over simpler optimization.

4. **Constraint satisfaction not guaranteed.** The method uses a penalty for constraint violations rather than enforcing constraints by construction (as the B-spline-based baselines likely do). The paper does not report violation rates or whether the final trajectories always satisfy all kinematic limits.

### Trivial

- The trajectory visualizations (Fig. 5) are qualitative but are standard as supplementary illustration. The numerical table provides the primary evidence.
- The chord error formula in line 226 has a small typographical artifact ("excecds" for "exceeds" — parser issue, not author error).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Reporting training time alongside inference time would contextualize the computational cost.
- Comparing against the cited integrated methods (Yang et al. 2015, Lin et al. 2019, Wu et al. 2023) would strengthen the paper considerably.
- Cross-validation or evaluation on held-out toolpaths (even if trained per-toolpath) would clarify whether the method overfits.

## Novel Insights

The reviews collectively surface a tension between the paper's framing and its execution. The core insight — that kinematic awareness during smoothing can be achieved by having an RL agent predict boundary states whose downstream trajectory quality is evaluated — is genuinely novel for the CNC domain. However, neither reviewer fully buys that this constitutes "integrated optimization" in the claimed sense, because the problem is decomposed into independent per-segment steps with a myopic reward. The more honest characterization of the contribution would be "kinematics-informed smoothing via RL-guided boundary state prediction," which is a meaningful step short of full integration. The reviews also highlight that the paper would benefit from a direct comparison against non-RL approaches for the *same* decomposed problem (e.g., optimizing boundary states numerically) to substantiate the claim that RL specifically is beneficial here.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the contribution** from "integrated optimization" to "kinematics-informed smoothing via RL-predicted boundary states." This better matches what the method actually does.
2. **Add statistical rigor:** run 5–10 random seeds and report mean ± std for all metrics.
3. **Add at least one integrated baseline** from the cited works (Yang et al. 2015, Lin et al. 2019, or Wu et al. 2023) — or a simple direct collocation approach — to demonstrate that RL integration outperforms non-RL integration.
4. **Add an ablation** that replaces the RL agent with a heuristic baseline (e.g., velocity proportional to cosine of turning angle, zero acceleration) to isolate the value of learned boundary states.
5. **Disclose all hyperparameters** (λ₁, λ₂, λ₃, C_penalty, learning rates, network architecture, etc.) and explain the relationship between the global trade-off w and the reward weights.
6. **Clarify the training/evaluation protocol:** if the method is per-toolpath fitting, say so explicitly and report training compute time. If there is any generalization capability, demonstrate it on held-out toolpaths.

## Score and Decision

**Originality:** Moderate. First RL-based method for this CNC problem, but the RL application is relatively straightforward (standard PPO/SAC on a custom MDP).

**Importance of research question:** High. Integrated toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning is a practically relevant problem in manufacturing.

**Claims supported by evidence:** Partially. The method produces smoother trajectories than decoupled baselines, but the evidence lacks statistical rigor, misses key baselines (integrated methods), and the core "integration" claim is overstated relative to the actual method.

**Soundness of experiments:** Weak. No variance reporting, no ablation studies, no comparison against integrated baselines, per-toolpath training without generalization assessment.

**Clarity of writing:** Adequate. The method description is clear, though some connections (w to λ's, training protocol) are under-specified.

**Value to the research community:** Moderate. The problem and MDP formulation could serve as a template for future RL-based CNC methods, but the current validation is too weak to be a reliable reference.

The paper has a novel idea and a reasonable formulation, but the experimental validation is insufficient to support the claimed contributions. The lack of statistical rigor, the missing comparison against existing integrated approaches (cited in the paper itself), and the overclaimed degree of integration are substantial weaknesses that prevent acceptance in the current form.
