## Summary

This paper introduces the Grothendieck Graph Neural Networks (GGNN) framework, an algebraic platform that generalizes the concept of neighborhoods in graphs through "covers" — collections of elements from a monoid Mod(G) generated by directed subgraphs. The framework provides a monoidal homomorphism Tr that maps covers to matrix representations, enabling alternative message-passing strategies. Building on this, the authors propose Sieve Neural Networks (SNN), a GNN model that uses sieve-based covers derived from the graph's topology. Empirical results show competitive performance on TUD benchmarks and the ability to distinguish strongly regular graphs (beyond 3-WL discriminatory power).

## Strengths

- **Novel algebraic framework for generalizing neighborhoods.** The construction of the monoid Mod(G) and the monoidal homomorphism Tr to Mom(G) (Definition 2.3.1, Theorem 2.3.3) provides a principled, algebraic way to define message-passing strategies beyond standard neighborhoods. The paper establishes that neighborhoods are a special case (Theorem 2.5.1), grounding the framework as a genuine generalization of MPNNs.

- **Invariant model with path-counting interpretation.** Theorem 3.1.1 establishes that SNN is isomorphism-invariant, essential for graph-level tasks. Tr acts as a path counter (Section 2.3), giving a clear mechanistic understanding of how the algebraic cover translates into message-passing — the entries of the output matrix encode the number of allowed paths between nodes under the chosen cover.

- **Empirical demonstration of expressivity beyond 3-WL.** On the Strongly Regular graphs benchmark, SNN(β,(-1,-1,-1)) distinguishes all graphs within each publicly available collection (Section 3.4), a task where 3-WL fails. On CSL it successfully separates all 10 isomorphism classes. This validates that the sieve-based cover yields strictly greater discriminative power than neighborhood-based methods.

- **Reduction to standard MPNN and competitive benchmarks.** Theorem 2.5.1 and Section 3.2 show that SNN_o(α,(0,1))/SNN_o(α,(1,0)) recovers the adjacency matrix, explicitly connecting the framework to existing practice. On TUD benchmarks (MUTAG, PTC, NCI1, IMDB-B, IMDB-M), SNN achieves top-three accuracy against a range of GNNs and graph kernels (Table 1), demonstrating practical viability alongside theoretical expressivity.

- **Complexity analysis with sparsity discussion.** The paper derives O(n⁴) worst-case complexity and notes reduction to O(|E|·|V|²) with sparse matrix operations (Section 3.3), giving a realistic picture of the model's scaling behavior.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. While the paper has the significant issues detailed below, none individually or collectively invalidate the core construction of the framework or the SNN model. The issues are fixable with revisions.

### Major

- **Theorem 2.1.1 (Rep is an isomorphism) is unsupported and almost certainly false.** The paper claims "every directed subgraph can be determined completely by its representation" and states Rep: DirSub(G) → MatRep(G) is an isomorphism (bijection). However, the matrix representation (Definition 2.1.2) records reachability (transitive closure), not the graph structure itself. Two different directed acyclic subgraphs can have identical reachability matrices — e.g., in a graph with nodes a,b,c and edges {a,b},{b,c},{a,c}, the subgraph D1 = {a→b, b→c} and D2 = {a→b, a→c, b→c} both yield the same reachability matrix (a reaches b and c; b reaches c). The paper provides no proof of injectivity, and the claim is not generally true without additional constraints (e.g., requiring the representation to be the adjacency matrix of the Hasse diagram of the partial order). This error also taints the characterization of Tr as "an extension of an isomorphism" (Section 2.3), although Tr itself remains well-defined as a monoidal homomorphism. **Why it matters**: The paper makes a verifiably incorrect mathematical claim that is presented as supporting the framework's foundations. While the downstream theorems (2.4.1, 2.4.2) and the SNN model may not depend on Rep being injective, the error damages reader confidence and needs correction — either a correct proof with appropriate constraints, or a weakened claim.

### Minor

- **Definition of "cover" is too informal.** The paper states "We define a cover for a graph as follows:" (after Definition 2.2.1) and then provides a prose description: "a cover as a collection of them [elements of Mod(G)] can be seen as a collection of traffic rules." The intent is clear — a cover is a subset of Mod(G) — but the lack of a formal Definition environment with explicit conditions is a presentational gap, especially for a concept central to the framework. The paper also intentionally avoids requiring that a cover "coat" any nodes or edges, which is defensibly flexible but should be stated explicitly.

- **Empirical comparisons omit several high-expressivity GNN baselines.** The TUD benchmark comparisons (Table 1) include standard GNNs and graph kernels but not other models known to capture substructures beyond 1-WL, such as k-GNN, PPGN, Ring-GNN, or GSN (the latter is cited in related work but not compared against). Given that one of the paper's central claims is about enhanced expressive power, including these baselines would substantially strengthen the empirical case.

- **SR/CSL experiments are empirical only.** The paper claims SNN "effectively differentiates between all graphs within each collection" of strongly regular graphs. This conclusion is drawn from evaluating on publicly available collections — it is an empirical observation on a finite set, not a theoretical proof or a bound on the model's discriminating power. A theoretical analysis (e.g., a bound on the Weisfeiler-Lehman type color refinement that SNN implements) would be needed to establish the general claim.

- **High computational complexity.** The O(n⁴) worst-case complexity (O(|E|·|V|²) with sparsity) limits applicability to graphs with more than a few hundred nodes. The paper acknowledges this but does not discuss how severe the limitation is in practice or suggest strategies (e.g., approximations, sampling) to mitigate it.

### Trivial

- The paper could benefit from a few brief proof sketches in the main text for the major theorems (2.4.1, 2.4.2, 3.1.1) to help readers assess plausibility without consulting the appendix. This is a readability concern, not a correctness concern.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A pseudocode description of the SNN algorithm would clarify the construction and help practitioners implement it.
- A theoretical analysis of SNN's expressive power relative to the WL hierarchy (e.g., which k-WL level it can exceed) would strengthen the expressivity claims beyond the empirical SR/CSL experiments.
- Discussion of how the GGNN framework relates to other algebraic approaches to GNN design (e.g., message passing on cellular sheaves, spectral methods on simplicial complexes) would help position the work.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix Theorem 2.1.1.** Either provide a correct proof that Rep is injective under an appropriate refinement of the matrix representation (e.g., using the adjacency matrix of the Hasse diagram of the reachability poset, rather than the transitive closure), or weaken the claim to state that Rep is surjective (which it is by construction) and describe what information the representation does and does not preserve. This is the single highest-leverage fix.

2. **Provide a formal definition of "cover"** in a Definition environment with explicit conditions, even if minimal (e.g., "A cover of G is a subset C ⊆ Mod(G)").

3. **Add baselines** (k-GNN, PPGN, Ring-GNN, GSN) to the TUD experiments, or at minimum discuss why they are omitted and acknowledge the limitation.

4. **Include theoretical analysis of SNN's expressive power** relative to the WL hierarchy to complement the empirical SR/CSL results.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a novel algebraic framework for GNN design that is genuinely creative and yields a model (SNN) with promising empirical expressivity. However, the paper contains a verifiable mathematical error in Theorem 2.1.1 (Rep is not injective as claimed), the definition of the central concept "cover" is presented too informally, and the empirical evaluation omits several relevant high-expressivity baselines. These issues are fixable with significant revision — the core framework and SNN model are not fundamentally broken — but they prevent acceptance in the current form.
