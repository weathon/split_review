## Summary

This paper introduces the Grothendieck Graph Neural Network (GGNN) framework, an algebraic construction based on monoids of directed subgraphs (Mod(G)) and their matrix transformations (Mom(G)), connected by a monoidal homomorphism Tr. The framework is meant to provide a systematic, algebraic way to define "covers" for graphs — alternatives to the standard neighborhood — and translate them into matrix representations. The paper instantiates this framework with a specific model, Sieve Neural Networks (SNN), which uses a hand-crafted cover of BFS-like layered directed subgraphs. Experiments on strongly regular graphs, CSL, and TUD benchmarks show that SNN can distinguish 3-WL-indistinguishable SR graphs and achieves competitive classification accuracy on small molecule and social graph datasets.

## Strengths

- **Novel algebraic framework for defining graph covers.** The construction of Mod(G) (a non-commutative monoid generated by directed subgraphs) and Mom(G) (its matrix counterpart under the ∘ operation), together with the Tr homomorphism, provides a genuinely novel algebraic perspective on designing message-passing strategies. Theorem 2.5.1 verifies that the standard neighborhood cover is a special case, showing the framework subsumes existing practice.

- **SNN empirically distinguishes 3-WL-indistinguishable strongly regular graphs.** The SR experiment (Section 3.4) shows that SNN(β, (−1,−1,−1)) separates all graphs within each publicly available collection of strongly regular graphs. Since 3-WL fails on SR graphs (Bodnar et al., 2021b), this demonstrates that SNN has expressive power beyond a well-known limitation of standard MPNNs.

- **Competitive results on TUD benchmark datasets.** Table 1 reports SNN achieving competitive accuracy on MUTAG (92.61%), PTC (66.67%), NCI1 (82.50%), IMDB-B (74.60%), and IMDB-M (50.73%), often matching or surpassing several graph kernel and GNN baselines.

- **Theoretical characterization of graph isomorphism via monoidal structures.** Theorems 2.4.1 and 2.4.2 show that Mod(G) and Mom(G), together with the Change-of-Order mappings, characterize graphs up to isomorphism. This provides a rigorous algebraic foundation for using the framework in graph-level tasks.

- **Flexible handling of edge features and path-length weighting.** As described in Section 3.1, SNN can incorporate edge features by replacing scalar 1s in Tr(D_i(v)) with feature vectors, and can be made sensitive to path length via a constant γ ∈ (0,1]. This adaptability is a practical advantage over fixed-neighborhood methods.

### Fatal

### Major

- **Disconnect between the elaborate algebraic framework and the actual model.** The GGNN framework develops a rich structure — non-commutative monoids, monoidal homomorphisms, path-aware composition — but the SNN model does not meaningfully exploit this apparatus. The cover of sieves is hand-crafted from BFS-like layered directed subgraphs, and the paper explicitly states that "directed edges in M_i(v) are not composable. Therefore, disregarding the order and noncommutativity of •" (lines 191–192). The non-commutative composition • is never used in a way that the simpler commutative ⊕ could not replicate; the ∘ operation on matrices reduces to sums of matrix products that correspond to standard path counting in layered adjacency slices. The paper claims the framework provides a "systematic" way to define covers, yet SNN's cover is defined ad hoc without guidance from the algebra, and no second cover is provided to demonstrate the framework's generative capacity. The framework's complexity is not justified by the model it produces.

- **Experimental evaluation lacks sufficient rigor to establish practical advantage.** Several issues undermine the empirical claims:
  - **No standard deviations or error bars** are reported for any TUD result. The paper mentions ten-fold cross-validation but gives no variance estimates, making it impossible to assess whether SNN's results are statistically distinguishable from baselines.
  - **No controlled baselines.** SNN preprocesses the graph into a weighted input for GraphConv/GINEConv, but the paper never reports the performance of these same GNNs on the unmodified (or simple k-hop-augmented) graph. Without this control, it is impossible to attribute any gains to the algebraic cover rather than to simply providing multi-hop information.
  - **Baseline numbers are likely taken from different papers with different protocols.** The paper does not describe how baseline accuracies were obtained, whether splits were held constant, or whether any form of fair comparison was enforced. Direct column-wise comparison to published numbers from separate papers is unreliable.
  - **The SR and CSL experiments demonstrate necessary but not sufficient expressivity.** Many existing expressive GNN methods (k-GNN, IGN, GSN, etc.) also distinguish these graphs. The paper does not compare to these or characterize what classes of graphs SNN can separate that other methods cannot.

- **Prohibitive complexity without practical discussion.** The complexity analysis (Section 3.3) gives O(n⁴) for SNN, reduced to O(|E|·|V|²) with sparsity. For a 1000-node graph, this is ~10⁹ operations when sparse, and far more for dense graphs. The paper's experiments are on tiny graphs (MUTAG average ~18 nodes, PTC ~26, IMDB ~20), which masks this limitation. The paper acknowledges sparsity but never discusses actual runtimes, limits of practical applicability, or what graph sizes/topologies justify the cost. This is a severe constraint on adoption that is simply not addressed.

### Minor

- **The invariance theorem (Thm 3.1.1) is standard and contributes no insight.** The construction is permutation-equivariant by design, so invariance is automatic. The paper does not prove any non-trivial expressivity bound — e.g., characterizing which non-WL-distinguishable graph pairs SNN can separate, or what the framework enables that simpler k-hop methods cannot.

- **Overclaimed connection to Grothendieck topologies.** The paper borrows terminology ("Grothendieck," "sieves," "covers") from sheaf/category theory but does not actually use a Grothendieck topology, covering sieve condition, or any sheaf-theoretic machinery. The "cover" definition (Def. 2.2.1) does not even require that every edge or node be covered, which is a basic property of covers. This framing creates expectations the paper does not meet.

- **The cover definition is misleadingly named.** Definition 2.2.1 defines a cover as a collection of elements from Mod(G) with no covering condition (no requirement that all nodes or edges appear). This departs from the standard meaning of "cover" in graph theory and topology, and the paper does not justify this deviation.

- **The conclusion defers crucial analysis to future work.** The paper's conclusion states: "Future work aims to conduct a more comprehensive theoretical comparison between SNN and the Weisfeiler-Lehman test." This is a central question for any GNN claiming improved expressivity, and its deferral weakens the paper's contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Bridge the framework-model gap.** Either (a) show that the framework enables qualitatively different covers beyond BFS layering by instantiating a second, distinct model, or (b) demonstrate that the algebraic structure (non-commutativity, path-aware composition) provably buys SNN something that simpler constructions cannot achieve.
2. **Add rigorous experimental controls.** Report standard deviations for all TUD results. Include a baseline of the same downstream GNN (GraphConv/GINEConv) applied to the original graph and to simple k-hop-augmented graphs, to isolate the effect of the SNN cover.
3. **Discuss scalability directly.** Acknowledge the O(n⁴)/O(|E|·|V|²) complexity as a limitation, report actual runtimes, and characterize the regime of graph sizes for which SNN is practical.
4. **Tone down the categorical framing** or justify it by actually using covering conditions and sheaf-theoretic properties. The term "cover" should require covering the graph, or the departure from standard terminology should be explained and motivated.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a genuinely novel algebraic framework for thinking about message-passing beyond neighborhoods, and the theoretical results (monoidal characterization of graph isomorphism) are interesting. However, the connection between this elaborate framework and the actual model (SNN) is tenuous — SNN's construction largely disregards the non-commutative algebra that is the framework's centerpiece, reducing to sums of layered adjacency products. The experimental evaluation lacks basic rigor (no error bars, no controlled baselines, no ablation), and the prohibitive complexity is not discussed as a limitation. The empirical evidence does not convincingly show that either the framework or the model advances the state of the art in a way that justifies the complexity.

The paper has merit in its theoretical ideas but does not deliver on its practical promises. Major revisions — particularly controlled experiments, a demonstration that the framework generates useful covers beyond BFS-layering, and a forthright discussion of limitations — would be needed before it meets the bar for acceptance.
