## Summary

This paper proposes Co-Boosting, a one-shot federated learning framework that iteratively improves both synthetic data quality and ensemble quality through adversarial data generation and ensemble reweighting. The method introduces three components: hard sample generation via gradient-based difficulty weighting (GHS), on-the-fly diverse hard sample perturbation (DHS), and ensemble enhancement via learned client mixing weights (EE). Experiments across five datasets, multiple non-IID levels, heterogeneous architectures, and varying client counts show consistent gains over existing one-shot FL methods (DENSE, F-ADI, F-DAFL, FedDF, FedAvg).

## Strengths

1. **Consistent and substantial improvements across a comprehensive evaluation.** Co-Boosting outperforms all baselines on all five datasets (MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) at all three non-IID levels (α=0.05, 0.1, 0.3) in Table 1. Gains reach 12.87% absolute on MNIST (α=0.05) and 9.29% on CIFAR-10 (α=0.1) over the best baseline DENSE. These margins hold under heterogeneous client architectures (Table 3), different data distribution shifts (Table 5), varying data amounts (Table 2 + Fig.), and up to 50 clients (Table 6).

2. **Well-designed ablation study that validates each component.** Table 4 systematically ablates all three components (GHS, DHS, EE) in all 8 combinations. Every component individually improves the baseline (e.g., GHS: +2.72% on SVHN, DHS: +2.92%, EE: +4.21%), and the full method combining all three achieves the best result (65.40% vs baseline 58.46% on SVHN). This granularity is stronger than what most papers in this area provide.

3. **Practical and server-side-only operation with heterogeneous architecture support.** Co-Boosting requires no client-side training modifications, no additional data or model transmissions beyond the initial one-shot upload, and naturally handles heterogeneous client architectures (Table 3 shows five distinct architectures: CNN, ResNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet, etc.). This directly addresses the model-market scenario the paper targets, where only pre-trained models are available.

4. **Ensemble reweighting on synthetic data is a clean approach.** Rather than modifying local training or requiring additional client transmissions (as in prior work like DENSE, FedDF, Heinbaugh et al.), Co-Boosting learns client mixing weights entirely on the server side via gradient descent (Eq. 9, 12) on the synthetically generated hard samples. Table 2 shows the Co-Boosting ensemble (48.75% on CIFAR-10 α=0.05) substantially outperforms both uniform averaging (42.34%) and data-amount-based weighting.

### Minor

1. **The "mutual co-boosting" narrative is somewhat overstated relative to the ablation evidence.** The paper frames the contribution as a "mutual boosting" cycle where data and ensemble synergistically enhance each other. However, the ablation (Table 4) shows that on SVHN, the full method (65.40%) improves less than the sum of its separable parts (GHS+DHS at 62.46% gains +4.00 over baseline; EE alone at 62.67% gains +4.21; 65.40 < 62.46 + (62.67−58.46) = 66.67). The gains are sub-additive on one dataset and modestly super-additive on the other (CIFAR-10: full 47.20 > 44.36 + (41.45−39.72) = 46.09). While the components clearly all contribute, the evidence for a strongly synergistic *mutual* cycle—where the improved ensemble generates better data that in turn improves the ensemble—is not directly demonstrated beyond the overall final accuracy. Showing the trajectory of ensemble quality and data quality across Co-Boosting's T epochs would strengthen the claimed mechanism. As-is, the paper's contribution reads more cleanly as an effective *composition* of three complementary improvements (better generation loss, better sample diversity, better ensemble weighting) than as a demonstrated virtuous cycle.

2. **The synthetic-data-based ensemble reweighting mechanism is not rigorously validated.** The paper learns client mixing weights by minimizing cross-entropy on the synthetic dataset generated *from the same ensemble being reweighted* (Eq. 9). Fig. 1(b) shows that weights learned on synthetic hard samples *outperform* weights learned on real validation data—a surprising result the paper acknowledges as a "disparity" in the Limitations paragraph but does not analyze. If robust, this finding deserves investigation (e.g., does the synthetic distribution probe decision boundaries where real data is sparse?). If not robust (e.g., a particular validation split), the claim is misleading. The risk is that the weighting procedure capitalizes on artifacts of the synthetic distribution rather than identifying genuine variations in client quality. The paper's own limitation section admits this gap but does not resolve it.

3. **The random projection perturbation (Eq. 11) is a heuristic with limited motivation.** The method perturbs each synthetic sample to maximize similarity between the ensemble output and a random vector u ~ Unif([-1,1]^d), claiming this "provides diversity and increases difficulty." While the ablation confirms DHS alone is empirically effective (e.g., +2.92% SVHN), the connection between maximizing output-random-vector alignment and producing meaningfully harder/diverse samples is not explained. Why not an adversarial perturbation targeting the ensemble's own predictions (e.g., FGSM) or the KL-divergence with the server (as in L_A)? The random direction could add noise rather than hardness. Diversity metrics or illustrative examples of perturbed samples would substantiate the design choice.

4. **The adversarial training dynamics lack stability characterization.** The method involves three interacting components (generator with a composite loss that includes the server, server distilled on generated samples, ensemble reweighted on those samples) updated alternately each epoch (Algorithm 1). This is a non-stationary multi-player process, but the paper provides no analysis of convergence, sensitivity to update order, or the effect of the number of generator steps T_G per epoch. The min-max formulation (Eq. 13) is presented as the underlying problem, but the algorithm is a heuristic alternating schedule. While full theoretical analysis is beyond what an empirical paper requires, a simple empirical check (e.g., does the loss in Eq. 13 trend appropriately across epochs?) would strengthen the presentation.
