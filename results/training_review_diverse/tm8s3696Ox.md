## Summary

The paper proposes Co-Boosting, a one-shot federated learning framework that iteratively improves both synthetic data quality (via hard-sample generation with an adversarial loss and on-the-fly input perturbation) and ensemble quality (via reweighting client contributions on synthetic data). The core claim is that these two components mutually reinforce each other through an iterative loop. Experiments across five datasets, multiple non-IID settings, heterogeneous architectures, and varying client counts show consistent and often large improvements over prior methods.

## Strengths

1. **Consistent state-of-the-art accuracy across diverse benchmarks.** Co-Boosting outperforms all baselines on all five datasets (MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) at three heterogeneity levels (α=0.05, 0.1, 0.3). The gains are largest under extreme non-IID (α=0.05), where Co-Boosting surpasses DENSE by margins such as 12.87% on MNIST, 5.85% on FMNIST, and 8.83% on CIFAR-10 (Table 1). This directly supports the claim that jointly improving data and ensemble yields a superior server model.

2. **Practical constraints satisfied without sacrificing performance.** Co-Boosting requires no modifications to clients' local training, no extra data or model transmissions beyond the one-shot communication, and supports heterogeneous client architectures (Table 3 shows a ResNet server outperforming baselines with a mix of CNN, ResNet, MobileNet, ShuffleNet clients). This matches real-world model-market scenarios.

3. **Ablation study validates the component contributions.** When GHS (hard-sample generation loss), DHS (on-the-fly diverse hard samples), and EE (ensemble enhancement via reweighting) are used together, accuracy on SVHN (α=0.05) reaches 65.40% vs. 58.46% for the baseline without any component. The best performance requires all three, confirming that the components are complementary (Table 6). No single component fully explains the gains.

4. **Robustness across varied practical conditions.** Co-Boosting maintains its advantage under different numbers of clients (5 to 50, Table 5), C-class-per-client partitions (Table 4), unbalanced data amounts (Table 7), and heterogeneous architectures (Table 3). This breadth strengthens the generalizability of the method.

### Minor

1. **The on-the-fly adversarial perturbation (Eq. 12–14 / DHS) has a weakly justified mechanism.** The perturbation in Eq. 14 maximizes \( \bm{u}^\top A_{\bm{w}}(\mathbf{x}_s) \) for a random vector \( \bm{u} \), which is a non-standard operation. The paper motivates this as making samples "harder and more diverse," drawing inspiration from adversarial learning, but the connection between maximizing the inner product with a random vector and increasing sample difficulty for either the ensemble or the server is not established. The ablation (Table 6) shows that removing DHS reduces accuracy (e.g., 65.40→64.40 on SVHN; 47.20→46.74 on CIFAR-10), which demonstrates a net benefit. However, the ablation does not isolate whether the *direction* of the perturbation matters over simply adding large-magnitude random noise. Without a controlled experiment (DHS vs. random perturbation of equal strength), the specific mechanism by which DHS contributes remains unclear. This does not threaten the paper's overall results but weakens the internal validity of the data-boosting narrative.

2. **The co-boosting loop is not directly tested against a non-iterative alternative.** The central claim is that iterating data generation and ensemble reweighting yields mutual enhancement. The ablation shows that combining all three components works best, which is consistent with this claim. However, the experiments do not compare Co-Boosting against a simpler two-step variant: (a) generate a fixed synthetic dataset using the initial averaged ensemble, (b) compute ensemble weights once on that fixed data, then distill. If the iterative re-generation and re-weighting yields meaningful gains over this two-step baseline, that would directly confirm that iteration itself (not just the individual components) is responsible for the improvement. Without this comparison, the "mutual boosting" claim rests on the combination of components rather than on evidence that the iterative loop adds value beyond a single round of each.

3. **No computational cost analysis.** Co-Boosting requires training a generator for \( T_G \) steps per epoch, computing per-sample perturbations, updating ensemble weights, and distilling the server — all within each epoch. The paper reports no runtime, wall-clock time, or epoch counts compared to DENSE or other baselines. Given that the method is more complex than prior approaches, reporting computational cost would allow readers to assess the trade-off between the large accuracy gains and any additional overhead. This is particularly relevant because one of the paper's value propositions is practicality for model-market scenarios.

4. **No accuracy-over-training-time convergence plots.** Since the algorithm is iterative (Algorithm 1), showing test accuracy as a function of epochs would help verify that the server model improves monotonically (or at least stably) as co-boosting proceeds, supporting the claimed mutual enhancement dynamic. No such plots are presented.

5. **The gap between synthetic-based and real-data-based reweighting is acknowledged but not quantified.** Figure 1(b) compares ensemble accuracy using weights learned on synthetic hard samples vs. weights learned on real data (an oracle upper bound). The paper notes this gap as a limitation but does not state the exact accuracy difference. Quantifying this gap would help calibrate reader expectations about the headroom for future improvement.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- **Criticism that the perturbation is computed solely from the ensemble and "there is no argument or evidence that increasing ensemble difficulty translates to increased difficulty for the server model":** The paper explicitly states (lines 104–105) that samples already hard for the ensemble can be easily fit by the server during repeated distillation, and the perturbation is motivated as preventing this overfitting by injecting diversity on the fly. The paper's argument, though incomplete, is present; the criticism overstates the absence of justification. The real concern (captured in Weakness 1 above) is about the *specific form* of the perturbation and the lack of comparison against random noise, not about a total absence of reasoning.

- **Criticism that the co-boosting loop may create a "zero-sum dynamic" with no discussion of convergence:** The paper frames the process as a minimax problem (Eq. 15), which is a standard way to describe adversarial optimization. While formal convergence analysis is absent (a valid minor point), this level of analysis is not standard for empirical method papers in this area. The concern is downgraded from its framing as a structural flaw.

- **Several points about missing details in the ablation:** The critic claimed the ablation does not separate GHS from DHS. In fact, Table 6 has separate columns for GHS and DHS and tests them independently. The critic's real point (that DHS itself could be further ablated into perturbation-direction vs. noise-magnitude effects) is captured in Weakness 1.

- **Criticism that the "on-the-fly" perturbation is not a standard adversarial attack:** The paper acknowledges this explicitly (line 109: "instead of the iterative adversarial attacks, we take only one step..."), so this is not a hidden flaw. The concern about whether this specific operation achieves the stated goal is preserved in Weakness 1.
