## Summary

This paper proposes PC-CLIP, a method that finetunes CLIP's text encoder (while freezing the image encoder) to align the *difference* between two image embeddings with an LLM-generated text description of the visual difference between those images. The synthetic comparison data is generated by LLaMA2-13B using captions from COCO and CUB. The paper demonstrates that this finetuning enables difference-based classification (ranking images by attributes like size and color), where standard CLIP is near-random, achieves modest zero-shot classification improvements on 4/5 benchmarks, and enables a comparative prompting inference technique that further improves accuracy on confused class pairs.

---

## Strengths

- **Enables a fundamentally new capability: difference-based classification.** PC-CLIP improves from near-random (~50–55%) to 58–67% accuracy on ranking images by attributes like size and color across four datasets (Table 1, with standard error bars and 5 seeds). CLIP and its finetuned variants (COCO FT, Rewrite FT) remain at chance level. This is the paper's cleanest and most compelling result.

- **Controlled baselines isolate the benefit of comparative supervision.** The paper compares against finetuning on the same COCO data (COCO FT) and on LLM-rewritten captions (Rewrite FT) using the same LLM. Neither improves difference-based classification, confirming that the *comparative structure* of the training signal—not simply exposure to more text or LLM-generated data—is responsible for the gains.

- **Broad evaluation across multiple tasks and metrics.** The paper tests difference-based classification (4 datasets), standard zero-shot classification (5 datasets), descriptive-prompt classification (5 datasets), comparative prompting (5 datasets), embedding-space alignment metrics (5 datasets), and text-to-image generation. This breadth supports the claim that improvements are general rather than task-specific.

- **Efficient and practical design.** Only the text encoder is finetuned, allowing precomputed image embeddings and fast training. The synthetic comparison data is generated automatically from existing image-caption pairs without human annotation.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The core contribution is valid, and the central result (difference-based classification) is convincing despite the issues below.

### Major

- **The training objective and all training details are underspecified.** The paper writes the objective as "min ℓ(g(I₁)−g(I₂), f(T₁,₂))" and states "ℓ can represent any particular loss function" — then never says which loss was actually used. No information is provided about the loss function (InfoNCE? cosine similarity with margin? MSE?), temperature/margin values, negative sampling strategy, batch construction, learning rate, optimizer, number of epochs, or any other training hyperparameter. This is a central omission for a methods paper: the experiments cannot be reproduced, and readers cannot determine whether the reported improvements are due to the idea of aligning differences or to specific loss-engineering choices. The appendix references are only for image generation details (`appx:image_gen`), not training details, so this information is not simply in a stripped appendix.

- **Zero-shot classification results lack variance estimates while difference-based results include them.** Tables 2, 3, and 4 (standard prompts, comparative prompting, extended prompts) report single numbers with no confidence intervals, standard errors, or significance tests. Many claimed improvements are small (e.g., CIFAR100: 85.59→86.12, SUN397: 72.46→73.58). Table 1 (difference-based classification) reports standard error over 5 seeds, so the authors clearly know how to do this. The selective omission weakens the evidence for the zero-shot claims and prevents assessing whether modest gains are significant.

### Minor

- **CUB appears in both training and evaluation, creating a distribution concern.** The paper generates comparatives from "two datasets, COCO and CUB-200-2011" using "1000 randomly sampled images" (Section 4.1). The exact composition of these 1000 images is never stated. Since CUB is used as a source of training comparisons, the CUB difference-based classification result (67.55% vs CLIP's 53.32%) is partially in-distribution and should not be treated as a pure generalization test. The paper notes a "significant distribution shift" for CUB but does not address the training-data overlap issue.

- **No quality assessment of the LLM-generated comparisons.** The entire method hinges on the quality of synthetic comparisons from LLaMA2-13B, yet the paper provides no human evaluation, no error analysis, no example generations, and no estimate of what fraction of comparisons are factually incorrect (even though "hallucinations" are mentioned as a limitation in the Discussion). A small human evaluation or automated sanity check would substantially increase confidence in the data quality.

- **Comparative prompting subset analysis (Table 3) compares on different class pairs for CLIP vs. PC-CLIP.** The paper acknowledges that "this subset of classes can be different for CLIP and PC-CLIP," meaning the accuracy change is computed on different test distributions for the two models. The overall results in Table 2 (which do not have this issue) still support the conclusion, but the subset comparison in Table 3 is not a controlled apples-to-apples comparison.

- **CUB zero-shot classification drops without adequate discussion.** PC-CLIP drops from 81.72 to 80.08 on CUB (Table 2) and similar drops appear in Table 4. The paper does not discuss why this particular dataset degrades, which is important since CUB was used in training data generation.

### Trivial

None beyond those already listed.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A triplet-loss baseline on image embeddings alone (without text supervision) would further isolate whether the benefit comes from text-based supervision or simply from seeing pairs of images.
- A discussion of whether finetuning the image encoder (or both encoders) would yield larger gains, since the current design inherits image-embedding quality from the frozen CLIP.
- Example LLM-generated comparisons in the main paper (not just visualizations of the pipeline) would help readers judge data quality.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

1. **"The comparative prompting equation assumes a strong linearity assumption not validated."** — The paper validates this empirically by showing comparative prompting works better with PC-CLIP (Tables 2, 3). An unvalidated assumption would be a weakness if results were negative; here the empirical results directly support the approach.

2. **"CLIPScore could be biased toward PC-CLIP."** — The paper uses a larger CLIP model for evaluation, and the generated images are evaluated independently by a different model. This is standard practice and not a meaningful bias concern.

3. **"No comparison to a triplet-loss baseline."** — A useful suggestion for future work, but not a weakness of the existing evaluation, which already includes two strong baselines (COCO FT, Rewrite FT) that control for data and LLM usage.

4. **"The image encoder is frozen."** — This is an explicitly stated design choice for efficiency, not an oversight. The paper discusses this in the methods section and the baselines use the same protocol.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface one novel observation beyond the paper's own contributions: the distinction between the difference-based classification results (which are large, robust, and properly errored) and the zero-shot classification results (which are modest and lacking variance estimates) creates a tension in how the paper is evaluated. A reader could accept the core claim about enabling difference reasoning while remaining skeptical about the broader benefits for standard classification. This suggests the paper might be strongest if it leans into the difference-based reasoning capability as the central contribution and treats the zero-shot improvements as secondary evidence.

---

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the exact loss function and all training hyperparameters** (loss type, temperature/margin, batch composition, optimizer, learning rate, number of epochs) — either in the main paper or a clearly referenced appendix. This is required for the paper to be a reproducible method contribution.

2. **Add variance estimates (over multiple seeds) to all classification tables**, or at minimum state how many seeds were used and whether the reported numbers are single runs. If some gains are not significant, discuss this honestly.

3. **Clarify the training data composition**: state exactly how many images from COCO and how many from CUB were used to generate the synthetic comparison dataset, and explicitly discuss the implications for CUB evaluation.

4. **Provide a small-scale quality check** of the LLM-generated comparisons (e.g., "5 human raters judged 100 random comparisons as reasonable/unreasonable") to establish the reliability of the synthetic supervision.

5. **Discuss the CUB zero-shot degradation** and whether it relates to CUB being in the training data or to some other factor.

---

## Score and Decision

The paper's core idea—aligning image-difference vectors with LLM-generated text descriptions of differences—is clever, well-motivated, and convincingly validated by the difference-based classification results. The controlled baselines effectively isolate the contribution of comparative supervision. However, the omission of the exact loss function and all training hyperparameters is a significant reproducibility gap, and the lack of variance estimates on the main zero-shot tables weakens those claims.

The contribution is genuine and the difference-based classification results are strong enough to warrant acceptance. The issues are addressable in a revision.
