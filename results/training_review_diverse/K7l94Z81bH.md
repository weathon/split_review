## Summary

This paper proposes RLD3, a multi-agent RL algorithm for designated driver dispatch that addresses sparsity through three mechanisms: (1) group-sharing of network parameters among similar drivers to handle heterogeneity and sparsity, (2) a dense neighborhood potential reward to provide frequent feedback, and (3) window-lasting action averaging to capture long-term agent cross-effects. The paper formulates the problem as a Dec-POMDP and evaluates on a simulator built from real-world data from Hangzhou.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated combination of mechanisms for a practically relevant problem.** Designated driver dispatch is a real industry challenge with different sparsity characteristics from standard ride-hailing. The paper clearly identifies three types of sparsity (dataset, feedback, interaction) and proposes targeted mechanisms for each. Section 3.2–3.4 provides clear intuition for why group-sharing, neighborhood potential rewards, and window-lasting averaging are needed.

2. **The group-sharing structure is validated by ablation.** Table 2 shows that introducing grouping improves MADDPG (from ~97 to ~115 orders) and improves RLD3 even without window averaging (97.1 → 113.9). The ablation on group number confirms that the grouping is not incidental — it provides a real performance lift.

3. **The window-lasting interaction technique is empirically distinct from single-step averaging.** Table 2 shows that removing the window averaging drops completed orders from 130 to 113.9, a larger drop than removing the state encoder or move cost. This supports the paper's claim that long-term cross-effects matter in this domain and that the window mechanism captures something single-step action averaging cannot.

4. **The ablation study systematically isolates each component.** Table 2 tests group number, window-lasting interaction, state encoder, potential reward, and move cost, each with multiple seeds. This provides good empirical grounding for the design decisions, even if some confounds remain in the main comparison.

5. **Simulator built from real operational data.** The use of a real-world dataset (~3,000 drivers, ~13,000 orders/day from Hangzhou) grounds the evaluation in actual market dynamics rather than fully synthetic environments.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Unfair baseline comparison confounds the main empirical result.** The paper states (lines 198) that DRL baselines (DDPG, MADDPG, MAMFRL, RND) are trained with only the immediate match reward and move cost, while RLD3 additionally receives the dense neighborhood potential reward. Table 2's ablation shows that removing the potential reward from RLD3 drops completed orders from 130 to 117. This means a substantial portion of RLD3's reported advantage over baselines (130 vs. 97 for MADDPG) could stem from the reward signal rather than the group-sharing or window-lasting mechanisms. The ablation partially mitigates this — RLD3 without potential reward (117) still beats standard MADDPG (~97) — but the margin shrinks considerably when comparing against MADDPG with grouping (~115), the fairest comparison. **Why this matters:** Without controlling the reward structure, the reader cannot attribute the observed gains to the paper's claimed algorithmic innovations (group-sharing, window-lasting). A cleaner experiment would give baselines the same potential reward or benchmark RLD3 against baselines that also receive it.

2. **Experimental scale does not match the sparsity narrative.** Training uses only 50 drivers and 500 orders (line 172), while the real platform has ~3,000 drivers and ~13,000 orders/day (line 191). The ratio of orders per driver shifts from ~4.3:1 (real) to 10:1 (experiment), meaning the sparsity dynamics in the experiment are fundamentally different from those the paper claims to solve. The paper provides no evidence — not even a discussion — of how the method would scale to hundreds or thousands of agents. The central claim of addressing "real-world sparsity at scale" remains unsupported. **Why this matters:** A method designed for sparsity must be shown to work under conditions resembling real sparsity. The current scale is too far from the target setting.

3. **The grouping mechanism is underspecified, harming reproducibility.** The paper states drivers "can be classified into several types based on their cost conditions" (line 94) and uses \(M=5\) groups, but never explains: (a) how drivers are assigned to groups (by what criterion?), (b) what the group-specific move costs \(c_j\) are, or (c) whether the grouping is based on actual data features (mileage limits, vehicle type, etc.) or is effectively arbitrary. The ablation on group number (Table 2) shows that having *any* grouping helps, but it does not validate that the *particular* grouping is capturing meaningful heterogeneity. The paper acknowledges this indirectly in future work ("make the grouping process trainable," line 254), which confirms the current grouping procedure is ad hoc. **Why this matters:** Without this information, the method cannot be reproduced, and it is unclear whether the reported results depend on a specific, unreported grouping heuristic that may not generalize.

### Minor

1. **Window-lasting action averaging representation is ambiguous.** The definition \(\hat{a}_i^t = \mathbb{E}[a_i^s]\) over a window does not specify whether the average is taken over the continuous Gumbel-Softmax outputs or over the one-hot/argmax discrete actions after sampling (lines 132–137). The paper uses Gumbel-Softmax during training to produce continuous outputs and deterministic discrete actions at execution (line 104). The semantics of averaging one-hot vectors versus continuous logits differ, and the mean-field effect \(g_j^t\) inherits this ambiguity. This is addressable in a few sentences but as written prevents precise re-implementation.

2. **K-Means encoder parameters undisclosed.** The state encoder uses K-Means (line 143) but the number of clusters and training procedure (joint with RL or precomputed?) are never reported. Since the encoder is a non-trivial component of the critic input, this is a consequential omission.

3. **"Order-oriented random-walk" baseline is not described.** This baseline is listed among optimization policies (line 200) but receives no explanation, leaving the reader to guess what it does.

4. **No sensitivity analysis on the immediate match reward value.** The match reward is set to a constant 50 with the justification of non-discrimination (lines 82–86), but no analysis shows how sensitive the results are to this value. A brief ablation would strengthen the reward design section.

### Trivial
- The paper could clarify whether the additive constant 0.1 and power index −0.5 in the potential reward were tuned or chosen by intuition (line 119). The paper does provide some justification for −0.5 (convexity to encourage approaching a specific order), but a brief sensitivity note would help.

## Novel Insights
Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface an important methodological insight about evaluating RL systems with multiple interacting mechanisms: when a system simultaneously introduces reward shaping, architectural grouping, and a new interaction mechanism, the standard "full system vs. baselines" comparison is insufficient to attribute gains. The ablation study (Table 2) is a good step, but it lacks a crucial control — giving baselines the same reward components — without which the contribution of each algorithmic innovation remains entangled with the reward signal. This observation applies broadly to RL papers that combine reward engineering with algorithmic novelty.

## Suggestions
1. **Re-run the main comparison (Table 1) with all baselines receiving the same potential reward and move cost as RLD3.** This is the single most impactful fix. If the computational cost is high, at least run this for the strongest baseline (MADDPG with grouping, which reaches ~115 orders with grouping alone). This would cleanly separate the contribution of the reward signal from the group-sharing/window-lasting components.
2. **Disclose the grouping procedure explicitly** — what features are used, how drivers are assigned, and what the \(c_j\) values are. Even a brief qualitative description would dramatically improve reproducibility.
3. **Specify whether \(\hat{a}_i^t\) averages Gumbel-Softmax outputs or discrete one-hot vectors.** Add one sentence to clarify.
4. **Add a sentence about the K-Means encoder** — report the number of clusters and whether it is trained jointly with the RL objective or as a separate preprocessing step.
5. **If full-scale experiments are infeasible, add a brief scaling discussion** acknowledging the limitation and explaining why the method is expected to transfer to larger agent counts (e.g., sub-linear complexity of the group structure).
