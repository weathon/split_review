## Summary

This paper proposes RLD3, a multi-agent reinforcement learning method for designated driver dispatch, addressing three challenges unique to this domain: dataset sparsity (few drivers relative to ride-hailing), sparse order-match feedback for individual drivers, and long-lasting cross-effects between agents. The method combines (1) a group-sharing structure where drivers are classified into groups sharing network parameters, (2) a dense neighborhood potential reward to compensate for sparse match signals, and (3) a window-lasting mechanism that averages actions over time for mean-field interaction modeling. Experiments on a simulator built from real Hangzhou data show RLD3 outperforming DRL and optimization baselines on completed orders and moving distance.

## Strengths

- **First Dec-POMDP formulation for designated driver dispatch.** The paper formally models this specific domain with a general Dec-POMDP framework (Section 3.1), capturing constraints (three driver statuses, partial 22-dim observations, 7 discrete movements) that prior fleet-management work on ride-hailing did not handle. This formalization grounds a problem with real practical importance (~$4B market in China).

- **Group-sharing structure empirically improves performance over non-grouped variants.** The ablation study (Table 2) shows RLD3 with group-sharing achieves higher completed orders than without it, and MADDPG+group-sharing similarly outperforms plain MADDPG. This provides quantitative evidence that the grouped architecture strikes a useful balance between heterogeneity and data efficiency.

- **Window-lasting interaction and potential reward both validated by targeted ablations.** Removing the window-average (Table 2) drops RLD3's completed orders, confirming its role in modeling cross-agent effects. Removing the potential reward also reduces orders and increases training instability. These ablation results directly support the claimed function of each component.

- **Real-world simulator and systematic benchmarking.** The simulator is built on a real Hangzhou dataset (~3,000 drivers, ~13,000 orders/day) and benchmarks against five baselines including both DRL methods (independent DDPG, MADDPG, MAMFRL, RND) and optimization approaches (Max-throughput, MPC). The method achieves the highest completed orders with competitive movement costs.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Experimental evaluation at a severely downsampled scale that does not match the claimed sparsity regime.** The paper motivates its sparsity-aware design using real-world statistics (~3,000 drivers, ~13,000 orders/day) but trains and evaluates on only 50 drivers and 500 orders — a 60× reduction in drivers and 26× reduction in orders (Section 4, line 172). Moreover, the downsampled setting has 10 orders per driver (500/50) versus ~4.3 in the full data, so the training regime is actually *denser* in supply-demand ratio. The method's effectiveness on the problem scale that motivated the work is untested. A sparsity-vs-performance curve (testing across 50, 200, 500, 1000 drivers) is needed to show the claimed advantage holds at realistic sparsity levels. Without this, the core thesis — that RLD3 handles the extreme sparsity of designated driving — is not convincingly supported.

- **Group assignment mechanism is unspecified, compromising reproducibility and the interpretation of the group-sharing contribution.** The paper states that drivers "can be classified into several types based on their cost conditions" (line 94) and that the experiment uses M=5 groups (line 198), but it never explains what determines group membership. Are groups based on empirical driver attributes (speed, mileage limits, vehicle type)? Learned via clustering during training? Assigned randomly? The move cost c_j is defined per group but its source is not described. The ablation on "group number" (Table 2) varies M from 1 to 5 but without knowing how agents are assigned to groups, the results cannot be interpreted — if assignment is arbitrary, the contribution reduces to parameter sharing with a tuned hyperparameter, which is standard practice. The paper acknowledges this may be important by listing "trainable grouping" as future work (line 254), but the current omission is a significant gap.

### Minor

- **The simulator's driver-order matching mechanism is not described.** The Dec-POMDP formulation references "matches between drivers and orders" as part of the state transition (lines 56, 74), and the matching outcome directly determines the match reward. Yet the paper never specifies the matching algorithm — is it proximity-based, first-come-first-served, or an optimization? This is essential for understanding the problem dynamics and for reproducibility.

- **Evaluation conflates two objectives (orders and distance) without a principled comparison framework.** Results (Table 1) report completed orders and moving distance as separate numbers. The paper claims RLD3 "had a smaller moving distance compared to methods that had similar completed order performance" (line 211), but this is a qualitative judgment. A combined metric (e.g., orders minus λ·distance) or a Pareto frontier would allow readers to verify dominance claims rigorously.

- **"Best episodic performance" selection inflates results relative to standard practice.** The testing protocol selects the "best episodic performance" across training (line 207) rather than evaluating the final policy or the mean over last N episodes. This is known to inflate reported performance and introduces variance. Standard practice in the RL community is to report final or averaged policy performance.

- **All DRL baselines use the same architecture and hyperparameters without evidence of per-algorithm tuning.** The paper states all DRL methods share "the same two hidden layers of dimension 64 and batch size of 512" with learning rate 0.01 (line 198). MADDPG, for instance, typically uses larger networks and lower learning rates in standard benchmarks. Without tuning evidence, the comparison may disadvantage certain baselines.

- **Window size W=60 (30 minutes) is not justified and sensitivity is not analyzed.** The ablation shows removing the window hurts performance, but no analysis of different window sizes is provided (line 198). Similarly, the potential reward constants (0.1, -0.5, the 0.1 weight on the sum) are presented without justification or comparison to alternative formulations.

- **Statistical reliability is limited.** Experiments use 4 seeds (line 172) and the IID generalization test uses only 10 episodes (line 207), which is few for a stochastic environment. While 4 seeds is not uncommon, the combination with best-episode selection amplifies variance concerns.

### Trivial

- The Gumbel-Softmax description (line 104) could be clearer about whether execution uses argmax of the continuous output or sampling from the Gumbel-Softmax distribution during execution. This is standard practice but the text is slightly imprecise.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation that isolates the potential reward on a simpler baseline** (e.g., adding the potential reward to independent DDPG or MADDPG) would clarify whether this component alone closes most of the gap, or whether the full RLD3 combination is needed.
- **A sparsity-vs-performance scaling experiment** (e.g., training at 50, 200, 500 driver scales) to demonstrate that RLD3's advantage grows as conditions become sparser.
- **Qualitative analysis** of learned driver trajectories or group-specific behaviors (e.g., do different groups converge to different hotspots?) would strengthen the paper's claims about heterogeneity modeling.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the group assignment mechanism.** State explicitly how groups are formed in the experiments — whether based on real driver attributes, a clustering procedure, or random splitting. If the latter, reframe the contribution accordingly and provide evidence that the grouping still captures meaningful heterogeneity.
2. **Justify the training scale or provide scaling experiments.** Either explain why 50 drivers / 500 orders is representative of the sparsity challenges (e.g., same spatial density), or run experiments at 50, 200, 500, 1000+ drivers to show that RLD3's advantage persists or grows at real-world scale.
3. **Fix the evaluation protocol.** Report final or mean-over-last-N-episodes performance instead of best-episode performance. Use a combined metric (orders minus λ·distance) or Pareto analysis for the two objectives.
4. **Describe the matching algorithm** used in the simulator to resolve driver-order assignments.

## Score and Decision

The paper tackles a practically important and understudied problem with a method that combines three sensible, domain-motivated components. The strengths — first formalization of the problem as a Dec-POMDP, empirically validated design components, and realistic simulator — are real. However, the experimental evaluation is weakened by testing at a scale far from the real problem (50 vs. ~3,000 drivers) without justification, the group-sharing mechanism (a core contribution) is underspecified to the point of irreproducibility, and the evaluation protocol uses best-episode selection which inflates results. These issues mean the current evidence does not fully support the paper's claims about handling severe sparsity at realistic scale. The paper could be publishable after substantial revision addressing these gaps. In its current form, the contribution does not stand.
