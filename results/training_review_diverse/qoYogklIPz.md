## Summary
This paper proposes the Embedding Language Model (ELM) framework, which injects domain embedding vectors into the token space of a pretrained LLM via trained adapter layers, enabling the LLM to generate natural-language descriptions of entities *from their embeddings alone*. ELM is trained on 24 movie-interpretation tasks (targets generated by PaLM 2-L) and a user-profile generation task using MovieLens 25M. The paper also introduces Semantic Consistency (SC) and Behavioral Consistency (BC) metrics, and evaluates on held-out entities, interpolated/hypothetical points, and CAV-driven attribute extrapolation.

## Strengths
1. **Novel and well-motivated framework for dynamic embedding interpretation.** ELM goes beyond static visualization (t-SNE, UMAP) or narrow concept vectors (CAVs) by enabling free-form natural-language dialogue about embedding content, including for hypothetical entities that do not exist in the training data (Section 2, Figure 1). This framing of the problem—teaching an LLM to "speak" about points in a pre-existing embedding space—is original and practically relevant.

2. **Introduction of two quantitative consistency metrics for hypothetical-entity evaluation.** SC (re-embedding generated text and measuring similarity to the source embedding) and BC (using generated text to make behavioral predictions) provide evaluation machinery for points in embedding space that have no ground-truth label. These metrics are used to demonstrate consistency for interpolated and extrapolated entities where no human reference exists (Section 3.2).

3. **Demonstrated generalization to interpolated/extrapolated entities and CAV manipulation.** The paper shows that ELM can describe linear interpolations between movies/users (Figure 3, Figure 4) and extrapolations along CAV attribute directions (Figures 4-5), with SC and BC scores that remain high across varying interpolation coefficients. This demonstrates a capability that static interpretability methods and text-only LLMs cannot easily replicate.

4. **Broad empirical scope.** The evaluation covers 24 diverse movie tasks (single-movie semantic, single-movie subjective, two-movie comparative) and a user-profiling task, with both automatic metrics and human ratings (100 raters per task, Table 1).

5. **Two-stage training procedure with clear motivation.** The paper explains why training continuous prompts directly can be problematic and proposes a two-stage approach (freeze LLM, train adapter first; then fine-tune all parameters), which is a practical design contribution (Section 2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Baseline comparison to text-only LLMs is insufficiently specified, making the claimed superiority unverifiable.** In §4, ELM is compared to PaLM 2-L and GPT-4 on interpolated embeddings. The paper states that text-only LLMs were "provide[d] ... with ground-truth examples of each task's output, and prompt[ed] ... to generate the task output" (line 205). However, it never explains what input the text-only LLM receives to represent the *interpolated embedding point*. If the LLM receives movie titles or names of the end-point movies, it has access to far more semantic knowledge than ELM (which only gets a single embedding vector). If it receives something else, the paper does not say what. Without this detail, the baseline experiment cannot be reproduced, and the conclusion that "ELM significantly outperforms text-only LLMs" (line 205) is not properly supported by the methodology as described.

2. **Behavioral Consistency (BC) is not defined for movie tasks, rendering Table 1's BC column uninterpretable.** The definition of BC in §3.2 (lines 170-174) is explicitly tied to *user profiles* ("For example, in our user-profile task, we can test the ability of ELM's generated user profile to predict the movie preferences of a user"). Table 1 reports BC (Spearman rank correlation) for all 24 movie tasks, but the paper never specifies: (a) what behavioral prediction is being made from the movie-description text, (b) what ground-truth ranking $R_u$ is used for movies, or (c) what the "off-the-shelf" ranker $\rho$ receives as input for movie tasks. The footnote on line 174 only clarifies the user-profile setting. Without this information, the BC scores for movie tasks cannot be evaluated as evidence.

### Minor
1. **Training targets are synthetic and SC metric shares the same encoder, creating partial circularity.** All training outputs for the 24 movie tasks are generated by PaLM 2-L (lines 106-108). ELM is trained to reproduce these from embeddings, then evaluated with SC, which re-embeds outputs using the *same* DLM that produced the original movie semantic embeddings (line 125-126). A high SC score could partly reflect that the DLM's encoding is self-consistent rather than that the domain embedding is being faithfully interpreted. This concern is mitigated by the use of human evaluation and BC as independent measures, but the paper would be strengthened by explicitly calibrating SC against human-written references for a subset of tasks.

2. **Human evaluation lacks variance and inter-rater reliability information.** The paper reports 100 raters per task but gives only point estimates for ratings (0.56–1.0) with no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or inter-rater agreement metrics (Table 1, lines 116-117, 186). Given that several scores are near ceiling and some are counterintuitively low (e.g., "neutral review" gets Task Quality of 0.56 while all other scores for that task are 1.0), variance information is essential for interpreting these results.

3. **Selective presentation of interpolation results.** The interpolation analysis varying $\alpha$ (Figure 4) shows SC curves for only 2 out of 24 tasks. While selective presentation is common for space reasons, the claim that ELM handles interpolation well broadly is not fully supported for all tasks.

4. **Adapter architecture details are underspecified.** The paper states a "two-layer MLP" is used (line 184) that maps $\mathcal{W} \mapsto \mathcal{Z}$ (a single token vector, per footnote 4 on line 76), but does not specify the hidden dimension(s) or activation function, which affects reproducibility.

### Trivial
None.

## Suggestions
1. **Clarify the text-only LLM baseline.** Explicitly state what input the text-only LLM receives for describing interpolated embedding points (movie titles? concatenated descriptions? Nothing?). If the LLM receives movie names, discuss why this comparison is still informative despite giving the baseline more direct semantic knowledge, or redesign the comparison to be fairer.
2. **Define Behavioral Consistency for movie tasks.** This may be as simple as adding a sentence explaining that the generated movie text is embedded via the DLM and compared (via Spearman correlation) to the original movie embedding's rank among a candidate set. Without this, the main results table is missing a clear definition for a key metric.
3. **Report variances or confidence intervals for human ratings.** Even bootstrapped confidence intervals would greatly improve interpretability of the already-collected data.
4. **For the SC metric, add a small human-annotated reference set** for a subset of tasks (e.g., summary, long description) to show that SC correlates with human judgments of fidelity, breaking the circularity concern.
