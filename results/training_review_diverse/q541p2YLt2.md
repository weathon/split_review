## Summary

This paper studies attention entropy collapse in transformers, comparing standard softmax-based attention with Lipschitz kernel attention (ReLU, ELU+1). Using a minimal architecture (self-attention layers only) trained on in-context linear regression, the paper demonstrates empirically that softmax attention leads to entropy collapse (attention probabilities concentrating toward one-hot vectors) and gradient explosion, while Lipschitz kernel attention maintains stable entropy and stable training. The paper attributes softmax's vulnerability to its sensitivity to attention logit variance, and argues that entropy collapse causes gradient explosion because the Frobenius norm of the attention probability matrix (∥P∥_F) appears in an upper bound on the gradient norm and grows as attention concentrates.

## Strengths

- **Clean empirical demonstration of different entropy dynamics**: Figure 1 clearly shows that softmax and QK-LayerNorm attentions suffer entropy collapse and gradient explosion, while ReLU and ELU+1 kernel attentions maintain high entropy and stable gradients throughout training. This directly supports the paper's central comparative claim and is visually compelling.

- **Well-designed controlled experiment isolating variance sensitivity**: Section 3.3 (Figure 3) fixes the query norm and samples keys with increasing variance σ², cleanly showing that softmax-based entropy collapses as variance grows while Lipschitz kernel entropy remains stable. This isolates variance as a key factor without confounds from training dynamics.

- **Connection between training-time variance growth and entropy collapse**: Figure 4 (Left) shows that attention logit variance grows rapidly during training for softmax but stabilizes for Lipschitz kernels. Figure 4 (Right) shows that at the same variance level, softmax yields much lower entropy than Lipschitz kernels. This bridges the controlled experiment to actual training behavior.

- **Identification of ∥P∥_F as a relevant quantity linking attention concentration to gradient norm**: Figure 5 shows that ∥P∥_F grows to √N (one-hot regime) for softmax while staying near 1 for Lipschitz kernels, and that ∥P∥_F correlates with gradient norm. The paper further notes that ∥P∥_F appears in a known bound on the self-attention derivative (Dasoulas et al., 2021), providing a plausible (though not causally proven) connection.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The causal claim linking entropy collapse to gradient explosion is not adequately supported.** The paper states that as attention probabilities concentrate, ∥P∥_F increases, and "Therefore, the more extreme the distribution of attention probabilities is, the larger the gradient becomes" (line 148). The evidence for this claim is (a) an upper bound from Dasoulas et al. (2021) in Eq. (9) where ∥P∥_F appears, and (b) the empirical correlation in Figure 5 (Right). An upper bound does not imply that increasing ∥P∥_F *causes* larger gradients — it only means the bound becomes looser. The actual gradient could be small even when ∥P∥_F is large, if the other terms in the bound are small. Correlation (Figure 5) is also not causation. The paper does not analyze whether the second term in the bound also grows, nor whether the bound is tight enough to explain observed gradient magnitudes. Since explaining *why* entropy collapse leads to instability is a central claim of the paper (stated in the abstract and conclusion), this gap weakens the paper's core explanatory narrative. The empirical observation that ∥P∥_F and gradient norm co-occur is genuine, but the causal mechanism is not established.

### Minor

2. **The theoretical grounding for why Lipschitzness stabilizes entropy is incomplete.** The paper states that "Theoretically, this stability arises because Lipschitz kernel functions have bounded gradients" (line 117), but it does not analyze the full attention mapping. In kernel attention (Eq. 5), the attention probabilities involve a denominator φ(Q_i)∑φ(K_j)^⊤ that depends on all keys in the sequence. Even if φ is Lipschitz, the overall mapping from inputs to attention probabilities may not be, because of this global normalization. The controlled experiment (Figure 3) provides strong *empirical* evidence of stability, which is valuable, but the paper's framing implies a theoretical justification that the analysis does not deliver.

3. **Narrow experimental scope limits generality of the claims.** The paper uses only self-attention layers (no MLPs, no residual connections, no layer norm) on a single synthetic task (in-context linear regression). This design choice is appropriate for isolating attention dynamics and is acknowledged in Section 3.1. However, the title ("Transformer Training Instability of Softmax and Lipschitz-Kernel Attentions") and abstract make general claims about transformer training without qualifying the scope. Real transformers include residual connections, MLPs, and normalization layers that could mitigate, exacerbate, or interact with these dynamics. The paper provides no discussion of whether or why the observed phenomena would extend to practical architectures.

4. **The Lipschitz property is confounded with non-negativity in the experiments.** The paper compares softmax (non-negative, not Lipschitz) against ReLU and ELU+1 (both non-negative AND Lipschitz). A Lipschitz kernel that can produce negative values would be needed to disentangle whether Lipschitzness alone (without the non-negativity constraint) is sufficient for entropy stability. This is a clean control the paper does not include.

### Trivial

5. **The explanation for why QK-LayerNorm also collapses is superficial.** The paper attributes this to "the inner product of the individually normalized query and key being re-weighted into an extreme probability distribution through the softmax function" (line 115-116). This essentially restates the problem rather than offering insight into why normalization fails to prevent collapse despite reducing logit magnitudes.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A formal limitations paragraph discussing how residual connections, MLPs, and layer norm in practical transformers might interact with the observed dynamics would improve the paper's completeness and honesty about scope.
- A direct gradient analysis deriving a lower bound (not just an upper bound) on gradient norm in terms of ∥P∥_F would substantially strengthen the causal claim about gradient explosion.
- A formal bound on how attention probabilities change under input perturbations as a function of the Lipschitz constant of φ would justify the claim that Lipschitzness yields stable entropy.
- It would be interesting to test whether variance growth is a cause or a consequence of instability (e.g., by artificially controlling variance during training).
- Reporting whether softmax training ever recovers after the gradient spike (beyond stating that it "renders the model unable to continue training") would provide a clearer picture of the phenomenon's severity.

However, this is the paper's own contribution, not a novel synthesis from the reviews.

## Suggestions

1. Tone down the causal language about the gradient explosion mechanism. Replace "Therefore, the more extreme the distribution... the larger the gradient becomes" with "Consistent with the bound in Eq. (9), we observe that ∥P∥_F and gradient norms are correlated (Figure 5)." Clearly distinguish between the proven upper bound and the plausible-but-unproven causal mechanism.
2. Qualify the title and abstract to reflect the paper's scope: "Analyzing Transformer Training Instability in Minimal Architectures" or similar.
3. Add a controlled experiment with a Lipschitz kernel that is not constrained to be non-negative (e.g., a clipped linear function that can take negative values) to disentangle Lipschitzness from non-negativity.
4. Add a brief discussion of how the findings might or might not transfer to architectures with residual connections and MLPs.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine empirical contribution by cleanly demonstrating that softmax attention leads to entropy collapse while Lipschitz kernel attention avoids it in a minimal setup, and by identifying variance sensitivity as the key differentiating factor. The controlled experiment (Figure 3) and the connection to training dynamics (Figure 4) are well-executed. However, the paper's central claim about *why* entropy collapse causes gradient explosion relies on an upper bound misinterpreted as a causal mechanism, and the theoretical attribution of entropy stability to Lipschitzness is not formally justified. These gaps, combined with the narrow experimental scope and lack of qualification in the title/abstract, prevent the paper from delivering on its full explanatory promise. The empirical findings are valuable enough to warrant acceptance, but the paper's framing needs significant revision.
