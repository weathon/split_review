## Summary

OMNI-EPIC introduces a framework that uses foundation models (LLMs/VLMs) to generate executable environment code (world, reward, termination functions) for open-ended learning. The method maintains a task archive and employs models of interestingness (MoI) both during task generation and post-generation verification. The paper presents qualitative evidence of diverse task generation (200-task long run), adaptive difficulty (short run with RL training), and quantitative comparisons against internal ablations showing the archive and MoI improve diversity and progress metrics.

## Strengths

- **Novel approach of generating full environment code rather than just parameters.** Prior open-ended methods (OMNI, POET, etc.) are confined to hand-specified parameterizations. OMNI-EPIC's environment generator (Section 3.3) translates natural language task descriptions directly into executable Python code (world, reward, termination, reset), fundamentally expanding the task space beyond what any prior open-ended system could express (Section 3, Figure 1). This is the paper's core contribution and is clearly novel.

- **Qualitative demonstration of continuous diversity and creativity.** The long run with simulated learning (Section 4, Figure 3) shows 200 iterations forming distinct task clusters (ball-kicking, navigation, retrieval) that diverge from seed tasks, with the system autonomously introducing dynamic elements (moving platforms, obstacles, levers) that were absent in the seeds. The t-SNE visualization provides concrete evidence of creative exploration.

- **Concrete evidence of adaptive task difficulty.** The short run with RL training (Section 5, Figure 4) shows the system responding to agent failures: after the agent failed to push a box on a dynamic platform (task 9), subsequent crossing-platform tasks omitted pushing objects; after failure on an obstacle course (task 12), the system generated an easier version with fewer obstacles (task 13). These examples demonstrate the adaptive curriculum the paper claims.

- **Quantitative evidence that the archive and MoI components contribute.** Section 7 shows that OMNI-EPIC achieves significantly higher cell coverage (task diversity) and ANNECS-OMNI scores than both controls (OMNI-EPIC w/o archive and OMNI-EPIC Learning Progress Only), with p < 0.05 (Mann-Whitney U test) and 95% confidence intervals reported (Figure 5). This validates the contribution of the two core design components.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No comparison to prior open-ended methods or simple baselines.** The quantitative experiments (Section 7) compare only against internal ablations of OMNI-EPIC itself. There is no comparison to OMNI (the direct predecessor), POET, or even a simple random-task generator. The paper states "That is a new high watermark in our field's longstanding quest to create open-ended algorithms," but this claim cannot be evaluated without external baselines. The ablations show that the archive and MoI help within OMNI-EPIC, but they do not tell the reader whether code-based environment generation is more effective than the parameter-based approaches used in prior work. This is the single most significant gap in the experimental validation.

- **Limited RL evidence for the core claim of open-endedness.** The paper reports 5 short runs with RL training but only visualizes and discusses one run in detail (Section 5, Figure 4). That run covers 23 tasks (16 successes, 6 failures, 1 uninteresting). Aggregate statistics across all 5 runs (mean/variance of success rates, diversity of solved tasks, distribution of task types) are not provided. While the paper acknowledges computational limitations, the central claim of generating tasks "endlessly" and "continuously" requires stronger evidence than a single visualized run of 23 tasks. The claim of open-endedness is not convincingly supported by the RL experiments as presented.

### Minor

- **Potential circularity in evaluation metrics.** The cell-coverage diversity metric uses text-embedding-3-small to embed task descriptions — but these tasks were themselves generated by LLMs, so the embedding space may capture linguistic similarity rather than simulation-relevant diversity. Similarly, the ANNECS-OMNI metric uses an FM to judge whether a new task is "interesting" compared to previous tasks, employing the same type of model used to generate the tasks. This creates a closed loop where FM preferences could be self-reinforcing rather than reflecting objective properties of the task space. The paper does not discuss this limitation or provide independent validation (e.g., human judgments of interestingness for a sample of tasks).

- **Success detector reliability is not evaluated.** The success detector (Section 3.6) is critical: it determines which tasks enter the archive, affecting all subsequent generation. The paper describes two approaches (LLM-based `get_success` function, and VLM-based assessment) but provides no accuracy evaluation, error analysis, or failure-case discussion. The paper even notes that "current VLMs are not yet accurate enough" for VLM-based detection, but does not evaluate the LLM-based approach either. Since the success detector gates the entire pipeline, its reliability directly affects the quality of the results.

- **The "new high watermark" claim is unsupported.** In the quantitative results (Section 7), the paper asserts "That is a new high watermark in our field's longstanding quest to create open-ended algorithms." This claim is based solely on comparisons to internal ablations, not to any prior method. Without external baselines, the reader has no basis to assess this claim. The paper should either provide such comparisons or temper the language.

## Suggestions

1. **Add at least one external baseline.** The most impactful improvement would be to compare OMNI-EPIC against OMNI (or a reimplementation) on a shared domain to validate that code-based generation yields a meaningfully broader task space. A simple random task generator would also serve as a lower bound. Without this, the paper cannot substantiate its strongest claims.

2. **Provide aggregate RL statistics.** Report mean and variance of success rates, diversity metrics, and task-type distributions across all 5 short runs. Even acknowledging computational limits, summary statistics across runs would transform anecdotal evidence into empirical evidence.

3. **Discuss and ideally validate the metric circularity.** Acknowledge that the diversity and interestingness metrics use the same family of models that generate the tasks. A small human evaluation of task interestingness for a random sample, or an alternative metric based on agent performance variance across tasks, would strengthen the evaluation.

4. **Evaluate the success detector's accuracy.** Even a small-scale manual check of whether `get_success` correctly identifies task completion would help the reader assess the reliability of the entire pipeline.

5. **Calibrate the language around "new high watermark."** This claim is currently unsupported by the evidence presented. Either provide comparative baselines that justify it, or replace it with a claim that accurately reflects what was shown (e.g., "the archive and MoI significantly improve diversity and progress compared to internal ablations").

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a genuinely novel and promising framework. The core idea — using FMs to generate executable environment code for open-ended learning — is creative and well-motivated. The qualitative demonstrations (long-run diversity, adaptive difficulty) are compelling and provide proof of concept. However, the experimental validation has significant gaps that undermine the paper's strongest claims: the quantitative comparisons lack external baselines, the RL evidence is limited to a single detailed run, and key components (success detector, evaluation metrics) are not independently validated. The paper makes a real contribution but needs stronger evidence to support its claims.
