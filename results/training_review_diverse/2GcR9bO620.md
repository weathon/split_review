## Summary

This paper makes two main contributions to deepfake audio detection: (1) **DeepFakeVox-HQ**, a large-scale dataset of 1.3M samples (640k fake from 14 TTS/VC sources, 690k real) that is 2–10× larger than prior benchmarks, and (2) **F-SAT (Frequency-Selective Adversarial Training)** , a method that focuses adversarial perturbations on high-frequency components (optimally 4–8 kHz) to improve robustness while preserving clean accuracy. Empirical results show F-SAT + RandAug achieves 98.0% average accuracy on the DeepFakeVox-HQ test set (+7.7 pp over RawNet3 baseline) and raises attacked fake audio accuracy from 17.9% to 92.8% under frequency-domain attacks.

## Strengths

1. **Large, diverse, and up-to-date dataset (DeepFakeVox-HQ)** . The dataset comprises 1.3M samples with 14 distinct TTS/VC sources, clean and noisy conditions, and includes out-of-distribution test samples from commercial services and social media. Table 1 shows it is 2–10× larger than prior datasets like ASVspoof2019 and In-The-Wild. The confusion matrix (Figure 3) visually demonstrates that training on DeepFakeVox-HQ substantially improves RawNet3's accuracy on the OOD test set compared to training on prior datasets.

2. **F-SAT achieves strong robustness gains with minimal clean-accuracy degradation.** On the DeepFakeVox-HQ test set, F-SAT+RandAug yields 98.0% average accuracy (+7.7 pp over RawNet3; Table 2). Under adversarial attacks, it achieves 87.0% (time-domain) and 92.8% (frequency-domain) fake accuracy, compared to 6.5% and 17.9% for the RawNet3 baseline (Table 3). The ablation in Table 4 confirms that the 4–8 kHz range provides the best trade-off.

3. **Comprehensive robustness evaluation across 24 corruptions and multiple attack types.** Figure 4a shows the method outperforms all baselines on every tested corruption (average +15.3 pp absolute). Figure 4b covers time-domain, frequency-domain magnitude, and frequency-domain phase attacks, demonstrating broad-spectrum robustness rather than narrow overfitting to a single attack type.

4. **Systematic ablation of design choices.** Table 4 evaluates six frequency ranges for F-SAT, identifying 4–8 kHz as optimal. Figure 5 explores the clean/robust loss trade-off (γ) and attack magnitude (ε), providing practical hyperparameter guidance grounded in empirical data.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The interpretation of the high-pass filter experiment (Figure 2) contradicts the paper's own claims about low-frequency reliance.** The paper states (line 36): "the low frequency signals can be heard by humans but are not relied on by the model to make predictions." However, Figure 2 applies a high-pass filter (removing low frequencies) and reports "a marked decline in detection performance for real audio starting at 4000 Hz and for fake audio at 6000 Hz." With a high-pass filter, increasing the center frequency removes more low-frequency content. If removing low frequencies degrades performance, the model *does* rely on them — directly contradicting the claim that they are "not relied on." The stated finding that detectors "depend on high-frequency features" is not supported by this figure as described. This is not a fatal flaw (the F-SAT method works empirically regardless, and Figure 5b independently shows high-frequency attacks are more damaging), but the motivational story needs substantial correction. The paper must either (a) correct the interpretation, (b) clarify that the figure actually shows a low-pass filter (if that was the real experimental setup), or (c) replace this figure with evidence that actually supports the claimed high-frequency dependence.

### Minor

2. **The 33% improvement from the dataset alone is not quantified with precise numerical comparison in a dedicated table.** The abstract claims the dataset boosts baseline performance by 33% (line 4), and line 41 specifies this is on the "out-of-distribution portion of our test set." Figure 3 (confusion matrix) provides visual support, but no table systematically compares "RawNet3 trained on prior datasets vs. RawNet3 trained on DeepFakeVox-HQ" with exact accuracy numbers. The claim is likely correct given the evidence, but a dedicated table with exact percentages would make it verifiable at a glance.

3. **Missing control: full-band frequency-domain adversarial training.** The key novelty of F-SAT is frequency *selectivity*. Table 3 compares F-SAT against time-domain AT but not against standard frequency-domain AT applied to the full frequency range (0–8 kHz). Without this control, it is unclear whether the benefit comes from selectivity or simply from operating in the frequency domain. Adding "RawNet3+RandAug+AT(Frequency, 0-8kHz)" to Table 3 would cleanly isolate the advantage of selective targeting.

4. **Attack configuration details for evaluation are underspecified.** The paper defines F-SAT with parameters ε, α, K, and q but does not specify the exact values used in the evaluation attacks (e.g., number of PGD iterations K, norm constraint q, step size α). Figure 6 explores ε, but the evaluation protocol itself is not fully reproducible from the main text. This should be addressed (likely in an appendix expanded beyond what the parser captured).

### Trivial
None of consequence. Minor presentation issues exist (e.g., "DeepFakeVoc-HQ" vs "DeepFakeVox-HQ" inconsistency in Section 5) but are negligible.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The hyperparameter analysis (Figure 6) is performed on 10% of the dataset, which the paper honestly acknowledges. While common for exploratory analysis, verifying that the optimal ε=0.01 and γ=0.1 transfer to the full dataset would strengthen the claims.
- A saliency-map or gradient-based visualization showing where F-SAT shifts the model's attention away from high frequencies would provide more direct support for the claim that the method protects vulnerable frequency bands.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, an interesting synthesis from the reviews is that the vulnerability profile of deepfake detectors is frequency-dependent in an asymmetric way: fake audio appears more reliant on genuinely high frequencies (6–8 kHz) than real audio (which relies on mid-frequencies up to ~4 kHz), as suggested by the combination of Figure 2 (different frequency drop-off points for real vs. fake) and Table 4 (narrowing the F-SAT range helps fake accuracy more). This asymmetry — where the same frequency band matters more for detecting one class than the other — is underexplored in the paper and could motivate class-conditional frequency selection in future work.

Rewrite lines 35–37 and the Conclusion to accurately describe what the figure shows. The F-SAT method's real motivation (high-frequency features are vulnerable to attack even if not solely relied upon for detection) is actually defensible and supported by Figure 5b.
