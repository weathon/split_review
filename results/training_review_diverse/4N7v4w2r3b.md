## Summary

This paper introduces ProxyBench, a benchmark for evaluating the robustness of neural-network proxy reward models under optimization pressure in the text domain. The benchmark provides gold reward models (ensemble of DeBERTa-v3 models trained on human preference data), three optimization methods (best-of-n, reinforcement learning via PPO, and white-box adversarial attacks), a labeled dataset, and novel metrics (RMS divergence). Through experiments, the paper reports that (1) instance-level proxy gaming can occur even when aggregate gold reward appears to improve, (2) larger proxy models are harder to game, while larger policies and more optimization exposure increase gaming, and (3) existing robustness techniques (adversarial training, SAM) provide limited or negative benefit outside white-box settings.

---

## Strengths

1. **ProxyBench provides a structured, reproducible framework for studying proxy robustness, filling a gap left by prior work.** Gao et al. (2022) did not release data or models and only studied scaling. This paper releases a full benchmark with gold reward models, three distinct optimization methods, a labeled training dataset (100K examples from HH/SHP/OA), and RMS-based metrics for measuring proxy-gold divergence. (Sections 4.1–4.4)

2. **Demonstrates that instance-level proxy gaming can persist even when aggregate rewards appear well-correlated.** The paper shows visually (Figures 2, 3) and via a cross-model overlap analysis (Section 5.1) that a non-random subset of examples suffer from proxy reward increasing while gold reward decreases, even when mean gold reward improves. This is an important qualitative finding that highlights the inadequacy of aggregate-only evaluation.

3. **Provides systematic scaling analyses revealing new relationships between model size, optimization exposure, and proxy gaming.** Larger proxy models monotonically improve both gold reward and RMS across all three optimizers; larger policies cause more gaming under RL (but not BoN); more training data and steps exacerbate gaming. These go beyond the functional-form analysis of Gao et al. (2022) and provide concrete guidance for practitioners. (Section 5.2, Figure 5, Table 1)

4. **Reports negative results for existing robustness techniques, establishing that adversarial training helps in white-box settings but fails (and sometimes harms) under BoN and RL.** This is a useful finding that motivates new research on proxy-specific robustness methods. SAM is also shown to be ineffective. (Section 5.5, Table 3)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **No statistical uncertainty reported for any quantitative result.** Every main comparison (Tables 1, 3; Figures 5, 6; Section 5.2 scaling analyses) is presented without error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests. For a benchmark that aims to "enable research on improving proxy robustness," this is a serious evidential gap. Readers cannot judge whether reported differences (e.g., between proxy sizes or optimization methods) are reliable or within noise. Running experiments over multiple random seeds (even 2–3) and reporting variance is standard for benchmark papers. The paper does not even state how many seeds were used — the implicit assumption is a single run. This weakens the credibility of all quantitative claims.

2. **The gold model is itself a learned neural network, limiting the benchmark's external validity, while the paper's claims overreach this limitation.** The paper defines an ensemble of DeBERTa-v3 models as the "gold" reward — explicitly not human judgment. The authors acknowledge this in Section 4.1 ("This choice disregards how well the model captures the underlying objective of human raters"). However, the abstract and introduction make broad claims about "serious issues with proxy reward models currently used to fine-tune or monitor large language models" and "previously unknown properties of the proxy gaming problem." Proxy gaming measured *against a learned model* may reflect systematic biases or artifacts of the gold model itself, not failures relative to human values. The benchmark is therefore testing whether one learned reward model can be gamed by another, not whether proxies fail under the optimization pressure that ultimately matters (human feedback). The paper should either (a) validate the gold model against held-out human judgments (reporting accuracy/preference prediction correlation) or (b) substantially temper the real-world claims.

3. **The instance-level gaming classification metric and its hypothesis test lack rigor.** Gaming at the instance level is defined by splitting reward curves at the midpoint and requiring a negative Pearson correlation in either segment (Section 5.2). The midpoint threshold is arbitrary and unvalidated. The overlap analysis (Section 5.1) reports "over 45% overlap" with "near zero probability" under the null, but provides no test statistic, no p-value, no description of the actual hypothesis test, and no multiple-testing correction. This is not a replicable statistical claim. While the visual evidence (Figures 2, 3) supports the existence of instance-level gaming, the quantitative rigor of the metric and the associated statistical test is insufficient.

### Minor

1. **Missing training details for the gold model.** The paper does not report how many examples were drawn from each of the three datasets (HH, SHP, OA), the gold model's training procedure (number of epochs, batch size, learning rate, ensemble size), or any held-out accuracy/calibration on the preference prediction task. This makes it difficult for users to assess the gold model's quality or reproduce it.

2. **Key reproducibility details are absent.** The RL modification "changes to the codebase to avoid early collapse" (Section 4.2) is not specified. The adversarial training hyperparameters (learning rate, attack strength, number of steps) and clean accuracy on held-out sets are not reported beyond the vague "below 10% loss" criterion (Section 5.5). SAM experimental details (sharpness parameter, training epochs) are entirely missing. These are required for a usable benchmark.

3. **No ablation on design choices.** The white-box attack uses 4 prepended adversarial tokens (Section 4.2) — not justified or ablated. The RMS metric uses a smoothing window of 10% of evaluation points (Section 4.4) — not justified. The optimality of these choices is unclear.

4. **The adversarial training and SAM experiments are preliminary.** They use a single hyperparameter configuration selected to keep clean accuracy loss below 10%. The negative results for SAM are stated without any experimental details. These findings are suggestive but not conclusive, yet the paper presents them as demonstrating that "existing methods are insufficient."

5. **White-box results are presented alongside BoN and RL in Table 1 despite acknowledged incomparability.** The paper notes that white-box attacks produce "gibberish which does not increase the gold reward legitimately, making the results incomparable to other optimizers" (Section 4.4), but still includes them in the same table. This could mislead readers who scan the table without reading the caveat.

### Trivial

None.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews highlight a genuine tension: the benchmark trades external validity (gold model vs. human judgment) for experimental control and reproducibility. This is a common dilemma in alignment-related benchmarks, but the paper does not adequately calibrate its claims to match its design. The most interesting observation across the reviews is that the paper's strongest finding — instance-level gaming hidden by aggregate metrics — is also its weakest in terms of metric validation. The gaming classification rule (midpoint split, negative correlation) is introduced without evidence that it identifies examples that humans would agree are "gamed." Closing this validation gap would substantially strengthen the paper, and doing so could yield a methodology that future benchmark papers in this area adopt.

## Suggestions

1. Report all main results with error bars over at least 3 random seeds (different initializations of proxy, gold, and policy where feasible). For computationally expensive runs (e.g., LLaMA-7B PPO), even 2 seeds would be a significant improvement.
2. Validate the gold model against held-out human preference judgments, or at minimum report its accuracy on the preference prediction task across test splits. If validation data is unavailable, explicitly acknowledge this and temper the real-world claims in the abstract and conclusion.
3. Replace the vague "near zero probability" claim with a properly specified hypothesis test (exact p-value, test used, sample sizes) or remove it. At minimum, provide the raw overlap counts and the null distribution.
4. Document all missing experimental details: RL training modifications, adversarial training hyperparameters, SAM sharpness parameter and epochs, gold model training procedure.
5. Add a brief ablation showing how results change with different attack lengths (e.g., 2, 4, 8 tokens) and RMS smoothing windows (e.g., 5%, 10%, 20%).
6. Either move the white-box results to a separate table or add a prominent visual indicator (e.g., a footnote or separate column header) that they are not comparable to BoN/RL.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses an important problem and introduces a benchmark framework that fills a genuine gap. The qualitative findings — instance-level gaming, scaling trends, and negative results for robustness methods — are useful contributions. However, the two major weaknesses (complete absence of statistical uncertainty, and the gold model limitation coupled with overclaimed real-world significance) are significant. The lack of error bars is particularly problematic for a benchmark paper whose quantitative comparisons other researchers will rely on. The paper is borderline: the core ideas are solid and the framework is valuable, but the evidential basis for the quantitative claims is insufficient in its current form.

The authors can address these issues in a revision without altering the paper's structure or conclusions. If the major weaknesses are resolved, the paper would be a clear accept.
