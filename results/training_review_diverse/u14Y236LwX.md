## Summary

OpenWaves is a large-scale USCT dataset containing over 16 million frequency-domain Helmholtz simulations on anatomically realistic breast phantoms derived from the FDA's VICTRE tool. It covers four breast density types, 8 frequencies (300–650 kHz), and 256 source locations mimicking a real annular USCT system. The paper benchmarks five forward neural operator baselines (UNet, FNO, BFNO, AFNO, MgNO) and three direct-inverse baselines (DeepONet, InversionNet, NIO), plus an optimization-based FWI approach, with analysis of scaling, frequency dependence, and out-of-distribution generalization.

## Strengths

1. **Massive scale with genuine anatomical realism:** OpenWaves provides 16M+ frequency-domain simulations across 8,000 phantoms derived from the FDA's validated VICTRE tool, organized into four clinically meaningful breast density categories (Abstract, Section 3.2.1, Table 1). This directly addresses the oversimplifications (Gaussian random fields, MNIST, layered structures) that limit prior datasets like PDEBench, OpenFWI, and WaveBench.

2. **Comprehensive dual-task benchmarking framework with actionable findings:** The paper systematically evaluates seven baselines across both forward simulation and inverse imaging, reporting RRMSE/Max Error for forward and SSIM/PSNR for inverse tasks. The analysis goes beyond leaderboard numbers to examine performance across breast types, frequency dependence (Fig. 4a), dataset-size scaling (Fig. 4b), source-location generalization (Fig. 4c), and OOD generalization across breast categories (Table 5, Fig. 5). Key findings — e.g., that MgNO and FNO variants scale better with data than UNet, and that forward models generalize better than inverse models OOD — are actionable for practitioners.

3. **Empirical validation of scaling laws and data efficiency:** The experiments show that neural operator accuracy consistently improves with more training data (4,000→8,000 phantoms) and that MgNO and the FNO family exhibit better data efficiency than UNet, which plateaus (Section 4.3.2, Fig. 4b). This validates the value of large-scale datasets for operator learning.

4. **Rigorous out-of-distribution analysis:** The paper tests generalization across breast types (Table 5, Fig. 5) and source locations (Fig. 4c). A notable finding is that models trained on more complex tissue types (e.g., heterogeneous) generalize better, and that forward models degrade less on OOD data than inverse models, supporting the insight that forward models learn physics while inverse models risk memorizing anatomy.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No quantitative results for the optimization-based FWI baseline, undermining a central comparative claim.** Section 4.2 and Fig. 3 present qualitative visual results for the gradient-based FWI using a pre-trained FNO surrogate. The text claims this approach "revealed significantly higher resolution than all direct inversion methods" and concludes that "forward operators better capture the underlying wave physics." Yet Table 4 (the quantitative evaluation of inverse imaging baselines) reports SSIM and PSNR *only* for the three direct-inversion baselines (DeepONet, InversionNet, NIO). The optimization-based method receives no row or footnote in Table 4. This is an evidential gap: the paper's narrative about forward models vs. inverse models is partly asserted rather than demonstrated with the same standard of evidence applied to the direct methods. Adding SSIM/PSNR for the optimization-based reconstruction across breast types would directly substantiate this claim and is the single highest-priority revision.

### Minor

- **Baseline results are reported without variance or statistical significance.** All results in Tables 3, 4, and 5 appear to come from single training runs. For a benchmark intended to serve as a community reference, differences as small as 0.001 RRMSE or 0.02 SSIM between methods cannot be assessed for significance. The paper's rankings (e.g., MgNO vs. FNO, NIO vs. DeepONet) would be more reliable with at least 3 seeds for a representative subset of models. This is a methodological gap that reduces the benchmark's value as a reference for future work.

- **Train/validation/test split is not specified.** The paper states that 8,000 phantoms are divided into four breast types but does not specify how they are split into training, validation, and test sets (e.g., 7,000/500/500, or some other ratio). For a benchmark paper, this is a reproducibility gap — future users cannot directly compare their results against the reported numbers without knowing the exact split.

- **Test-time source count is ambiguous.** Section 4.1 states the models are trained on "64 uniformly sampled sources out of 256." It is not specified whether test-time evaluation uses the same 64 sources or all 256. If the latter, the models must generalize to unseen source locations during testing, which would make the task harder and the results more informative — but the current text does not clarify this.

- **Preprocessing details for neural network inputs/outputs are missing.** The paper does not specify: (a) whether the sound speed field \( c(x) \) is encoded as a single input channel; (b) whether wavefields are normalized globally or per sample; (c) how the real and imaginary parts of the complex wavefield are channeled. These details are necessary for reproducibility, especially for practitioners seeking to extend the benchmarks.

- **Sound speed derivation from VICTRE phantoms is underspecified.** Section 3.2.2 describes phantom generation from VICTRE but does not explain how the VICTRE tissue labels (which are anatomical classes, not acoustic properties) are mapped to sound speed values within the 1400–1600 m/s range stated in Table 1. This is needed for reproducing the dataset or understanding its physical fidelity.

- **The "anatomically realistic" framing slightly overstates the physical fidelity.** The phantoms themselves are indeed anatomically realistic (FDA VICTRE tool), which is a clear step up from Gaussian random fields. However, the simulations are 2D, neglect attenuation, density variation, anisotropy, and shear waves, and use only sound speed variation with the Helmholtz equation. The limitations section acknowledges this, but the abstract and introduction do not calibrate the realism claim as precisely as they could (e.g., "anatomically structured phantoms with simplified wave physics"). This is a framing issue, not a factual error.

### Trivial

- The scaling factor for breast size variation is mentioned but not quantified (e.g., range or distribution of factors).
- Training times for the baselines are not reported (acceptable for a dataset paper but useful for practitioners).
- The frequency range justification (why 300–650 kHz rather than the full 300–1500 kHz of the real system) is implicit through the wavenumber ROI size; an explicit sentence would help.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Add synthetic noise to measurements:** The dataset currently contains clean simulated data. Adding controlled noise levels (Gaussian, speckle) would broaden its applicability for realistic inverse problem research.
- **Report computational cost of dataset generation:** CBS wall-time per phantom-frequency-source combination and total GPU-hours would contextualize the dataset's value and help users estimate extension effort.
- **Sound speed map of water background:** Table 1 lists tissue sound speed range but the water background value is not stated; for a Helmholtz problem with constant background, this matters for the reference wavenumber.

No deception exists.

## Novel Insights

The reviewers' most useful lens is that the paper's core insight — forward neural operators learn physics while direct inverse networks memorize anatomy — is well-motivated and supported by the OOD generalization comparison but critically under-quantified for the optimization-based FWI that is supposed to exemplify it. The central evidential gap (qualitative vs. quantitative evidence for the same method) is unusual for a paper that is otherwise thorough about metrics. This is a presentation and completeness issue rather than a methodological flaw, and fixing it would substantially strengthen the paper's main conceptual claim.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a row to Table 4** with SSIM and PSNR for the FNO-based gradient optimization reconstruction across all four breast types, matching the same test set used for direct methods. This is the single most impactful fix.
2. **Specify the train/validation/test split** (e.g., a concrete number of phantoms per split, stratified by breast type) and the test-time source configuration.
3. **Report at least 3-seed variance** for one representative forward model (e.g., FNO) and one inverse model (e.g., NIO) on the main test set to give the community a sense of expected variability.
4. **Document preprocessing details** (normalization scheme, input channel layout, how VICTRE labels map to sound speed values) either in the main text or a clearly referenced appendix/dataset card.
5. **Calibrate the realism language** in the abstract and introduction — e.g., change "anatomically realistic" to "anatomically structured" when referring to the overall setup (keeping "anatomically realistic" for the phantoms themselves, which is accurate).
