## Summary

This paper introduces Growth Inhibitors for Erasure (GIE), an inference-time method for concept erasure in text-to-image diffusion models. GIE extracts cross-attention maps for a target concept (e.g., "nude"), re-weights them with a suppression scale predicted by a lightweight trained adapter, and injects the result back into the prompt's attention group during generation. The approach avoids fine-tuning the base model and is evaluated on NSFW content, artistic style, and object erasure tasks, showing competitive or superior erasure rates with preserved image quality.

## Strengths

1. **Inference-time erasure without fine-tuning the base model.** GIE operates entirely during the diffusion process without modifying model weights. This avoids the catastrophic forgetting and per-concept retraining overhead of fine-tuning baselines (ESD, SPM, SalUn). The method is evaluated against eight baselines on the I2P dataset and achieves the highest NSFW removal rate (Figure 4).

2. **Demonstrated effectiveness on implicit unsafe prompts.** The paper shows that attention maps extracted for a target concept like "nude" are also activated by synonyms, capitalized forms, and noun variants (Figure 1), indicating that the method can capture inappropriate features even when prompts contain only associative terms rather than explicit keywords. This directly addresses a limitation noted for text-filtering and fine-tuning approaches.

3. **Preserved image quality and semantic alignment.** On COCO-30K, GIE achieves the best CLIP score among all methods and competitive FID (Table 1), showing that the injection operation does not distort normal image content. This contrasts with some fine-tuning baselines that substitute inappropriate concepts with unrelated objects (e.g., forests, bedrooms). The method also maintains low non-target object removal rates (≤0.05 in Table 2), suggesting specificity.

4. **Generality across concept types.** GIE is evaluated on NSFW content, artistic styles (van Gogh, Monet, Hokusai), and common objects (car, airplane, cat), achieving strong results in each category. The multiple-concept erasure experiments (Figure 7) show that separate growth inhibitors for different target concepts can be injected without catastrophic interference, with a simple semantic-distance filter preventing redundant erasure.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The core injection mechanism is insufficiently explained.** The paper describes extracting attention maps for the target concept, multiplying by a suppression scale W (Eq. 6), and injecting the result into the prompt's attention group (Eq. 7), while also inserting the target concept's text embedding into c and recalculating K′, V′ (line 106). However, it never provides intuition for *why* this operation suppresses the target concept rather than amplifying it. The term "growth inhibitor" is used as a name but not grounded in a clear physical or mechanistic explanation. Without understanding the sign convention, the role of W, or how injecting the target concept's own attention pattern leads to erasure, the reader cannot assess whether the effect is expected or an artifact. This is the paper's central claim, and the explanation is incomplete. The experimental results suggest the method works empirically, but the lack of mechanistic clarity is a significant weakness.

### Minor

2. **GPT-4o-based adapter training is not validated.** The adapter is trained on labels from GPT-4o, which scores images generated with different suppression scales "with the criterion of maximizing the elimination of the target concept while maintaining image quality and semantics" (line 118). No prompt template, human correlation study, inter-rater reliability check, or comparison to standard metrics (e.g., NRR+FID Pareto fronts) is provided. Using a black-box proprietary model as the optimization oracle weakens confidence in the adapter's outputs, especially since the adapter's inferences (§4.2) are then used in quantitative experiments. While using LLMs as judges is common practice, some validation (even a small human study or agreement with NRR/FID) would substantially strengthen the results.

3. **No ablation studies isolate the claimed mechanism.** The paper never ablates: (a) injecting the target concept's attention vs. a random or null attention map, (b) the effect of W's magnitude or sign, (c) different injection positions, or (d) whether the trained adapter outperforms a simple grid-searched fixed scale. Without these controls, it is unclear whether the reported erasure arises from the specific design of the growth inhibitor or from incidental properties of the injection (e.g., any extra tokens in the attention group may cause the model to redistribute attention). For a paper whose core novelty is a specific manipulation of attention maps, the absence of component-level ablations is a notable gap.

4. **The adapter generalization claim is not experimentally substantiated.** Section 4.2 claims that training the adapter on "cat" can generalize to "dog" and "airplane" (line 126). However, the object erasure experiments in Table 2 do not clarify whether a *single* adapter was trained on one concept and tested on held-out concepts, or separate adapters were trained per concept. If separate adapters were used, the generalization claim is vacuous. If a single adapter was used, the paper should show a cross-validation table demonstrating this explicitly. As presented, the claim is stated but not evidenced.

5. **The paper shows GIE achieving the best CLIP score on COCO-30K, even exceeding the unfiltered SD v1.4 baseline (Table 1).** While this could be explained if the base model sometimes generates NSFW content for benign COCO prompts and GIE suppresses it, the paper does not discuss or analyze this potentially counterintuitive result. Without error bars or statistical tests, the reader cannot assess whether this improvement is meaningful or an artifact of metric noise. Similarly, no statistical significance tests are reported for the NRR or ORR results.

6. **No discussion of limitations or failure cases.** The paper does not acknowledge: (a) that the method requires the user to predefine each target concept, (b) that the adapter requires training (even if quick) for each concept family, (c) how the method behaves with safe prompts, very short/long prompts, or overlapping target concepts, or (d) potential failure cases where the injection distorts content. A limitations paragraph would improve the paper's scientific rigor.

### Trivial

7. **The adapter training procedure is underspecified.** The paper says training requires "a few dozen images" (line 119) but does not specify the exact number, how concepts are chosen for training, or how the suppression scale candidates are sampled for generating the training images. These details would aid reproducibility.

8. **The phrase "precisely transformed into appropriate ones" (line 17) is vague.** It gives the impression of hand-waving around the mechanism described in Weakness 1. Similarly, "control feature expression in the image space" (line 19) is used repeatedly but never concretely defined relative to text-space methods.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison against the "naive approach" of directly weakening similar-attention tokens (mentioned but not quantitatively compared in Section 4.1) would strengthen the claim that the specific injection mechanism matters.
- Reporting the computational overhead of GIE (adapter inference, GPT-4o API calls, generation of training images) per image relative to standard SD inference would help practitioners assess deployment costs.
- A human evaluation on a subset of I2P images (e.g., rating whether NSFW content remains) would complement the NudeNet-based NRR metric, which has known limitations with artistic/non-sexual human images.

## Removed Points

These points were flagged for removal; treat them with caution.

- **Criticism about "broken equations" and "grammatical errors" in metric definitions (e.g., "norigninal−nerase").** These are PDF parsing artifacts, not author errors. The original submission does not contain these issues.
- **Characterization of the injection mechanism as "conceptually reversed" or "adding positive attention — the opposite of suppression."** This is a misinterpretation. The operation is not simply "adding attention values" — it injects the target concept's attention maps as new rows into M while also inserting the target's text embedding into c and recalculating K′, V′ (line 106). The mechanism is more nuanced than the critic's framing suggests. The real issue is insufficient explanation (captured in Weakness 1), not a sign error.
- **"NudeNet is unreliable" as a fatal criticism.** NudeNet is a standard detector used consistently across all methods in the comparison. While its limitations are real, the paper's use is consistent with field norms and the comparison remains fair. The concern is noted but does not rise to a structural weakness.
- **Missing related works references.** I have no external source to confirm their existence, so this cannot be evaluated.
- **Complaint about "no confidence intervals" for metrics.** Standard in this benchmark setting.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the injection mechanism.** Add a paragraph explaining the physical intuition: injecting the target concept's attention maps (along with its text embedding) tells the cross-attention mechanism where the concept is and that it is present in the prompt, enabling the diffusion process to redirect features away from it. Visualize attention maps before and after injection to confirm that target-concept attention is actually reduced.
2. **Add ablation experiments** comparing against: (a) random attention injection, (b) fixed vs. adapter-predicted W, (c) at least two injection positions, and (d) the naive token-weakening baseline mentioned in Section 4.1.
3. **Validate the adapter**, either via a small human study on a held-out set or by showing that the adapter's predicted scale correlates with NRR/FID Pareto-optimal scales.
4. **Substantiate the generalization claim** with an explicit table showing adapter trained on concept A → tested on concepts B and C.
5. **Add a limitations section** addressing predefined target concepts, potential failure modes with safe prompts, and the reliance on GPT-4o labels for adapter training.

## Score and Decision

This paper makes a genuine contribution: an inference-time concept erasure method with strong empirical results across NSFW, style, and object tasks, with favorable preservation of image quality. However, the core injection mechanism is under-explained, several important claims are not experimentally substantiated, and the absence of ablations makes it difficult to attribute the reported results to the specific design choices. These are addressable weaknesses but nontrivial in scope. The paper would benefit from major revisions before being ready for publication.
