## Summary

This paper proposes GIE (Growth Inhibitors for Erasure), a method for suppressing inappropriate concepts in text-to-image diffusion models that operates entirely in image space (cross-attention maps) without fine-tuning. The core idea is to extract attention-map features of a target concept (e.g., "nude"), re-weight them with a learned suppression scale, and inject the resulting "growth inhibitor" directly into the prompt's attention map group. An MLP adapter trained on GPT-4o-labeled data predicts the per-generation suppression scale from intermediate cross-attention values. Experiments across NSFW, style, and object erasure tasks show strong erasure performance with competitive FID and CLIP scores.

## Strengths

1. **Novel image-space intervention without fine-tuning.** GIE operates on attention maps rather than model weights or text embeddings, which is a genuinely different approach from prior work (ESD, SalUn, Safe-CLIP). This design avoids per-concept retraining and catastrophic forgetting. The method is well-motivated: Figure 1 shows that even when prompts contain no explicit unsafe tokens, target-concept attention maps remain focused, providing a lever for intervention. **Evidence:** The method's distinctiveness is clear from Section 4.1 and the overview in Figure 2.

2. **Strong NSFW erasure with minimal quality degradation.** On the I2P dataset, GIE achieves the highest NSFW removal rate (NRR) among all eight compared baselines (Figure 4), while simultaneously attaining the best CLIP score (0.32) and competitive FID (13.8) on COCO-30K (Table 1). These results directly support the central claim that GIE can erase concepts effectively without sacrificing image quality or semantic alignment.

3. **Effectiveness on implicit unsafe prompts.** The method extracts target-concept features (e.g., "nude") regardless of whether the prompt contains explicit unsafe words. Figure 1 shows that synonymous, capitalized, and noun variants of "nude" all produce usable attention maps. The I2P dataset (where <1.5% of prompts are explicitly toxic) provides quantitative evidence that GIE handles the implicit-prompt scenario better than text-space methods like Safe-CLIP.

4. **Generality across concept types.** GIE is demonstrated on NSFW content (Figures 4-5), artistic styles (Figure 6), and common objects (Table 2, Figure 8), showing the approach is not limited to a single category. Object erasure is quantified with ORR >0.9 for three target objects and ORR ≤0.05 for non-target objects.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **GPT-4o labeling pipeline is a black box with unexamined biases.** The adapter training relies entirely on GPT-4o-generated scores as labels (Section 4.2), but no prompt is provided, no example scores are shown, no consistency analysis across multiple GPT-4o calls is given, and no human agreement study is conducted. The paper states only "with the criterion of maximizing the elimination of the target concept while maintaining image quality and semantics" — this is underspecified for a process that generates the ground-truth labels for the adapter's loss function (Eq. 9). If GPT-4o systematically prefers certain types of erasure (e.g., blurrier images that happen to lack the attribute), the adapter learns a biased mapping. While the strong downstream NRR and FID results provide indirect validation that the adapter is learning something useful, the opacity of the labeling process means the reader cannot evaluate the quality of the training signal. This is a methodological gap that undermines confidence in the adapter's design.

2. **Adapter generalization claim is asserted but not properly evidenced.** Section 4.2 claims the adapter "can also be extended to previously unseen concepts of the same type" and gives the example of training on "cat" and generalizing to "dog" and "airplane." However, Table 2 (object erasure) never states which adapter was used for each target concept. Were separate adapters trained per concept, or was a single adapter (e.g., trained on "cat") applied to all three? The distinction is critical: if concept-specific adapters were used, there is no evidence of generalization. The paper needs a controlled experiment where an adapter trained on one concept is tested on several unseen concepts and compared to concept-specific adapters (or to a grid-search optimal scale). Without this, the claimed practical benefit of the adapter — one training sufficient for many concepts — is unsubstantiated.

3. **Style erasure is purely qualitative.** Figure 6 shows erasure of van Gogh, Monet, and Hokusai styles, but no metric is provided — no style classifier accuracy, no user study, no style removal rate analogous to NRR. The paper states "the pictures outside the dotted box do not change much" (Section 5.2), but this is an assertion, not a measurement. Style erasure is presented alongside NSFW and object erasure as a key application, yet it receives no quantitative evaluation, making it impossible to assess whether the erasure is reliably effective across diverse prompts or whether the examples are cherry-picked.

### Minor

1. **Limited and somewhat dated baseline set.** The eight baselines span 2022–2024 (ESD, SPM, Safe-CLIP, SLD, SD v1.4/v2.1, negative prompting). Given the submission date, there may be more recent methods in the same class. More importantly, the comparison against inference-time intervention methods is thin — SLD and negative prompting are the only such baselines, making the reported gains over "state-of-the-art" less informative than they could be. The paper should either update the baseline set or appropriately scope its claims.

2. **Semantic distance threshold for multi-concept filtering is unspecified.** Section 4.3 describes filtering similar concepts "based on the semantic distance" (cosine distance in Eq. 10) but does not state the threshold used. Without it, the multi-concept procedure is underspecified and cannot be exactly replicated.

3. **Multiple-concept erasure is only evaluated qualitatively.** Figure 7 shows examples of joint erasure of "nude"+"horror" and "nude"+"van Gogh", but no NRR or ORR is reported for the multi-concept setting, and there is no analysis of interference between simultaneously injected inhibitors.

4. **No computational cost comparison.** Fine-tuning methods require hours of training per concept; GIE adds a forward pass to extract attention maps. A brief comparison (e.g., "ESD requires X GPU-hours per concept; GIE adds Y seconds per generation") would help contextualize the practical trade-off, which is central to the paper's motivation.

### Trivial

- The paper does not discuss NudeNet's reliability on stylized or non-photographic images, though this is a secondary concern.
- No error bars or confidence intervals are reported in any quantitative table.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human evaluation or consistency check on GPT-4o labels (repeat labeling with varied prompts, compare to human ratings on a small subset) would substantially strengthen the adapter validation.
- Quantitative style erasure evaluation using a style classifier (e.g., fine-tuned CLIP) to report a style removal rate, analogous to NRR.
- Reporting of confidence intervals for the main quantitative results (Figure 4, Tables 1-2) would help assess whether differences between methods are statistically reliable.

## Suggestions

1. **Run a controlled adapter generalization experiment.** Train the adapter on one concept (e.g., "cat"), test it on several unseen concepts ("dog," "airplane," "car"), and report ORR compared to concept-specific adapters and a grid-search optimal scale. This single experiment would either substantiate or bound the generalization claim, and either outcome is informative.

2. **Open the GPT-4o labeling box.** Provide the exact prompt used, show examples of scores for a range of suppression scales, and report consistency across repeated API calls (e.g., standard deviation over 5 runs on the same image). Even a small human agreement study on 50 samples would substantially increase trust in the training signal.

3. **Quantify style erasure.** Report a style removal rate using a classifier (e.g., a CLIP-based style classifier or a fine-tuned artist identification model), analogous to how NRR and ORR are used for NSFW and object tasks. Also measure preservation of non-target artist styles.
