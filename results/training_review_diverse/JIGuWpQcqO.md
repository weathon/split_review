## Summary

This paper proposes RDNet, a reversible decoupling network for single-image reflection removal. The architecture combines a multi-column reversible encoder (inspired by GLOM's part-whole hierarchy) with a transmission-rate-aware prompt generator that estimates per-channel scaling factors to modulate features. The method achieves state-of-the-art PSNR/SSIM across five standard real-world benchmarks, and ablation studies confirm the importance of both the invertible connections (2.6 dB drop when removed) and the prompt generator (1.13 dB drop when removed).

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-validated multi-column reversible encoder.** The paper introduces an architecture that combines reversible cross-level connections with a multi-column design grounded in GLOM's part-whole hierarchy. The ablation study (Table 4, Setting F vs. Ours) provides strong evidence: removing the reversible connections (replacing with U-Net connections) causes a 2.6 dB PSNR drop — a large margin that clearly demonstrates the empirical value of the reversible design. This goes beyond standard ablation and directly supports the core claim.

- **Transmission-rate-aware prompt generator with well-designed ablations.** The TAPG estimates per-channel α,β parameters via a ConvNeXt backbone and uses these to modulate intermediate features rather than naively adjusting the input. The ablation sequence (Settings A→B→C→Ours in Table 4) cleanly disentangles the contributions: removing all transmission-rate information drops PSNR by 1.13 dB; naive input adjustment recovers only 0.47 dB; while the full TAPG yields the full gain. This is a well-executed ablation that convincingly supports the design choice.

- **State-of-the-art results across five real-world benchmarks.** RDNet achieves the best average PSNR in both training settings (25.95 dB without Nature data, 26.65 dB with Nature data) and sets new records on all five datasets (Real20, Objects, Postcard, Wild, Nature). The gains are non-trivial — e.g., +0.55 dB over DSRNet in the w/o Nat. setting and +0.90 dB in the w Nat. setting on average — and the method is particularly strong on the challenging Real20 dataset (+0.20 dB over DSRNet w/o Nat., +1.67 dB over DSRNet w Nat.).

- **Principled problem diagnosis.** The paper clearly identifies two specific limitations of prior work — information loss from hypercolumn compression and fixed interaction patterns in dual-stream networks — and designs reversible cross-scale connections to address both. The motivation is grounded rather than ad-hoc.

- **Strong qualitative generalization.** Results on in-the-wild cases (Fig. 4/5) show RDNet handling dense reflections where competing methods largely fail, demonstrating practical robustness beyond benchmark performance.

### Minor

1. **Terminological overreach on "reversible."** The paper calls its connection "information lossless" and "reversible," drawing an explicit connection to RevNet literature (Gomez et al.). However, the claim is more limited than full RevNet-style invertibility: the reverse operation (Eq. 4) can recover F_j^{i-1} from F_j^i only given additional forward-computed quantities (F_{j-1}^i, F_{j+1}^{i-1}) rather than from the output alone. The mathematical relationship is correct — the connection *is* information-preserving in the sense that F_j^{i-1} can be reconstructed during the forward pass — but the terminology invites expectations of stricter invertibility. The paper would benefit from clarifying that the "reversible" property is a local information-preservation property within the multi-column forward computation, not global network invertibility à la RevNet. This is a clarity issue, not a structural flaw (the architecture works and the ablation confirms its value), and the authors should reframe their claim to avoid overpromising.

2. **Ambiguous evaluation of the prompt generator's standalone performance.** The paper states: "When testing the input image using the six parameters generated by the prompt generator, we can obtain an average PSNR of 24.34 dB across four benchmark datasets... surpassing the previous SOTA method by Dong et al." The procedure for obtaining this number is not clearly described — it is not obvious whether this means simply computing (I−β)/α per channel and comparing to ground truth T, or some other protocol. Moreover, comparing a simple affine-transformed input to Dong et al.'s full network output is an apples-to-oranges comparison. The 24.34 dB figure should either be clearly explained with the exact evaluation protocol stated, or removed in favor of a more interpretable evaluation (e.g., correlation with ground-truth parameters).

3. **Missing architectural details for reproducibility.** Several components are described at a high level without sufficient specificity: the exact architecture of ω (number of layers, kernel sizes, channels), the down/upsampling methods used for θ and δ (learned vs. fixed), the architecture of the Level Decoders, and the precise nature of γ ("learnable reversible channel-wise scaling" — is this a per-channel scalar, a diagonal matrix, or something else?). These details would significantly improve reproducibility. A supplementary table with channel dimensions, layer counts, and activation functions per component would be valuable.

4. **No computational cost analysis.** The paper motivates the reversible encoder partly as memory-efficient (citing the high cost of MAXIM and HRNet) but reports no FLOPs, parameter counts, inference time, or memory usage for RDNet versus competitors. Given that the method uses multiple columns (each with multiple levels), the computational overhead is non-trivial to assess without numbers. The ablation shows Setting D (dual-stream) is close in performance (0.28 dB lower) — a cost-benefit comparison would strengthen the case for the multi-column design.

5. **Limited connection between Information Bottleneck motivation and actual mechanism.** The introduction motivates the method via the Information Bottleneck principle and argues that reversible units prevent semantic loss in deep layers. However, the actual mechanism (multi-scale reversible connections across columns) does not engage with IB theory in a formal or analytical way. The IB framing serves as high-level motivation but is not connected to the architecture through any quantitative or theoretical analysis. This does not harm the paper's empirical contributions, but the framing is somewhat disconnected from the implementation.

6. **No failure cases or limitations discussion.** The conclusion is confident but does not acknowledge any limitations (e.g., sensitivity to the prompt estimator, potential difficulty with non-linear reflections, computational cost, the short 20-epoch training schedule without convergence discussion). A brief limitations paragraph would increase scientific rigor.

### Trivial

- The γ operation is described as "learnable reversible channel-wise scaling" — the mechanism for ensuring invertibility (e.g., how non-zero scaling is enforced) is not specified.
- Level-4 as an "End Level" without cross-level connections is mentioned but not motivated. What role does this level serve?
- The prompt generator's training procedure is described in the "Training Objective" section (Sec. 3.3) rather than in Sec. 3.2 where the prompt generator is introduced, making the description less self-contained.

## Removed Points

- **Critical Issue 1 (reversibility as structural/fatal flaw):** The reviewer claimed the reverse operation is not computable because θ(F_{j-1}^i) and δ(F_{j+1}^{i-1}) are not available. This is incorrect: during the forward pass through column i, F_{j-1}^i (lower level of same column) and F_{j+1}^{i-1} (higher level of previous column, already computed) are both available. The mathematical relationship in Eq. 3⇔Eq. 4 is correct, and the connection genuinely preserves information about F_j^{i-1}. The criticism is based on a misunderstanding of the multi-column architecture. Retained only as a minor clarity/terminology point above.

- **Critical Issue 3 (data contamination/lack of variance):** The concern about Real20 overlapping with training data is unwarranted for anyone familiar with the SIRR literature: the 90 training pairs from Zhang et al. 2018 and the Real20 test set (also from Zhang et al. 2018) are standard, well-known disjoint sets. Similarly, the Nature dataset (20 test samples) and the "200 additional real image pairs" from Li et al. 2020 are separate sources with no overlap. The lack of variance reporting is standard practice in this field and not a meaningful weakness.
