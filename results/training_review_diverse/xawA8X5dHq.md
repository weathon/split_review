## Summary

This paper introduces a novel methodology for evaluating LLMs on medical MCQs by creating entirely fictional medical content (the "Glianorex," a fictitious gland). GPT-4 generates a textbook and 264 four-option MCQs in both English and French. Twelve models (proprietary, open-source, domain-fine-tuned) are evaluated zero-shot. All models score ~67% on average (well above 25% chance), with negligible pairwise effect sizes, and perform worse in French (63.8%) than English (69.5%). The paper argues this uniform above-chance performance on content where models have zero knowledge demonstrates that MCQs primarily measure pattern recognition rather than genuine clinical understanding, calling into question the validity of existing MCQ-based benchmarks.

## Strengths

- **Novel experimental design cleanly isolates pattern recognition from knowledge.** By creating an entirely fictional gland (Glianorex) with no real-world referent, the paper ensures models have zero prior knowledge. This is a direct, clean intervention that prior work (which evaluates LLMs on real benchmarks where prior exposure cannot be ruled out) cannot replicate. The skewed distribution of correct answers (Figure 3) is particularly compelling: most questions are answered correctly by almost all models, directly supporting the pattern-recognition interpretation (lines 35–39, 175–177).

- **Multilingual comparison adds an important second dimension.** Translating the same fictional benchmark into French reveals that all models perform worse (63.8% vs. 69.5%) and the correct-answer distribution becomes more balanced. This provides concrete evidence that models rely on language familiarity rather than deep comprehension — an insight that English-only benchmarks cannot provide (lines 41, 120, Figure 1, Figure 3).

- **Diverse model selection strengthens generality of findings.** The study evaluates 12 models spanning proprietary (GPT-4, GPT-3.5), open-source (Llama-3 8B/70B, Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8x7B, Qwen 1.5 7B/32B/110B, Yi-1.5 9B/34B), and domain-fine-tuned medical models (InternistAI, Meerkat). The uniformity of performance across this range makes it unlikely the result is an artifact of a single architecture or training set (Table 1, Figure 1).

- **Statistical rigor supports the core uniformity claim.** The paper reports Cohen's d (showing most pairwise effect sizes are negligible, e.g., d=0.012 between Yi-1.5-34B and Qwen1.5-110B), p-values, and binomial tests against random guessing. This quantitative foundation strengthens the central argument that MCQs fail to differentiate genuine ability (lines 120, Table 2, Table 3).

- **Explicit discussion of real-world stakes.** While some of the clinical safety discussion goes beyond what the data directly prove, the paper grounds its methodological critique in concrete practical concerns about patient safety and overreliance on benchmark claims, giving the work practical urgency (lines 193–199).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core findings are well-supported by the experimental design and data. The weaknesses below are substantive but addressable and do not threaten the central contribution.

### Minor

- **The GPT-4 confound is acknowledged but inadequately resolved.** Both the textbook and all 264 MCQs were generated by GPT-4, and GPT-4 variants (turbo, 4o, 3.5-turbo) are among the evaluated models. The paper argues in the Limitations (line 218) that "any bias introduced by GPT-4 was likely present in the real-world data" — but this is an unsupported assumption. GPT-4's specific output patterns (phrasing preferences, logical structures) may not generalize to human-authored benchmarks. That said, this concern is substantially mitigated by the fact that *all* models (including non-OpenAI models like Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Yi) perform similarly. If the questions contained exploitable GPT-4-specific artifacts, one would expect GPT models to outperform others, which they do not. The concern therefore weakens the *external validity* claim about real benchmarks but does not undermine the core finding that models use pattern recognition on these questions. The paper would benefit from a more measured treatment of this limitation rather than the unsupported "likely present in real-world data" defense.

- **The transition from fictional benchmark to real-world benchmark critique outruns the evidence.** The paper states that its findings "call into question the effectiveness of current MCQ-based benchmarks" and that "performance claims based on MCQs could misrepresent the actual capabilities of these models." The experiment demonstrates that pattern recognition *can* operate in the absence of knowledge, but it does not quantify how much of real-benchmark performance is attributable to pattern recognition versus genuine knowledge. Real benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA) contain actual medical content that models may have seen during training. The paper's core contribution — showing that MCQs *can* be gamed — is important and well-supported. The leap to "existing benchmarks are misleading" would be stronger if the paper explicitly positioned itself as probing one mechanism (pattern matching) rather than as a critique of MCQs as a whole. Tempering these claims would better match the evidence (lines 187, 195, 228).

- **No human validation of question quality.** No medical professional reviewed the 264 questions for internal coherence, answer unambiguity, or whether the "correct" answer is truly the only correct answer in the context of the textbook. While this doesn't affect the core finding (models still use patterns regardless), it makes it harder to interpret specific failure modes or question-level patterns.

- **Translation quality is not evaluated.** The French questions were produced via GPT-4 one-shot translation with no quality check. Differences between English and French performance (63.8% vs. 69.5%) could partially reflect translation artifacts (unnatural phrasing, errors) rather than genuine language-processing differences. The paper does not assess this (line 41).

- **Knowledge coherence limitation contains a contradictory statement.** The paper writes (line 214): "these potential inconsistencies do not undermine the overall conclusions about the models' performance and does affect the internal validity of the study." The first clause claims inconsistencies don't undermine conclusions; the second says they *do* affect internal validity. These are in tension. The intended meaning is presumably that inconsistencies don't undermine the *conclusions* but do affect *internal validity as a construct*, but the phrasing creates confusion.

### Trivial

- The sample size of 264 questions is acknowledged in Limitations but limits the statistical power for sub-analyses (language splits reduce to ~132 per language). The paper correctly notes this is within an order of magnitude of standard benchmarks.
- The textbook generation process would benefit from slightly more detail (e.g., number of chapters, any consistency verification beyond key-point summaries) for full reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **A controlled probe to directly measure pattern recognition.** A valuable follow-up would add questions where options are scrambled into nonsense or made linguistically indistinguishable. If models drop to chance on such questions, that would cleanly demonstrate pattern use. This would strengthen the paper's chosen direction without changing its nature.
- **Releasing the exact 264 questions** (with appropriate licensing) would facilitate reproduction and extension by other researchers. The current GPT-4 stochasticity means the exact set is not reproducible.
- **A small human baseline** (even non-expert guessers) would further calibrate "high performance," though this is not needed for the paper's core argument (models score well above chance on content they have zero knowledge of, which is sufficient).

A human baseline would not change this comparison. ## Suggestions

1. **Temper the real-benchmark critique** to match the evidence more precisely. Replace "call into question the effectiveness of current MCQ-based benchmarks" with language like "demonstrate that pattern recognition alone can produce above-chance MCQ performance, suggesting that benchmark scores should be interpreted with caution as they may conflate pattern matching with genuine understanding."

2. **Address the GPT-4 confound more honestly** in the Limitations. Instead of claiming GPT-4 biases "were likely present in real-world data" (which is speculative), acknowledge that the confound exists, explain why the uniform performance across *all* model families (not just GPT) mitigates it, and note that this concern primarily affects external validity claims about real benchmarks rather than the core finding.
