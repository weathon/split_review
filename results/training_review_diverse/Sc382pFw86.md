## Summary

This paper proposes a structure-aware domain knowledge injection method (SCPT + SSFT) for LLMs. The key idea is to automatically extract knowledge hierarchies from training corpora (e.g., medical textbooks), condition continual pre-training on the knowledge path (SCPT), and then fine-tune with structure-aware QA pairs that elicit explicit knowledge structure in model outputs (SSFT). Experiments on LongBench and MMedBench show that SCPT+SSFT outperforms standard CPT+SFT baselines, and that using only 0.3% of the MMedC corpus achieves roughly half the gain of the full MMedLM training.

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated two-stage structure-aware training (SCPT + SSFT).** The idea of conditioning language modeling on extracted knowledge hierarchy paths (Equation 1) and then eliciting structure in fine-tuning (SSFT) is a genuine methodological contribution. On LongBench closed-book QA, SCPT+SSFT achieves 31.5% recall vs 24.2% for CPT+SFT (Table 1), demonstrating the benefit of the approach.

- **Dramatic reduction in training data with meaningful retention of performance.** On MMedBench, using only 76M tokens (0.3% of MMedC's 25.5B), the method achieves 63.05 vs 67.30 for full MMedLM training on InternLM2-7B — roughly 51% of the gain (4.46/8.71). This data efficiency claim is well-supported by Table 2.

- **Consistent improvements across model architectures and scales.** The method shows gains on Llama2-7B (+8.78%), Llama2-13B (+6.17%), InternLM2-7B, and Llama3-8B (Tables 2–4), supporting broad applicability.

- **Automatic knowledge structure extraction without human annotation.** The pipeline for extracting hierarchical taxonomies from raw corpora using LLMs (Section 3.1) reduces annotation cost and is validated by downstream results showing structure-awareness helps over no-structure baselines.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The scaling claim (5% data → comparable performance) rests on 3 data points and is unverified.** The paper fits quadratic curves to only 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% of the MMedC corpus (Figure 2) and extrapolates 10× to 5%. No error bars, no fourth data point, and the authors acknowledge they have not verified this prediction. Yet the abstract ("reducing the training costs to 5%") and intro ("potential of comparable performance with only 5% of training costs") present it as a central result. This is over-claiming. The paper would be stronger if this were positioned as a speculative observation rather than a quantitative "scaling law" (Equation 3). The core contribution does not depend on this claim, but it inflates expectations.

- **The "50% improvement" language in the introduction is ambiguous and potentially misleading.** The abstract says "50% of traditional knowledge injection performance" (clear about being a fraction), but the introduction says "we achieve a **50% improvement** in knowledge injection compared to the SOTA MMedLM2" (line 61). A reader would naturally read "50% improvement" as a 1.5× multiplier (relative improvement), not as roughly half the gain. The actual numbers (4.46 vs 8.71 gain) confirm it's about 50% of the gain. This framing appears in a prominent position and should be corrected for precision.

### Minor

- **Ablation analysis is partially confounded.** In Table 4, Base+SFT (10K) achieves 35.91 avg vs SCPT+SSFT (10K) at 35.78 avg — very similar. The English split does show a clear improvement (49.96 vs 44.54), but on average across languages the structure-awareness benefit is not visible without the extra 8K synthetic data (SSFT*). The comparison of SSFT (10K) vs SSFT* (18K) mixes data quantity with structure-awareness. The paper mentions an appendix control with randomly generated SFT pairs (app:sec:abl_sft_synth), which partially addresses this, but the main-text ablation alone does not cleanly isolate the contribution of structure-awareness. This weakens the claim that structure-awareness specifically drives the improvement.

- **No variance or significance reporting for any result.** All tables report single numbers with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple seeds. For a paper making strong efficiency and performance claims, this is a gap — especially on LongBench subsets with small test sizes where a few points' difference may not be significant. While single runs are common in costly LLM training, some measure of stability (e.g., bootstrap over the test set) would strengthen confidence.

- **"Recall" metric for free-form QA on LongBench is cited to prior work but could be more self-contained.** The paper cites zhu2023physics32 for the recall metric and provides F1-score evaluation in the appendix (app:sec:longbench_f1). This is standard practice, but the main text could briefly explain what "recall" means for free-form generated text (token-level recall? longest common subsequence?) to help readers interpret the numbers without looking up an external paper.

- **Inconsistent naming: MMedLM vs MMedLM2.** The abstract and introduction refer to "MMedLM2" while Section 4.2 consistently uses "MMedLM" without explaining whether these are the same or different. This is a minor presentation issue.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation across the reviews is the design tension in the SCPT+SSFT pipeline: SCPT pre-trains the model to generate content conditioned on a knowledge path (where the path is prepended as context with no loss), while SSFT expects the model to independently output the knowledge structure. The paper does train structure recall via Equation 2 (modeling the mindmap probability distribution), so this is not an inconsistency, but it raises an interesting design question about whether a model that can generate content conditional on a structure can also recall that structure without the conditioning prompt. The positive mindmap recall results (Table 4 inset: 0.61 ROUGE-L, 0.87 BERTScore) suggest it can, but the mechanism deserves more analysis.
