## Summary

This paper provides a principled, statistically-motivated approach to learning forward search heuristics. The authors model the learned heuristic as a Truncated Gaussian distribution, where an admissible heuristic (e.g., lmcut) provides the lower bound. They derive a novel loss function from the negative log-likelihood of the truncated Gaussian, arguing via the principle of maximum entropy that this is the correct modeling choice when bounds on the heuristic values are known. Experiments across four classical planning domains with multiple architectures and configurations show that the truncated Gaussian loss yields more accurate heuristics (lower MSE) and better planning performance (coverage and node evaluations) compared to standard MSE-based learning.

## Strengths

- **Principled theoretical foundation via maximum entropy**: The paper provides a clear statistical justification for why a truncated Gaussian is the correct distributional choice when admissible bounds are known (Section 3, citing Dowson 1973). This goes well beyond ad-hoc loss functions and gives a principled *why* for the proposed approach.

- **Novel loss function derived from first principles**: The negative log-likelihood for the Truncated Gaussian (Equation 7) is explicitly derived, with clear explanation of how it differs from MSE (which is the NLL of a Gaussian with fixed σ). This is a concrete, reusable contribution for the heuristic learning community.

- **Consistent empirical superiority**: Table 1 shows that Truncated Gaussian models consistently achieve lower MSE than Gaussian models across all four domains and multiple configurations, often by large margins (e.g., ferry: 141.05→8.63 in learn/none; blocks: 3.26→2.71). Table 2 shows these translate to improved planning coverage and fewer node evaluations in GBFS.

- **Thorough experimental design with meaningful ablations**: The paper tests 3 model architectures (NLM, HGN, linear regression), ablates over learned vs. fixed σ, residual learning vs. none, and clipping — all with 5 random seeds per configuration. The "+clip" baseline cleanly separates the effect of simple prediction clipping from the full truncated Gaussian treatment.

- **Clear articulation of the limitations of existing practices**: The paper explicitly argues why learning from poly-time admissible heuristics as training targets is futile (Section 3) and why MSE implicitly assumes fixed, state-independent uncertainty — a point often overlooked in prior work.

### Minor

- **Limited domain coverage**: The method is evaluated on only four classical planning domains (blocksworld-4ops, ferry, gripper, visitall), all of which are relatively small and unit-cost. While the results are consistent across these domains, the set is narrow enough that it is unclear whether improvements generalize to larger, more challenging domains with non-unit costs or complex action schemas. This is the most significant limitation of the empirical evaluation. (The theoretical contribution stands on its own, but the empirical generality is not yet demonstrated.)

- **Convergence claim is supported by only one figure**: Figure 2 shows a single training curve comparing Gaussian and Truncated Gaussian validation MSE loss, but the caption does not specify the domain or configuration. The paper claims that "TN tends to converge faster during training" (line 625), but this claim is substantiated by a single figure whose provenance is unclear. Quantitative convergence data (e.g., steps to reach a given loss threshold, or curves for all domains/configurations) would strengthen this claim considerably.

- **No statistical significance tests**: The paper reports means and standard deviations over 5 random seeds but conducts no formal significance tests (e.g., paired t-test or Wilcoxon). Given the small number of seeds, claims of superiority on every domain would be stronger with explicit significance testing.

- **Inference-time computational cost not discussed**: The method requires computing lmcut (and FF if residual learning is used) for every state during search. While the paper deliberately avoids runtime metrics, the computational overhead of these symbolic heuristic computations during inference is not acknowledged or discussed. This would help readers understand the practical trade-offs.

- **Only two of four planning configurations shown in Table 2**: Table 2 reports planning results for learn/ff and fixed/none, but omits learn/none and fixed/ff. Since the accuracy table (Table 1) shows all four configurations, having the full set for planning would allow readers to verify that accuracy improvements translate to planning gains in all cases. (The paper likely omitted these for space, but the missing data is noticeable.)

### Trivial

- **Checkpoint selection with validation MSE**: The paper selects checkpoints based on validation MSE for both N and TN models. Since the evaluation metric is MSE, this choice is consistent, but a brief discussion of whether using validation NLL would change results would make the methodology more transparent. (This does not affect the validity of the reported results since both models are treated identically.)
