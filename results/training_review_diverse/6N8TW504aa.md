## Summary

This paper proposes Graphical Multioutput Gaussian Process (GMOGP), a multi-output regression framework that models each output's conditional dependence on other outputs through an attention mechanism. Unlike traditional LMC-based MOGPs that use symmetric covariance matrices, GMOGP learns directed, asymmetric attention coefficients to encode which outputs depend on which others. The model is trained via separate marginal likelihoods per output with shared kernel hyperparameters optimized through multi-objective optimization (weighted sum), enabling a distributed scheme that avoids the O(S³N³) cost of classic MOGPs. Extension to non-Gaussian data (TGMOGP) is achieved via element-wise normalizing flows applied to each output's prior. Experiments on synthetic and real-world data (SARCOS, KUKA) show predictive improvements over baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Attention-based asymmetric dependence modeling.** The scoring function (Eq. 18) learns directed attention weights α_{i,j} ≠ α_{j,i} between outputs, directly encoding conditional dependence in a way that is fundamentally different from the symmetric covariance parametrization of LMC-based methods. Table 3 validates that learned coefficients (e.g., α_{2,4}=0.8978) align with empirical Pearson correlations on real data.

2. **Distributed training via multi-objective optimization.** Decomposing the MOGP objective into S separate marginal likelihoods (Eq. 19) and solving via weighted sum (Eq. 20) is a principled alternative to the single O(S³N³) joint likelihood of classic MOGPs. As Remark 3.2 notes, this requires no block-diagonal approximations, prediction fusion steps, or data segmentation, making distributed implementation straightforward.

3. **Consistent empirical gains across settings.** On synthetic data (Table 1), GMOGP (without transformation) achieves competitive or better RMSE/NLL than five baselines including FICM, GPRN, and CMOGP. On real data (Table 2), TGMOGP with 100 inducing points outperforms V-LMC₁₀₀ and even matches/exceeds full-data models, while requiring less wall-clock time (e.g., 16.6 h vs 18.2 h on SARCOS₂). Gains are consistent across training size variations (Figure 5a).

4. **Flexible non-Gaussian extension.** The TGMOGP variant (Section 3.4) applies element-wise normalizing flows (SAL flow) to each output's prior, enabling modeling of skewed or heavy-tailed data. This is a natural fit for the SARCOS data (negatively-skewed) and is validated by the improved performance of TGMOGP over Gaussian GMOGP.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The joint distribution over outputs is not defined as a coherent DAG.**  
   The standard DAG factorization is ∏ p(v_i | pa_i) over all nodes. The paper's Eq. (14) instead writes  
   p(f_X^{(1)}, …, f_X^{(S)}) = p(f_X^{(i)} | pa_i) ∏_{j∈pa_i} p(f_X^{(j)}),  
   which only correctly represents a graph where one designated node is the child and all others are independent parents. The paper acknowledges "Alternately, we can treat each output as the target node" (line 119) — but doing this for all S outputs produces S different factorizations, not a single consistent joint distribution over all outputs. The model trains S separate marginal likelihoods (Eq. 19), each with a different target and parents.  

   The result is that the "graphical model" framing is imprecise. The framework is better understood as a collection of conditionally-defined predictors (each output modeled given other outputs) rather than a proper Bayesian network over the outputs. While the model still works for prediction (each output has a well-defined marginal likelihood), the paper overclaims the graphical model formalism. **Why it matters:** The mismatch between the claimed probabilistic-graphical-model framing and the actual implementation undermines a core aspect of the paper's positioning. The framework is still useful and novel, but the authors should either define a proper joint DAG (e.g., impose a topological ordering) or reframe the contribution more modestly.

2. **Limited real-data baselines.**  
   On real datasets (Table 2), only two baselines are reported: SGPRN and V-LMC. While these are reasonable scalable MOGP methods, the absence of other recent scalable MOGP approaches (e.g., variational versions of CMOGP, structured kernel interpolation MOGPs, or other variational LMC variants with different inducing-point schemes) makes it hard to assess where GMOGP sits relative to the state of the art. The synthetic experiments include more baselines (FICM, GPRN, CMOGP), but the real-data evaluation is narrower. **Why it matters:** The paper claims to "significantly outperform state-of-the-art MOGP alternatives" — with only two real-data baselines, this claim is only weakly supported.

### Minor

1. **No error bars or multiple trials.**  
   All experiments (Tables 1 and 2) report single numbers without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or indication of multiple independent runs. Given the variability typical of GP training (especially with variational inference), this is a significant gap in reporting rigor. **Why it matters:** Without error bars, we cannot assess whether the reported improvements are statistically significant or within run-to-run variability. This weakens the core empirical claim.

2. **The attention scoring function uses y-values during training.**  
   The paper states (line 168): "In practice, we substitute the observation values ⟨y^{(i)}, y^{(j)}⟩ at the initial learning phase." The scoring function thus incorporates the training labels to help determine which outputs depend on which. While the attention parameters w_{ij}, c_{ij} are still learned through the marginal likelihood objective, using y-values to influence the graph structure is somewhat circular from a Bayesian perspective — the observed labels help determine the model's dependency structure, and then the same labels are used to evaluate fit. This can lead to underestimated uncertainty and potential overfitting of the graph structure. **Why it matters:** It limits the Bayesian/generative interpretation of the model and raises concerns about double-use of data. The practical impact is likely small (this is initialization, not a separate model selection step), but it should be acknowledged and ideally mitigated (e.g., using input-based features or cross-validation).

3. **No ablation study.**  
   The experiments jointly evaluate the attention mechanism, the graphical structure, the multi-objective optimization, and (for TGMOGP) the normalizing flows. There is no ablation isolating the contribution of individual components — e.g., how much does the attention-based parent selection help over a fully-connected graph? How much does the MOO framework help over standard joint optimization? **Why it matters:** Without ablations, it is unclear which design choices drive the reported improvements.

### Trivial

- The complexity of the attention learning step (computing inner products of N-dimensional vectors for all output pairs) is not analyzed — this is an O(S²N) cost that could become relevant for large S.
- The choice of the number of inducing points M is not described, and sensitivity to M is not explored.
- No discussion of how test-time prediction works when parent outputs are not observed (the paper implicitly handles this correctly via training-data-only conditioning, but a clarification would help).

## Novel Insights

The most interesting tension revealed by the reviews is between the paper's "graphical model" framing and its actual behavior: the model defines S separate graphs (one per output as target) rather than a single DAG. This is actually an underexplored design point — most MOGP literature is split between fully joint models (costly) and fully independent models (no information sharing). The GMOGP occupies a middle ground where each output gets its own conditional expert, and information is shared only through attention-weighted parent influences and shared kernel hyperparameters. Whether this "collection of conditionals" approach can be given a proper joint probabilistic foundation (e.g., via composite likelihood or pseudo-likelihood) or whether it should remain an engineering approximation is a useful open question that the paper does not address but the reviews surface.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the model's relationship to a proper graphical model.** Either define a valid topological ordering over outputs that yields a single DAG factorization, or reframe the model as "attention-conditioned output-specific GPs" rather than "graphical MOGP." A composite likelihood perspective could salvage the probabilistic interpretation honestly.

2. **Address the y-data dependency in attention by also running an input-only variant** (e.g., inner product of input features or learned input embeddings) to show that the attention mechanism does not rely on label leakage to work well.

3. **Add error bars** to all experimental tables via multiple random initializations (3–5 runs).

4. **Include at least one more real-data baseline** — e.g., a variational CMOGP or a recent scalable LMC variant — to strengthen the claim of SOTA superiority.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a genuinely novel combination of attention-based asymmetric dependence learning with MOGP, achieves credible (though under-reported) empirical gains, and provides a useful distributed training scheme. However, the imprecise graphical model framing and the lack of statistical rigor in experiments are significant weaknesses that prevent a strong acceptance. The core technical contributions are real, but the paper overstates its formalism and under-delivers on experimental substantiation.
