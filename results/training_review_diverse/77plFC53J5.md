## Summary

This paper identifies the **Feature Overlapping Phenomenon** in Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), observing that feature maps across consecutive time steps are highly redundant due to weight sharing and identical input encoding. To exploit this redundancy, the authors propose **Temporal Differential Decoupling (TDD)**, which decomposes features into static (reusable across time) and dynamic components by operating in a differential domain. They further propose a **Gradient Sensitivity Criterion (GSC)** to identify and retain only the most impactful dynamic features, and integrate these into the **Differential Domain Low-Sparsity Approximation (DDLA)** algorithm. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with ResNet-19 and Spikedriven Transformer report up to 80.9% reduction in spikes per timestep and 57.8% total spike count reduction.

## Strengths

- **Discovery and formalization of the Feature Overlapping Phenomenon.** The paper provides the first explicit definition (Definition 1) and visualization (Figure 1) of feature redundancy across time steps in SNNs. This reframes the efficiency problem from "reduce the number of time steps" to "reduce redundant within-step computation," opening a new axis for SNN optimization.

- **Temporal Differential Decoupling (TDD).** The decomposition of features into static (reusable) and dynamic components via the differential domain (Eq. 4–7, Table 1) is a clean formulation. The reuse of static features directly follows from the weight-sharing structure of SNNs and is theoretically justified by the linearity of the feedforward transformation.

- **Significant spike count reductions are reported and visually documented.** The paper provides concrete reduction numbers (80.9% per timestep, 57.8% total) supported by figures (Figures 4 and 5) showing spike count distribution across time steps for multiple architectures and datasets. These reductions are substantial and suggest meaningful potential for energy savings in event-driven SNN hardware.

- **Theoretical error bounds for the approximation.** Theorems 1 and 2 derive bounds on the approximation error in terms of firing rates, filtering ratio *k*, number of time steps *T*, and weight norms. The bound's dependence on *k* and *T* is intuitive and provides a principled basis for the accuracy-efficiency trade-off claimed by the method.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **No accuracy results are reported anywhere in the paper.** The paper's central claim (Contribution 3, lines 27–28) is that DDLA "ensure[s] accuracy comparable to baseline models." Yet the entire experimental section reports only spike counts and spike reductions. Table 2 is rendered as an unreadable placeholder and its caption refers to "spike number and simulation length T," not accuracy. The text contains zero accuracy numbers — not in the abstract, not in captions, not in the experimental discussion. A method that reduces spikes but collapses accuracy is not a contribution. Without any accuracy evidence, the paper's core claim is empirically unsubstantiated. This alone makes the paper impossible to accept in its current form.

### Major

- **The Gradient Sensitivity Criterion (GSC) derivation exhibits a domain mismatch.** The GSC is derived (Eq. 8–10, Definition 2) using a first-order Taylor expansion and defined on the original-domain output *O*_{*l*,*i*}^*t*. However, the DDLA algorithm applies sensitivity analysis in the *differential* domain (Δ values). The paper never justifies why a criterion derived for *O* carries over to Δ without modification, nor does it re-derive the criterion in terms of Δ. The Taylor expansion also relies on an independence assumption across features (line 157) that is acknowledged as unrealistic but never tested — no ablation or comparison to oracle/random filtering is provided. This makes the theoretical grounding of the core filtering mechanism suspect.

- **The serial execution requirement is acknowledged but its practical impact is unexamined.** The paper states (lines 240–241) that the method requires the network to run step-by-step serially, because the output at time *t* is needed to determine sensitivity at time *t*+1. Standard SNN inference is often parallelized across time for higher throughput; serial execution increases latency and may partially offset the energy savings from reduced spikes. The paper provides no wall-clock time, latency, or energy measurements, nor does it discuss whether the serial constraint can be relaxed. Without this analysis, the net practical efficiency benefit is unclear.

### Minor

- **The theoretical error analysis (Theorems 1–2) is not empirically validated or connected to the experiments.** The theorems derive bounds involving firing rates, covariance terms, and norm bounds, but the experiments never measure these quantities or verify that the bounds are informative. The corollary about increased *T* reducing errors is stated but not tested experimentally. The theory and experiments exist as separate contributions rather than reinforcing each other.

- **No pseudocode or algorithmic listing for DDLA.** Section 5.2 describes the algorithm in prose, but a pseudocode block would clarify the exact flow: how static features are reused, how GSC scores are computed and thresholded, and how the differential features are fed forward. This absence makes the method harder to implement and verify.

- **No experimental comparison to prior SNN efficiency methods.** The paper compares DDLA only against the unmodified TET baseline. Comparisons to methods that dynamically adjust time steps (e.g., SEENN, which is cited in the related work) or other sparsity-inducing techniques are absent. While the paper's within-step approach is conceptually different, some quantitative context for the reported spike reductions would strengthen the evaluation.

### Trivial

## Suggestions

2. **Reconcile the GSC derivation.** Either re-derive the criterion explicitly in the differential domain, or justify why the original-domain derivation carries over. Add an ablation study (oracle, random, magnitude-based filtering) to validate that GSC selects useful features.

4. **Provide a pseudocode listing** for the DDLA forward pass to clarify the computational pathway.

5. **Empirically connect the theory to experiments.** Even a simple check — e.g., does the error bound from Theorem 2 correlate with the observed accuracy loss across different *k* values? — would greatly strengthen the paper.
