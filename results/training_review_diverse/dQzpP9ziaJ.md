## Summary

This paper introduces LongSafetyBench, the first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating the safety of long-context LLMs, comprising 1,203 instances across 10 tasks spanning Fully Harmful, Partially Harmful, and Harmless contexts (average 41,889 words). The authors evaluate 10 long-context models, finding that they generally exhibit safety issues that do not always align with short-context safety performance, that models tend to ignore harmful content in long contexts (demonstrated via a controlled NIAH/HarmfulNIAH experiment), and that supervised fine-tuning on long-context safety data (11k samples) yields substantial and generalizable safety improvements.

## Strengths

1. **First comprehensive benchmark for long-context LLM safety.** The paper introduces 10 structured task categories spanning diverse safety scenarios (illegal activities, misinformation, offensiveness/bias), going well beyond the single-attack methodology of prior work like ManyShotJailbreak. The benchmark provides 1,203 instances averaging 41,889 words (Table 1), with careful manual curation and verification across crime novels, medical textbooks, Wikipedia, and real documents.

2. **Careful option design enabling fine-grained evaluation.** The four-option scheme (Section 4.1) cleanly separates *harm awareness* (HA) from *safe response* (SR), allowing the paper to diagnose whether a model detects harmful content but fails to refuse it. Random shuffling of option labels mitigates positional bias, a methodological strength for multiple-choice evaluation.

3. **Empirical evidence that models tend to ignore harmful content in long contexts.** The controlled NIAH vs. HarmfulNIAH experiment (Section 5.3, Figure 4) on GPT-4-turbo shows that replacing a harmless needle ("eat a sandwich") with a harmful one ("rob a bank") causes the model to claim the information is missing — directly supporting the claim that models overlook harmful content in lengthy texts.

4. **Supervised fine-tuning improves long-context safety and generalizes to held-out tasks.** Training on only four tasks (HarmfulNIAH, CountingCrimes, PoliticallyIncorrect, MedicalQuiz) yields substantial gains on held-out tasks like LeadingQuestion, HarmfulExtraction, and HarmfulTendency (Table 3; e.g., Llama3-8b-chat SR on HarmfulExtraction rises from 0.08 to 0.89). This demonstrates the benchmark can drive practical improvements beyond its own distribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Uncontrolled short-context comparison weakens the "misalignment" finding.** The paper claims that long-context safety performance "does not always align with that in short-context scenarios" (Section 5.2), but this rests on comparing LongSafetyBench results against an entirely different third-party evaluation (EnkryptAI's safety leaderboard) using different tasks, different metrics, and potentially different model versions. For example, the claim that Llama3.1-70b-Instruct performs poorly in short-context but well in long-context cannot be validated without controlling for task format and evaluation protocol. The paper acknowledges this implicitly but draws strong conclusions regardless. A controlled experiment — evaluating models on the same questions *without* the long context — would directly measure the incremental safety challenge of context length. As presented, this finding is suggestive but not rigorously supported. The paper's other contributions (benchmark, NIAH analysis, SFT results) do not depend on this finding, but the over-claiming should be corrected.

### Minor

2. **Table 2 and Table 3 have a column header error.** Column 9 is labeled "DA" (DocAttack) when it should be "PI" (PoliticallyIncorrect); "DA" appears twice in the header row. The underlying data is still interpretable (the values are identical in the HA and SR sections, consistent with PoliticallyIncorrect being a 2-option task where HA=SR), and the caption correctly lists all abbreviations. This is a presentation error that must be fixed, but it does not make the tables "uninterpretable" as the reviewer claims.

3. **ManyShotJailbreak task analysis is under-specified.** The paper states that options were "generated by Qwen2-72b-Instruct following our predefined option construction standards" with manual verification, but does not describe the option structure or provide examples. Since this task is inherently adversarial (it borrows Anthropic's attack method), it is unclear whether the metric measures jailbreak resistance or harm recognition — these could diverge. The paper should clarify how options were constructed and discuss whether this task confounds the two.

4. **NIAH vs. HarmfulNIAH analysis is shown for only one model (GPT-4-turbo).** This is one of the paper's most interesting qualitative findings, but showing it for a single model limits generality. Extending to at least 2-3 more models (e.g., Claude-3.5-sonnet, Llama3.1-70b) would substantially strengthen the claim that this is a general phenomenon.

5. **SFT gains may partly reflect heuristic learning.** The training data and evaluation share the same multiple-choice format. The trained models may learn a heuristic like "choose the safe option" rather than genuinely processing harmful content in long contexts. The generalization to held-out tasks (which also share the multiple-choice format) partially addresses this, but the concern remains. Analyzing attention patterns or probing internal states would strengthen the argument.

6. **Truncation policy impact is not discussed.** When input exceeds a model's context length, the paper truncates from the middle (Section 5.1). For tasks where information is distributed throughout the document (e.g., CountingCrimes with multiple needles), this could affect results. The truncation rate per model and its potential impact should be reported and discussed.
