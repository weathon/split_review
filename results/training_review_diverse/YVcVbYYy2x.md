## Summary

This paper proposes MeCo, a method that trains a linear PCA-based probe on LLM hidden representations to decide when to invoke external tools (or retrieval), using labels generated by GPT-4-turbo. The probe's score is used with per-token thresholds to override the model's own first-token decision. Experiments across Llama-3-8b/70b and Mistral-7b on Metatool and a new benchmark (MeCa) show consistent accuracy improvements over the model's raw output. The paper also unifies adaptive tool use and adaptive RAG under the same trigger mechanism.

## Strengths

- **Consistent accuracy gains across multiple models and settings**: MeCo outperforms Naive and P_Yes baselines across Llama-3-8b, Llama-3-70b, and Mistral-7b on both Metatool and MeCa, with and without context, and before/after SFT (Tables 1, 2). For example, Llama-3-8b on Metatool without context improves from 75.5%→87.5%. The consistency across diverse settings is a genuine empirical strength.

- **Fine-tuning-free, low-overhead mechanism**: MeCo requires only a PCA-based linear probe trained on a small set of hidden representations — no parameter updates to the backbone LLM (Sections 3.1–3.2). This contrasts with fine-tuning approaches that can suffer from dataset quality issues and poor OOD transfer (Section 6.1), and is computationally practical for deployment scenarios.

- **Unified treatment of adaptive tool use and adaptive RAG**: The paper frames RAG as a special case of tool use and applies the same MeCo trigger to both, showing improvements on MeCa-RAG (Table 3). This simplification of the solution space is a conceptually clean contribution.

- **New benchmark (MeCa) with broad coverage**: MeCa provides 7,000 queries across 6 tool-use tasks and a RAG subset, covering provided-tool evaluation, multi-turn interactions, and scenarios where tools are absent (Section 4). This addresses the limited scope of existing benchmarks like Metatool.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The probe does not measure meta-cognition or self-assessment — it predicts an external oracle's label, undermining the paper's core conceptual framing.**  
   The paper defines meta-cognition as *"the model's ability to self-assess its own capabilities and limitations"* (Section 1; Section 3). Yet the training labels are generated by GPT-4-turbo, which decides whether a query *should* require a tool (Section 3.1: *"we employ leading proprietary LLM to generate user queries...and their corresponding responses"*). The probe is therefore a supervised classifier trained to predict GPT-4's judgment from the target model's internal activations. This conflates *"what a stronger model thinks the task requires"* with *"what the target model knows about itself."* The paper never discusses this confound. The practical method (using oracle-supervised probes to improve tool-use decisions) may still work, but the central conceptual contribution — adaptive tool use grounded in self-assessment — is unsupported. This is a framing issue that requires substantial revision (reframing the method and toning down claims), not a minor clarification.

2. **Missing critical baseline: comparison against a simple learned probe on the same representations.**  
   MeCo is compared only to Naive (model's own binary first token) and P_Yes (a soft version of the same). Both are baselines that use the model's generated output, not learned classifiers. The correct attribution experiment compares MeCo (PCA-based RepE probe) against a standard logistic regression or SVM trained on the same hidden states with the same GPT-4 labels. Without this, it is impossible to know whether MeCo's advantage comes from the specific PCA/RepE procedure or simply from having *any* learned classifier on top of the representations. Given that basic probing often outperforms raw model outputs, this gap is substantial.

3. **Figure 3's probe comparison does not support the claim that meta-cognition is a stronger signal.**  
   Figure 3 compares the classification accuracy of the meta-cognition probe (on its own contrastive pairs) against honesty and confidence probes (on their respective contrastive pairs). Since these probes are evaluated on *different tasks with different data*, the comparison does not establish that meta-cognition is a "stronger" or "better" signal — it merely shows that each probe can separate its own training classes, which is expected. The paper should instead compare multiple probes (including a simple logistic regression) on the *same tool-necessity decision task* using actual tool-use accuracy. This weakens the key evidence for the probe's claimed effectiveness.

### Minor

1. **MeCa benchmark lacks validation details.**  
   The human verification step is described in one sentence: *"they underwent a rigorous human review process"* (Section 4). No information is provided about the number of reviewers, their qualifications, inter-annotator agreement, or quality checks. The label distribution (positive vs. negative cases) is not shown — Figure 4 reports only dataset sizes. These omissions reduce confidence in the benchmark as a reliable evaluation resource.

2. **No variance or significance reporting for main results.**  
   Tables 1–2 report point estimates without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Some improvements are small (e.g., Llama-3-70b on Metatool without context: Naive 72.2 vs. MeCo 73.6). Without variance estimates, it is unclear whether these gains are statistically reliable.

3. **Fine-tuning procedure and probe training data size are underspecified.**  
   The SFT models are described as fine-tuned with *"data generated by leading proprietary LLM"* (Section 5) but no details are given about data size, number of training steps, learning rate, or hyperparameters. Similarly, the probe training data is described as *"only a small number of queries"* (Section 3.1) without specifying the actual count. Both omissions affect reproducibility.

4. **Design choices (layer selection, first-token reduction) lack systematic validation.**  
   The decision to use the first token and select a single probe from layers -5 to -2 is justified by experimental observation (Section 3.2), but no ablation is presented showing how results change under alternative choices (e.g., averaging over layers, using the last layer, or aggregating over multiple tokens). The sensitivity of results to these choices is unknown.

5. **Figure 5 is largely expected and does not independently validate the approach.**  
   Figure 5 shows higher meta-cognition scores for correct responses than incorrect ones. Since the probe was trained on GPT-4's correctness labels, this pattern is expected — the probe assigns higher scores to representations associated with labels it was trained to match. This does not constitute independent evidence of meta-cognitive awareness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A failure-case analysis: when does MeCo make wrong decisions? Are there systematic patterns (ambiguous queries, niche topics, multi-tool scenarios)?
- An estimate of computational overhead (added latency from the probe forward pass vs. latency saved by avoiding unnecessary tool calls).
- A baseline using surface-level features (query length, entity count, perplexity) to rule out the possibility that the probe simply captures query difficulty.
- A simple rule-based baseline (e.g., confidence-thresholded Yes-score on a held-out validation set) as a cleaner comparison point.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the method honestly.** Drop the "meta-cognition" / "self-awareness" narrative and describe MeCo as a probe-based tool-use trigger trained on oracle (GPT-4) labels. The practical contribution stands on its own merits.

2. **Add the missing baseline.** Compare MeCo to a simple logistic regression (or any off-the-shelf classifier) trained on the same hidden state representations with the same labels. If MeCo's PCA-based approach outperforms standard probing, that is a meaningful claim. If not, the paper should acknowledge that any learned probe provides the benefit.

3. **Provide validation details for MeCa.** Report the number of human reviewers, inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ or similar), and the label distribution (positive vs. negative) for each task. Add example queries in the appendix.

4. **Report variance.** Include standard deviations or confidence intervals for key results, or at minimum perform a statistical significance test (e.g., McNemar's test) for the main comparisons.

5. **Specify the exact probe training data size** and the SFT hyperparameters in the appendix to improve reproducibility.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a practical method for improving tool-use decisions via oracle-supervised probing of LLM representations, with consistent empirical gains. However, the conceptual framing is misleading (the probe measures oracle agreement, not self-assessment), and the evaluation lacks a critical baseline (comparison to a simple learned probe on the same representations) needed to attribute the gains to the specific RepE approach. The MeCa benchmark is a contribution but is insufficiently validated. Minor reproducibility gaps compound these concerns. With major revisions — particularly reframing and adding the missing baseline — the paper could be acceptable, but in its current form it overreaches on its central conceptual claim and undersupports its key experimental attributions.
