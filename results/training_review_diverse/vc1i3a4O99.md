## Summary

This paper identifies a "frequency bias" problem in existing sparse autoencoder (SAE) explanation methods (TopAct, N2G) — they tend to produce trivial lexical/linguistic patterns rather than semantic, discourse-level explanations. To address this, the authors propose explaining SAE features using a fixed vocabulary set with a mutual-information-based objective that normalizes word activations across all features. They further propose two steering strategies (Amplification and Calibration) that use the explained features to modify LLM behavior during runtime, and demonstrate this pipeline for jailbreak defense on Mistral-7B-Instruct. Empirical results show substantially higher explanation rates (61.3% vs. 25.8% for TopAct) and that the steering approach reduces jailbreak attack success rate (81.6→72.8) with minimal helpfulness degradation and latency overhead.

## Strengths

1. **Identifying and formalizing the frequency bias problem in SAE explanations.** The paper articulates a concrete limitation of existing SAE explanation methods — their tendency to latch onto frequent n-gram patterns rather than capturing semantic content — that prior work observed but did not name or analyze. The theoretical framing (Section 3.1) provides a conceptual vocabulary for this issue, even if the connection to actual SAE features is more analogical than rigorously proven. The observation is further backed by qualitative evidence (Table 1) showing TopAct/N2G explanations dominated by rigid phrases like "used to" or "CSV" while the proposed method produces topic-level summaries like "Emergency response and location tracking."

2. **The mutual-information-based explanation method on a fixed vocabulary set clearly outperforms baselines in explanation rate.** Table 2 shows the proposed method achieves 61.3% overall explanation rate vs. 25.8% (TopAct) and 32.1% (N2G) — a large and clean margin. Even restricted to features activated on the validation set, the gap is substantial (75.6% vs. 46.8%/35.5%). This is the paper's strongest quantitative evidence that the approach mitigates frequency bias in practice.

3. **Demonstrating that discourse-level explanations are practically usable for steering.** The jailbreak defense experiment (Table 3, Figure 3) shows that features selected via the proposed explanations enable effective steering (ASR 81.6→72.8 on Salad-Bench), while features from TopAct/N2G explanations fail to produce safety improvements even with the same steering mechanism. This directly validates the claim that discourse-level explanations matter for downstream control — not just as an interpretability nicety. The method also adds negligible latency (1.1× vs. no defense), which is practically significant compared to perturbation-based defenses (30–80×).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Word embeddings are underspecified, compromising reproducibility.** The core explanation procedure (Equations 4–5, Section 3.2) computes dot products between word embeddings **e**_w and SAE feature vectors **W**_c, but the paper never states which word embeddings are used. The vocabulary is constructed via regex over training data (line 147), but **e**_w could be the LLM's input token embeddings, the SAE decoder weights, or external word vectors — each would produce very different word selections. The SAE features **W**_c live in the LLM's hidden state space (dimension D), so **e**_w must also lie in that space. For a method whose central mechanism is this dot-product-based selection, omitting which embeddings are used makes independent reproduction and fair comparison impossible. This is not a minor omission — it is a structural gap in the method description that needs to be resolved for the paper to be actionable by the community.

### Minor

1. **The theoretical analysis (Section 3.1) is a conceptual analogy, not a rigorous derivation of frequency bias as a property of SAEs trained on LLM representations.** The analysis starts from a topic-model random walk for *text generation*, assumes SAE reconstruction approximately recovers this generative process, and concludes that discourse features are rarer than linguistic features. However, no evidence connects the topic-model discourse vectors to what SAE features actually encode in LLM hidden layers. The paper's key frequency-bias claim is empirically corroborated (Tables 1–2), so this does not invalidate the contribution, but the presentation in Section 3.1 and the contribution list ("Our theoretical analysis identifies a key challenge") oversell what is best described as a motivating conceptual framework rather than a formal analysis of SAE properties.

2. **Explanation evaluation relies entirely on GPT-4o without human validation, and the metric may favor the proposed method.** The paper uses GPT-4o to judge whether explanations are relevant to summaries, following prior SAE work. However, the comparison is asymmetric: baselines provide multi-token spans while the proposed method provides lists of individual words. GPT-4o may find word lists easier to match to summaries than spans, independent of actual semantic quality. The reported gap (12.5% proposed vs. 3.1% explanation rate per feature on activated features) could partly reflect this structural advantage. A human agreement study on a random subset (even 100 feature–explanation pairs) would substantially strengthen this evaluation.

3. **Experiments are limited to one model (Mistral-7B-Instruct), one layer (8th), and one SAE configuration (Top-K, C=2^16).** The layer choice (8/32 layers ≈ 24%) is motivated by proportional scaling from Gemma2-9B, but this does not establish that similar functional roles exist at proportionally equivalent layers across model families. The steering results could be sensitive to layer depth, SAE expansion factor, and activation sparsity K. Testing on at least one additional model and one additional layer would meaningfully strengthen claims of generalizability.

4. **The steering strategies themselves are simple activation engineering operations; the paper's novelty lies in the full pipeline, not the steering mechanism.** The Amplification and Calibration operations (adding scaled projections, replacing with a mean vector) are standard. The paper would benefit from acknowledging this more explicitly and better situating the steering component relative to prior work, to avoid inflating the apparent novelty of the operations themselves.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting insight from the review process is the tension between the paper's two evaluation modes. In the explanation evaluation (Table 2), the method's advantage is measured by a GPT-4o judge that may favor word-list explanations. But in the steering evaluation (Figure 3), the same explanations enable a downstream task (jailbreak defense) where TopAct/N2G explanations provably fail — and this downstream task is a behavioral outcome, not a machine judgment. This asymmetry is telling: even if the GPT-4o-based explanation metric has bias, the steering experiment provides independent, behavioral validation that discourse-level explanations are more practically useful. The paper would benefit from explicitly framing the steering experiment as the stronger form of validation, rather than relying primarily on the GPT-4o annotation metric.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the word embeddings immediately** — state clearly which embeddings **e**_w are, how multi-token words are handled, and release the explanation code. This is the single highest-priority fix.
2. **Add a human validation study** — at least 100 feature–explanation pairs judged by human annotators, with agreement rates, to validate the GPT-4o metric.
3. **Run the steering experiment on a second model** — even a single layer from Llama-3-8B or Gemma-7B would significantly strengthen generalizability.
4. **Tone down the "theoretical analysis" claim** in the contributions — reframe Section 3.1 as conceptual motivation supported by empirical evidence (Tables 1–2), rather than a formal derivation.
5. **Add a limitations paragraph** discussing features that remain unexplained, potential sensitivity to hyperparameters (vocabulary size, K, C), and conditions where the method may fail.
6. **Acknowledge the standard nature of the steering operations** and add a brief comparison to or citation of relevant activation steering literature (without claiming novelty for the operations themselves).

## Score and Decision

This paper identifies a real and useful problem (frequency bias in SAE explanations), proposes a well-motivated solution, and provides empirical evidence that the solution is both more interpretable and practically usable for steering. The explanation rate improvements are large and the steering results (with failed TopAct/N2G controls) make a compelling case that discourse-level explanations matter. However, the underspecification of word embeddings is a genuine barrier to reproducibility that must be addressed, and the limited experimental scope (one model, one layer, no human validation) tempers the strength of the empirical claims. The paper has clear contributions but needs non-trivial revision before it is fully actionable.
