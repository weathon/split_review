## Summary

This paper addresses membership inference attacks by proposing a framework that (1) valuates individual samples' privacy levels (privacy-safe, privacy-vague, privacy-risky) using an ensemble-based overlap-ratio method, (2) introduces the "data breakdown" concept to explain when sample-level differentiation becomes meaningful, and (3) applies CycleGAN to transform samples between privacy domains—making real samples more "privacy-safe" and generated fakes more "privacy-risky"—to occupy model capacity and protect real data. The method is evaluated on CIFAR-100 and TinyImageNet across three architectures and five MIAs.

## Strengths

- **Sample-level privacy valuation**: The paper moves beyond aggregate dataset-level privacy metrics to per-sample categorization, supported by the overlap-ratio metric (Eq. 1) and cross-model consistency evidence (Fig. 3d showing agreement between CE-based and RxL-based rankings). This addresses a gap in prior work that treated all training samples uniformly.

- **Data breakdown concept**: The paper identifies and formalizes "data breakdown"—a regime where model capacity exceeds data complexity, causing near-complete memorization and making per-sample privacy levels indistinguishable. The contrast with the "data outlier effect" is motivated via loss-distribution analysis (Fig. 2), providing a conceptual lens for when generative augmentation is needed.

- **Novel application of CycleGAN to privacy defense**: Applying CycleGAN for domain transformation between privacy-safe and privacy-risky domains is an interesting and genuinely novel idea. The adversarial and cycle-consistency losses (Section 4, Eqs. 1–3) are clearly formulated.

- **Comprehensive evaluation scope**: The method is tested on three architectures (MobileNetV3, ResNet18, HiViT), two datasets (CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet), and five MIAs (Correctness, Confidence, Entropy, M-Entropy, LiRA). The LiRA evaluation (Fig. 8) provides some evidence that the approach pushes the ROC curve toward the random baseline.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Privacy domain transformation mechanism is not directly verified.** The paper never shows that a sample classified as privacy-safe *before* CycleGAN transformation actually becomes privacy-risky *after* transformation (or vice versa) when evaluated by an MIA. The discriminators are trained on domain labels derived from the valuation step, but there is no loss term that ties the generator's output to a measured change in privacy risk, and no analysis (qualitative or quantitative) confirming that the transformation actually shifts a sample's privacy domain. The core claim—that the method works through privacy-domain transformation—rests on an untested assumption.

- **Experimental design does not isolate the effect of domain transformation.** The main comparison (Fig. 7) contrasts "CE + fake data" (generated by Gong et al., 2019) against "CE + fake & transformed data" (CycleGAN-based). Since different generative models are used, the observed privacy benefit could come from (a) adding any additional CycleGAN data, (b) the CycleGAN architecture itself, or (c) the privacy-domain transformation objective. A critical missing control is training with CycleGAN-generated data from *random* domain splits (not privacy-defined) to isolate whether domain labeling drives the benefit.

- **Unfair comparison against baseline defenses.** SELENA and RelaxLoss are training-time defenses, while the proposed method is a data-augmentation technique trained with plain cross-entropy. A fairer comparison would either give SELENA/RelaxLoss access to the same generated data, or apply the proposed data generation on top of SELENA/RelaxLoss as the training method. As presented, the baselines are evaluated without the benefit of additional generated data, making the comparison asymmetric.

- **No statistical significance or variance reported.** Neither Fig. 7 nor Fig. 8 includes error bars, confidence intervals, or any measure of variance across independent runs. This makes it impossible to assess whether the reported privacy improvements are statistically reliable or within the noise of single runs.

### Minor

- **Sample-level valuation details are underspecified.** The voting mechanism for identifying privacy-safe/risky samples (Fig. 4 caption: "the ensemble votes") is not described concretely. The paper does not specify how disagreement among the 5 ensemble models is resolved, how the threshold for labeling a sample as "privacy-safe" is determined from the overlap ranking, or what proportion of samples are assigned to each category (safe/vague/risky). These gaps hinder reproducibility.

- **TinyImageNet failure is acknowledged but not analyzed.** The paper mentions in one sentence that the generator produces poor-quality samples for TinyImageNet, but provides no analysis of *why* the CycleGAN transfer fails on higher-resolution data, no attempt to improve generator capacity or training, and no discussion of whether this indicates a fundamental limitation of the approach or simply a need for a different generator backbone.

- **Overclaimed novelty of observations.** The abstract and introduction state that "existing studies only take into account an overall dataset's privacy," yet the paper itself cites Carlini et al. (2022b), Kaya & Dumitras (2021), and others that examine per-sample memorization. The observations about asymmetry between data complexity and model capacity, while well-motivated, are also substantially grounded in prior work. The paper's novelty lies in the proposed *solution*, not in these observations, but the framing conflates the two.

- **LiRA evaluation lacks error bars or multiple trials.** Only one ROC curve per method is shown (Fig. 8), with no indication of variance. Given the known variance in LiRA evaluations across training runs, single-curve plots weaken the evidence.

### Trivial

- The conclusion states the approach is "significantly capable of defending privacy attack attempts," which overstates the mixed empirical results (e.g., worse performance on correctness-based MIAs).

## Nice-to-Haves

- A direct comparison of the proposed sample-level valuation against per-sample difficulty measures (e.g., influence functions, loss-at-memorization from Carlini et al. 2022b) would help situate the contribution.
- A computational cost analysis (the pipeline requires training multiple models for valuation, then two CycleGANs, then retraining the target model) would be useful for practitioners considering adoption.
- The "data breakdown" concept could be strengthened with a more rigorous criterion (e.g., a threshold based on the divergence between train/test loss distributions) rather than purely qualitative analogy.

## Novel Insights

The most penetrating observation from the reviews is that the paper's central claimed mechanism—privacy-domain transformation via CycleGAN—is never directly verified. The paper presents end-to-end results (privacy improves when domain-transformed data is added) but provides no evidence at the *sample level* that the CycleGAN actually altered a sample's vulnerability to MIAs. The confounded experimental design (different base generators for fake vs. transformed data) further undermines attribution. These two issues together mean the paper's main technical contribution remains an interesting hypothesis rather than a demonstrated result. The sample-level valuation and data breakdown concepts are better supported and could stand as independent contributions, but the generative transformation claim requires substantially more direct evidence.

## Suggestions

1. **Verify the transformation mechanism directly**: Take samples before and after CycleGAN transformation and run them through a separate MIA evaluation pipeline. Show that a privacy-safe sample becomes privacy-risky after G_s2r transformation (and vice versa for G_r2s). This is the minimal experiment to validate the method's name and core claim.

2. **Ablate the experimental setup**: Compare (a) CE + raw fake data (Gong et al.), (b) CE + CycleGAN-generated data from random domain splits (not privacy-defined), and (c) CE + CycleGAN domain-transformed data. This isolates whether the privacy benefit comes from the domain labeling or just adding any CycleGAN data.

3. **Report variance**: Add error bars or confidence intervals to all main results (Figs. 7 and 8), computed over at least 3–5 independent training runs.

4. **Equalize baseline comparisons**: Either give SELENA/RelaxLoss access to the same generated data, or combine the proposed data generation with SELENA/RelaxLoss as the training method.

5. **Specify the valuation mechanism concretely**: Describe the ensemble voting rule (majority? threshold-based?), the exact proportion of samples assigned to each category, and the criterion used to label a sample as privacy-safe vs. privacy-vague vs. privacy-risky.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes an interesting high-level idea—sample-level privacy valuation combined with generative domain transformation—but the execution has significant gaps. The core mechanism is not directly verified, the experimental design cannot isolate whether domain-transformation drives the observed benefit, and the evaluation lacks statistical rigor. These are major rather than fatal weaknesses: the idea is novel, the LiRA results are encouraging, and the sample-level valuation and data-breakdown conceptual contributions have standalone value. However, as presented, the evidence does not convincingly support the paper's central claim. A major revision addressing the mechanism verification, ablation, and experimental fairness is needed.
