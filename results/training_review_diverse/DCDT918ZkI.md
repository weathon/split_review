## Summary

This paper proposes GOOD-AT, an OOD-detection-based defense for GNNs. Instead of relying on hand-crafted graph properties (which adaptive attacks can easily bypass), the method trains an ensemble of binary MLP detectors on adversarial edges generated by PGD attacks. During inference, edges flagged as OOD by any detector are removed before classification. For poisoning attacks, the paper adapts a self-training strategy from prior work. The paper also formulates a trade-off hypothesis: adaptive attacks designed to evade OOD detectors inherently reduce their own effectiveness. Experiments on the adversarial unit test from Mujkanovic et al. (2022) show GOOD-AT achieving higher RAUC than 7 competitive baselines on Cora and Citeseer.

## Strengths

1. **Novel OOD-detection-based defense framework that bypasses dependency on hand-crafted properties.** The paper reframes graph adversarial attacks as an OOD detection problem and trains detectors directly on generated adversarial edges rather than relying on pre-specified graph properties (e.g., homophily, spectral properties). This directly addresses the vulnerability identified by Mujkanovic et al. (2022) — that property-based defenses are easily bypassed by adaptive attacks that incorporate the same properties as constraints. The framework is clearly described in Section 4 and Figure 1, with the detection rule formalized in Eq. (5).

2. **Strong empirical performance under the standard adversarial unit test.** On both Cora and Citeseer, GOOD-AT (evasion) and the self-training variant (poisoning) achieve the highest RAUC among all 7 compared defenses (Figure 3). On Cora, GOOD-AT's RAUC approaches the theoretical upper bound (0.61), and the self-training RAUC is 3–5× higher than competitors. These results are based on 25,000+ perturbed graphs across 5 random data splits, and the low standard deviation indicates stability across splits.

3. **Transferability analysis demonstrates robustness across diverse attack strategies.** In Figure 4, GOOD-AT achieves the best RAUC on 6 out of 7 adaptive attacks from the Mujkanovic unit test when transferring attacks designed for one defense to others. This shows the OOD-based approach generalizes across different attack types rather than overfitting to a single attack distribution.

4. **Ablation on detector count provides practical guidance.** Table 1 shows accuracy against PGD improves steadily from 1 to 15 detectors with diminishing returns beyond that, validating the ensemble design and offering actionable tuning guidance (K ≈ 10–15 is sufficient).

5. **Formulation of the trade-off between attack effectiveness and defensibility (Hypothesis 1).** The paper identifies a principled reason why OOD-oriented defenses may have inherent advantages: attacks that try to evade detection must reduce the distribution shift, which also reduces the attack's own destructive power. This is a genuinely novel conceptual contribution to the graph adversarial attack literature.

1. **The custom adaptive attacks described in Section 6 are described but not empirically evaluated — a significant gap between claims and evidence.** The abstract states the paper "develop[s] adaptive attacks against our methods, revealing a trade-off between graph attack efficacy and defensibility." Section 6 describes two evasion attack strategies (generating perturbations that evade the detectors, and incorporating detector output as a regularization term in PGD) and one poisoning attack strategy (distributing perturbations across the entire graph). Section 6 also claims "we find that both strategies will lead to a notable decrease in attack effectiveness." However, the experiments section (7) evaluates only the Mujkanovic et al. (2022) unit test — attacks designed for *other* defenses — and does not report any experimental results for the attacks described in Section 6. The empirical evaluation of these custom attacks is the cornerstone that the paper promises but does not deliver. This does not invalidate the core contribution (the unit test results still support GOOD-AT's superiority over baselines), but it means the paper's claims about having demonstrated adaptive robustness against attacks *specifically designed to break the OOD detector* are unsupported. The trade-off hypothesis (Hypothesis 1), while insightful, is presented as an empirical finding but has no quantitative validation.

2. **The "theoretical analysis" of adversarial training failure (Section 4) is overstated.** The paper writes "We theoretically show it will lead to the model learning incorrect mapping relationships" and uses the word "theoretically" twice. What follows is a single-paragraph formal observation: under perturbation, a node's ground-truth label distribution may shift (arg max p_d(y|x,S_x) ≠ arg max p_d(y|x,^S_x)), but adversarial training forces the model to predict the original label. This is a valid insight and a reasonable motivation, but it is not a theory — there is no theorem, bound, or proof. For an empirical defense paper this level of motivation is acceptable, but the use of "theoretically" overpromises. The paper's narrative positions this as a key justification, so the overstatement warrants correction.

### Minor

3. **No analysis of the ensemble detector's false positive rate.** The inference rule (Eq. 5) is aggressive: if *any* of the K detectors flags an edge, it is removed. The paper does not report precision/recall on clean edges vs. adversarial edges. The high RAUC suggests catastrophic false positives are not occurring, but analyzing detection quality would clarify *why* the method works and where its failure modes lie (e.g., does the OR rule remove many clean edges that happen to look like adversarial edges? How does the threshold t affect this trade-off?).

4. **No analysis of computational cost.** The method trains K separate detectors, each requiring a PGD attack on the clean graph and MLP training. The paper does not report training time, inference overhead from running all K detectors on all edges, or how this scales to larger graphs.

5. **The self-training defense for poisoning (Section 5) is adapted from prior work (Li et al., 2023) with minor modifications.** The paper is transparent about this ("defense against such attacks is not the focus of this paper"), but it means the claimed contribution on the poisoning side is primarily the OOD reinterpretation, not a novel defense method.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews — and one that the paper itself touches on but does not fully develop — is that the OOD-detection framing creates a fundamentally different adversarial dynamics than property-based defenses. With property-based defenses, the attacker knows the exact criterion being used (e.g., Jaccard similarity threshold) and can directly optimize against it. With an OOD detector trained on attack-generated examples, the attacker faces a moving target: to evade detection, the attacker must make perturbations that look more like clean edges, which are *exactly* the perturbations that are less effective at degrading classification. The paper calls this the trade-off (Hypothesis 1), but the real depth is in how this asymmetry changes the attacker's optimization landscape — the defender can always generate new attack examples and retrain, while the attacker must balance two competing objectives (stealth vs. destructiveness) that are fundamentally in tension by construction of the detection task. This is a genuinely different defense paradigm from the property-based approaches that dominate the graph robustness literature.
