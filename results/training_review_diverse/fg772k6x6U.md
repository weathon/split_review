## Summary
This paper proposes CariNet, a framework that (1) detects deepfakes using a semi-supervised Artifact Attention module trained on human annotations of video artifacts, and (2) generates "Deepfake Caricatures"—amplified versions of artifacts—to help human observers detect fakes. The machine detection model achieves competitive cross-dataset performance (87.4% overall AUC), and a user study shows caricatures improve human detection by up to 43 percentage points relative to unmodified deepfakes.

## Strengths

- **Caricatures dramatically improve human deepfake detection across all tested timepoints.** The user study (Figure 3) shows caricature-aided detection is significantly above standard deepfake detection at every presentation time from 300ms to 5s (Bonferroni-corrected p<0.0083 for each comparison). At 5 seconds, caricatures achieve a 43 percentage point improvement. This effect holds across engagement levels, supporting the practical viability of the approach.

- **The caricature idea is creative and well-motivated.** The connection to human face perception and caricature art (Section 2) provides a principled grounding for the approach. The mechanism—amplifying frame differences weighted by human-informed attention maps—is technically sound and the paper correctly identifies that the attention maps need to suppress natural motion while amplifying artifacts.

- **Competitive cross-dataset detection performance.** CariNet34 achieves the highest overall AUC (87.4%) across CelebDFv2, DFDCp, FaceShifter, and DeeperForensics, outperforming LipForensics (86.8%) and other baselines (Table 1). This validates that the human-guided attention framework builds robust artifact representations.

- **Robustness to unseen perturbations is competitive.** On clean and perturbed FF++ videos (Table 3), CariNet34 matches or exceeds prior methods on 4 of 5 perturbation types, supporting the hypothesis that human-informed attention generalizes to degraded inputs.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The human study lacks controls needed to attribute the effect to *artifact-specific* amplification.** Caricatures amplify frame differences weighted by attention maps, but there is no condition comparing caricatures to *uniform* amplification (same motion magnification without attention guidance) or amplification guided by random attention maps. Without this, we cannot determine whether improvement stems from amplifying *artifacts specifically* or simply from adding any visible distortion. The central mechanistic claim ("amplifying attention to artifacts increases human detection") requires this control.

- **No analysis of false alarm rate on real videos.** Caricatures are generated for both real and fake videos in the study (since participants see both). If caricatures make real videos appear distorted, they could increase false alarms (classifying real videos as fake). The human study only reports detectability of *fake* videos. The effect on real video misclassification is unreported, which is a significant gap for evaluating the practical utility of caricatures as a signaling mechanism.

### Minor

- **The "more than 5%" accuracy boost conflates human supervision with the overall attention mechanism.** The claim (line 29) that human supervision "boosts its accuracy by more than 5%" is computed by comparing the full model (86.04%) against the "no attention mechanism" ablation (80.78%). But this comparison removes ALL attention (self-attention + human modulation). The component specifically attributable to human modulation is the comparison between "w/o modulation" (83.72%) and the full model (86.04%) — a 2.32% gain. The 5% figure includes the benefit of self-attention architecture itself, not just human supervision. The paper should clarify this decomposition.

- **Statistical reporting in the human study is unclear.** The reported F(2,1630)=17.12 for a main effect of condition (caricature vs. standard) has 2 numerator degrees of freedom, but a two-level factor should have df=1. The denominator df=1630 is also unusually round and large, suggesting a pooled error term that may not account for participant-level clustering. While the enormous effect size (43pp at 5s) makes the qualitative finding robust, the reported statistics do not clearly match the described design. The paper defers to the supplement for "full statistical reporting," but the main text should at minimum correctly state the model structure.

- **The caricature mechanism could amplify natural motion as well as artifacts.** Equation 3 amplifies consecutive-frame differences, and the attention map is trained on human artifact annotations. However, the paper provides no evidence that the attention map *suppresses* natural motion regions (head turns, expressions). If the attention map has false positives in natural-motion regions, caricatures would amplify those too, partially explaining the detection improvement as "amplified motion signals unnaturalness" rather than artifact-specific amplification.

- **The "surpassing all previous alternatives" claim (Section 4.2) overstates the case.** CariNet34 trails LipForensics on FaceShifter (93.9% vs. 97.1%) and on blur perturbation (91.8% vs. 96.1%). The paper does add "except for FaceShifter" but the surrounding language remains stronger than the data warrant.

- **No confidence intervals or variance estimates for detection tables (Tables 1–4).** AUC point estimates without error bars make cross-method comparisons uncertain, especially on smaller datasets like CelebDFv2 (590 real videos).

- **Attention map dimensionality is under-specified.** Equation 1 uses element-wise multiplication (W_K x_i) ⊙ A. If A is a 2D spatial map and (W_K x_i) is a 3D tensor (spatial × channels), the broadcasting is not described. This is a minor technical clarification.

### Trivial

- The "boyd2022value" citation for the claim that heatmaps did not increase acceptance rates would benefit from a brief explanation of that study's design and relevance (currently just cited).

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Comparison against existing signaling formats (text, heatmaps).** The motivation argues that text-based indicators and heatmaps have low compliance, yet caricatures are only evaluated against no signal. A direct comparison would strengthen the claim that caricatures are a *better* decision aid.

- **"Machine-only" attention baseline.** The ablation already includes a "w/o modulation" condition (regular self-attention without human maps), which partially addresses this. A more stringent test would train the attention module on a self-supervised artifact-detection objective (without human data) and compare its modulation effect. This would cleanly isolate the value of human supervision.

- **Subjective user experience measures.** Data on whether users find caricatures helpful, trustworthy, or annoying would strengthen the practical argument.

- **Engagement probe design details.** The engagement probes are "standard deepfakes with very large artifacts." Clarifying what "very large" means operationally and whether these probes could unblind participants to the caricature condition would improve replicability.

## Suggestions

1. **Add control conditions to the human study**: uniform amplification (same motion magnification, but with uniform/spatial-average attention maps) and, if feasible, amplification with random attention maps. This would directly test whether *artifact-specific* amplification drives the improvement.
2. **Report false alarm rates** on real videos when shown as caricatures. This is critical for deployment.
3. **Clarify the statistical model** used for the human study: report whether it is a mixed-effects model, the random-effects structure, and verify the numerator df for the condition effect.
4. **Decompose the ">5%" improvement claim** into the component from adding attention architecture vs. the component from human-informed modulation specifically.
5. **Add error bars or confidence intervals** to the detection performance tables (Tables 1–4) for the proposed method.

## Score and Decision

This paper introduces a genuinely creative and well-motivated idea (deepfake caricatures) and provides compelling evidence that the approach improves human detection dramatically. The machine detection results are competitive, and the ablation confirms the value of the human-informed attention module. However, the experimental design lacks the controls necessary to fully support the stated mechanistic claims (artifact-specific amplification), the statistical reporting in the human study is unclear, and the false alarm rate on real videos is unexamined. These are fixable issues that do not invalidate the core empirical finding—caricatures clearly help—but they limit the paper's ability to support its full set of claims. With revisions, this would be a strong paper.
