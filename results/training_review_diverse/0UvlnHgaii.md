## Summary
This paper tackles the problem of Inverse Constraint Learning (ICL) from demonstrations — specifically the fact that ICL is ill-posed because multiple constraints can explain the same expert behavior. Rather than recovering a single constraint as in prior work, the authors propose **ExICL**, which aims to recover a *diverse set of feasible constraints* (constraints under which the expert policy remains optimal). To efficiently verify candidate constraints, they design a **Generative Diffusion Verifier (GDV)** that uses guided denoising to generate optimal trajectories under a candidate constraint without iterative policy updates, then compares those trajectories to expert data. A contrastive regularization term encourages diversity among discovered constraints. Experiments on PointMaze navigation and MuJoCo locomotion tasks show that ExICL achieves lower constraint violation and competitive or higher reward than several ICL and imitation learning baselines, and that its contrastive exploration yields more diverse cost predictions than random baselines.

## Strengths

- **Novel problem formulation.** The paper reframes ICL from recovering a single constraint to recovering a *set of feasible constraints* (Definition 3.1), which directly addresses the well-known ill-posedness of ICL. This is a genuinely different and practically motivated framing — practitioners can select from the feasible set based on deployment needs. The paper explicitly distinguishes this from prior work that focuses on minimum-constraint or single-constraint solutions.

- **Generative Diffusion Verifier (GDV) for efficient constraint verification.** The core technical innovation is using a diffusion model with guided sampling (via the Lagrangian dual of the CRL problem, Equation 10) to generate candidate optimal trajectories under a proposed constraint without iterative policy updates. This is a creative application of diffusion planning to the ICL setting. The efficiency claim is supported empirically: Figure 6 shows ExICL achieves higher reward with fewer data points than baselines.

- **Strategic exploration via contrastive regularization.** The InfoNCE-based exploration objective (Equation 13) explicitly encourages discovered constraints to differ from previously found ones in their predicted feasibility values. The diversity experiments (Figure 4) demonstrate that ExICL produces substantially higher variance in cost predictions for constraint-violating trajectories compared to random-noise or random-initialization baselines, providing concrete evidence that the exploration mechanism works as intended.

- **Broad empirical evaluation.** The paper tests ExICL across 6 environments (3 PointMaze + 3 MuJoCo) covering spatial, dynamic, and kinematic constraints, and compares against 6 baselines (BC, LS-IQ, ICSDICE, OptiDICE-c, SMODICE-c, SMODICE). ExICL consistently achieves lower costs and competitive or higher rewards, demonstrating generalizability across task types.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The Lagrange multiplier λ is treated as a fixed hyperparameter without justification or analysis.** The guided distribution (Equation 10) relies on λ to trade off reward and constraint satisfaction. The paper cites strong duality (Theorem 4.1) to justify the penalty-form transformation, but strong duality only guarantees *existence* of some λ* for the optimization problem — it does not guarantee that a *fixed* λ works for the probabilistic guided sampling used in GDV. The paper never specifies how λ is chosen, whether it is tuned per environment/constraint, or how sensitive results are to its value. Since the verification mechanism is the paper's centerpiece, this missing detail weakens the reproducibility and rigor of the core claim. This is not fatal (empirical results suggest the method works with some reasonable λ), but it is the single most significant gap in the current manuscript.

- **The nominal trajectory dataset \(\mathcal{D}^P\) is critically underspecified.** Equation 11 (noise-robust constraint update) requires \(\mathcal{D}^P\), described only as "nominal trajectory data generated by policy π under CMDP \(\mathcal{M}_c\) (with the estimated cost before update)." It is never stated which policy π generates these trajectories — the behavior policy? The diffusion model outputs? If \(\mathcal{D}^P\) comes from the GDV itself, the resulting self-training loop (constraint model influences generation, which trains the constraint model) is not discussed for potential training instability. If it comes from a separate policy, that policy is not defined. This underspecification is a reproducibility issue.

### Minor

- **The diversity evaluation does not independently verify that discovered constraints are individually feasible.** Section 5.2 shows that different δ values produce different cost predictions (Figure 4), but it does not directly test whether each discovered constraint makes the expert policy optimal (i.e., is *feasible* per Definition 3.1). The control performance evaluation (Section 5.1) only uses the constraint with the largest δ, so we don't know what fraction of the claimed feasible set is genuinely feasible. Showing that costs vary across trajectories is not the same as showing each constraint passes the feasibility test.

- **No discussion of limitations or approximation errors.** The paper has no limitations section. Several potentially important caveats are unaddressed: (a) the GDV guidance is an approximation (classifier guidance with a learned cost model on noisy trajectories), and the paper does not discuss how errors in the cost model compound; (b) the guided diffusion is trained on a mixed dataset (expert + non-expert + violating trajectories) and must steer toward optimal constrained trajectories — the paper does not analyze whether the diffusion model can generalize to this out-of-distribution generation; (c) the method assumes known reward, which limits applicability in settings where reward is also unknown.

- **No analysis of λ sensitivity or hyperparameter δ.** The paper uses λ without discussing its value or sensitivity. The sparsity/diversity parameter δ appears in both the sparsity term and the contrastive term (Equation 13), and it is swept across values in Figure 4 — but there is no guidance on how to choose δ in practice or how performance varies with it.

- **The connection between strong duality and the probabilistic guidance is asserted rather than derived.** The paper moves from the hard-threshold optimality condition (Equation 8: \(p(O|\tau) = e^{\sum r}\) if constraint satisfied, 0 otherwise) to the smooth penalty form (Equation 10: \(p_\theta(\tau)e^{\sum(r-\lambda c + \lambda\epsilon)}\)) by citing strong duality. While this follows common practice in Lagrangian-based RL, the equivalence is between the *optimization objectives*, not between the probabilistic models, and the paper does not discuss the approximation gap introduced by this replacement. The empirical results suggest the approximation is reasonable, but the theoretical reasoning is incompletely spelled out.

### Trivial
- Equation 3 uses \(\pi_{\mathcal{M}_c}\) without explicitly defining it as the optimal policy under the CMDP; this is clear from context but could be made explicit.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most interesting observation emerging from the reviews is the tension between the strong-duality motivation and the practical use of λ as a fixed hyperparameter. The reviewers' disagreement on this point highlights an underappreciated gap in the literature: many papers using Lagrangian dual methods in RL cite strong duality to justify the form of the objective, but then treat λ as a tuned hyperparameter without discussing when the approximation is valid. A systematic study of how far λ can deviate from λ* before the guided generation degrades would be a valuable standalone contribution. The paper's contrastive exploration mechanism also suggests a general principle for inverse problems: when the forward model (here, the diffusion planner) is cheap enough to run multiple times, one can use the divergence between predicted behaviors under different hypothesized explanations as an exploration signal.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify how λ is chosen.** State whether λ is fixed, tuned per environment, or learned. Add an ablation study varying λ and measuring whether generated trajectories actually satisfy the constraint to validate the verification mechanism.
2. **Clarify \(\mathcal{D}^P\).** Specify which policy generates the nominal trajectories. If the GDV outputs serve as \(\mathcal{D}^P\), state this explicitly and discuss potential training dynamics (or lack thereof, if the diffusion model is frozen during constraint updates).
3. **Add a limitations section.** Acknowledge that feasibility verification via guided diffusion is approximate, that the method assumes known reward, and that the exploration mechanism does not guarantee all discovered constraints are feasible.
4. **Strengthen the diversity evaluation.** For at least a few discovered constraints, independently verify feasibility by checking (e.g., via rollouts or planning) that the expert policy is optimal under each. Report the number of distinct feasible constraints discovered.
5. **Include a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis** for λ and δ.
