## Summary

The paper proposes InstructDET, a data-centric pipeline that uses foundation models (LLaVA, LLaMA, CLIP) to automatically generate diverse, human-like instructions for referring object detection (ROD). The resulting InDET dataset contains 3.6M instructions covering single-object descriptions (via global/local prompt pipelines with CLIP filtering) and multi-object commonalities (via semantic clustering and LLaMA summarization). The authors train a DROD model on InDET and report improvements over existing VG methods on both the InDET test set and standard REC benchmarks (RefCOCO/g/+).

## Strengths

- **Novel data-generation pipeline combining two complementary LLM/VLM approaches.** The dual-pipeline design (global prompt via LLaMA for relationship-focused instructions, local prompt via finetuned LLaVA for detail-/reasoning-focused instructions) is principled and well-motivated. The CLIP-based visual-textual verification with a dynamic threshold that discounts global-image descriptions (Eq. 1) is a thoughtful addition to filter hallucinations. (Sec. 3.1–3.3)

- **Comprehensive dataset analysis demonstrating diversity.** The paper establishes a 6-group guideline (G1–G6) covering single-object categories through multi-object commonalities, and quantitatively shows that InDET has longer average instruction length, larger vocabulary (63K vs. ~3K for RefCOCO), lower intra-object semantic similarity (Fig. 2b), and more balanced group distribution than existing REC datasets (Fig. 2c). This convincingly demonstrates that InDET covers a wider range of expression types. (Sec. 4, Fig. 2)

- **Clever shuffled-expression experiment for understanding vs. keyword-matching.** The paper evaluates on word-shuffled InDET test data and shows DROD's AP drops more (8.46 points) than UNINEXT's (5.76), indicating the model has learned to process whole-expression semantics rather than relying on noun spotting. This is a nice diagnostic that goes beyond raw accuracy comparison. (Table 1, lines 176–177)

- **Scalable methodology with practical potential.** The pipeline is explicitly designed to incorporate "any image with object bbxs" and relies on frozen foundation models plus a single finetuned linear layer, making it cheap to apply to new domains. This extensibility is a genuine practical strength. (Sec. 4, Sec. 7)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central claim — that instruction *diversity* drives improvement — is not isolated from data *quantity*.** DROD is trained on O365 + CC + InDET (~3.6M instructions) while the closest competitor UNINEXT is trained on O365 + CC + RefC (~150K human expressions). This is roughly a 24× difference in instruction data. Without a controlled experiment (e.g., training DROD on RefC alone at matched scale, or training UNINEXT on InDET), the improvements on standard REC benchmarks (Table 2) cannot be attributed to diversity specifically rather than simply having more training data. The paper's thesis is about diversity, but the evidence confounds it with quantity. (Table 2, lines 198–199)

- **The InDET test set is generated by the same pipeline used to produce training data, so high AP on it does not demonstrate generalization to natural user instructions.** The test instructions come from the same LLaVA + LLaMA + CLIP distribution as training, making the large gains in Table 1 (62.24 vs. 43.37) expected and uninformative about whether the model would handle real user queries. This would be less concerning if the standard-benchmark comparison (Table 2) were cleanly controlled, but it suffers from the confound above. Together, these two issues mean the paper's experimental design does not cleanly support its core claims.

- **Claims of "surpassing existing methods" (abstract, introduction) are overstated given the uncontrolled comparison.** On RefCOCO+ val and testA, the margin over UNINEXT is only +0.13 and +0.17 AP respectively — negligible differences given the massive data disparity. While the paper is honest in reporting numbers, the narrative framing ("surpasses," "favorable performance") implies a methodological advantage that the experiments do not isolate. (Abstract, Table 2)

### Minor

- **No human evaluation of instruction quality.** The CLIP-based filter may still pass instructions that are plausible but incorrect (e.g., describing a property the object does not possess). CLIP's known biases in fine-grained visual-textual matching are not discussed. A small human rating study on a sample of generated instructions would substantially strengthen confidence in dataset quality. (Sec. 3.3)

- **The phrase "largest real-world REC dataset at present" (line 107) is imprecise.** The images are real-world, but the instructions are synthetically generated by foundation models, not collected from human annotators. "Largest semi-synthetic REC dataset" would be more accurate.

- **No analysis of CLIP filter statistics.** The paper does not report what fraction of generated instructions are filtered out, nor which types of expressions (e.g., color descriptions, relational descriptions) are disproportionately kept or discarded. This makes it hard to assess potential biases in the filtering stage. (Sec. 3.3)

- **Multi-object commonality summaries are not validated.** The DBSCAN clustering + LLaMA summary pipeline (Sec. 3.4) produces abstract descriptions of object groups, but no evidence (qualitative examples or human evaluation) is provided to show these summaries correctly capture the semantics of the cluster. (Sec. 3.4)

- **LLaMA-based group assignment accuracy is unverified.** The 6-group instruction classification (Sec. 4, line 117) uses LLaMA with in-context learning, but no human agreement check or error analysis is reported. The group-distribution plot in Fig. 2(c) therefore rests on an unvalidated automatic labeling step.

- **No error bars or significance tests.** All experiments report single-run AP values without confidence intervals. While the test sets are large enough that intervals would be narrow, their absence makes it harder to assess whether small margins (e.g., +0.13 on RefCOCO+ val) are meaningful. (Tables 1, 2)

- **Training data sizes for compared methods are not reported.** Table 2 lists the data sources (e.g., "O365,CC,RefC") but not the actual number of instructions each model was trained on. Reporting these numbers would help readers evaluate the fairness of the comparison.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation that emerges from the reviews is the tension between the paper's two evaluation tracks. The InDET test set (same-distribution) shows a massive +18.87 AP gain, while the standard REC benchmarks (cross-distribution) show much smaller gains (+0.13 to +2.33) that correlate roughly with the degree of data increase. This pattern suggests the primary benefit of InDET may be scaling the training data volume rather than diversity per se — a hypothesis the paper's current experiments cannot rule out. The shuffled-expression experiment is genuinely clever but stays within the synthetic distribution, so it tests *comprehension of meaning* but not *generalization to real instructions*. The paper would be strengthened by acknowledging this distinction explicitly.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a controlled experiment** training DROD on RefC alone (upsampled to match InDET's instruction count) and compare with DROD trained on InDET. This single addition would resolve the most serious weakness.
2. **Adjust the narrative framing** to acknowledge that the evidence supports InDET as a useful *scaling* resource for ROD, while the diversity-specific benefit requires further isolation.
3. **Add a small human evaluation** (200–500 samples) rating whether generated instructions correctly describe the target object, to validate the CLIP filtering step.
4. **Report CLIP filter retention rates** and analyze what kinds of instructions are disproportionately kept or discarded.
5. **Correct "largest real-world REC dataset"** to "largest semi-synthetic REC dataset" for precision.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a novel and scalable pipeline for generating diverse ROD instructions, and the InDET dataset is a useful resource. However, the experimental evaluation contains a major confound that prevents the paper from supporting its central claim about the role of *diversity*. The model-level results on standard benchmarks, while positive, are modest given the large data advantage, and the within-distribution test set does not demonstrate generalization. The paper's contribution as a data-generation methodology and dataset release is solid, but in its current form the evidence for the stronger claims is not clean. A controlled experiment isolating diversity from quantity could substantially strengthen the paper, but this is missing.
