## Summary

This paper proposes Nira (Noise-Guided Robust Aggregation), a backdoor defense for federated learning that introduces a surrogate dataset of pure Gaussian noise (generated by an untrained StyleGAN) shared across all clients. Clients train on both their local data and the noise data, with a feature distribution alignment loss (domain adaptation) to preserve generalization on real data. The server then evaluates client models on the noise data through a two-step filter (accuracy test + feature distance test) to identify and exclude malicious models. The core idea — obtaining an untainted metric by evaluating on data the attacker cannot manipulate — is conceptually novel and addresses a genuine limitation of existing defenses.

## Strengths

1. **Untainted metric via noise surrogate dataset**: The paper correctly identifies that existing defenses compute metrics (distance, norms, cosine similarity) *using malicious models*, which become biased when attackers are numerous. By evaluating client models on pure noise data that is independent of any client's model, Nira sidesteps the tainted-metric problem entirely. This is a genuine conceptual advance over prior work.

2. **Effectiveness in high-attacker regime (10-client setting)**: Figure 1b-c demonstrates that with 10 clients, Nira maintains attack rates below 1% when up to 70% of clients are malicious, while baselines (Krum, Median, NormClip, RFA) fail catastrophically above 50% attackers. This directly supports the paper's central claim.

3. **Competitive performance in standard 50-client setting (Table 1)**: Across CIFAR-10, FMNIST, and SVHN with up to 12/50 (24%) attackers, Nira consistently achieves the lowest attack rate among all defenses while maintaining high main-task accuracy. The improvement is often substantial (up to 7.82% attack rate reduction vs. second-best).

4. **Incidental benefit even without attackers**: Nira improves total accuracy on benign federations (e.g., 90.88% vs. FedAvg's 90.06% on CIFAR-10 with 0 attackers in Table 1), attributable to reduced client drift from the shared noise dataset. This is a nontrivial secondary benefit.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **High-attacker regime validation is split across inconsistent experimental setups**. The paper's central claim — that Nira excels precisely where other methods break (high attacker ratios) — is supported only by Figure 1b-c, which uses 10 clients with 100% participation per round. The main comparative experiments (Table 1) use a more standard 50-client setup but cap attackers at 12/50 (24%). This means the headline result (attack rate <1% with 70% attackers) and the main comparative evaluation exist in *different experimental configurations* (different client counts, different participation patterns). The paper would be substantially stronger if the main Table 1 experiments included conditions with, e.g., 30–70% attackers in the 50-client setting, so that the claimed advantage can be verified under the same controlled conditions as the baselines.

2. **Feature distribution alignment method is underspecified for reproducibility**. The alignment loss (Eq. 5) uses a generic distance measure $d(\mathcal{D}_k(\phi(x)|y), \mathcal{D}_n(\phi(x)|y))$ but never specifies how this is computed. The paper references the domain adaptation literature (Long et al., 2017; Ganin et al., 2016) for inspiration but does not state which concrete technique is employed — whether Maximum Mean Discrepancy with a particular kernel, a domain classifier with gradient reversal, or something else. The hyperparameter $\lambda$ is introduced but not given. As the alignment term is central to the claim that noise-trained models generalize to real data, this omission prevents reproduction and makes it hard to assess whether the approach is a proper implementation of an existing technique or a novel adaptation.

### Minor

3. **Theorem 3.1 is too vague to provide meaningful support**. The theorem states that training with Eq. 5 "elicits the bounded statistical robustness" but does not specify the bound, its dependence on problem parameters (e.g., the degree of distribution shift, the capacity of the model, the value of $\lambda$), or how this translates into backdoor defense. The definition of "statistical robustness" (expected distance to closest adversarial example) is not directly connected to the filtering task. As stated, the theorem adds little beyond intuition and may overstate the level of formal support.

4. **Communication and computation overhead is acknowledged but unquantified**. Nira requires distributing a noise dataset (2,000 images) to all clients, who must train on it locally, and the server must run two filtering tests on every received model. The paper mentions overhead as a limitation (Section 4.3) but provides no measurements: no bytes-per-round overhead, no extra FLOPs for clients, no server-side filtering time. For a method that modifies the standard FL protocol in a non-trivial way, this gap weakens the practical contribution.

5. **Adaptive attack evaluation is limited to a single scenario**. The tested adaptive attack — where attackers avoid aligning noise with poisoned data — is reasonable, but the paper does not consider other natural variants: e.g., attackers who craft their model updates to specifically maximize accuracy on noise data while preserving the backdoor (a direct evasion of the first filtering step), or attackers who tune their feature distance matrices to match the benign distribution. The claim of adaptive robustness rests on a single data point.

6. **Only FedAvg results are shown in the main evaluation**. The paper claims Nira "can be combined with most FL algorithms" (line 156) and mentions an ablation with FedProx in the extended experiments, but the main results table and all comparative defenses are evaluated only under FedAvg. Showing at least one additional aggregation algorithm in the main results would better support the generality claim.

### Trivial

7. The t-SNE visualization (Figure 4) is illustrative and qualitatively supports the feature-distribution claim, but the observation that "the attacker's feature distribution is more scattered" could be quantified (e.g., within-class feature variance for benign vs. malicious clients).

8. ## Score and Decision

The paper presents a genuinely novel idea — using noise as an untainted evaluation proxy for backdoor defense — and provides evidence that it works across multiple datasets and settings. The strengths are real: the tainted-metric critique is well-founded, the noise dataset approach is clever, and the empirical results are promising. However, the evaluation has a structural gap: the headline high-attacker results (up to 70% attackers) and the main comparative evaluation (50 clients, max 24% attackers) are in different experimental setups, meaning the paper's strongest selling point is not directly validated in the same controlled comparison as the baselines it claims to outperform. Additionally, a core component of the method (the distance measure $d$ for feature alignment) is left underspecified, harming reproducibility. These are addressable issues, but they are not trivial — they affect the believability of the central claim and the ability to build on this work. The paper is a solid borderline paper that would benefit from major revision.
