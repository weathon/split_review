## Summary

This paper proposes Nira (Noise-guided Robust Aggregation), a defense against backdoor attacks in Federated Learning. The key idea is to distribute a surrogate dataset of pure Gaussian noise (generated by an untrained StyleGAN) to all clients, who train on both local data and noise. The server then filters malicious models by evaluating their accuracy and feature consistency **on the noise data** — a metric that cannot be tainted by attackers. A conditional feature distribution alignment loss prevents performance degradation from training on noise. Experiments on CIFAR-10, FMNIST, and SVHN compare against FedAvg, Krum, Multi-Krum, coordinate-wise median, norm clipping, and RFA.

## Strengths

- **Novel approach to the "tainted metric" problem.** The paper correctly identifies that existing defense metrics (distance, median, cosine similarity) are computed using all client models including malicious ones, creating a bias that worsens as attackers grow numerous. Using a shared noise dataset as an independent, attacker-incorruptible reference metric is a conceptually clean and genuinely novel idea (clearly articulated in Section 3.1 and the introduction).

- **Consistent empirical advantage across datasets and attack configurations.** In Table 1, Nira achieves substantially lower attack success rates than all baselines while maintaining competitive main accuracy on CIFAR-10, FMNIST, and SVHN. For example, with 12 attackers out of 50 on CIFAR-10, Nira achieves a 1.36% attack rate versus the next-best method (Krum) at 9.18%. This pattern holds across varying numbers of attackers and all three datasets.

- **Flexible integration with existing FL algorithms.** Nira modifies only the client training objective and adds two filtering steps at the server, making it compatible with FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova (Section 3.4). The paper demonstrates this by coupling Nira with FedProx in ablation experiments.

- **Visualization supports the mechanism.** The t-SNE visualization (Figure 4) shows that Nira's filtering causes backdoored features to become scattered and separable from benign features, while benign-client features remain compact — providing intuitive evidence for why the noise-based filtering works.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The paper's central claim about handling high attacker ratios is not supported by the main experimental setup.** The introduction (line 18) asserts that Nira "keep[s] the attack rate below 1% when the proportion of attackers is less than 70%," and the paper's motivating argument is that existing defenses break when attackers exceed ~50%. Yet the main experiments (Table 1) test only up to **12 attackers out of 50 clients (24%)**. The only evidence for the claimed high-attacker regime is Figure 1b, which uses a different configuration (10 clients, all selected each round) that is not replicated in the main tables. A defense paper whose distinguishing advantage is handling high attacker fractions must evaluate at those fractions. This is an **evidential gap that directly limits the paper's contribution** — the core claim is tested at a regime far below what the paper itself frames as decisive.

- **Threshold selection relies on an unjustified assumption that the server knows benign clients.** Section 3.3 states: "To efficiently select thresholds, we assume that the server can identify a small number of benign clients and simulate the training process" (line 149). If the server already knows some clients are benign, the core difficulty of the defense problem is largely circumvented. The paper provides no protocol for obtaining this knowledge, no analysis of how many known-benign clients are sufficient, and no sensitivity analysis of the thresholds σ₁ and σ₂. While an "interval-based filtering strategy" is mentioned as an alternative, the primary procedure depends on an assumption that undercuts the very problem the paper sets out to solve. This is a **structural limitation** for practical applicability.

- **Results are reported without variance or multiple seeds.** Table 1 presents point estimates for three metrics across multiple attacker configurations and three datasets, but reports **no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or indication of how many independent runs** each entry reflects. Federated learning experiments involve randomness from client selection, data partitioning, attacker sampling, and model initialization. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to assess whether reported improvements (e.g., attack rate reductions of a few percent) are reliable or noise. This is an **evidential gap** that weakens the entire empirical case.

### Minor

- **Theorem 3.1 is too vague to provide meaningful theoretical support.** The theorem states that the proposed feature distribution alignment "elicits the bounded statistical robustness" but does not specify the bound, state its dependence on any parameters (e.g., λ, dataset size), or connect the abstract definition of statistical robustness (Definition 3.1) to the actual objective function in a quantitative way. As presented, the theorem does no analytical work and does not constitute a rigorous theoretical guarantee.

- **The adaptive attack evaluation is narrow.** The paper tests one adaptive variant where attackers only align benign portions of their data with noise. But a more natural informed strategy exists: attackers could train to achieve high accuracy on noise and align backdoored features to noise features of the target class, exactly mimicking benign behavior on the surrogate metric. Testing only one adaptive variant (where Nira's performance does degrade, even if still better than baselines) does not convincingly demonstrate robustness against a truly informed adversary.

- **The noise label distribution problem in Non-IID settings is not discussed.** The paper states (line 107) that "this allows us to generate appropriate noise data with the same label distribution as the real data." But in Non-IID FL, different clients have different label distributions. The noise dataset's label distribution is presumably uniform across classes (one style per class via StyleGAN). The mismatch between a client's local label distribution and the noise's uniform distribution could cause problems, especially under high Non-IID skew. This is not analyzed or discussed.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface an important tension that the paper does not fully address: the threshold-selection problem reveals that the method's independence from attacker metrics is partially illusory. While the noise dataset itself provides an untainted reference, *setting* the thresholds σ₁ and σ₂ still requires either knowledge of benign behavior (circular) or external validation. This means the defense is not fully self-contained in the way the "untainted metric" framing suggests. The paper's strongest regime — high attacker ratios — also happens to be where threshold selection would be hardest, because fewer benign models are available to calibrate against. This interplay between the defense's conceptual advantage and its practical dependency on benign-client knowledge is a deeper issue than the paper acknowledges.

## Suggestions

1. **Run experiments at attacker ratios of 40%, 60%, and 70%** (e.g., 20, 30, 35 out of 50 clients) using the same 50-client, 20-selected-per-round configuration as the main experiments. Without this, the paper's motivating advantage over existing methods is not tested in the regime that matters most.
2. **Replace or better justify the benign-client assumption** for threshold selection. Options include: using a held-out validation set of noise only (no benign clients needed), percentile-based automatic thresholding on the observed accuracy distribution, or a sensitivity analysis showing that results are robust across a wide range of σ₁/σ₂ values.
3. **Add error bars over multiple seeds** (3–5 independent runs with different random partitions, client selections, and model initializations) for all main results.
4. **Design a stronger adaptive attack** where attackers train to achieve high accuracy on noise and align backdoored features to noise features, then honestly report whether Nira filters them.

## Score and Decision

The core idea — using a surrogate noise dataset as an external, attacker-independent reference metric — is genuinely novel and conceptually appealing. The empirical results at the tested configurations (up to 24% attackers) are consistently strong across three datasets. However, the paper makes its central claim about superiority at high attacker ratios (>50%) but does not evaluate that regime in its main setup. The threshold selection procedure relies on knowing benign clients, which is a significant practical limitation. Results lack variance estimates. These are real evidential gaps that prevent the contribution from standing as submitted. The paper points in a promising direction but requires substantially more evidence — particularly in the high-attacker regime and with proper error characterization — before its claims are fully supported.
