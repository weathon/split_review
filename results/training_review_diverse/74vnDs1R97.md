## Summary

This paper conducts a large-scale empirical study (4,800 embeddings, 40 concepts, 4 datasets, 3 models) investigating whether fine-tuned word embeddings that encode visual concepts are transferable across different multimodal models (Stable Diffusion for generation, OWL-v2 for detection, CLIP/DFN for classification). The authors find that these embeddings are model-specific and non-transferable, and discover a "fracturing" phenomenon: performant solutions exist within small $\ell_2$-balls around **any** anchor word, including words completely unrelated to the target concept. They further show that these perturbative embeddings target the final layers of text encoders.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale systematic evaluation across models, tasks, and concepts.** Training 4,800 embeddings across 40 concepts, 4 datasets, and 3 models with 10 randomized trials each provides a solid empirical foundation. The cross-task design (evaluation on all six ordered model pairs) is thorough and gives the paper's claims genuine breadth (Section 4, Figure 3).

- **Discovery and characterization of the "fracturing" phenomenon.** The finding that performant solutions exist near any anchor word—with identical images generated from perturbations of unrelated words (Figure 6/4)—is genuinely interesting and non-obvious. The constrained optimization experiment with $\delta$ thresholds quantifies this: performance saturates at $\delta=0.5$, showing the phenomenon is robust across models (Section 5.1, Figure 5/4).

- **Mechanistic analysis of why embeddings fail to transfer.** The truncation experiment (Figure 5/5) and t-SNE visualizations showing that early-layer activations cluster around the anchor word while only the final layers encode the target concept provide direct evidence for why transferred embeddings fail. This goes beyond merely documenting non-transferability to explaining its mechanism (Section 5.2).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The linear transfer function is used without validation of its accuracy.** The paper defines a linear least-squares map (Eq. 1–2) between embedding spaces using >40,000 shared words, relies on this map to transfer concept embeddings, and concludes they are non-transferable. However, it never reports reconstruction error on held-out common words, nor checks whether the map is accurate in the regions where fine-tuned concept embeddings lie. If the relationship between embedding spaces is substantially nonlinear (plausible given different architectures and training objectives), the poor transfer performance could partially reflect map failure rather than embedding non-transferability. This affects the transfer results in Section 4.3 (Figure 3). **However**, the paper's two other main findings—the fracturing phenomenon (Section 5.1) and the mechanistic analysis of final-layer targeting (Section 5.2)—are **independent** of this transfer function, so the core claims remain partially supported. The paper's claims should be caveated as reflecting linear transfer, or the map should be validated.

### Minor

- **Fracturing analysis lacks quantitative verification of solution distinctness.** The paper claims "identical images are generated" from different anchors (Section 5.1) but only provides anecdotal examples (Figure 6/4) without quantitative similarity metrics (e.g., pairwise cosine similarity between solutions, LPIPS distance between generated images). The claim that solutions are genuinely distinct rather than merely multiple local minima of the same region would be much stronger with quantitative backing.

- **Saturation claim at $\delta=0.5$ may be overstated given error bar widths.** The paper states performance saturates at $\delta=0.5$ (Figure 5/4 caption), but does not report the error bar magnitudes or test whether the performance at $\delta=0.5$ and $\delta=1.0$ are statistically distinguishable. Without this, the saturation claim is qualitative.

- **Limited reporting of optimization failures.** With 4,800 trials, it would be informative to report how many failed to converge or produced degenerate embeddings (e.g., collapsed to the anchor). This affects calibration of the reliability of the optimization procedure.

- **Adversarial analogy noted but not tested.** The paper likens the perturbative embeddings to adversarial examples (Sections 2, 6) but does not test whether they exhibit adversarial properties (e.g., whether they cause the model to ignore the context prompt, or whether they transfer across prompts). A simple ablation (generations with vs. without the optimized token in the prompt) would substantiate the analogy.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a tension that the paper itself does not fully resolve: the fracturing phenomenon is the paper's most novel and robust finding (it requires no cross-model machinery), but the paper packages it as a secondary result supporting the non-transferability claim, when in fact it is the deeper and more interesting result. The fracturing finding—that embedding spaces are so "dense" with performant solutions that any initialization works—raises fundamental questions about what these soft prompts actually learn and whether the training objective is well-posed. The mechanism analysis (final-layer targeting) partially addresses this, but the implication that these embeddings are essentially model-specific adversarial perturbations carved into the embedding space suggests that transferability may be structurally impossible for current architectures, not just empirically difficult. This is a more provocative finding than "embeddings don't transfer well."

## Suggestions

1. **Validate or replace the linear transfer function** before acceptance. Report reconstruction error on held-out shared words. If the linear map performs poorly, either replace it with a learned nonlinear map or clearly scope the paper's claims to "embeddings are not *linearly* transferable via this specific mapping procedure." The fracturing and mechanism findings stand independently regardless.

2. **Add quantitative support for the fracturing claim.** Measure pairwise cosine similarity between embedding solutions from different anchors for the same concept. Report distribution statistics across all 4,800 trials rather than only qualitative examples.

3. **Add statistical tests for the saturation claim** at $\delta=0.5$ vs. $\delta=1.0$ to confirm it is not an artifact of error bar overlap.

4. **Add a simple adversarial test:** compare generations/detections with and without the optimized word in the prompt to verify the embedding actively overrides the context rather than merely complementing it.

The weaknesses are addressable and do not threaten the central claims.
