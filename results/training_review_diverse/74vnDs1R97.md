## Summary

This paper conducts a large-scale empirical study (3 models × 40 concepts × 4 datasets × multiple constraint thresholds = up to 4,800 trained embeddings) to investigate whether soft prompts encoding visual concepts transfer across text-to-image generation (Stable Diffusion 2.1), open-set object detection (OWL-v2), and zero-shot classification (DFN CLIP). The paper reports two main findings: (1) soft prompts are model-specific and largely non-transferable across model–task pairs, and (2) the embedding space is "fractured"—performant solutions exist within an ε-ball of *any* anchor word, including words semantically unrelated to the target concept. The paper also provides a mechanistic explanation: these perturbative solutions target the final layers of the text encoder.

## Strengths

1. **Discovery of fracturing in the embedding space (Section 5, Figure 4).** The finding that performant solutions for arbitrary visual concepts exist within an ε-ball of *any* existing word embedding—including semantically unrelated anchors (e.g., generating a duck from perturbations near "vase" or "candle")—is genuinely novel and practically significant. This is convincingly demonstrated across 3 models, 4 datasets, and 40 concepts, with the observation that performance saturates at δ=0.5, well before the nearest-neighbor boundary.

2. **Mechanistic explanation via layer-wise analysis (Section 5.2, Figure 6).** The truncation experiment (showing that early-layer activations cluster around the anchor word and generate the anchor concept instead of the target) and the TSNE visualizations provide a concrete, testable explanation for why these embeddings fail to transfer. This is the paper's strongest scientific contribution—it goes beyond "it doesn't work" to "here is *why* it doesn't work."

3. **Large-scale, multi-task experimental design.** Training 4,800 embeddings across 3 models, 4 datasets, 4 constraint thresholds, with 95% confidence intervals over 100 randomized trials, is a substantial empirical effort. The unified framework (Table 1) that defines compatible loss functions and metrics for generation, detection, and classification enables systematic benchmarking that did not previously exist.

4. **Robust statistical reporting.** Results are reported with 95% confidence intervals over 100 randomized trials with multiple anchor initializations, ensuring the observed fracturing and non-transferability are not artifacts of single seeds or lucky initializations.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Confound between model change and task change (structural).** Every transfer experiment changes *both* the model and the task simultaneously (e.g., generation on SD → detection on OWL-v2, classification on DFN → generation on SD). The paper's central claim is that embeddings are "model-specific," but the failure to transfer could equally (and perhaps more plausibly) be due to task mismatch—an embedding optimized to maximize CLIP similarity for classification is a poor guide for diffusion denoising. The paper never includes a control that holds task constant while varying only the model (e.g., generation-to-generation between two different diffusion models, or classification-to-classification between two CLIP variants). This confound means the paper's headline claim about *model-specificity* is not supported by the evidence as presented. The results are better characterized as evidence of *cross-task* non-transferability, which is a different (though still interesting) finding.

2. **Linear transfer assumption unvalidated (methodological gap).** The paper restricts the transfer function to a linear least-squares estimator (Equation 2) and concludes that embeddings are non-transferable. But the paper never tests whether a nonlinear mapping (e.g., a shallow MLP or nearest-neighbor lookup) could succeed. If a nonlinear map *did* transfer successfully, the core conclusion would be reversed—the failure would then be about the limitations of linearity, not genuine model-specificity. The paper acknowledges the simplification ("to simplify the investigation") but does not test whether this assumption drives the negative result. Given >40,000 shared words, a nonlinear function could be estimated and tested. The fracturing finding (Section 5) does not depend on this assumption, but the transferability claim (Section 4) does.

### Minor

3. **Generation evaluation metric is a proxy without validation or absolute reporting.** The paper evaluates generation quality via a CLIP-based classifier ("probability that generations contain the target visual concept, measured by OpenAI's pretrained CLIP L-14 model"). This is a reasonable automatic metric but is not validated against human judgment. More importantly, transfer results are reported only as *percentages of in-domain performance* (e.g., "up to 84%") without the absolute metric values in the text. Without knowing whether in-domain accuracy is 90% (making 84% reasonably useful) or 50% (making 42% near chance), the reader cannot assess the practical significance. The figures likely show absolute values, but they should also be stated clearly in the text.

4. **Inconsistency in reported embedding count.** The abstract and introduction state "4,800 new embeddings," while Section 4 states "training 1200 new words." The discrepancy arises because 4,800 includes the constraint-level sweep (δ ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0}) from Section 5, while 1,200 is the unconstrained experiment. This should be clarified explicitly in the abstract to avoid confusion.

5. **Modest concept coverage per dataset.** Ten concepts per dataset (40 total) is a reasonable starting point, but the paper does not discuss whether these 10 were randomly sampled or strategically selected from the available classes, nor whether they are representative of the full dataset. The qualitative case studies (Figure 7) show only 4 concepts from 2 datasets.

### Trivial

6. **Detection loss formulation could be clearer.** The detection loss in Table 1 uses a weighted similarity formulation ($w \in \{-1,1\}$) that is explained in the caption but is non-standard and could be presented more clearly (e.g., as a margin-based ranking loss).

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Same-task control experiment:** Adding an experiment that holds task constant (e.g., generation-to-generation between SD 2.1 and SDXL, or classification-to-classification between OpenCLIP and DFN) would resolve the most serious ambiguity in the paper and dramatically strengthen the model-specificity claim.
- **Nonlinear transfer test:** Testing even a simple nonlinear mapping (e.g., shallow MLP with one hidden layer) on a subset of concepts would either strengthen the non-transferability finding (if it also fails) or reveal that linearity is the bottleneck (if it succeeds).
- **Human evaluation of generation quality:** A small-scale human evaluation (e.g., on 5 concepts) to validate the CLIP-based generation metric would address legitimate concerns about the proxy metric.
- **Absolute metric values in the text:** Reporting the raw in-domain metric values alongside the relative percentages would improve interpretability.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface one key insight: the paper's most robust finding is the **fracturing phenomenon** and its **mechanistic explanation** (layer-wise targeting), not the cross-model transferability claim. The fracturing result is supported by within-model experiments that do not suffer from the model–task confound, and the layer-wise analysis provides a concrete, falsifiable hypothesis about *why* these embeddings fail to transfer. If the authors reframed the paper to foreground fracturing and the layer-wise mechanism (positioning the cross-model transfer experiments as a secondary analysis with acknowledged limitations), the contribution would be stronger and more defensible.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the paper's central claim.** The evidence supports "cross-task soft-prompt non-transferability" more strongly than "model-specificity." Either add same-task cross-model control experiments to support the latter, or adjust the framing to match what is actually tested.
2. **Test a nonlinear transfer function** on a subset of concepts. This is a relatively inexpensive experiment that would either strengthen or refute the main negative result.
3. **Report absolute in-domain metric values** in the text alongside relative percentages so readers can assess practical significance.
4. **Clarify the 1,200 vs. 4,800 distinction** in the abstract.

## Score and Decision

This paper identifies a genuinely interesting phenomenon (fracturing) and provides a novel mechanistic explanation (layer-wise targeting). The empirical scale is impressive and the statistical methodology is sound. However, the central claim about model-specificity is undermined by a structural confound (model and task covary in every transfer experiment) and an unvalidated assumption (linear transfer). These issues are not fatal—the fracturing finding and layer-wise analysis stand independently—but they prevent the paper from fully delivering on its headline contribution. With substantial revision (especially same-task control experiments and nonlinear transfer testing), the paper could become a strong contribution. In its current form, it is a borderline paper with interesting findings but claims that outrun the evidence.
