## Summary

NutriBench introduces the first publicly available benchmark for evaluating LLMs on nutrition estimation from natural-language meal descriptions. The dataset comprises 11,857 human-verified entries derived from real dietary intake surveys (WWEIA and FAO/WHO GIFT, covering 11 countries), annotated with carbohydrates, protein, fat, and calorie labels. The paper evaluates 12 LLMs under four prompting paradigms (Base, CoT, RAG, RAG+CoT), compares them against three professional nutritionists, and simulates the real-world impact of LLM-based carbohydrate estimates on blood glucose management in Type 1 diabetes across 44,800 trials.

## Strengths

- **First publicly available natural-language nutrition benchmark.** The paper correctly identifies and fills a genuine gap — no prior public dataset allowed evaluation of LLMs on this task. The dataset release with a public URL is a concrete contribution. (Abstract; Section 1, line 22; Section 2, line 73)

- **Systematic, multi-dimensional evaluation across 12 models and 4 prompting strategies.** The coverage of open-source (Llama 3/3.1, Gemma 2, Qwen 2), closed-source (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini), and medical-domain (OpenBioLLM) models with Base, CoT, RAG, and RAG+CoT makes this the most thorough assessment of LLM nutrition estimation to date. (Section 4, Section 5)

- **Real-world risk assessment with 44,800 simulations grounded in real patient data.** The glucose simulation uses real anonymized Tidepool parameters (CIR, ISF, basal rates), the FDA-cleared Loop algorithm, and systematically varies starting glucose levels — going well beyond point-estimate accuracy to demonstrate clinical relevance. GPT-4o's estimates achieve highest %TIR (69.88%) and lowest BGRI (17.83%). (Section 7, Table 3)

- **Analysis of performance variation across serving types and cultures.** The comparison of natural vs. metric serving descriptions (Figure 3) reveals systematic biases in model training data, and the cross-country analysis (Figure 4) uncovers meaningful performance disparities that point to fairness concerns. (Section 5.1, Section 5.3)

- **Human-verified data quality.** The verification step corrected LLM hallucinations (e.g., "a whole pizza" → "a piece of pizza"), ensuring the textual descriptions faithfully match the ground-truth nutrition labels. (Section 3.3)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None. No single issue invalidates the paper's core contribution — the dataset fills a genuine gap, the release is public, and the evaluation is extensive.

### Minor

- **GPT-4o-mini as single source of all descriptions (confound not discussed).** All 11,857 meal descriptions were generated by a single LLM (GPT-4o-mini). The paper acknowledges this transparently in Section 3.2 and applies human verification for factual accuracy, but it does not address whether the distribution of phrasing, serving-size expressions, and food-naming conventions systematically favors models similar to GPT-4o-mini. The limitation section (Section 8) only mentions the US-centric imbalance and omits this concern entirely. At minimum, the paper should discuss whether model rankings could reflect language-style compatibility rather than nutrition estimation ability, and ideally validate this with a small human-written hold-out set. The fact that GPT-4o (a different model) tops the rankings somewhat mitigates the concern, but the issue deserves explicit treatment.

- **Nutritionist comparison claim needs tighter framing.** The abstract and introduction state that "LLMs can provide more accurate and faster estimates" than nutritionists. The experimental condition is clearly disclosed (Section 6, line 277: "instructed the participants not to search nutrition apps or the web"), but the headline claim is unqualified. In real practice, nutritionists routinely consult databases and apps. The comparison is valid as a constrained ablation (both sides without external tools), but the paper's framing in the abstract risks overclaiming. This is fixable with more precise language in the abstract and introduction.

- **Evaluation limited to carbohydrates despite broader labeling.** The dataset is annotated with protein, fat, and calories, and the title/abstract position it as a general nutrition benchmark. Yet all experiments — the main figure, prompting ablations, RAG analysis, nutritionist comparison, and risk simulation — focus solely on carbohydrates. Adding even one additional macronutrient (e.g., protein) for the top models would significantly strengthen the claim of general-purpose utility. As it stands, the benchmark's value for non-carbohydrate tasks is unverified.

- **Human verification protocol lacks detail.** The paper reports one author acted as verifier (Section 3.3) and two error types were caught, but no inter-rater reliability, verification protocol, or error-rate statistics (e.g., what fraction of generated descriptions required correction) are provided. For a dataset positioned as a benchmark, this level of reporting is thin.

- **No statistical significance or confidence intervals.** No significance tests or confidence intervals are reported for any of the main results (Acc@7.5, MAE), despite the dataset containing 11,857 samples where even modest differences could be significant. For the nutritionist comparison (72 samples), this is especially important.

- **Data leakage between natural and metric serving versions.** The same 5,532 WWEIA meals appear twice in the dataset — once with natural servings and once with metric servings (Section 3.1, line 102). The paper states "we maintain either natural or metric serving sizes — ensuring there is no mix-up between the two," but if both versions are in the test set simultaneously, a model could benefit from seeing both phrasings of the same meal. The paper should clarify whether natural and metric versions are partitioned or separated during evaluation.

- **Country-level analysis rests on very small samples.** The FAO/WHO GIFT contributions include Ethiopia (22 meals), Sri Lanka (34), and India (18) (Table 1). The cross-country MAE comparison (Figure 4, right) is informative but should acknowledge that these small counts make the disparities vulnerable to sampling noise, especially since they correlate with average carbohydrate content per meal.

- **RAG retrieval effectiveness not independently evaluated.** The RAG pipeline (Section 4) parses meals into food items and retrieves nutrition facts via semantic search, but the paper does not measure retrieval recall or precision independently. Without this, it is unclear whether poor RAG performance stems from retrieval failures or LLM reasoning failures — though the paper does offer a plausible hypothesis (metric-serving mismatch).

- **Limitations section is too narrow.** The single-paragraph limitation (Section 8) only acknowledges the US-centric nature of the dataset. The LLM-generation confound, the single-macronutrient evaluation, and the constrained nature of the nutritionist comparison are not mentioned. Selective disclosure weakens the paper's credibility.

### Trivial

- The random seed for selecting one of five candidate descriptions (Section 3.2) is not specified.
- The asymmetry in serving-style coverage between WWEIA (natural + metric) and FAO/WHO GIFT (natural only) is stated but not explained.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Evaluation on at least one additional macronutrient** (e.g., protein) for the best-performing models would significantly strengthen the claim that NutriBench is a general nutrition benchmark rather than a carbohydrate-specific one.
- **Full prompt templates** for all four prompting strategies should be included in the supplementary material, given LLM sensitivity to prompt wording.
- **A small human-written validation set** (e.g., 200–500 descriptions) from the same dietary data would directly test whether model rankings hold on naturally-produced descriptions, addressing the GPT-4o-mini confound.
- **Recall@k evaluation** for the RAG retrieval step would help diagnose whether poor performance stems from retrieval failures or reasoning failures.

However, the reviews also surface a pattern where the paper's framing consistently outruns its evidence — the "global" claim (93% US), the "nutrition benchmark" claim (only carbohydrates evaluated), and the "outperform nutritionists" claim (both sides denied external tools, which is not real-world practice for professionals). ## Suggestions

1. **Rebalance the limitations section.** Acknowledge the GPT-4o-mini generation source, the single-macronutrient evaluation, and the constrained nature of the nutritionist comparison alongside the existing US-centricity point. This will improve credibility without requiring new experiments.
2. **Qualify the nutritionist comparison in the abstract.** Change "LLMs can provide more accurate and faster estimates" to something like "when both have access only to internal knowledge, LLMs can provide more accurate and faster estimates than nutritionists" — this is factually precise and removes the overclaim concern.

5. **Clarify whether natural and metric versions of the same WWEIA meal are separated during evaluation** to address the potential data leakage concern.
