## Summary

The paper proposes TokenCount, a training-free object counting framework built on SAM. It introduces two novel components: (1) a probabilistic prompt generation stage that iteratively samples prompts from an exemplar-guided probability distribution over the query image, and (2) an output-token-based verification stage that uses SAM's decoder output tokens—rather than conventional image embeddings—with a TS-SS geometric similarity metric to distinguish positive from negative instances. On FSC-147, TokenCount achieves MAE 16.25 (best among SAM-only training-free methods), and on CARPK it achieves MAE 4.68, outperforming all supervised baselines. The approach uses no auxiliary models and processes images at 1.69s per image.

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated use of SAM's output tokens for verification.** Rather than using image-encoder embeddings (as prior SAM-based counting methods do), the paper leverages SAM's decoder output tokens—which integrate semantic and positional information through cross-attention—as a discriminative feature space. This is the paper's most interesting technical insight and is supported by both quantitative evidence (Table 5: output token space achieves 16.25 MAE vs. 16.94 for image embedding space) and qualitative PCA visualization (Figure 5), where positive/negative instances form clearly separable clusters in output-token space but overlap in image-embedding space.

- **Strong benchmark results, particularly on CARPK.** On CARPK, TokenCount's MAE of 4.68 and RMSE of 6.13 surpass every supervised method listed in Table 1. On FSC-147, it achieves the best performance among SAM-only training-free methods (16.25 MAE), beating TFOC (19.95), C-Anything (27.97), and even the training-based Counting-DETR (16.79). These results substantiate the core claim that the proposed two-component design enables competitive training-free counting.

- **Thorough ablation studies.** The paper validates each design choice: the verification metric (Table 3, comparing dot product, cosine, L2, and TS-SS), the iterative sampling parameters including an early-stopping mechanism (Table 4), and the choice of verification space (Table 5, output tokens vs. image embeddings). These ablations provide evidence-backed justification for the architecture and give practitioners a clear picture of the accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs.

- **Training-free efficiency without auxiliary models.** The method uses only SAM (no CLIP, DINO, or other models), achieving 1.69s per image on FSC-147. This is a practical advantage over methods like TFCAC, which achieves slightly better accuracy (12.26 MAE) but requires an additional DINO encoder and uses more prompts, resulting in lower computational efficiency.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **The abstract overstates performance.** The abstract claims TokenCount "outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods." This is directly contradicted by Table 1, where LOCA (10.79), PseCo (13.05), SAFECOUNT (14.32), and BMNet+ (14.62) all achieve lower (better) MAE than TokenCount's 16.25 on FSC-147. The introduction uses more careful phrasing ("outperforms mostly existing training-free methods"), but the abstract's blanket claim is inaccurate and undermines credibility. The paper should precisely state where it does and does not outperform training-based methods.

### Minor

- **Support/query exemplar pipeline is underspecified in equations.** Section 3.1's Equation (1) operates on a single "input image I" without distinguishing between the support image (where the exemplar is defined) and the query image (where counting occurs). The text mentions computing "cosine similarity between the exemplar mask embedding and the query image embedding" (line 160), which implies two images, but the equations use F^I throughout without disambiguation. This creates unnecessary confusion about whether the exemplar feature F^E is extracted from a support image or the query image itself. The method is likely correct—the general approach of matching support exemplar features against the query image is inferable—but the notation needs to explicitly separate the two images for reproducibility.

- **TS-SS improvement over cosine similarity is marginal.** Table 3 shows TS-SS achieves 16.25 MAE vs. 16.48 for cosine similarity—a difference of 0.23. While TS-SS consistently ranks best among the four metrics tested, the gap over the second-best metric (cosine) is small and reported without confidence intervals or multi-run statistics. The claim that TS-SS "overcomes the similarity plateau" (line 340) is qualitatively reasonable given the PCA evidence (Figure 5), but the quantitative case for TS-SS specifically (vs. cosine) as a critical differentiator is thin. The larger gap between dot product (19.58) and the other metrics is more convincing.

- **No direct baseline isolating the probabilistic prompting contribution.** The paper argues (line 88) that deterministic grid/superpixel prompting is limited and that probabilistic exploration is superior, and it discusses uniform grid prompting as a baseline approach in the related work (Section 2.2). However, no experiment compares the full probabilistic method against a uniform grid prompting strategy equipped with the same output-token verification and TS-SS metric. The ablation in Table 4 shows that more prompts improve performance, which validates the iterative mechanism, but does not isolate whether the probabilistic distribution itself is better than a grid with comparable prompt counts. This makes it harder to assess the standalone contribution of the probabilistic component.

### Trivial
- The TS-SS threshold value (300) is stated without discussion of sensitivity or how it was selected, which would be useful for practitioners.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Reporting confidence intervals or standard deviations across multiple runs for the main MAE/RMSE results, particularly in the ablation studies, would help readers assess whether the observed differences (especially the 0.23 MAE gap between TS-SS and cosine) are statistically meaningful.
- A more thorough failure-case analysis beyond the brief mention of "dense distributions of very small instances" (Section 5) would help practitioners understand reliability boundaries.
- A comparison with a uniform-grid baseline (same number of prompts, same verification pipeline) would cleanly isolate the probabilistic prompt generation benefit.

## Novel Insights

The paper's key insight—that SAM's decoder output tokens, which fuse semantic and positional information through bidirectional cross-attention, form a more discriminative space for instance verification than the image encoder's embeddings—is genuinely novel within the object counting literature. Prior SAM-based counting methods (TFOC, TFCounter, TFCAC) all operate in the image embedding space for similarity comparison. The demonstration that output tokens produce more separable positive/negative clusters (Figure 5) and yield better counting accuracy (Table 5) opens an interesting direction: decoder-internal representations of foundation models may be generally underutilized for downstream verification tasks beyond the specific outputs they were designed to produce.

## Suggestions
- Revise the abstract to accurately reflect the performance landscape: TokenCount is state-of-the-art among SAM-only training-free methods on FSC-147 and outperforms all supervised methods on CARPK, but several training-based methods (LOCA, PseCo, SAFECOUNT, BMNet+) achieve lower MAE on FSC-147.
- In Section 3.1, explicitly separate support and query images in the notation (e.g., I_support and I_query) and clarify that F^E is extracted from the support image, while the probability distribution P(I; e) is computed over the query image.
- Consider reporting standard deviations or bootstrap confidence intervals for the ablation tables to contextualize small-margin results.
- Discuss the sensitivity of the TS-SS threshold (300) and how it was chosen.
