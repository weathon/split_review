## Merged Review

### Summary

DNNs can break the curse of dimensionality when learning functions that decompose into compositions of smooth components. The paper introduces Accordion Networks (AccNets) — structured as compositions of shallow subnetworks — and derives generalization bounds combining the F₁-norm (Barron norm) and per-layer Lipschitz constants via a covering number argument. The global minimizer of the regularized loss can learn a target \(f^* = h \circ g\) from few observations if \(g\) is smooth/dimension-reducing (e.g., the modulo map of symmetries) even when \(h\) has low regularity. Regularity is measured by Sobolev norms with varying differentiability levels. Empirical scaling laws show phase transitions depending on whether \(g\) or \(h\) is harder to learn, as predicted by theory.

### Strengths

- **Novel framework**: Using AccNets to model compositions and deriving generalization bounds based on the F₁-norm and per-layer Lipschitz constants is a novel, insightful contribution (R1, R2, R4). The F₁-norm captures the minimal norm of a network representation, which is original compared to prior work (R2).
- **Thorough theoretical analysis**: Mathematics are correct and proofs are detailed (R1, R3, R4). The covering number argument (proving a covering number result for shallow networks and chaining them) is elegant, reduces the problem to shallow cases, and yields clean proofs (R4). The decomposition into smooth components is novel (R3).
- **Well-organized presentation**: The paper is clearly written, well-structured, and easy to follow (R1, R2, R4). The story is coherent, with useful pedagogical sections (e.g., Sec. 1.1, Sec. 2, Sec. 4.1.1) (R2).
- **Solid empirical support**: Experiments show meaningful phase transitions and align with theory (R1). Results improve on previous bounds for compositional functions, which is a reasonable assumption (R4).
- **Reasonable focus on generalization**: The assumption that the trained network attains its global minimum is strong but necessary for deep networks; making it allows proving interesting results about generalization and decomposition (R3). Most reviewers find this a fair trade-off (R1, R3, R4).

A minority reviewer (R2, high confidence) is more critical of the experimental validation and comparisons, while another reviewer (R3) is especially positive about the theoretical correctness and novelty. The majority (R1, R4) rate the contribution as solid but acknowledge the listed weaknesses.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of comparison with related bounds**: The bound in Theorem 2 resembles those of Bartlett et al. (2017) and Golowich et al. (2017). It is unclear when the new bound is better, what the trade-offs are, and how it compares to extensions for compositional architectures (e.g., Ledent 2019, Graf 2022, Truong 2022, Galanti 2023). Direct numerical or theoretical comparisons are missing (R2, high confidence). Additionally, the paper does not contrast with bounds that are relatively tight on standard datasets (Dziugaite 2017, Zhou 2019, Lotfi 2022).
- **Experimental shortcomings**:
  - Experiments use only small-scale models (basic FCNN on MNIST, WESAD); applicability to large-scale, real-world networks is unclear (R1, R2). More sophisticated architectures and comparisons to existing bounds in the same setting are needed (R2).
  - Figure 1 (experiments related to Theorem 2) only shows scaling laws w.r.t. dataset size \(N\), while theory focuses on dependence on regularity of intermediate layers vs global regularity (R4). The figure’s bound seems loose and not strongly correlated with test performance (R2).
  - Much of the experimental setup is confined to the appendix, and details are insufficient: e.g., meaning of dashed lines, calculation of the generalization bound (including constants?), whether the square root of the loss is used, training loss, computation of \(R(\theta)\) and the F₁-norm (R2).
- **Theoretical concerns**:
  - Theorem 4 appears to depend only on the largest ratio \(r_{\max}\) and not on the number of layers \(L\). It seems possible to reduce \(r_{\max}\) by increasing \(L\) and rescaling, which might make the bound vacuous. The current statement may not be entirely correct; the proof of Theorem 5 (Appendix Theorem 12) does not resolve the confusion (line 1343 has a summation over \(L\) terms that vanishes awkwardly) (R4). Clarification is needed.
  - The paper relies on smoothness/regularity assumptions (e.g., Sobolev norms) and does not sufficiently address how deviations affect performance (R1). Examples of functions that belong to different classes (Lipschitz vs Sobolev, practical compositions) are missing, making it hard to assess practical relevance (R3).
- **Applicability and guidelines**:
  - Clear guidelines for when to use each proposed regularization method are lacking; the two methods are difficult to optimize and may not be straightforward to apply (R1). Alternative methods to control Lipschitz constants that could simplify optimization are not discussed (R1).
  - The assumption that the trained network attains its global minimum, while reasonable for theory, is strong for real-world training where local minima are common. Its implications are not discussed thoroughly (R3).
- **Unaddressed questions that overlap with weaknesses**:
  - How sensitive are the theoretical results to violations of regularity assumptions? (R1)
  - Is there evidence that target functions decomposable into low-dimensional components are prevalent in practice? (R3)
  - Are inductive biases for gradient descent known to yield bounded F₁-norm solutions in deeper models? (R3)
  - Do the bounds apply when intermediate functions are high-dimensional but have low intrinsic dimensionality (e.g., single-index models)? (R3)
  - The remark on line 256 about randomly initialized weights contributing much to the parameter norm is unclear (R4). How does this compare to small initialization in practice?
- **Presentation issues**: Failure probability denoted by \(\delta\) in some bounds and \(p\) in others; empirical loss referred to as \(\tilde{L}_N\) and \(\hat{L}_N\); lines 150-151 should clarify dimensions of \(W\) and \(V\); line 196 typo "covering number number" (R4). Add parentheses to the complexity measure on line 70 to indicate that the product applies only to Lipschitz terms (R3). In Theorem 4, \(r^*\) and \(r_{\max}\) appear to be confused; this needs clarification (R3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R2, high confidence, score 5) is particularly critical, emphasizing insufficient comparison with existing bounds, unclear experiments, and lack of tightness evaluation. Another reviewer (R3, score 8) is more positive, viewing the mathematics as correct and the decomposition as novel, and finds the focus on generalization over optimization reasonable. The remaining reviewers (R1, R4, scores 6) rate the paper as solid but agree with most of the weaknesses listed above.
