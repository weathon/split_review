## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper tackles task-id prediction in Class Incremental Learning (CIL) by proposing TPLR (Task-id Prediction based on Likelihood Ratio). Instead of using a traditional out-of-distribution (OOD) detector, TPLR leverages replay data to estimate the distribution of non-target tasks, enabling a more principled likelihood-ratio based score for task-id prediction. The method is integrated with Hard Attention to the Task (HAT) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. Extensive experiments on CIFAR and Tiny-ImageNet show that TPLR outperforms many replay-based and non-replay-based CIL baselines.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation** – The paper clearly argues why a traditional OOD detector is suboptimal for task-id prediction in CIL and proposes a principled alternative that exploits replay data and learned tasks (Reviewers 1, 4).  
- **Simple and well-explained method** – The likelihood ratio approach is justified theoretically (Section 3, 4) and presented in an understandable manner (Reviewers 1, 4).  
- **Novelty and originality** – TPLR uniquely applies likelihood ratios for task-id prediction, distinctively diverging from previous OOD-based methods; it creatively uses replay data to estimate complement-task distributions (Reviewer 3). The integration with HAT is an inventive combination (Reviewer 3).  
- **Strong empirical results** – TPLR outperforms a large array of baselines (17 methods including 11 replay-based and 6 non-replay-based) on CIFAR and Tiny-ImageNet (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The ablation study validates each component (Reviewer 2).  
- **Robust to class imbalance** – Since TPLR uses task-wise classifiers, it is more robust to imbalance caused by large differences between replay buffer size and training data (Reviewer 2).  
- **High-quality theoretical foundation** – The method is underpinned by a strong theoretical framework and the proofs in Sections 3 and 4 are sound (Reviewers 3, 4).  
- **Potential significance** – The approach may influence future research in CIL and broader continual learning (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Presentation clarity** – The paper frequently uses abbreviations and jargon, especially in the introduction, making it hard to follow (Reviewer 4). The organization is overly complex; components (likelihood ratio estimation, logit-based score, task-id probability conversion) are introduced without a clear sequential explanation, and the integration with HAT is not sufficiently disentangled (Reviewer 3). The figure is noisy and should be reorganized to read left-to-right, top-to-bottom (Reviewer 3).  
- **Incomplete related work coverage** – The paper does not clarify whether it uses a memory buffer (rehearsal-based) or is memory-free (online CIL) in the introduction and related works (Reviewer 4). It leaves out memory-free CIL approaches and recent balanced CIL works such as SS-IL and TKIL (Reviewer 4).  
- **Ambiguous comparison in Table 1** – It is unclear whether HAT (which requires task labels at inference) is evaluated with task labels or with a heuristic. If task labels are used, the comparison is unfair; the paper should explicitly state the inference protocol for HAT (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing baselines and biased comparisons** – Since TPLR requires a replay buffer, comparisons should focus on replay-based CIL methods, especially those using task-id prediction, to clearly demonstrate advantages (Reviewer 4). The baseline AFC is missing (Reviewer 4).  
- **Overstated claim about “infinite” universal set** – The statement that the universal set of classes is “at least very large if not infinite” is unsupported; Biederman (1987) suggests 10k–30k visual categories humans can recognize, implying estimating the distribution may be feasible (Reviewer 1).  
- **Method relies heavily on existing techniques** – TPLR is largely an extension of prior work (replay buffer, pretrained models, forward pass per task). The limitations (memory overhead, reliance on pretrained models, computational cost) are not adequately discussed in the main text (Reviewer 1).  
- **Scalability and computational overhead** – Performing a forward pass for each learned task is a significant limitation for long sequences. Running time comparisons in Table 9 only cover four methods; a comprehensive comparison across all baselines is missing and should be in the main manuscript (Reviewer 1). Parallel computing does not eliminate the higher cost (Reviewer 1).  
- **Dependence on pretrained models** – Performance without a pretrained model (e.g., trained from scratch) is not evaluated. If the pretraining dataset (like ImageNet-1K) contains classes similar to those in the target tasks, task prediction becomes more difficult; experiments with randomly selected subsets of ImageNet-1K are needed (Reviewer 2).  
- **HAT only mitigates forgetting** – HAT does not guarantee no forgetting; the binary mask approximation via a large sigmoid introduces a hyperparameter (temperature) that requires tuning. The paper should discuss sensitivity to this parameter and quantify residual forgetting (Reviewer 1). Methods that guarantee no forgetting (e.g., PNN, PackNet, Ternary Masks) might be more suitable for TPLR (Reviewer 1). Capacity limitations of HAT as the number of tasks grows are not addressed (Reviewer 1).  
- **Task-prediction accuracy under semantic similarity** – When classes across tasks are semantically similar, task-prediction accuracy may drop, hurting overall performance. This scenario is not analyzed (Reviewer 2).  
- **Unclear inference procedure** – It is ambiguous whether the model predicts task-id and class-id simultaneously or sequentially, and whether any fine-tuning step is involved (e.g., like iTAML). The computational load of integrating likelihood ratio predictions should be clarified, and the benefits and drawbacks discussed (Reviewer 4).  
- **Lack of visualizations** – The paper would benefit from visualizing data distributions (e.g., the feature-based likelihood ratio score in Figure 1) to provide strong evidence that TPLR works (Reviewer 4).  

*Minority contrast: While Reviewers 1, 2, and 4 see TPLR as a limited extension of existing work with significant overhead, Reviewer 3 (score 8) regards the originality, theoretical quality, and experimental validation as strong contributions.*
