## Merged Review

### Summary
This theoretical paper studies a simplified single-layer transformer trained on data with two components: an easy-to-fit component (P, analogized to syntax) and a hard-to-fit component (Q, analogized to semantics). Using a specific learning rate schedule (constant then annealing), the authors prove that the model first learns P and then Q. Empirical results on Counterfact and HotpotQA datasets and spectral analysis of attention weights are presented. Reviewer 1 (score 6, confidence 2) finds the theoretical approach impressive but notes confusions; reviewers 2, 3, 4 (score 3, confidence 2, 5, 2 respectively) raise significant concerns about proof validity, unjustified mapping to syntax/semantics, weak empirical evidence, and unrealistic assumptions.

### Strengths
- Provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of training dynamics and finite-time convergence for a single-layer transformer with in-context learning for classification (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- Addresses a phenomenon—staged progression in learning—that has lacked formal theoretical backing (Reviewer 1).
- Derives an interesting spectral property of attention weights as a corollary (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is well-written, with thorough related work, clear remarks, and detailed proofs (Reviewer 3).
- Attempts to connect theoretical insights to real-world language model behavior, which is valuable given the scarcity of such connections (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unjustified mapping of P/Q to syntax/semantics** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4): The paper assumes without justification that easy-to-fit features correspond to syntactic information and hard-to-fit features to semantic information. This alignment is the crux of the paper but remains unsupported; syntactic abilities in real language involve much more than category prediction, and semantic knowledge is not limited to factual/world knowledge. Prior work (e.g., SyntaxGym, BLiMP, Language Model Behavior survey Section 3.3, Belrose et al. 2024) already studies syntactic learning early in training and is not engaged. Reviewer 1 explicitly discourages using the terms syntax/semantics as misleading.

- **Significant error in proof of Theorem 2** (Reviewer 3, confidence 5): The derivation from lines 2238 to 2243 contains an inequality that does not hold, especially as dimension \(d\) goes to infinity. Specifically, \(\max(\log(2) - \frac{1}{2}( \epsilon_{W,1} + \frac{\sqrt{d}\log(d)}{L} \epsilon_W), \log(2) + \frac{1}{2}( \epsilon_{W,1} + \frac{\sqrt{d}\log(d)}{L} \epsilon_W)) \le \epsilon_{W,1} + \frac{\sqrt{d}\log(d)}{L} \epsilon_W\) is not justified under the given conditions. This undermines the claim that the upper bound converges to zero and casts doubt on the validity of Theorem 2 and its implications. Reviewer 3 also questions other steps (lines 2220-2222: using \(\log(\text{Poly}(d)) \le \log(d)\); lines 2228-2230: how \(\frac{\sqrt{d}\log(d)}{L} \epsilon_W \le \frac{\sqrt{d}\log(d)}{\text{Poly}(d)}\) follows from \(\epsilon_W=(\text{Poly}(d))^{2/3}\) and \(L=\text{Poly}(d)\)).

- **Unclear generative process for data** (Reviewer 1): The description suggests that the Bayes optimal classifier might not need semantic features—syntactic features appear sufficient asymptotically, contradicting the claim that Q captures specialized knowledge. The reference to Li et al. (2019) suggests a stochastic choice that is not present here. This needs better explanation.

- **Unrealistic block-diagonal assumption (Eq. 2)** (Reviewer 1): Assuming a block-diagonal structure for the weight matrix predetermines the separability of feature spaces (easy vs. hard) and misses complex interactions in real data. It also assumes knowledge of which features are easy/hard in advance.

- **Neglect of adaptive gradient methods** (Reviewer 1): The analysis uses SGD, but practice uses Adam/AdaGrad which rescale gradients. Such methods could amplify updates for hard features (x2) and alter the staged progression.

- **Sensitivity to learning rate schedule** (Reviewers 1, 2): The two-stage result relies on a specific schedule (constant then annealing). Reviewer 1 asks what happens if the initial large-learning-rate phase is skipped and annealing begins immediately. Reviewer 2 asks whether the constant/annealing distinction is crucial and whether it is observed in practice. Without exploration, it is unclear if the staged learning is an artifact of the schedule or a more fundamental property.

- **Unrealistic linking of optimization parameters to data generation** (Reviewer 1): The choice of learning rate in the second stage is tied to the data generation procedure (e.g., dimension, noise). It is unclear whether the staged learning is robust to typical hyperparameter choices or is an artifact of this specific setup.

- **Weak empirical evidence** (Reviewers 2, 4): The empirical part is very limited. No quantitative metrics for syntactic or semantic accuracy are reported per stage; only LM loss is measured. The claim that models learn syntax first is supported only by a few qualitative examples (e.g., “the mother tongue of Danielle Darrieux is → the”) which is not a valid operationalization—the output “the” could begin many syntactically valid continuations. No systematic evaluation using established benchmarks (SyntaxGym, BLiMP) is performed. Only one type of syntax (category constraint) and one type of “semantics” (factual knowledge) are tested, not justifying a broad claim about syntax vs. semantics. The paper should provide accuracy curves over training and a detailed study of the length of the first stage (Reviewer 2).

- **Experimental setup lacks detail** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4): 
    - The practical model uses GPT-2 architecture. Is it still one-layer? If not, how do one-layer theoretical results extend? Dimensionality \(d\) is not specified (Reviewer 3).
    - How is syntactic ability verified beyond the single example? Are metrics or human evaluations used? (Reviewer 3)
    - The transition point at epoch 5 (T=5) is observed but not compared to a theoretical bound \(t_1\) (Reviewer 3).
    - Prompt construction details are missing: Are prompt lengths fixed? How are prompts derived from the dataset? In Figure 5, do all questions use the same prompts? Do prompts change during training? (Reviewer 3)
    - Training details for the GPT-2 model used to produce Figure 5 are absent (e.g., optimizer, batch size, number of tokens, compute) (Reviewer 4).

- **Lack of analysis of pre-trained model properties** (Reviewer 3): Beyond training dynamics, analysis of error behavior under distribution shift, generalization, and varying context length would strengthen the applicability.

- **Minority reviewer contrast**: Reviewer 1 (score 6) views the theoretical contribution as valuable despite confusions and suggests the paper is better suited for a journal (56 pages). The other three reviewers (score 3) are much more critical, especially regarding the proof error, mapping to syntax/semantics, and empirical validation. This disagreement is not resolved.
