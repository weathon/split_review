## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents Jamba, a hybrid Transformer-Mamba mixture-of-experts (MoE) language model that interleaves Transformer and Mamba layers with MoE in some layers to balance memory, throughput, and capacity. Two open-weight configurations are released: Jamba-1.5-Large (94B active parameters) and Jamba-1.5-Mini (12B active parameters). The model supports an effective context length of 256K tokens, the longest among open-weight models, and is competitive on standard language modeling and chatbot benchmarks. The authors introduce ExpertsInt8 quantization to enable cost-effective inference on 8×80GB GPUs without quality loss. Ablations on architectural choices (e.g., layer ratio, Mamba version) are provided. Reviewers highlight the novelty of the large-scale hybrid architecture, long-context efficiency, and interest in the open release, but raise substantial concerns about missing training details, confounded comparisons, reproducibility, and insufficient analysis of component interactions and quantization impacts.

### Strengths
- **Hybrid architecture with flexibility:** The combination of Transformer layers, Mamba layers, and MoE allows configurable trade-offs between memory, throughput, and capacity, addressing limitations of each component individually. One reviewer (R1) stresses this as a key advantage; another (R4) notes the large-scale novelty. The flexibility is demonstrated via different layer ratios and Mamba versions (R3).
- **Long-context capabilities:** Jamba achieves 256K effective context length, competitive on RULER and ∞BENCH, offering high throughput and low memory footprint at long contexts. (R1, R2, R3 strongly agree.)
- **Cost-effective inference via ExpertsInt8 quantization:** This novel int8 quantization technique for MoE experts allows fitting Jamba-1.5-Large on 8×80GB GPUs for 256K-token contexts without quality degradation and provides latency advantages on A100 GPUs (R1, R4). R4 notes similar work in MoE quantization but still calls the int8 approach novel.
- **Competitive performance on benchmarks:** Jamba-1.5 models perform similarly to other open-weight models of comparable active parameter counts on standard language modeling, chatbot, and multilingual tasks. (R1 finds it “competitive,” R2 says “close to SOTA public models,” R3 highlights strong long-context results.)
- **Open-source release:** Both model sizes are publicly available, enabling further research. (R3 stresses this.)
- **Detailed architectural ablations:** The paper provides analysis of attention/Mamba layer ratios, Mamba-1 vs. Mamba-2, and ways to mix experts, offering insights for the community. (R2, R3 appreciate this, though R2 finds the ablations limited in scale and downstream evaluation.)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of training details and reproducibility:** Critical training information is not disclosed, including pre-training data source, data mixture ratios, number of tokens, context length, training throughput/FLOPs, tokenizer training (corpus size, software stack), MoE routing loss function and hyperparameters, post-training algorithm and compute budget, and synthetic data generation protocol (model, size, protocol). (R2, R4 both highlight this; R4 calls it “hard to reproduce” and “hard to quantify apples-to-apples.” R2 notes this makes it impossible to assess generalizability.)
- **Insufficient fair comparisons to demonstrate architectural effectiveness:**
    - Comparisons with Llama and Mistral are confounded by different data, model size, and compute; the paper lacks an apples-to-apples comparison of Jamba vs. pure Transformer, Mamba, and other hybrid attention-SSM architectures (e.g., YOCO, Samba [R3]) under the same training condition (data, compute budget, scaling). (R2, R3, R4 all raise this.)
    - The ablation studies (Appendix B) are limited in training compute, model size, and evaluation tasks; they do not include post-training evaluation, making it insufficient to demonstrate architectural effectiveness at scale or on comprehensive downstream tasks. (R2)
    - The paper does not demonstrate the scaling ability of Jamba (e.g., LM loss or downstream performance vs. training compute). (R2)
    - A comparison with other MoE quantization methods (e.g., [2]) is missing; no latency comparison or discussion is provided. (R4)
- **Missing analysis of MoE and Mamba layer interaction:** The paper provides limited analysis of how MoE and Mamba layers cooperate. There is no detailed breakdown of computational overhead introduced by MoE layers, how it scales with context length, or whether benefits are consistent across lengths. Activation patterns and information flow between these layers are not characterized. (R1, also echoed by R1’s question on how the layer ratio was determined.) The lack of study on when MoE overhead may outweigh gains is noted. (R1)
- **Quantization evaluation gaps:**
    - No empirical evidence is provided on downstream performance regression when using ExpertsInt8 quantization (e.g., on NarrativeQA or MMLU). (R4)
    - Figure 2 focuses on latency but misses throughput; throughput data is needed for fair comparison. (R4)
    - The software stack used for latency/throughput comparisons is not specified; it is unclear whether baseline models use quantization and under what settings. (R4)
- **Evaluation reporting issues:**
    - Compute budget (FLOPs or pre-training tokens) is not disclosed, so performance comparisons based on model size alone are insufficient to conclude architecture superiority. (R4)
    - Inference details (floating-point precision, quantization) for Table 4 numbers are missing; some results are self-reported while others are taken from external sources without citations, making fairness uncertain. (R4)
    - The claim in Figure 5 that Jamba-1.5-Large is “of similar size” to Llama 3.1 70B is overreaching because Jamba is larger in both total and active parameters; error bars also make the comparison weaker. (R4)
    - It is unclear why Gemma is included in Table 3 if it does not support the 256K context window, and what “strict/flexible” means in Table 4. (R4)
    - The statement “While not our main focus” in Section 7.2 is unclear; the paper should clarify its primary focus. (R4)
- **Missing evaluation on edge devices and CPUs:** Performance on low-resource hardware is not evaluated; the paper does not discuss challenges like quantization effects or Mamba kernel performance on CPU. (R1)
- **Reproducibility details absent:** The paper lacks description of optimization parameters tuned for each architecture in ablations (e.g., learning rate, β values) and whether parameter counts were matched, making it hard to assess fairness. (R4) Also missing details on training throughput and FLOPs per step to evaluate pre-training efficiency. (R4)
- **Minority reviewer contrast:** One reviewer (R1) is markedly more positive, assigning a score of 8 with high confidence, emphasizing the architecture’s flexibility and long-context utility, and viewing quantization as a clear strength. The other three reviewers (R2, R3, R4) are more critical (scores 5, 6, 6), with R2 being the most negative, focusing on confounded comparisons, lack of training details, and insufficient scaling analysis, and R4 raising the most detailed reproducibility and evaluation concerns. R3, while positive on long-context results, notes the lack of fair comparison with other hybrid SSM models. The contrast is preserved: the strengths above reflect the positive view, while the weaknesses are driven primarily by the more critical reviewers.
