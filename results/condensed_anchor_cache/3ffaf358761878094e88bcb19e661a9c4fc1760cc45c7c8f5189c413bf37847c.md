- Decision: Accept
- Scores: 8, 6, 3, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Distribution Backtracking Distillation (DisBack), which extends existing score distillation (e.g., Diff-Instruct) by recording a degradation path from a pre-trained teacher model to the initial untrained student, then training the student to backtrack through that path. Reviewers agree the idea is simple and intuitive, and that DisBack improves convergence speed and final quality over the baseline. However, there is significant disagreement: one reviewer (score 8) is strongly positive, citing novelty and strong empirical results; another (score 3) is strongly negative, questioning the reliability of the reported performance and the fairness of the experimental setup. Two intermediate reviewers raise substantial methodological concerns about experimental rigor, missing ablations, and overclaimed generality.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation and intuition.** The paper identifies the initial score mismatch in diffusion distillation and proposes a natural solution: using a trajectory of teacher distributions (R1, R2, R4). The idea of backtracking along the degradation path is well-reasoned (R2).
- **Effective convergence improvement.** Experiments show that DisBack converges significantly faster than the Diff-Instruct baseline and achieves competitive FID scores (e.g., 1.38 on ImageNet 64×64) (R1, R2, R3). Qualitative samples are high-quality (R1).
- **Versatile and orthogonal design.** The method can in principle be integrated into other distillation frameworks (R2, R4). The degradation recording algorithm is reasonable (R2, R3).
- **Clear presentation.** The paper is generally easy to follow; technical content is presented clearly (R1).
- **Reasonable negative results.** The authors include a discussion of failure cases in the appendix (R1).
- **Conceptual explanation via figures.** Reviewer 3 specifically appreciated the explanation in Figures 12 and 13 about how using an inaccurate teacher with wider support helps score matching, and suggested connecting this to the score mismatching literature (DLSM, TIW-DSM).

### Weaknesses

- **Overclaiming and lack of causal analysis.** Reviewer 1 notes that the paper claims to have “identified” that the issue arises because methods focus on the endpoint, but no such diagnosis is provided—only evidence that using a trajectory helps. Similarly, the claim that “the fat convergence speed is because constraining the convergence trajectory provides a clear optimization direction” is not supported. A deeper theoretical explanation for *why* DisBack works is missing (R1, R4).
- **State-of-the-art claims are questionable.** Two reviewers raise serious concerns about performance reliability:
    - **Training budget discrepancy.** DisBack on ImageNet 64×64 uses far more training iterations than baselines. Reviewer 2 calculates: if “500,000 epochs” is literal (with ∼40K images/epoch), the iteration count is ∼20G, exceeding Diff-Instruct’s 50K by 400,000×. Even if “epochs” is a typo for “steps,” it is still 10× Diff-Instruct’s budget. DMD2 with extended training (550K iterations) achieves FID 1.28, beating DisBack’s 1.38. This makes the claimed state-of-the-art status misleading (R2).
    - **Student outperforms teacher.** Reviewer 3 points out that on ImageNet 64, the teacher (EDM) has FID 2.44, but the student achieves FID 1.38, which is counter-intuitive for a distillation method. Similarly, a user preference study claims the student beats the SDXL teacher, which contradicts typical distillation expectations. Detailed evaluation methodology (participant numbers, criteria) is missing, and comparison with other metrics (IS, perceptual scores) is absent (R3).
    - **The baseline variant already beats the original Diff-Instruct.** In Table 3, the “w/o convergence trajectory” variant (essentially Diff-Instruct) achieves FID 12.26 on FFHQ vs. the original Diff-Instruct’s 19.93, suggesting that other factors (e.g., score network initialization) may be responsible for part of the improvement, not solely the backtracking (R2).
- **Insufficient ablation and sensitivity analysis.** The ablation study is minimal, only comparing DisBack to the original method (R1). Missing ablations include:
    - Number of degraded teachers \(N\) (R3, R4). A progressive study from \(N=1\) (Diff-Instruct) to large \(N\) is needed.
    - Effect of different student initializations (R1).
    - Effect of different degradation schemes (e.g., using the actual training trajectory of the teacher) (R1).
    - Number of degradation epochs and number of checkpoints; influence on mismatch reduction (R4).
- **Limited training curve and cost accounting.** Figure 1 only shows the first 2,000 epochs, not the full training process (R2). Moreover, the figure excludes the computational cost of Stage 1 (Degradation Recording), overstating the efficiency (R3). Reviewers need the full convergence curve and a fair cost comparison (R2, R3).
- **Backtracking occupies only a small fraction of training.** Each intermediate checkpoint is trained for only ∼1,000 steps, while the majority of training uses the original teacher. This raises doubts about the overall contribution of the backtracking strategy (R2).
- **Missing scheduling details for teacher transitions in Stage 2.** The paper does not describe the schedule for switching from a degraded teacher to the next, nor how to determine when to transition. This may require extensive trial-and-error (R3).
- **Orthogonality claim not substantiated.** Reviewer 4 points out that the paper asserts DisBack can be integrated with other distillation methods (e.g., consistency distillation, adversarial score distillation) but provides no experimental evidence. A demonstration as a drop-in enhancement is needed.
- **Insufficient baselines and metrics for high-resolution experiments.** In Table 4 (likely SDXL experiments), baselines such as SDXL-Lightning, LCM-LoRA, PixArt-Δ, DMD2, and others are missing. Metrics are inadequate: standard FID at 1024 resolution is unreliable due to inception network downsampling; patch-FID (as used by SDXL-Lightning) and text-image alignment scores (CLIP, CompBench, VQA scores) should be reported (R3).
- **Theoretical and empirical gaps regarding score mismatch.** Reviewer 4 notes that while the degradation path reduces one mismatch (between generated samples and teacher prediction), it may increase another (between teacher prediction and true score), creating a trade-off that is not analyzed. No theoretical or empirical analysis of this trade-off is provided.
- **Minor issues.** (R1) Fig. 2 contains the same sample twice (top middle and middle). (R4) In Eq. (6), \(\partial x_t/\partial \eta\) should be inside the expectation; noise notation should use bold \(\epsilon\) and clarify the relation \(x_t = x_0 + \sigma_t \epsilon\); Algorithm 1&2 lines for calculating \(x_t\) are unnecessary and misleading; labels in Fig. 7 are wrong.
- **Code not available.** Reviewer 3 asks if the code will be released; no public code is provided at review time.
- **Training overhead for intermediate checkpoints.** Reviewer 4 asks about the added overhead of fitting \(N-1\) intermediate teacher checkpoints.
- **Generality of score estimation.** Reviewer 4 notes that the score estimation in Eq. (7) is not general (e.g., DDPM uses a mean scale \(\alpha_t\)), and questions whether teacher and student noising methods must be consistent during distillation.

**Contrast between reviewers:** Reviewer 1 (score 8) finds the idea novel and well-executed with only minor overclaiming and shallow ablation, while Reviewer 3 (score 3) strongly doubts the reported results due to performance contradictions and insufficient experimental rigor. The two intermediate reviewers (scores 6 and 5) raise specific methodological issues that undermine the strength of the conclusions unless addressed.
