## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper provides a convergence analysis of mini-batch SGD under four scheduling strategies for batch size and learning rate: constant batch size and decaying learning rate, increasing batch size and decaying learning rate, increasing batch size and increasing learning rate, and increasing batch size with warm-up decaying learning rate. It uses an upper bound from a descent lemma to argue that increasing both batch size and learning rate accelerates SGD, and it presents experimental results on image classification.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and clearly presented, with rigorous theoretical assumptions and statements.
- It explicitly compares a wide range of popular schedulers, clearly demonstrating their effects on the convergence bound.
- The theoretical proofs appear correct, and the analysis is comprehensive for nonconvex smooth objectives.
- The work is adequately positioned within the context of related work.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited technical novelty and contribution (R2, R3, R4; R3 most critical with high confidence).** The theoretical analysis is a straightforward extension of existing SGD convergence analyses (e.g., Ghadimi and Lan 2013), with Lemma 2.1 being a standard result. The subsequent manipulation of the bound is technically simple, involving only summation of well-defined functions. One reviewer emphasizes that the main message—increasing both batch size and learning rate—is already well-known from prior work (Goyal et al. 2018), significantly weakening the contribution.

2. **Lack of bound tightness and questionable acceleration claims (R2, R4, with support from R1).** The analysis rests on a single upper bound (Lemma 2.1), and it is unclear how loose this bound is. A smaller upper bound does not guarantee faster convergence, especially if the bound is very loose. Several reviewers are suspicious of the claim that an increasing learning rate schedule accelerates convergence: a constant learning rate with a similar maximal value might converge faster in practice, and the bound may not be tight enough to capture the true trend (the O() constants may be significant). Additionally, one reviewer notes that the convergence analysis measures steps T but ignores the per-step computational cost of increasing batch size; thus, the claim of acceleration may be void if each step becomes more expensive.

3. **Correctness concerns about the increasing learning rate schedule (R1).** The learning rate grows exponentially without bound in scheduler (iii), which may not lead to convergence even for a simple noise-free quadratic counter-example \(f_i(\theta) = \|\theta\|^2\). This raises questions about the correctness of the analysis for that schedule.

4. **Insufficient empirical validation (R2, R3).** The experimental results are limited and offer little insight beyond existing work (e.g., Goyal et al. 2018). There is insufficient empirical evidence to substantiate the claimed practical effectiveness of schedules that increase both learning rate and batch size.

5. **Lack of explanation for practical phenomena (R2, R3).** The paper does not adequately explain why certain schedules (e.g., learning rate warm-up) are preferred in practice, nor does it connect the theoretical results to empirical observations. For instance, it does not clarify why warm-up is needed rather than starting with a high learning rate.

6. **Strong assumptions (R1).** The bounded noise assumption (Assumption 2.1, A2) is strong and could be weakened to quadratically bounded noise.

7. **Specific technical issues (R1, R4).**
   - In Lemma 2.1, the optimal loss \(f^*\) is incorrectly set equal to the average of \(f_i^*\); the optimal solutions for individual losses are generally different from the global optimum.
   - It is inappropriate to consider a constant learning rate as a “decaying” learning rate.
   - The notation \(L_n\) is ambiguous.

8. **Inconsistent claims (R2).** The claim in the introduction that scheduler (iv) minimizes faster than (ii) or (iii) may not be consistent with the results in Section 3.4 or the abstract.
