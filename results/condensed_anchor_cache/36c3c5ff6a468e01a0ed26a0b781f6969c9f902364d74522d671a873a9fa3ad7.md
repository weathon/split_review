## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper tackles out-of-distribution (OOD) test-time adaptation for graph neural networks by proposing GOAT, a self-supervised method that uses a low-rank attention-based adapter to modify node features of test graphs. The adapter is trained with a loss combining symmetry (similar GNN outputs on original and transformed graphs) and consistency (transformed GNN output equals GNN output of transformed graph). Experiments on six node classification benchmarks across three OOD scenarios show GOAT achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance with high computational and memory efficiency. Reviewers hold mixed opinions: two find the work timely and well-performing, while two view the novelty as limited and the presentation as unclear.

### Strengths
- **Computational and memory efficiency**: The low-rank adapter is lightweight and runs quickly with low memory usage (R1, R2, R4).
- **Self‑supervised adaptation without test labels**: The method adapts pre-trained GNNs using only unlabeled test data, which is a practical contribution (R4). It does not modify pre-trained weights (R3).
- **Interpretability of OOD representations**: The adapter produces an explicit matrix (e.g., environment representation) that offers insight into the distribution shift (R4). R1 also notes the continuous problem formulation as a positive.
- **Novelty of the self‑supervised loss**: The idea of enforcing symmetry and consistency between augmented views of the test graph is new (R1). R3 adds that the loss accounts for domain-specific requirements like symmetricity.
- **Addresses an important problem and achieves strong performance**: The paper tackles a relevant OOD problem and shows state‑of‑the‑art results (R2). However, other reviewers note that performance gains are sometimes marginal (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and strong similarity to existing methods**: The approach closely resembles GTrans (both modify node attributes via a learned representation, both use dropEdge for contrastive sampling). The main differences are the low-rank adapter and the loss function (R1). All ingredients – attention‑based adaption, contrastive‑style loss, and an extra trainable branch – are known techniques, making the paper a combination of existing ideas (R3). The low-rank adapter is a straightforward application (R1).
- **Poor explanation and unclear notation**: The paper is poorly written; many key notations are insufficiently explained (R2). Specific confusions: the meaning of \(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{te}\) in Eq. (2), the definition of \(\mathbf{H}\) on line 211/240, how \(\mathbf{K}', \mathbf{V}'\) are learned (line 243), the origin of \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) in Eq. (6), and what \(L_R\) refers to in Eq. (12) (R2). The expression \(g_\psi(f(\mathcal{G})) \approx f(g_\psi(\mathcal{G}))\) (Section 3.3) is inaccurate because \(g_\psi\) takes a graph as input, not the GNN’s output (R1). Proposition 1’s statement about modifying graph structure within the pre‑trained parameter space is unclear (R1). The LROG example on page 5 and Figure 3 do not convincingly illustrate what LROG learns or how it captures environment changes (R1). Repeated misspelling of “augmentation to augmentation” (R2). Wording in Assumption 1 is poor and confusing (R2).
- **Problem formulation issues**: The paper does not justify why the optimal expected supervised loss can be obtained from a point‑estimation problem when test labels \(Y_{te}\) are unavailable (R1). The overall framing is more akin to unsupervised test‑time domain adaptation than to out‑of‑distribution generalization, since OOD tasks typically do not assume access to test data for training an adaptor (R3).
- **Limited applicability to other graph tasks**: The method is designed and evaluated only for node classification. Its applicability to graph classification or link prediction is not discussed, and the augmentation strategy appears tightly coupled to node‑level features (R4).
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: The paper relies solely on empirical results; no theoretical justification is given for why the symmetric and consistency losses are optimal or how they interact. A theoretical framework would clarify conditions under which GOAT converges (R4).
- **Experimental results not overwhelmingly strong**: Performance improvements over baselines are sometimes very small (e.g., 94.35 vs 94.32, 94.79 vs 94.76, 55.83 vs 55.82 in Table 2), making the average rank comparison potentially unfair (R1). Results are not consistently better across all settings (R3). R2 notes the improvement is not overwhelming and does not earn the benefit of the doubt given the poor presentation.
