## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers acknowledge that the paper introduces a novel opponent modeling method (BRS) extending LOLA/POLA by differentiating through a best-response approximation (detective) with a QA-based conditioning mechanism. Evaluations on IPD and Coin Game with various ablations are provided. However, significant concerns are raised regarding fundamental assumptions (symmetric NE, parameter sharing), missing comparisons (PSRO, diverse best response, multi-step lookahead), clarity of presentation, statistical rigor (only three seeds), exploitability of BRS, and incomplete justification of key claims. Scores range from 3 to 6, with Reviewer 4 being strongly negative (score 3, confidence 5) due to a fundamental assumption issue, while Reviewer 2 is slightly more positive (score 6) but still critical.

### Strengths
- The detective mechanism (differentiating through a best-response approximation) is original and interesting, building on prior reciprocity-based cooperation work (R1, R2, R3; R4 notes the attempt).
- The paper provides good empirical evaluation in the challenging Coin Game and IPD, with a large number of relevant ablations (R1, R2).
- The significance of addressing general-sum games and improving social welfare is timely (R2).
- The paper is well-structured and describes relevant literature (R1, R2).
- The paper extends existing work from a few RL steps to policy change via policy conditioning (R4, though with major reservations).

### Weaknesses
- **Fundamental Assumption of Symmetric NE and Parameter Sharing** (R4 strongly, not raised by others):
  - The paper assumes symmetric Nash equilibria throughout, which is an extremely strong assumption. Symmetric games do not imply symmetric NE (counterexamples: XOR game, modified chicken game). Parameter sharing across agents cannot represent asymmetric NE, whereas LOLA does not adopt parameter sharing (R4, with reference to arXiv:2206.07505).
  - The claim in Section 4.3.2 that Equation (7) is equivalent to “self-play with reward sharing” is incorrect; it is only reward sharing with parameter sharing. Self-play with reward sharing can learn NE in the XOR game, but Equation (7) cannot (R4).
  - The statement “in zero-sum games, this update will have no effect” is correct but meaningless: in a symmetric zero-sum game with identical policies, the expected reward is always zero, so the gradient is zero everywhere (R4).
  - The concept of symmetric game only applies to two-player games; for general N-player games, this assumption essentially implies a cooperative game (R4).

- **Missing Comparisons with Key Related Work**:
  - The training loop of BRS is closely related to Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO; Lanctot et al. 2017), which is not mentioned or discussed (R2).
  - Important references on diverse best-response dynamics (Perez-Nieves et al. 2021) and determinantal Q-learning (Yang et al. 2020) are missing; comparison and discussion are needed (R1).
  - Recent work on multi-step lookahead reasoning (Recursive Reasoning in Minimax Games) suggests scalability issues for POLA may not be serious; the paper should compare computational complexity and performance for multiple lookahead steps and discuss whether BRS can incorporate similar mechanisms (R3).

- **Clarity and Presentation Issues**:
  - The writing does not make clear that the detective and agent take turns during optimization. The title of Algorithm 1 (“a single iteration”) is the only indication; otherwise it seems the detective is first trained on a pre-selected buffer B. Sections 4.1 and 4.2 reinforce this misimpression (R2).
  - BRS-NOSP is used in Figure 2 but defined much later (R2).
  - The labeling of θ1 and θ2 is ambiguous. In Equation 4, θr takes the place of θ1 but actually represents the detective, while the agent is θ2 (R2).
  - The “Simulation-Based Question Answering” mechanism is overcomplicated. The conditioning vector is essentially Q-values of each action of a random opponent policy in that state, estimated by sampling trajectories. This simpler description is obscured (R2).
  - δA is described before Q^simulation is motivated, causing confusion (R2).
  - Section 4.2 is hard to follow; “detective’s conditioning” is not formulated; “behavior of the agent against a random agent” is ambiguous; “agent” is used inconsistently (R4).
  - Action b in Equation 6 is not introduced (R4).
  - Equation 12 is not written as a bi-level optimization, making it appear single-level (R2).
  - It is unclear why paragraphs on Diplomacy and self-play from human data are included; the paper should explain (R2).
  - Section 2.1 presents an N-player formulation, but the paper only uses two-player settings (R4).

- **Statistical Rigor**:
  - The analysis is based on only three seeds (stated in Appendix), which is a small number given the high variance observed from POLA. Using more seeds for POLA because “observed higher variance” introduces a stopping rule problem (R2).
  - Training curves for baselines and proposed methods are missing (R3).

- **Exploitability and Performance Issues**:
  - BRS is more exploitable than POLA in Figure 3 against AD, contradicting the paper’s goal. BRS-NOSP improves exploitability but at the cost of social welfare (R2).
  - Head-to-head results in Appendix Figure 7 show POLA outperforming BRS, yet these are not shown in the main text, further indicating BRS is exploitable (R2).
  - The paper advocates using a best-response opponent (MCTS) as a comparison point, but POLA and LOLA were designed for online learning against learning opponents, making head-to-head comparisons questionable. The justification for this evaluation setting is missing (R2).
  - BRS-NORB performs near-identically to BRS and is significantly simpler; why not just use BRS-NORB? (R2)

- **Incomplete Justification of Key Claims**:
  - The explanation for why BRS outperforms POLA is unclear. The claim that POLA can be exploited by a learning opponent is not supported by showing BRS avoids this; a detailed analysis of how the detective prevents exploitation and whether its approximation is accurate enough is lacking (R3).
  - The claim that POLA cannot differentiate through all opponent optimization steps is not supported by ablations varying the number of steps used by POLA (R3).
  - The paper states interactions mirror the Stackelberg setup but does not provide rigorous argument or empirical evidence that convergence to Stackelberg equilibrium improves social welfare (R3).
  - The claim at the end of Section 4.1 that (θ**1, θ*2) is a Nash equilibrium is incorrect; a counterexample is rock-paper-scissors. NE is defined over a minimax operator, not two separate equations (R4).

- **Scalability and Practical Limitations**:
  - The method requires access to the detective’s rewards (and vice versa), limiting application to settings with private rewards (R1).
  - Calculating δA at every timestep is expensive; sample efficiency in terms of total simulator steps is not reported (R2).
  - The Good Shepherd is criticized as not scalable, but BRS seems similarly difficult to scale (R2).
  - The abstract mentions scalability and non-exploitability, but it is unclear how the method adapts to larger games or beyond two players (R1).
  - The cooperation regularization is not validated on mixed-motive games where some states require cooperation and others competition (e.g., weakest link) (R1).
  - Why not train directly against the MCTS opponent instead of using the detective? (R2)

- **Missing Ablations and Additional Analysis**:
  - What is the intuition for conditioning on Q-values of a random policy? (R2)
  - Reasoning for separate optimizers in Equation 8; has this been ablated? (R2)
  - Experiments only on IPD and Coin Game; need testing on more complex social dilemma environments like Sequential Social Dilemmas (R3).
  - It would be informative to evaluate performance when NE is asymmetric and parameter sharing is turned off (two separate policies) (R4).
  - Ablate the number of opponent optimization steps used by POLA to support the claim that POLA cannot differentiate through all steps (R3).

- **Potential Proof Error**:
  - In Corollary D.3, the condition r^1(s,a,b)+r^2(s,b,a)=0 implies R^1(τ) = -R^2(τ), but Proposition D.2 uses Lemma D.1 which assumes r^1(s,a,b)=r^2(o,b,a) implying E[R^1] = E[R^2]. This appears inconsistent, and the proof of Corollary D.3 may be incorrect (R3).

- **Typos and Formulation Issues**:
  - Algorithm 1: θ1' ← θ1+z should be θ1 ← θ1+z (R3).
