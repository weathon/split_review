## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper argues that using object detectors retrained on enhanced low-light images for evaluation is prone to overfitting, reducing reliability. To address this, the authors introduce LIME-Bench, an online platform collecting human preferences to validate the link between human perception and automated metrics. They also propose LIME-Eval, a label-free evaluation framework using pre-trained detectors without fine-tuning or annotations, employing an energy-based strategy to assess output confidence maps. Experiments on ExDark and synthetic datasets are provided. Reception is mixed: two reviewers (scores 8, confidence 4 each) are very positive, one (score 3, confidence 5) is highly negative, and one (score 6, confidence 4) is moderately positive with concerns.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses the challenge of evaluating low-light image enhancement using object detection by proposing LIME-Bench (human preference collection) and LIME-Eval (energy-based evaluation without retraining or annotations). [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- It demonstrates that higher detection accuracy does not necessarily correlate with superior enhancement quality, and fine-tuning detectors for evaluation is prone to overfitting and not rigorous. [R1, R2, R3]
- The paper is well written, easy to understand, and includes extensive experiments providing thorough insights into correlations between detector performance, human ratings, and LIME-Eval metrics. [R1]
- LIME-Bench provides a structured way to incorporate human preferences, bridging machine-based metrics and human perceptual quality, which could aid future low-light vision research. [R1]
- The energy-based modeling technique effectively bypasses the need for fine-tuned detection models and annotation labels. [R2]
- LIME-Eval considers both human vision and machine vision, offering a label-free assessment metric. [R3]
- The study systematically identifies shortcomings of previous evaluation methods, introduces a new human-preference dataset, and proposes a novel unlabeled image quality assessment method. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Table 1 “enhanced before data augmentation” setting**: The use of color jitter and normalization is unclear. Could color jitter substantially degrade low-light enhancement results? Provide more detail on the augmentation process. [R1]
- **Dataset scope**: The paper mainly uses ExDark and synthetic datasets. Can LIME-Eval also be applied to low-light face detection datasets such as UG2+ DARK FACE? [R1]
- **Missing references**: Prior low-light object detection works using self-supervised learning and domain adaptation (e.g., [1], [2], [3]) are not discussed in the related work section. [R1] Also missing citations for YOLOX, LOL dataset, and LPIPS. [R4]
- **Role of background prediction in LIME-Eval**: The purpose of considering background prediction in the energy-based evaluation function is vague. Describe its benefit explicitly. [R2]
- **Qualitative comparisons inadequate**: Samples from ExDark lack conviction. The augmentation strategy is random and not a fair input to the target detector. [R3]
- **Integration with Retinexformer**: How is LIME-Eval integrated into Retinexformer? Details are missing from both paper and appendix. [R3]
- **Gradient involvement concern**: If LIME-Eval is integrated into detection and participates in gradient calculation during training (i.e., acts as a loss function), then the low-light enhanced image is fed to the detector for training, making the evaluation non-referential as claimed in Table I. [R3]
- **Abrupt introduction of quantization in Chapter 3**: The need to verify information loss due to quantization is sudden. Provide details: bit depth, method for mapping floating-point values to discrete levels, and how quantization impacts the comparison between “Enhancement first” and “Augmentation first” schemes. [R4]
- **Lack of analysis for unlabeled evaluation in Chapter 5**: The transition from problem to method is abrupt. Explain why the energy-based approach can evaluate enhancement quality without labels, why the lowest energy image is optimal, and how the energy function correlates with perceptual quality. Detail the theoretical underpinnings. [R4]
- **Variable x_bg inconsistency**: x_bg is called “object output” in line 402 and “background prediction” in line 415. Clarify the meaning. Specify how x_bg is obtained (scalar or spatial map). If a map, how is it aggregated? If the detector outputs multiple bounding boxes, how is x_bg combined per box to produce a single energy score? [R4]
- **Insufficient correlation comparisons in Table 2**: Only one IQA algorithm’s Spearman correlation is shown. Include both full-reference and no-reference metrics known to correlate with human perception and commonly used in low-light enhancement, with justification for selection. [R4]
- **Incomplete detection metrics in Table 3**: Report improvements on recall (mAR, AR50) as well. Include additional detectors (e.g., YOLOX) to improve persuasiveness, and justify the choice of detectors. [R4]
- **Generalizability to other restoration tasks**: The paper only discusses low-light enhancement. Assess applicability to rain removal, fog removal, deblurring, etc., including potential challenges and adaptations, perhaps in a future work section. [R4]
