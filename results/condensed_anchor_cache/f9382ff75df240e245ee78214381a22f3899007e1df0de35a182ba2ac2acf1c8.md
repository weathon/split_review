## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Force-guided Bridge Matching (FBM), a conditional generative model that integrates physics-based force guidance into a bridge matching framework for time-coarsened full-atom molecular dynamics. FBM targets a Boltzmann-constrained distribution by incorporating an intermediate force field to guide generation, aiming to improve simulation efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Experiments on two peptide datasets demonstrate improved distribution similarity, validity, and transferability to unseen systems.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written, easy to follow, and the task is important.
- The integration of force-guided priors within the bridge matching framework is innovative and provides an intuitive way to add physics-derived constraints to generated trajectories, going beyond standard generative modeling.
- Comprehensive evaluation on two peptide datasets shows FBM consistently outperforms baselines across multiple metrics: distribution similarity (JS divergences of collective variables), validity (bond clashes, breaking), structure flexibility relative to ground truth, and contact metrics.
- Code and detailed hyperparameters are provided, facilitating reproducibility.
- The model shows transferability to unseen systems.
- A direct ablation study (through a tunable guidance-strength hyperparameter) demonstrates the usefulness of the force guidance term.
- Careful dataset splitting (clustering by cluster) supports claims of generalizability.
- A robustness/explanatory study with hyperparameter tuning (Table 6) is provided.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty (minority reviewer R1 negative vs. R2/R3 positive):** One reviewer (R1) considers the work to be a straightforward combination of existing works ([1], [2], [3]) without proper references, noting that learning dynamics from trajectory pairs was introduced in [1] and force-guided training in [3], and that the change from protein backbones to full-atom representation or from diffusion to bridge matching does not introduce substantial methodological novelty. Other reviewers (R2, R3) find the approach innovative and well-executed.
- **Theoretical soundness concern:** Reviewer R1 questions the use of bridge matching for this task, arguing that all samples along a trajectory are expected to follow the same Boltzmann distribution, so a constant transformation (not point-to-point correspondence) is theoretically required. The current point-to-point mapping appears theoretically unsound.
- **Limited experimental scope:** Experiments are conducted only on small peptides. The model’s scalability to larger peptides or more complex molecular structures (e.g., the smallest protein backbone Chignolin, as requested by R1) is not demonstrated. A scaling analysis with system size is missing.
- **Insufficient comparison with traditional MD:** The paper lacks a rigorous analysis of computational cost – including wall-clock time and resource utilization – compared to conventional MD simulations, which is essential for assessing practical advantages.
- **Unclear TIC visualization:** The TIC plots in Figure 4 are difficult to interpret. The color scheme and/or alternative visualizations could be improved, and additional quantifiable metrics would help highlight differences in sampling efficiency and accuracy.
- **Metrics hard to link to performance:** The reported JS values and other metrics are not always accompanied by direct visual comparisons (e.g., distribution plots like Figure 6) for the same samples, making it hard to interpret what a given metric value means. Providing such figures for both best-performing and less successful trajectories would improve understanding.
- **Effect of guidance strength not fully shown:** While validity and contact metrics are shown to follow guidance strength, the effect on other metrics (e.g., distribution similarity, flexibility) is less clear. Showing trajectory evolution for a few peptides as guidance strength increases would be informative.
