- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies the effectiveness of pre-training for robustness under distribution shifts. It characterizes shifts into two types: poor extrapolation (out-of-support) and dataset biases (in-support, e.g., spurious correlations). The authors claim that pre-training helps with the former but not the latter, supported by simplified theoretical analysis and experiments. They then explore two implications: (1) pre-training and debiasing interventions have complementary robustness benefits, and (2) fine-tuning on a small, non-diverse but de-biased dataset can yield more robust models than fine-tuning on a large, diverse but biased dataset.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses a very interesting and practically relevant topic: understanding when pre-training helps or fails under distribution shift.
- It is generally well-written and easy to follow; the discussion of in-support and out-of-support shifts is a potentially useful axis for understanding robustness.
- The ideas are original and the relevant literature is sufficiently discussed (Reviewer 2). The paper is well-contextualized with a plausible technical structure (intuitions, motivating examples, formal analysis, empirical verification).
- The later sections on developing more robust models (Section 6) are interesting case studies showing how to apply the insights from earlier sections (Reviewer 2).
- A reviewer appreciates the careful discussion in defining and designing in-support and out-of-support shifts (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

**1. Conceptual and definitional gaps**
- The central notions of “in-support” and “out-of-support” are not formally defined. The description in Section 4 is imprecise: “out-of-support” are examples with zero density under the training input distribution, but it remains unclear how this relates to the classifier’s behavior. A proper definition and connection to max-margin classifiers is missing (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). Even for the logistic regression example, the effect of in-support shifts on the decision boundary is not explained (Reviewer 3). 
- The definition of robustness itself is never stated (Reviewer 3).
- The paper claims to estimate density ratios to classify examples, but this process is not clearly described or justified. Using a classifier to separate two distributions is not equivalent to estimating the support of the training distribution (Reviewer 4).
- The splitting method for sketch images into ID and OOD subsets w.r.t. ImageNet is based on whether they “look like” ImageNet classes. The method is referred to “Appendix B 3.2” but that appendix section does not exist (Reviewer 3). This undermines reproducibility.

**2. Theoretical analysis is too simplified and has major gaps**
- **Theorem 3.1 / Theorem 1**: The key variable `w_ref` is undefined, making the theorem uninterpretable (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). The logistic regression assumption is not justified, and `proj_{W_ref}` is never formally introduced. The statement “initialization determines how the model extends outside of W_ref” is unclear because W_ref is contained in `proj_{W_ref}`, so both terms are influenced by W_ref (Reviewer 2).
- The theorem only shows that pre-training/initialization affects in-distribution accuracy but not OOD accuracy. However, this effect can be positive or negative—the paper assumes it helps, but the conditions under which pre-training helps, hurts, or is neutral are not specified. This is a major logical gap (Reviewer 3). 
- The analysis assumes a deterministic convex model (logistic regression). For convex models, pre-training is irrelevant because the solution depends only on the data, not on initialization. The paper then jumps to stochastic-gradient-trained neural networks without explaining how the analysis generalizes to non-convex, stochastic settings (Reviewer 3). 
- The theoretical analysis does not provide any insights into the generalization behavior of models trained with pre-training and balanced datasets, nor does it consider the effect of the size or diversity of the balanced dataset (Reviewer 4).

**3. Experimental confounds and insufficient ablations**
- **Figure 3 (Section 4)**: The compared pre-trained and random-initialized models use different architectures (e.g., ResNet-18 vs. ResNet-50, DenseNet-121). Improved robustness may arise from larger model capacity rather than pre-training alone. A fair comparison requires the same architecture with and without pre-training (Reviewer 1).
- **Shift intensity not ablated**: No experiments vary the degree of bias or the extent of extrapolation. It is unknown how the strength of a spurious correlation or the amount of missing support changes pre-training’s effectiveness. The authors should explore a spectrum from balanced to systematically missing groups (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Figure 5 correlation is weak**: The negative correlation in the middle panel is -0.112, which is very weak. A confidence interval should be provided; the result seems to suggest independence rather than complementarity (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). Moreover, the positive correlation in the right panel (sum of individual gains vs. combined gain) is poorly motivated and may be a mathematical tautology; the argument needs rigorous justification (Reviewer 1).
- **Section 6 curated dataset design**: The proposed de-biased dataset removes the spurious feature (gender in CelebA) entirely. This turns the problem into an extrapolation one, where simple augmentation might work. The ablation of aggressive filtering vs. counterfactual generation is missing—it is unclear which component is necessary for the claimed benefits (Reviewer 1).
- **In-support and out-of-support test composition**: Figure 4 does not report what percentage of test samples are classified as in-support for each dataset. It is unclear whether a test set fully composed of in-support samples would achieve similar absolute accuracy as the iid test set (Reviewer 1).

**4. Presentation, clarity, and self-containedness**
- Figure 1’s legend has overlapping text, making it unreadable (Reviewer 2). 
- Key measures are not defined clearly: “Effective Robustness” (ER) is used as a central measure but is not explicitly defined in the main text. The y-axis label of Figure 5 (right) is unclear, and the connection between ER and the margin gain defined in equation (4) is not explained (Reviewer 4).
- The proposed approaches (interventions to prevent exploiting biases, fine-tuning on de-biased data) merely cite existing works without any description, making the paper incomplete and not self-contained (Reviewer 3).
- The appendix is included as part of the main paper (32-page PDF), which is not standard (Reviewer 3).

**5. Practical contributions and takeaway are unclear**
- The overall message is ambiguous: the main paper suggests pre-training is always desirable (larger gains for out-of-support shifts), but Appendix D.2 discusses that pre-training can instill harmful biases. Without knowledge of the test distribution, it is impossible to know whether pre-training is beneficial—so the advice cannot be applied in practice (Reviewer 2).
- The practical guidance is weak: distribution shifts in practice are likely a mix of out-of-support and minority subpopulation effects. The paper does not provide a method to identify out-of-support examples or to construct a balanced dataset free of spurious correlations (Reviewer 4).
- Section 6 does not convincingly demonstrate that the de-biased dataset can be small and non-diverse. No trade-offs or clear limits are given (Reviewer 4).

**6. Unclear contributions and missing technical details**
- Both theoretical and empirical contributions are mild; the paper essentially suggests using a pre-trained model and a balanced dataset, which is already standard practice (Reviewer 4). 
- The paper does not present novel methods, only analyses that confirm intuitive expectations, and the experimental evidence is insufficient to back the stronger claims (Reviewer 4). 
- A reviewer (Reviewer 3) found the paper a very difficult read and could not understand the core theorem despite multiple attempts, calling many arguments ungrounded.

**Reviewer disagreement**: While three reviewers (1,2,4) largely agree on the value of the topic and presentation quality, one reviewer (3) is far more critical, finding the technical details missing and the arguments unconvincing to the point of “poor” soundness. This strong negative assessment on theoretical grounding and definitions contrasts with the moderate criticisms of the others.
