## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies backdoor attacks on CLIP models, analyzing the representation of poisoned samples. The authors find that backdoor-poisoned samples exhibit sparser local neighborhoods than clean samples in the learned feature space. Based on this, they propose using traditional density ratio-based local outlier detectors (e.g., SLOF, DAO) to detect backdoor samples. Experiments show high detection rates at very low poisoning rates (0.01%). The paper also reveals an unintentional backdoor already present in the original CC3M dataset that was trained into a popular OpenCLIP model. The method can clean a million-scale dataset like CC3M in 15 minutes using 4 Nvidia A100 GPUs.

### Strengths
- Focus on a compelling and timely topic in AI security (backdoor attacks on large-scale contrastive models).
- Discovery that backdoor-poisoned samples in CLIP models have distinctive local subspace characteristics: their local neighborhoods are sparser than those of clean samples.
- High detection accuracy even at very low poisoning rates (e.g., 0.01%).
- Efficient: can clean a million-scale dataset (CC3M) in 15 minutes using 4 A100 GPUs, important for practical dataset cleaning.
- Robustness: the method shows stable detection performance across different poisoning rates (including up to 10% with adjusted k).
- Reveals an intriguing and previously unknown unintentional backdoor in the widely-used CC3M dataset, which has been trained into a popular open-source OpenCLIP model.

### Weaknesses
- Technical novelty is very limited: the core idea is simply applying existing outlier detection metrics (SLOF, DAO) without major innovation (one reviewer).
- Only considered simple, naive attack settings with direct poisoning using fixed noise. It is unclear whether the method can detect optimized triggers (e.g., as considered in related work). The authors should compare and comment on more sophisticated attacker settings.
- Lack of discussion and evaluation against adaptive attacks where the attacker knows the defense and can constrain the trigger to avoid sparse neighborhoods.
- No detailed runtime comparison with other baselines. Several detection metrics rely on k-nearest neighbors, and it is not clear why the method would be efficient for very large datasets when nearest-neighbor computation is involved.
- Sensitivity to the localization parameter `k`. The paper does not provide a clear methodology for selecting the optimal `k`, nor does it systematically explore sensitivity across different `k` ranges. As poisoning rate increases, the choice of `k` becomes crucial; the relationship between `k` and poisoning rate needs deeper analysis.
- Dataset dependency: evaluation is performed only on CC3M. Validity on other datasets (e.g., WIT, RedCaps) is not validated. The paper lacks analysis of how dataset properties (image resolution, text length, semantic diversity) affect detection performance, limiting generalizability.
- Limited model architectures: experiments use only ResNet-50 and ViT-B-16. Different architectures (e.g., varying layer depth, attention mechanisms, embedding dimensions) may have different sensitivities to backdoor attacks; the method should be tested on more architectures.
- There is a potential contradiction with prior work [1] that suggests backdoor examples form denser regions, while this paper claims they are sparser. The paper does not adequately explain this difference. The definition of local neighborhoods and the specific context in which sparsity/density is measured needs clarification.
