## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper considers sampling from strongly log-concave distributions on convex and compact supports. It proposes a proximal framework using Euclidean and Gauge projections (the latter via a membership oracle) to enforce constraints, and analyzes several Langevin-type algorithms (Vanilla Langevin, Kinetic Langevin, randomized midpoint implementations) within this framework. The main theoretical contribution is an upper bound on the Wasserstein-1 and Wasserstein-2 errors between the target distribution and its surrogate (obtained by smoothing the indicator function of the support, e.g., via a Moreau envelope). No experiments are provided; the paper is purely theoretical.

### Strengths
- The paper provides new runtime bounds for multiple Langevin variants (Vanilla, Kinetic, randomized midpoint) when sampling from a strongly log-concave and smooth density on a compact convex set, covering both Wasserstein-1 and Wasserstein-2 distances. (Reviewer 2)
- Handling the constraints via proximal or Gauge operators is a technically nontrivial step, as first-order algorithms like LMC cannot directly handle the indicator constraint. (Reviewer 2)
- The bound on the Wasserstein distance between the indicator of the support set and its approximation is tight for the W1 distance, which is a key technical lemma. (Reviewer 3)
- The framework is flexible enough to accommodate different projection oracles and to be combined with several existing sampling methods, yielding convergence guarantees for constrained log-concave sampling. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- One reviewer (score 5) was notably more positive, emphasizing the value of the new runtime bounds and the use of proximal/Gauge projections, while the two other reviewers (scores 3) were less convinced of the novelty and practical significance.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of experimental validation and motivating examples.** The paper contains no simulated or real-data experiments, and the motivation for the problem is not clearly tied to applications relevant to the ICLR community. Reviewers questioned why this problem is especially important for ICLR. (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited novelty.** The paper appears to be a straightforward combination of a new approximation (bounding the indicator in Wasserstein distance) with existing log-concave sampling methods; it does not substantially improve runtime bounds over prior work, nor does it clearly state whether it improves upon the best known algorithm for this problem. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Scope of accuracy assessment.** The approximation analysis is restricted to Wasserstein distances; it is unclear whether it can be extended to other metrics such as total variation or KL divergence, which would be more comprehensive for non-smooth log-concave sampling. (Reviewer 3)
- **Unclear complexity analysis.** The paper does not provide explicit statements about query complexity for the gradient oracle of \(f\) or the membership oracle for \(\mathcal{K}\), making it hard to compare with existing upper and lower bounds (e.g., Kook et al. 2024). (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing comparisons.** The paper does not compare its smoothing approach with other common smoothing techniques (e.g., \(\ell_2/\ell_1\) ball smoothing, Gaussian smoothing) or with existing algorithms for constrained log-concave sampling in terms of oracle complexity. (Reviewer 3)
- **Assumption 2.1 is not well motivated.** It is unclear whether this assumption is without loss of generality, how it differs from the ball-containment assumption in Brosse et al. (2017), and why the assumption is needed beyond a trivial \(r > 0\). The conditions \(m\) and \(M\) in Assumption 2.2 should be explicitly defined. (Reviewer 1)
- **Presentation weaknesses.** The introduction does not clearly delineate the novel technical contributions from borrowed techniques, making it difficult to assess the paper’s originality. There are also minor typos/grammatical errors (e.g., “we the define the”, “set \(\mathcal{D}\) satisfy” should be “satisfies”). (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Potential complexity issues.** Because the non-smooth part is confined to the boundary, bounding the Lipschitz constant of the gradient may lead to poor complexity results, yet this is not addressed. (Reviewer 3)
