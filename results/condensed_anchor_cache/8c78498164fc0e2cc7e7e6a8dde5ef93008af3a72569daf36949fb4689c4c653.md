## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces VARAN, a layer aggregation technique for fine-tuning self-supervised speech models, motivated by variational inference. VARAN adaptively reweights the outputs of different model layers per sample by learning posterior distributions and applying a prior. Experiments on SUPERB benchmarks (ASR, speaker verification, emotion recognition) show improvements over using only the last layer. The method is novel and yields good performance especially on SER.

### Strengths

- VARAN adopts variational inference to semi-automatically extract layer-wise information, providing novel insights into per-sample layer weighting (Reviewers 1,2,4).
- The approach explicitly adapts weights conditioned on each sample, which is shown very effective for the SER task (Reviewer 1).
- The experiments and analyses demonstrate that the actual posterior distribution follows the set prior distribution, and good performance is achieved on several downstream tasks (Reviewer 3,4).
- The paper is written in a clear and concise manner, making the ideas and experiments easy to follow (Reviewer 2).
- The experimental results support the claim that VARAN outperforms last-layer counterparts on ASR, speaker recognition, and emotion recognition (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **No comparison to adapters** (e.g., CHAPTER: Exploiting Convolutional Neural Network Adapters for SSL Speech Models) which are more parameter-efficient and better suited for limited data. Direct comparison is essential given that performance gains over adapters may not be significant (Reviewer 1).
- **ASR evaluation is insufficient.** It uses only LibriSpeech, a read-speech dataset; results may not generalize. Challenging robust benchmarks (e.g., Common Voice, TED-LIUM, or those in arxiv:2401.10446) are needed to verify gains beyond noise from 2nd decimal WER differences (Reviewers 2,3,4). Table 1 shows VARAN fails to outperform weighted combination methods, undermining the claim that sample variation matters (Reviewer 2). Additionally, no language model integration is used, unlike typical SOTA ASR (Reviewer 2).
- **Reliance on human knowledge for prior distribution** is a major practical limitation; one must already know the optimal layer weight distribution for each task. Even within a single task (e.g., ASR), it is unclear if one prior can handle variations across languages, dialects, and noise conditions (Reviewers 1,2,3). Tables 4 and 5 confirm that selecting the right prior is crucial for performance gains (Reviewer 1). Hyper‑parameter tuning (regularization weight in Section 5.2) is still required, undermining the claim that the method avoids tuning (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing technical details**:
  - How discrete variational inference (prior and posterior) is modeled—is Gumbel-Softmax used? The paper only mentions softmax (Reviewer 2).
  - How the architecture differs between speaker‑dependent and speaker‑independent tasks (Reviewer 2).
  - How multihead self‑attention works in Figure 1 is unclear (Reviewer 1).
  - How the method works during inference (weighted average of pooled hidden states?) (Reviewer 4).
  - How baselines (e.g., “weighted”) are exactly computed (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited task diversity**: only three tasks tested. More tasks (speech translation, keyword spotting) would reveal further strengths/limitations (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation issues**: Title and abstract do not mention layer aggregation, giving a confusing impression (Reviewer 4). Typo in line 34: “with these classical” should be “with classical” (Reviewer 1).
- **Only WavLM and Data2vec used** as SSL models; no experiments with other models to assess generality (Reviewer 3).
- **Extra parameters and latency** from the additional multi‑headed attention layer are not quantified (Reviewer 4).
