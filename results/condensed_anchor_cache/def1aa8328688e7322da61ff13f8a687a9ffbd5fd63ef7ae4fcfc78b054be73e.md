## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates how allocating a fixed number of learnable weights across a neural network affects its expressivity, using a student-teacher framework and defining expressivity as the probability that the student can replicate the teacher’s outputs. Theoretical results are derived for linear RNNs, linear MLPs, and extended to shallow ReLU networks, concluding that spreading learnable weights enhances expressive power. The concept is seen as interesting and the theoretical analysis thorough by some reviewers, but others raise concerns about strong assumptions (e.g., matrix invertibility), limited practical relevance, insufficient novelty, and the use of only toy models and synthetic tasks. Reviewers are divided: two are positive (ratings 8 and 6), praising originality and rigorous theory; two are negative (ratings 3), arguing the core result is trivial and the assumptions too restrictive.

### Strengths
- The paper introduces an interesting and relatively underexplored question: how the allocation of a fixed number of learnable weights impacts neural network capacity. This connects to continual learning (e.g., Kirkpatrick et al. PNAS 2017) and the lottery ticket hypothesis.
- A theoretical framework is developed for analyzing expressivity as a function of learnable weight allocation, inspired by parallels with brain mechanisms.
- Theoretical results are provided for linear RNNs and deep linear feedforward networks, concluding that spreading learnable weights across the network yields stronger expressive power. The analysis extends to shallow ReLU networks, showing that predictions from simpler models generalize.
- The theoretical analysis is rigorous, well-structured, and the mathematical derivations appear accurate. The use of a simplified linear model builds intuition effectively.
- The paper is clearly written and well-organized. Figures are clear, informative, and support the main content.
- Both theory and empirical simulations are presented. A benchmark is introduced that could facilitate comparison of different allocation strategies.
- Reviewers 3 and 4 were particularly positive, noting the paper’s potential to deepen understanding of model expressivity in the context of rising training costs and to offer insights for neuroscience. Reviewer 4 called it “a great piece of research” and highlighted the uniqueness of the question.

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumptions limit applicability**: The paper assumes that “any drawn square matrix is invertible” (l197), which is not generally true for arbitrary distributions. This assumption is a major concern (R1, R2); its implications for practical relevance are not adequately addressed. The student-teacher setup also requires matching architectures, making extension to real-world applications difficult even heuristically (R2). Experiments use only simple, fully-connected networks and synthetic datasets; it is unclear how conclusions extrapolate to convolutional networks, structured connectivity, or more complex datasets (R4).
- **Triviality and insufficient novelty**: Two reviewers (R1, R2) found the main conclusion—that more widespread allocation of learnable weights increases expressivity—trivial. The paper does not sufficiently differentiate itself from existing work on network pruning, lottery ticket hypothesis, or resource allocation (R1). The study focuses only on expressivity, ignoring other important evaluation criteria (generalization, transfer, continual learning), limiting the scope and potential insights (R2). (Reviewers 3 and 4 disagreed, finding the question novel and valuable.)
- **Proofs and presentation concerns**: Proofs are written informally, making validation difficult (R2). Key assumptions are dispersed throughout the text and should be explicitly listed when stating theorems (R2). A notation issue at L147 (joint vs. conditional) is flagged (R1). Theorem 3.3 could be developed further, potentially in the appendix (R3). An introductory figure summarizing the student-teacher setup would improve accessibility (R3).
- **Weak neuroscience connection**: The abstract claims that “biological systems like the brain… only a subset of synaptic weights are modified during learning,” a grandiose and unsubstantiated statement that should be supported with evidence or removed (R4). The paper should more clearly connect its findings to neuroscience applications and strengthen the link between neural networks and brain mechanisms (R3).
- **Limited generalizability of benchmarks**: The theoretical benchmarks rely on toy models; extending analysis to larger, more complex networks would increase impact (R3). Results are validated only on synthetic tasks (R2). The role of weight distribution may become less significant as network size increases, because more polynomials increase the probability of finding a solution; this scaling behavior is not explored (R3).
- **Missing experiments and comparisons**: Why were experiments not run with gradient descent? Doing so might allow access to larger networks (R3). Are correlated or structured teacher weights considered (R2)? The degree of polynomials in the analytical framework may also play a role (R2). The effect of network initialization and layer scaling on expressivity under a given learning algorithm is not addressed (R3). Connections to weight protection strategies in continual learning could be explored further (R4).
- **Lack of explicit limitations**: The paper would benefit from a more explicit discussion of where the theoretical approach may diverge from empirical phenomena observed in practical neural networks (R3). A clearer connection between theorems and real-world training/performance is needed (R1).
