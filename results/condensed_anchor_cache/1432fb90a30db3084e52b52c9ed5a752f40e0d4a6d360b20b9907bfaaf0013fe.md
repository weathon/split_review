## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes HAct – activation histograms of neural network layer outputs – as a descriptor for OOD detection. For each class, the Wasserstein barycenter of training-set activation histograms is stored; during inference, the distance between a test sample’s histogram and the barycenter of its predicted class is thresholded to decide ID vs OOD. A sub-sampling strategy accelerates inference. The method shows extremely high performance on ImageNet benchmarks with ResNet50 and MobileNet-v2 (e.g., 0.03% FPR95, 99.98% AUROC).

### Strengths
- The use of Wasserstein barycenters of activation histograms to quantify distributional differences between ID and OOD samples is intuitive and interesting.
- The method is simple, efficient, and easy to implement.
- On the ImageNet benchmark, performance is exceptionally high: e.g., 0.03% FPR95 at 95% TPR and 99.98% AUROC, outperforming prior state-of-the-art by 20.67% in FPR; these results are described as “amazing” and “essentially solving the problem.”
- The presentation is easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 5) is substantially more positive about the paper, while the other two (score 3) are more skeptical, particularly about the reported results.
- **Lack of explanation for extreme performance**: The extremely high performance on ImageNet (Table 1) is presented without sufficient discussion or analysis. For example, if FPR95 is nearly zero on Places, one reviewer asks whether this implies that leave-one-out class training could achieve 95% accuracy on Places, and requests an experimental demonstration.
- **Missing experiments and comparisons**:
  - ID classification accuracy is not reported in Table 1; if HAct can perfectly separate ID from OOD per predicted category, the classification accuracy should be extremely high.
  - The performance on CIFAR (Table 2) is much worse than on ImageNet, yet no discussion is given.
  - Experiments on only two architectures (ResNet50, MobileNet-v2); other common networks (e.g., BiT, RepVGG) and Vision Transformers (ViT) are not tested.
  - The chosen OOD datasets (SUN, Places) are known to have substantial overlap with ImageNet-1K (see [c] “In or Out? Fixing ImageNet Out-of-Distribution Detection Evaluation”, ICML 2023). The authors should test on cleaner OOD benchmarks such as OpenImage-O [d] and ImageNet-O [e].
  - Classical baselines (MSP [a], Mahalanobis [b]) are not reviewed or compared.
- **Unclear technical details**:
  - How is the “most likely category” \(c^*\) determined? By softmax prediction or by histogram distance?
  - For multiple layers, how are the thresholds \(\tau\) chosen such that the overall FPR95 criterion (95% TPR) is satisfied when ID is returned only if all layers say ID?
  - How is the AUROC computed when multiple layers are used? The text suggests a logical AND combination, but AUROC requires a continuous score for ranking.
  - In Eq. (1), how are the partition values \(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m\) determined? What are typical values of \(m\) and \(\alpha_i\), and what is the effect of different \(\alpha_i\) on performance?
  - Why is the absolute value \(|W_{ij} x_j|\) used for computing activation histograms instead of ReLU or the network’s original activation functions? Why is the bias term ignored? If the original network has bias parameters, are they simply omitted?
  - Why not compute finer histograms per input channel \(i\) or output channel \(j\)?
- **Evaluation concerns**:
  - Is the ImageNet-1K validation set used in the computation of FPR95/AUROC? The training set used for tuning histograms must be independent of the evaluation set.
  - To verify that the method does not simply detect any non-ImageNet sample, the reviewers request testing on the ImageNet-1K test set as if it were an OOD dataset.
  - The surprisingly high AUROC (99%) and low FPR95 (0.x%) on overlapping OOD datasets raise questions about the evaluation protocol.
