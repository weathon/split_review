## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Geometric Invariance Hypothesis (GIH), arguing that during training the input-space curvature of a neural network remains invariant under transformations in certain architecture-dependent directions. It defines two maps—the average geometry (\(G_F\)) and the average geometry evolution (\(\Delta_F\))—to compactly summarize the input-space geometry and its training dynamics. Starting from a non‑linear binary classification problem on a hyperplane in a high‑dimensional space, the authors observe that an MLP can solve it regardless of the hyperplane’s orientation while a ResNet cannot. Through theoretical analysis and experiments on a binarized subset of CIFAR‑10 with MLPs, CNNs, and ViTs, the paper shows that \(\Delta_F\) at initialization approximates the projection of data covariance onto \(G_F\), causing the invariance when \(G_F\) is low‑rank. The paper then links GIH to generalization and empirically observes consequences such as collapse to noise and memory. One reviewer (score 6) is more critical, noting imprecisions and questioning broader validity; the other three (score 8) are more positive but raise complementary concerns about experimental scope and intuitiveness.

### Strengths
- The introduction of GIH is novel, extending the Neural Anisotropy Directions (NADs) of Ortiz‑Jimenez et al. (2021) to non‑linear decision boundaries.
- The concepts of average geometry and average geometry evolution provide new architectural‑dependent tools to quantify input‑space geometry, moving beyond parameter‑space analyses (e.g., NTK, AGOP).
- The paper builds up to the main hypothesis with a clear chain of reasoning, extensive mathematical proofs, and explicit statements of assumptions and limitations.
- Experiments on real networks (ResNets, ViTs) and a real dataset (CIFAR‑10) support the theoretical claims, despite the settings being highly overparameterized and relatively simple.
- The conclusions are sensible: directions in input space that do not support the training data distribution may remain unchanged by training dynamics.
- Section 5 provides plausible practical implications, giving motivation for this very theoretical direction.
- The paper is reasonably well‑written; the material is technically dense but mostly coherent after thorough reading.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of intuitive explanation** for why ResNets behave differently from MLPs. The paper could benefit from illustrative examples before the mathematical formalism, especially clarifying how skip connections influence input‑space geometry relative to direct mapping.
- **Limited architectural comparison.** Only ResNets are examined in depth; it is unclear whether the observed behaviors are due to skip connections or are common across other architectures (e.g., AlexNet, dense connections, different pooling types).
- **Experimental scope concerns.** Experiments are conducted on small datasets (a subset of CIFAR‑10 with binarized labels) and shallow networks. Generalizability to larger datasets (full CIFAR‑10, ImageNet), deeper models, and tasks requiring learning latent features is questionable. The paper does not measure weight evolution to confirm whether training stays in the “lazy” (NTK) regime.
- **Definitional gaps and imprecisions.** Several terms are left undefined or used imprecisely: “SSE loss,” \(y_\mu\), “input‑space curvature,” “input geometry,” “noise dimension,” “all possible values of \(\theta”\) without distribution. Also, “momentums” (L244, L246) should probably be “moments.”
- **Theory is based on expected second moments of the data**, which captures the smallest supporting linear subspace. The manifold hypothesis (small intrinsic dimension despite large linear dimension) is not captured; this may limit the theory’s ability to track experiments and generate prescriptive insights.
- **Motivation for Conjectures about training dynamics is weak.** Little intuition is given for why predictions made at initialization should transfer to training dynamics. It is unclear whether an infinite‑/large‑width assumption is implicitly made and whether the conjectures are meant to hold in scaling limits or at reasonable scales.
- **Missing references and related work.** The main text lacks a comprehensive overview; important related work such as Neural Anisotropy Directions is only cited in the appendix. The authors should move more discussion from Appendix A.1 into the main text.
- **Confusion between expressivity and generalization.** Lines L058+ seem to conflate approximation and statistical guarantees. The fact that an MLP is universal does not imply it can generalize effectively.
- **Assumption i.i.d. parameters is insufficient** to conclude \(G_F \propto I\); a Gaussian with non‑zero mean is a counterexample. The assumption of zero‑mean i.i.d. must be made explicit in the main text.
- **Claim that MLPs have “no inductive bias” (L210, L224, L249) is incorrect.** Even in the NTK regime, MLPs exhibit strong spectral bias for low frequencies. It should be stated that MLPs at initialization have no preferred input‑space directions (i.e., an isotropic prior) rather than claiming no inductive bias.
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms or missing ablations:**
  - Does simple early‑stopping solve the generalization gap observed in Figure 1? (R1)
  - Is the conclusion that \(\Delta_F\) becomes label‑independent solely due to \(\mathbb{E}_\theta[\theta]=0\) at initialization? (R1)
  - What is the motivation for adding label noise (L406)? Would the same trends occur without it? (R1)
  - Could the phenomenon be fully explained by overparameterization and lazy training? (R1, R4)
  - How does GIH behave under SGD with large learning rates? (R3)
  - Is the removal of normalization from networks crucial? Would the trend deteriorate with normalization? (R4)
  - Is the approach useful for architecture‑based optimal dataset subsampling? (R2)
