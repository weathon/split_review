## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether diffusion models can generate samples with new information beyond the training data, using information theory to argue that ideal models produce no new information. It introduces the concept of Diverse Samples (DS)—generated images that deviate from the training manifold due to model approximations—and proposes a method to identify DS via feature extraction and boundary detection. Experiments on a Chest X-ray dataset show that DS can improve classification accuracy for underrepresented classes, suggesting a role in data augmentation.

### Strengths
- Raises an important and essential question about whether diffusion models can generate truly novel samples and thus serve as reliable data augmentation.
- Reveals limitations of diffusion models in generating out-of-distribution data through both information-theoretic analysis and experiments.
- The proposed DS-based augmentation demonstrates significant performance improvements on imbalanced chest X-ray classification tasks compared to traditional generative model-based approaches.
- The paper’s concepts and algorithms are clear and easy to follow; experiments seem reproducible and source code is provided.

### Weaknesses
- The claim that diffusion models do not generate new information during training and inference is well-established in generative modeling and not novel. Prior works (e.g., Ramachandran et al., AAAI 2024; Graham et al., CVPR 2023) have already addressed out-of-distribution generation in diffusion models. The paper should include and discuss the differences from these works.
- The mathematical analysis is not sound. Equation (13) states that “the ideal mutual information at the start and end should be the same,” which is questionable because \(x_T\) is pure Gaussian noise and should carry no information relevant to \(x_0\). Additionally, a proof for equations (9) and (10) is missing; it is unclear if the property holds when using a scheduler that ensures zero SNR at the final timestep \(T\) (e.g., Lin et al., WACV 2024).
- The arguments are not convincing. The paper uses a pre-trained latent diffusion model that could have been trained on data that includes the four datasets used, so generation of diverse samples does not imply they contain “information not present in the training data.” Moreover, the assertion that DS samples contain novel augmentation-worthy information is questionable; it is more likely they represent low-probability, low-quality outputs that the model incorrectly generated. The decreasing trend of DS samples with improved samplers (Table 1) supports this interpretation.
- The proposed DS method bears significant similarity to the technique in improved Precision-Recall (Kynkäänniemi et al., NeurIPS 2019) for identifying out-of-distribution samples. The DS method relies on a less robust approach (distance-based threshold on deep features versus KNN), potentially making it more susceptible to noise and outliers.
- The t-SNE dimension used for feature extraction is not explicitly mentioned. Using only top significant features may miss out-of-distribution information present in less significant features. The correlation between the number of diverse samples and the number of t-SNE dimensions should be evaluated.
- Experimental results are not compelling. The DS accuracy for the PNEUMONIA class is lower than that of the original images (Table 2). Additional experiments on different datasets are needed, not just one Chest X-ray dataset. It is also unclear whether the performance boost comes from increased data volume or from subtle variations; this should be examined. The effectiveness of DS augmentation in balanced datasets and general image classification tasks beyond chest X-rays is not shown.
- The paper’s perspective on generative data augmentation diverges from the prevailing understanding: data augmentation is typically used when training data is a limited subset of the true distribution, and generative models are expected to approximate that true distribution. The paper’s view that generated samples may not contain distinct information does not align with this widely accepted view (e.g., Chen et al., Neurocomputing 2024).
- The proposed DS method is susceptible to incorrect sampling, which can generate erroneous data samples. The paper does not discuss techniques to address this limitation. Also, a comparison with methods that use diffusion models for minority sample generation (e.g., Um et al., ICLR 2024) is missing.
