## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers found the research question—using curriculum learning and co-evolution to address sparse rewards in multi-agent systems—relevant and significant. One reviewer (score 5) considered the direction promising and the approach reasonable, while the three others (score 3) raised serious concerns about novelty, experimental rigor, presentation, and theoretical grounding. All reviewers agreed that the paper’s weaknesses substantially undermine its current quality.

### Strengths
- Integrating curriculum learning with evolutionary algorithms for multi-agent cooperation in sparse-reward settings is a promising research direction.
- The paper clearly motivates why the sparse-reward problem is especially challenging in multi-agent environments.
- The research question (leveraging curriculum learning for MARL) is both relevant and important to the RL community.
- The proposed approach appears reasonable on an intuitive level.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and theoretical foundation.** The method largely combines existing techniques (automatic curriculum learning, evolutionary algorithms) without clearly distinguishing contributions. No theoretical justification is given for why the particular design (e.g., co-evolution, crossover/mutation) should work; engineering or theoretical motivations behind core design choices are missing. Some reviewers noted that using an evolutionary algorithm for curriculum design in MARL is not new (e.g., Song et al., CoRL 2024 – *Genetic Algorithm for Curriculum Design in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning*), and this work should be compared and discussed.
- **Inadequate experimental evaluation.**  
  - Environments (MPE, HnS) are considered too simple; in some tasks, baselines without curriculum learning also achieve optimal results, suggesting sparse rewards may not be a real issue for those domains.  
  - No learning curves are provided – only tables (Table 2) and a few figures (Figs. 3–4). Figures lack variance/confidence intervals (no shaded areas), making it impossible to judge statistical significance or learning dynamics.  
  - Table 2 uses unconventional “timesteps” units; clarity is questioned.  
  - The experimental section merely reports results without analysis or insights; ablation results are sparse (e.g., only mutation is missing; the effect of many hyperparameters is not analyzed).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons.** Important multi-agent baselines are omitted: Policy Space Response Oracle (PSRO, Lanctot et al., 2017), RACE (which also combines evolution and MARL), and Song et al. (2024). These are needed to contextualize performance and differentiate the proposed method.
- **Poor presentation and writing quality.**  
  - Logical structure is weak: Section 4 lacks a clear introduction; sub-section titles on L233 and L243 are identical.  
  - Figures 1 and 2 lack corresponding in-text descriptions, making them hard to interpret.  
  - Many writing issues: inconsistent font bolding, improper spacing in Formula 8, incomplete proofreading (e.g., \( f^{\widetilde}_{all} \) notation error).  
  - Redundant phrasing (e.g., line 246 repeatedly states MAPPO details will not be discussed).
- **Specific unanswered design questions (overlap with criticisms).**  
  - Formula 3: How can agents and tasks be directly “measured”? Does this assume a distance in state space that is only feasible for simple tasks?  
  - Formula 4: Why is it designed that way?  
  - How is crossover implemented? How are tasks represented and encoded?  
  - Why are “Variational Individual-perspective Crossover and Mutation” more efficient than alternatives?  
  - Line 249: What does “better initial policy performance” refer to? Better than what, and why must it be better?
- **One reviewer (score 5) was more positive overall** and indicated the score could be raised if questions are addressed, but still noted the same weaknesses regarding experiments, writing, and missing ablation/hyperparameter analysis.
