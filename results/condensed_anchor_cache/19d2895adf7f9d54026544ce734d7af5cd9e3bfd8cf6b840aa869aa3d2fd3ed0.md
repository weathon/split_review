## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviews (scores 8, 6, 6) evaluate DGPPO, a framework that learns both a discrete graph CBF (DGCBF) and a high-performance safe policy for multi-agent systems with unknown discrete-time dynamics, changing neighborhoods, and input constraints. DGPPO combines RL with discrete-time CBFs, does not require a nominal policy, and claims constant hyperparameters across environments. Reviewer 1 (score 8) is more positive, praising the elegant handling of unknown dynamics and theoretical foundation. Reviewers 2 and 3 (score 6) appreciate the RL+CBF combination and motivation but raise significant concerns about theoretical justification, scalability, performance metrics, and hyperparameter sensitivity. All reviewers note the contribution of extending CBFs to discrete-time and the thorough empirical comparison.

### Strengths
- **Novel discrete-time CBF + RL integration**: DGPPO elegantly solves the discrete-time distributed MASOCP with unknown dynamics, an assumption absent in prior work that required differentiating through known transition functions (Reviewer 1).
- **Solid theoretical foundation**: Theorems provide rigorous support for discrete-time setting (Reviewer 1); rigorous theoretical presentation appreciated (Reviewer 2); extension to discrete-time CBFs and introduction of DGCBFs for neighborhood adaptability overcomes limitations of continuous-time CBFs (Reviewer 2).
- **No need for predefined nominal policy**: Approach is well-motivated and tackles safety in unknown environments without requiring a nominal policy—a substantial improvement for MARL (Reviewer 2). The problem is general and challenging as neither system model nor nominal policy is available in advance (Reviewer 3).
- **Safety without additional filters**: The learned policy is safe and does not need safety filters during implementation (Reviewer 3).
- **Extensive simulations**: State-of-the-art baselines are compared across multiple multi-agent environments (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical issues (Reviewer 3, echoed by Reviewer 1)**:  
  - The modified constraint (11b) requiring the expectation of the CBF to be negative is too strict and cannot be satisfied by common policy classes like Gaussian policies, potentially leading to overly conservative policies or preventing convergence.  
  - The gradient in (12) when safety is violated is not clearly linked to the score function gradient in (41); the update rule is unclear.  
  - Theorem 3 is difficult to understand, uses an impractical orthogonality assumption, and replaces the state distribution induced by the policy with a constant distribution—undermining gradient validity. The purpose of Theorem 3 and its relationship to (14) is unclear.  
  - The advantage of using discrete graph CBFs over a standard discrete CBF on the entire multi-agent system (treated as a single agent) is not justified. The distributed control nature could still be preserved with an end-to-end observation-based policy.  
  - Theorem 4 lacks a clear link between discrete graph CBF and standard discrete CBF; forward invariance of the safe set under the graph CBF is not explicitly shown.  
  - Discrepancy: safety constraint (11b) uses a stochastic policy, but Figure 1 uses deterministic policies for estimating the CBF.  
  - Agents are assigned different GAEs, implying heterogeneity that is not explained in the homogeneous setting.

- **Limited scalability (Reviewer 1 and 2)**: Scalability experiments only test up to 7 agents (Reviewer 1). Testing only 5 agents (vs. 3) is too limited to claim scalability (Reviewer 2). The algorithm likely requires retraining for each new number of agents, unlike continuous-time GCBF+ which was trained on 8 and deployed to 1024 (Reviewer 1). The computational complexity of GNNs in larger MAS with frequent neighborhood changes may impact real-time applicability (Reviewer 2).

- **Missing task performance metrics (Reviewer 2)**: Results focus on safety metrics; without reporting mean global success rate or reward, it is unclear whether agents achieve the global objective or simply stay stationary. Since DGPPO does not incorporate a nominal policy, there is a risk that safety constraints dominate task completion. The paper does not explain how safety constraints are prevented from excessively dominating the objective.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity vs. claimed robustness (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 1)**: The claim that DGPPO uses a constant set of hyperparameters is not fully supported. Figure 6b shows that the parameter ν—controlling the weight on constraint minimization—significantly impacts performance, with misalignment leading to overly conservative or unsafe policies. How the benefit of constant hyperparameters is demonstrated (e.g., wall-clock time comparison of tuning) is not provided (Reviewer 1, question 3).

- **Stability explanation lacking (Reviewer 1)**: The stability of DGPPO relative to baselines is not appropriately explained—is it purely empirical or is there theoretical justification?

- **Unknown dynamics assumption questioned (Reviewer 1)**: The environments considered are the same as those in prior work [1] but used without the available dynamics. It would be more interesting to apply DGPPO to environments with genuinely complex unknown dynamics (e.g., Mujoco robots).

- **Nominal policy not needed? (Reviewer 1)**: Since a simple nominal policy like PID is easy to construct for the given environments, the advantage of not requiring one is questioned.

- **Sample inefficiency (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 1)**: The approach requires both stochastic and deterministic rollouts to enforce DGCBFs, introducing significant sample inefficiency that may limit scalability to larger MAS (Reviewer 2). Training time is reported as 12 hours, but no sample efficiency or wall-clock comparison with baselines is provided (Reviewer 1).

- **Scalability comparison (Reviewer 1)**: The experiments in Figure 5 on scalability appear quite close to baselines; a better or more revealing comparison is needed.

- **Clarity on observations (Reviewer 1)**: The LiDAR part of the observation is unclear; only in LiDAR environments are 32 equally spaced rays used? How are obstacles in VMAS presented to the agent?
