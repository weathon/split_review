## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Spark Transformer, an architectural variant that incorporates sparse activations in both the FFN and attention via an approximate statistical top‑\(k\) algorithm (using a Gaussian quantile function to estimate the top‑\(k\) threshold from the mean and standard deviation of activation maps) and a low‑rank predictor. The sparsity regularizers are applied during pretraining (using the Gemma 2 recipe), thereby avoiding post‑training adapters. The method achieves 8% nonzeros in FFN activations, a maximum of 256 attended tokens, 3.1× FLOP reduction, and 1.70× prefill / 1.79× decoding speedup on a 16‑core CPU VM. The paper provides theoretical error‑bound analysis for the approximate top‑\(k\) and a custom sparse GEMM implementation. Reviewers agree the problem is timely and the approach is mathematically sound, but raise significant concerns about novelty relative to prior statistical top‑\(k\) work [1–3,6], limited experimental validation (no GPU/TPU results, no speedup comparisons against sparsity‑based LLM baselines such as DejaVu, ProSparse, LLaMA‑ReGLU), the overhead of pretraining from scratch, and the absence of an ablation on the importance of pre‑training versus fine‑tuning the regularizers.

### Strengths
- **Important domain and timely problem**: Sparsity‑based LLM inference acceleration is a critical area, and the paper tackles it with a drop‑in replacement structure that exploits sparsity in both FFN and attention simultaneously.
- **New approach in the pipeline**: Unlike most prior works that apply sparsity post‑training, Spark Transformer incorporates the sparsity regularizers (statistical top‑\(k\) and low‑rank predictor) during pretraining, potentially saving later adapter training and preserving model quality close to the dense baseline.
- **Mathematical clarity**: The paper provides a solid analytical derivation of the statistical top‑\(k\) algorithm, including clear error‑bound proofs, and explains how it reduces expensive top‑\(k\) selection to a lightweight mean/std computation.
- **Hardware‑efficient design on SIMD machines**: The approximate top‑\(k\) method is algorithmically efficient (\(\mathcal{O}(2d)\) vs. \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\)) and, when combined with the sparse vector‑matrix multiplication (table look‑up), yields measurable speedups on commercially available CPUs.
- **Concrete FLOP analysis**: The paper quantifies the FLOP reduction (3.1×) and demonstrates a custom sparse GEMM that makes the theoretical savings practical.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty of the statistical top‑\(k\) core**: The statistical top‑\(k\) algorithm for threshold estimation has been extensively studied in gradient/activation compression [1–3] (and [2] introduced Gaussian‑based threshold estimation, while [1] extended it to various distributions). The paper must clearly differentiate its contribution from these existing methods. Additionally, reference [6] proposes a similar partial‑weight approach to predict approximate token scores, but no comparison is provided.
- **No speedup comparison with existing sparsity‑based LLM inference methods**: All CPU speedup results are shown only against the original dense Gemma 2, not against state‑of‑the‑art sparsity approaches such as DejaVu [4–6], ProSparse, or LLaMA‑ReGLU. The provided table (Table ? at L324) indicates that for the first four benchmarks, ProSparse and LLaMA‑ReGLU achieve lower FLOP/token, yet no direct inference latency comparison is made.
- **Missing comparisons with token‑pruning methods**: The paper does not compare against token pruning or other sparse attention baselines, which would help contextualize the speedup claims.
- **No experimental validation of the approximation error**: The paper provides only a theoretical error bound for the approximate top‑\(k\) but fails to measure the overlap between the approximate selection and the true top‑\(k\), nor does it analyze how this gap affects downstream performance (e.g., accuracy or generation quality).
- **Hardware generality unproven**: All experiments are performed on a 16‑core CPU VM. The paper does not show speedup on GPUs, TPUs, or other accelerators, where the table‑look‑up approach is not friendly to tensor‑core or systolic‑array hardware. It is unclear whether the method can scale to these mainstream platforms, even for edge deployment.
- **Concerns about pretraining from scratch**: The method requires training the model from scratch (Gemma 2 recipe), incurring a large computational overhead. The paper does not demonstrate that this is more practical than existing post‑training sparsification pipelines (e.g., DejaVu). The importance of applying the regularizers during pretraining vs. fine‑tuning (post‑training) is not ablated; if post‑training fine‑tuning suffices, the approach would be more widely applicable.
- **Insufficient accuracy evaluation**: The accuracy benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, AGIEval, BBH, Winogrande, HellaSwag) are limited. More extensive evaluation (e.g., via lm‑evaluation‑harness [5]) is needed, and one reviewer notes that the HumanEval score for Gemma 2 in Table 2 is much lower than the published value – the discrepancy needs explanation.
- **Lack of analysis for batched inference on CPUs vs. GPUs**: It is unclear whether the tokens/watt or tokens/second/watt on CPUs can outperform GPUs during batched inference (even batch size 1 is shown), especially given the sparsity advantage.
- **No discussion of distributed training / tensor parallelism**: The paper does not address how the statistical top‑\(k\) behaves when activations are sharded across devices; this could introduce extra communication overhead or skewed top‑\(k distributions.
- **Missing comparison with other efficient top‑\(k\) implementations**: Quasi‑sort with \(\mathcal{O}(\log N)\) complexity [7] and JAX’s built‑in top‑\(k\) are not compared to the statistical top‑\(k\) algorithm.
- **Typographical error**: On page 6, line 315, Eq. (13) should be Eq. (14).
- **Unanswered reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms**: 
  - What is the actual distribution of activation scores and how much does it deviate from the Gaussian assumption? (Impact on approximate top‑\(k\) fidelity.)
  - What is the overlap between the approximate statistical top‑\(k\) and the true top‑\(k\) selection, and what is the resulting performance cost?
  - Can the regularizers be applied as a post‑training fine‑tune stage to avoid full pretraining?
  - Why is the HumanEval score for Gemma 2 lower than reported?
  - What is the origin of the factors 0.5 and 1.5 in Eq. (L065)?
  - Is the model trained with tensor parallelism, and if so, how does activation sharding affect the top‑\(k\) overhead?
