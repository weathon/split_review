## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes GameOpt, a game-theoretic approach to combinatorial Bayesian optimization (CBO) that reformulates the problem as finding a Nash equilibrium of a cooperative game over variables, using GP-UCB as the utility. It is applied to protein design. Two reviewers (scores 8, 8) are positive, citing the innovative formulation, sample efficiency guarantees, and comprehensive protein experiments. Two reviewers (scores 3, 3) are negative, raising major concerns about clarity, lack of global convergence guarantees, limited evaluation against modern baselines, and insufficient novelty. There is strong disagreement on the method's practical value and theoretical contribution.

### Strengths

- **Innovative formulation**: The paper introduces a novel game-theoretic perspective to combinatorial Bayesian optimization by framing variable interactions as a cooperative game and seeking Nash equilibria, which is an interesting conceptual shift from kernel design (R1, R2). R3 also finds the solution novel. However, R4 argues that treating each variable as a player is not novel (citing Shapley value GP and additive kernels) and the equation on line 295 closely resembles an existing optimistic game-theoretic approach [5]; this reviewer considers the combination straightforward.
- **Sample efficiency guarantee**: The algorithm provides a sample efficiency guarantee (convergence to an $\epsilon$-Nash equilibrium), which is valuable for costly experimental design (R1, R2). R4 notes that this does not equate to global optimum convergence, and R3 finds the regret bound not insightful because it is similar to GP-UCB but only guarantees local optima.
- **Comprehensive protein design experiments**: The experiments on four real-world protein datasets are substantial, and ablation studies are provided (R2). R3 criticizes the lack of diverse synthetic benchmarks and insufficient comparison with state-of-the-art combinatorial BO baselines. R4 argues evaluation is limited: no comparison with modern deep learning approaches (diffusion, latent BO) or simple dimensionality reduction baselines, making it hard to assess practicality.
- **Important problem**: Combinatorial Bayesian optimization is an important subfield with applications like protein design, and the paper addresses the challenge of large discrete spaces (R1, R2, R3).
- **Clear presentation**: R3 finds the presentation clear and easy-to-follow, but R4 disagrees strongly, stating that the clarity is the weakest part due to many unexplained symbols and confusing definitions (especially Sections 1–3).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and mathematical presentation**: The paper suffers from severe clarity issues, particularly in the problem statement and Section 3. Key symbols (e.g., $x$, $r^{(i)}$, $v$) are undefined or used inconsistently. The difference between $d$ and $n$ is confusing; the definition of $f(x)$ and how UCB functions as a reward per player is unclear. The meaning of "arg eq" and Equation (2) is not explained. The payoff $v$ in Algorithms 2 and 3 is not defined (R4, high severity; R2 also asks "What is the $v$ function in Algorithm 2?").
- **Lack of global convergence guarantee**: The method finds local Nash equilibria, not necessarily the global optimum of the objective function. There is no mechanism to avoid getting stuck in local optima, and exploration-exploitation balance is not explicitly considered (R3, R4). R3 suggests random restarts could help, similar to trust-region BO. R4 asks how the algorithm guarantees global convergence and argues that the paper should state it is a local optimization approach. R1 notes that the price of anarchy discussion is insufficient to justify the trade-off between local and global optimality. Reviewers 1 and 2 do not see this as a major weakness given the sample complexity guarantees, but R3 and R4 consider it critical.
- **Limited novelty in BO guarantees**: The theoretical results largely inherit from standard GP-UCB, and the regret bound does not improve over existing guarantees for local optima (R1, R3). R4 notes the equation on line 295 is nearly identical to an existing optimistic game-theoretic approach [5] (not cited). The idea of treating each variable as a player is not new (R4).
- **Insufficient comparison with baselines**: The paper does not compare against high-dimensional BO methods (e.g., trust region, variable selection), modern deep learning approaches for protein design (diffusion models, latent BO), or tree-based surrogate models (R1, R3, R4). The baseline GP-UCB is not tailored for batch optimization, and the batch size $B$ is not properly ablated (R3). R4 suggests that simply reducing embedding dimensionality could allow standard GP-UCB to work well. R2 asks about computational efficiency of Step 5 in Algorithm 1 (finding top $B$ equilibria) and wants complexity analysis.
- **Missing limitation**: The method assumes each dimension has the same number of discrete variables $|\mathcal{X}^{(i)}| = d$ (typically $d=20$ for amino acids), which does not hold in general CBO problems (e.g., hyperparameter optimization with mixed continuous/discrete spaces). The existence of a Nash equilibrium cannot be guaranteed in such cases (R4). R2 also asks: "Does this algorithm work without assumption $|\mathcal{X}^{(i)}|=d$? It means that in this case the numbers of possible decision variables are different."
- **Challenges in finding Nash equilibrium**: Finding a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete, and practical solvers may not guarantee convergence to a meaningful equilibrium, especially in high-dimensional spaces. The paper does not adequately discuss why the benefits outweigh the computational challenges or how solvers are applied in practice (R1). R2 also notes the need for more explanation of how equilibrium finding relates to global optimality and how top $B$ equilibria are selected efficiently.
- **Insufficient discussion of exploration-exploitation trade-off**: The method uses GP-UCB, which inherently balances exploration and exploitation, but the equilibrium-seeking acquisition function does not explicitly incorporate exploration beyond UCB. The regret bound only guarantees local convergence, which may undermine the data efficiency of BO (R3).
- **Limited synthetic evaluation**: The experiments focus only on protein design. Well-known synthetic CBO problems (e.g., from state-of-the-art combinatorial BO works) are not included, making it hard to evaluate performance in a controlled setting (R3).
- **Price of anarchy justification is weak**: The brief discussion of price of anarchy does not convincingly motivate the game formulation for protein design or quantify the potential suboptimality (R1). 
- **Influence of batch size unclear**: The paper does not ablate the batch size $B$ sufficiently. R3 recommends considering smaller $B$, such as $B=1$, first, and asks in what case the algorithm may perform worse.
- **Clarity on tractability**: R3 asks: "How to prove that solving equation (2) is more tractable than solving other acquisition functions?" The paper does not provide a direct explanation of why the Nash equilibrium formulation is computationally easier than standard acquisition maximization.

### Notes on Reviewer Disagreement

- Two reviewers (1 and 2, scores 8) value the theoretical guarantees and find the method promising, while two (3 and 4, scores 3) consider the theoretical contribution weak and the evaluation insufficient.
- Reviewer 4 finds the clarity severely lacking, while Reviewer 3 finds it clear.
- Reviewer 4 explicitly states the novelty is limited and cites missing prior work; other reviewers consider the formulation innovative.
- Reviewer 3 wants more diverse synthetic benchmarks and clear batch-size ablation; reviewers 1 and 2 do not raise these issues.
- Reviewer 4 emphasizes the lack of comparison with modern deep learning methods; reviewers 1 and 2 mention missing high-dimensional BO baselines but not deep learning.
