## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces Adaptive Transformer Programs, building on the “Learning Transformer Programs” framework to create more expressive and interpretable Transformer models. Three main modifications are proposed: (1) a smooth transition mechanism between Gumbel‑Softmax and Sparsemax for better discrete optimization; (2) an uncertainty‑aware gating (based on Jensen–Shannon Divergence) that allows the model to choose between categorical attention (interpretable) and score‑based attention (more expressive); and (3) position‑aware attention that supports numerical values while keeping queries and keys categorical. Experiments on in‑context learning, algorithmic problems (sorting, Dyck languages), and NLP benchmarks (named entity recognition, text classification) show improvements in accuracy and reduced program length (lines of code) over the original Transformer Programs. An ablation study on one RASP task evaluates each component.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important problem: making intrinsically interpretable models more expressive while retaining interpretability.
- All three modifications are well‑motivated and clearly explained; particularly the Gumbel‑Sparsemax hybrid and the JSD‑based gating mechanism.
- Experimental evaluation is solid and comprehensive, covering synthetic/RASP tasks and NLP tasks.
- The use of lines of code as an interpretability metric is interesting.
- A thorough ablation study is provided, demonstrating the contribution of each component.
- The proposed method produces programs with fewer lines of code and slightly higher accuracy, and scales better (less performance degradation with larger vocabularies and sequence lengths).
- Paper is well‑written and easy to follow; the presentation of new architectural components is clear.
- One reviewer praised originality, noting no follow‑up work to the original Learning Transformer Programs was found; another reviewer found the novelty marginal, indicating a split in opinion on this point.

### Weaknesses

**Missing details and insufficient clarity**

- The definition of “lines of code” as a measure of interpretability is not clearly justified; a representation comparison example (e.g., a learned program from the proposed model vs. the original) is needed to show how fewer lines improve interpretability.
- No appendix is provided. The paper would be strengthened by including one with:
  - Example programs extracted from the new model, compared with those from the original method.
  - Details on how programs are extracted (assumed same as previous work, but may differ due to new attention types).
  - The full list of hyperparameters and optimizers used across all experiments.
- Reference attention \(A_\text{ref}\) is introduced but its calculation during training is not explained (Section 3.3).
- Score‑based attention is never clearly defined as a counterpart to categorical attention; naming is confusing because categorical attention also uses scores.
- The intuition for how the Gumbel‑Sparsemax transition improves optimization is not explained. How does it differ from temperature annealing of Gumbel‑Softmax? Sparsemax is deterministic, which may be a key difference – this needs clarification.
- The mathematical relation (or mixing) between pure Gumbel‑Softmax and pure Sparsemax in Equation (8) is not justified; a more principled interpolation might exist.
- The claim in lines 377–378 that removing all enhancements yields the lowest accuracy (76.44) is incorrect: keeping only the Smooth Transition Mechanism results in even lower accuracy (73.61) according to Table 2.

**Evaluation limitations and missing comparisons**

- Evaluated NLP tasks are limited to classification and NER; including generative tasks would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method’s impact.
- No case analysis of failure instances is provided.
- The paper states that the goal is to narrow the performance gap between standard Transformers and interpretable models, but results often show that Transformer Programs already outperform standard Transformers (Tables 1 vs. 3,4). A task where the proposed architecture actually narrows that gap would strengthen the claim.
- The ablation study is only on one RASP task; the claim that the “direct transitions” are interesting is not fully supported.
- The original Transformer Programs paper noted that score‑based attention would lead to longer programs, yet the proposed uncertainty‑aware attention decreases lines of code. An ablation including a pure score‑based attention variant would clarify this discrepancy.
- Sparsity benefits of the proposed method over Gumbel‑Softmax are claimed but no direct comparison (e.g., sparsity rate) is provided.

**Specific technical issues**

- The statement “In low uncertainty (low JSD), CatAttention is preferred” is confusing: since ScoreAttention can mimic standard attention, why would it ever be worse than CatAttention? The authors should justify why CatAttention is useful beyond interpretability.
- The phrase “performance variations” in the motivation for the hybrid mechanism is vague; explicitly stating that some tasks favor categorical and others score‑based would be clearer.
- In Figure 1 (RASP tasks), why does the Adaptive Transformer Program outperform Standard Transformers on Sort but not on Most Frequent? (Reviewer question)
- In Table 2, accuracy appears to decrease after integrating the Smooth Transition Mechanism – how should this be interpreted? (Reviewer question)
- Line 96: MHA computation omits the normalization factor \(1/\sqrt{n}\).
- Minor typos and citation issues (e.g., line 109 missing space, line 178 missing citation for the claim of Gumbel‑Softmax suboptimality, line 235–236 “neural network layer” better called a module, line 310 “small‑scale datasets” without reference to appendix).
