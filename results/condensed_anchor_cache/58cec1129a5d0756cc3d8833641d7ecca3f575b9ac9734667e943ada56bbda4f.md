## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces **F³Set**, a video benchmark for detecting Fast, Frequent, and Fine-grained (F³) events, focusing on sports. The dataset currently centers on tennis (11,584 clips, 42,846 shots from 114 professional matches; 25–30 FPS, high resolution) and is being extended to badminton and table tennis. It features over 1,000 event types with precise timestamps and multi-level granularity. A semi-automated annotation toolchain is provided. The authors also propose **F³ED**, a model combining a video encoder, multi-label classifier (MLC), and a contextual module (CTX) using GRUs. Experiments benchmark F³ED against SlowFast, TSN, TSM, and other methods, showing superior performance on F³Set. Reviewers acknowledge the dataset effort and the problem’s relevance, but raise significant concerns about scope, experimental rigor, clarity, generalization, and representation learning.

### Strengths
- **Dataset effort and quality**: The dataset is collected with notable effort and is well-organized with detailed annotations (shot type, direction, outcome, handedness). It contains 11,584 video clips, 42,846 tennis shots, and over 1,000 event types, enabling comprehensive event analysis.
- **Clear problem formulation**: The paper identifies an interesting and under-explored research direction—fast, frequent, and fine-grained event detection—and provides clear examples of multi-level granularity (e.g., forehand vs. backhand, shot outcomes). Tennis terminology is well explained.
- **Practical annotation tool**: A semi-automated toolchain is provided, making the dataset scalable and reproducible. The codebase is well-structured.
- **Dataset diversity**: Includes high-resolution videos from professional matches with both men and women players, and annotations for right- and left-handed players. Frame rates at 25–30 FPS make it suitable for additional tasks (e.g., pose estimation).
- **Informative comparisons (Table 1)**: The benchmark comparisons across existing datasets are a nice and interesting contribution.
- **Strong experimental results (R1)**: Results demonstrate substantial challenges for existing methods and show F³ED achieves superior performance on multiple granularity levels.
- **Useful insights (Sec. 5.1)**: Some valuable observations are provided about model behavior.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset scope and generalization**: The dataset is currently restricted to tennis (and a few similar sports like badminton/table tennis). Reviewers question how it generalizes to more complex sports (e.g., soccer, basketball) that require higher FPS, multi-player interactions, and more stochastic events. **R3 (8)** explicitly calls this a significant limitation; **R2 (5)** says the contribution is limited because the dataset is only tennis and the scope of the benchmark is unclear. The paper does not adequately address this challenge.
- **Paper flow and presentation**: The paper appears rushed and not well-structured. Sec. 3 (dataset) and Sec. 4 (model) are confusing or insufficiently explained. Terms like “T”, “body”, “wide” in the Introduction are unclear. Fig. 3 has low resolution and unreadable fonts; Fig. 4 is unclear (the “plus” symbol that combines LCL and MLC outputs is not defined, red squares in CTX are unexplained, and the caption lacks detail). **R2** (score 5) rates presentation 1/5 and notes “incomplete sentence in Sec. 3.1”. **R3** (8) also notes that Fig. 3 and 4 need improvement.
- **Justification of design choices**: Several architectural decisions lack sufficient reasoning. (i) **Frame-wise vs. clip-wise features (line 398–399)**: The claim that frame-wise extraction is “crucial” is unsupported; clip-wise methods may be more robust to noise and better capture motion. A comparative analysis is missing. (ii) **GRU over Transformer in CTX**: The choice of GRU for context modeling is not justified given transformers’ superior long-range dependency capture. (iii) **LCL module’s impact**: The system heavily depends on the LCL (localizer) module, but there is no numerical analysis of how F³ED performs when LCL is inaccurate. If good LCL is a hard prerequisite, generalizability is questionable. (iv) **Input resolution**: The benchmark uses 224×224 crops despite collecting higher-resolution videos. **R3** questions whether low resolution smooths subtle details, making the task artificially easier. Testing higher-resolution inputs is needed to confirm whether performance gains stem from architecture or training regime.
- **Limited evaluation and comparisons**: (i) The set of video encoders tested (Table 2) is limited; **R2** and **R3** both note that only models with similar architectural foundations (3D CNNs) are compared, while fundamentally different approaches (e.g., pose-estimation-based methods) are ignored. (ii) Evaluation metrics and their justification are not provided. (iii) Results in Sec. 5.1 are presented as a list without in-depth analysis or visualizations (e.g., plots). The claim that F³ED “achieves optimal performance among all methods” lacks explanation of how and why.
- **Lack of related work and representation learning focus**: The paper does not thoroughly review existing works on fast/frequent/fine-grained events, nor does it explain how the proposed method differs from them. **R3** notes that the paper does not address how F³ED learns representations that could generalize or transfer to other domains, which is a key weakness for an ICLR submission. The paper does not explore alternative paradigms like pose estimation for event detection.
- **Dataset robustness and real-world variation**: The dataset is collected from professional matches with consistent camera angles, lighting, and court conditions. The impact of different camera angles, court types, weather, and illumination is not analyzed. **R3** asks for experiments testing robustness to such variations, as they are common in real-world settings.
- **Minor issues**:
  - Missing reference: Sports video analysis on large-scale data, ECCV 2022.
  - The fine-grained concept should be introduced earlier (in Introduction).
  - “Conflicting samples were resolved using a majority vote criterion” is not explained.
  - No notation section for mathematical symbols.
  - In problem formulation (Sec. 4), the variable “j” is unclear.
  - The paper does not clarify how the proposed method differs from existing works on fast/frequent/fine-grained events.
