## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces L-CiteEval, a multi-task benchmark for evaluating long-context models (LCMs) on both response quality and citation faithfulness, covering 11 tasks with context lengths from 8K to 48K tokens. It uses fully automated metrics and includes two variants (L-CiteEval-Length, L-CiteEval-Hardness). Testing on 11 open- and closed-source LCMs shows that open-source models lag behind closed-source in citation accuracy, RAG improves faithfulness but slightly reduces generation quality, and citation behavior correlates with attention mechanisms. Reviewers largely agree that the benchmark addresses an important need and includes thorough experiments, but they raise concerns about novelty relative to LongCite, evaluation metric limitations, figure errors, and the practicality of insights for model development. One reviewer with high confidence (Reviewer 3) is strongly critical, arguing the paper is unfit for ICLR, contains incorrect claims about GPT-4o’s stability, and has presentation issues (missing axis labels, insufficient data for trend claims). Another reviewer (Reviewer 1) questions the evaluation automation consistency and notes a typo. Reviewers 2 and 4 (both score 5) value the automated evaluation and citation focus but want deeper analysis (e.g., per-task challenges, context length thresholds, padding vs. genuine long texts).

### Strengths
- L-CiteEval covers more tasks and longer contexts (up to 48K) than existing benchmarks, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of LCMs (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The benchmark assesses both response quality and citation faithfulness, which is crucial for applications requiring accurate source attribution (Reviewer 2, 4).
- The use of fully automated evaluation reduces biases from LLM-based judges (e.g., GPT-4) and improves consistency and reproducibility (Reviewer 4).
- Experiments are conducted on a wide range of open-source and closed-source models, identifying clear performance gaps and showing that RAG can significantly improve citation reliability (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is well-written and provides a solid literature review, with good analysis of the results (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty relative to LongCite**: The benchmark largely assembles pre-existing datasets with a padding strategy to extend context lengths; the primary innovation is the padding process, and L-CiteEval does not introduce new data or methods for long-context representation (Reviewers 1, 4). This raises the question: what research questions can L-CiteEval answer that LongCite cannot? (Reviewer 1)
- **Inconsistency about automatic evaluation**: The paper first claims an “entirely automatic evaluation” as an advantage over LongCite, but later admits current metrics “still heavily rely on human judgment or model outputs” as a limitation (Reviewer 1). This confuses whether precision/recall for QA benchmarks are automatically computed or not (Reviewer 1’s question).
- **Figure and text errors**:
  - Figure 4(a) x-axis labels are inconsistent: subplots show (Easy, Medium, Hard) but caption and Section 4.2.1 describe varying context length values. This misleads interpretation of the results (Reviewers 1, 3).
  - Figures 3 and 4 lack axis labels on both x- and y-axes, making results hard to read (Reviewer 3).
  - Figure captions are overly short (one‑liners) and insufficient to convey the information; readers are forced to read the main text (Reviewer 3).
  - Line 253 has a typo: “L-CiteEval-Quality” should be “L-CiteEval-Hardness” (Reviewer 1).
  - Line 477: “performance of retrieval” – likely a missing word or phrasing error (Reviewer 3).
- **Incorrect or insufficiently supported claims**: The claim that “closed-source LCM (GPT-4o) maintains a relatively stable performance, showing minimal degradation” (line 418) is contradicted by visible degradation in several subplots (e.g., NarrativeQA F1, GovReport F1, NarrativeQA Rec, HotpotQA Rec, GovReport Rec). Moreover, relying on only three data points on the x‑axis is insufficient to establish a trend or claim stability; the paper requires more extensive benchmarking and careful inference (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited exploration of improvement methods**: Beyond RAG, the paper explores few approaches to enhance citation faithfulness, leaving the usefulness of the benchmark for guiding practical improvements unclear (Reviewer 2). It is not evident how the insights from L-CiteEval can be directly leveraged to advance LCM design (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Heavy reliance on automated scoring**: The evaluation may miss nuanced aspects of citation faithfulness (e.g., in multi‑reference scenarios) that human evaluation could capture (Reviewers 2, 4). The current citation metrics may not comprehensively assess whether models cite accurately and contextually (Reviewer 4).
- **Padding and noise concerns**: Padding from Named Entity Recognition (NER) may not fully eliminate noise, potentially affecting model response consistency (Reviewer 4). The paper does not compare model behavior on genuinely long texts versus artificially padded ones to validate the padding approach (Reviewer 4’s question). Alternative metrics that evaluate citation relevance (e.g., based on context similarity) were not considered (Reviewer 4’s question).
- **Missing analysis**:
  - Whether certain tasks are particularly challenging for citation faithfulness (Reviewer 2’s question).
  - Trends or critical context-length thresholds beyond which models struggle with citation or response quality (Reviewer 2’s question).
- **Venue fit**: Reviewer 3 (high confidence, score 1) strongly states the paper is unfit for ICLR, as it lacks core machine learning or representation learning content, and references are predominantly from arXiv/computational linguistics; the paper would be better suited to NLP venues. (This criticism is not shared by the other reviewers, who rated the paper higher.)
