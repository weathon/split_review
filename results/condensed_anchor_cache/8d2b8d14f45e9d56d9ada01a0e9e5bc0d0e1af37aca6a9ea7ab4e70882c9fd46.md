## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Explanation-Assisted Data Augmentation (EA-DA) for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). The core idea is to use graph explanation subgraphs—subgraphs that are "almost sufficient" statistics for the classification label—to identify label-preserving perturbations: edges not in the explanation can be perturbed with high probability of label invariance. The authors provide theoretical analysis of sample complexity under the PAC learning framework, showing that explanation-assisted learning can have arbitrarily lower sample complexity than explanation-agnostic learning, but if the learning rule does not distinguish between augmented and original data (i.e., uses standard ERM) then sample complexity can be worse due to out-of-distribution perturbations. They then propose an explanation-assisted ERM (EA-ERM) learning rule that differentiates between original and augmented data. Practical implementations using GNNExplainer and PGExplainer are tested on several graph classification datasets with 50 training samples.

### Strengths
- **Novel integration of explainability and data augmentation** for graph learning. The idea of leveraging explanation subgraphs to guide label-preserving perturbations is innovative.
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis** including sample complexity bounds, VC dimension analysis, and conditions under which EA-DA improves or worsens complexity. The distinction between EA-ERM and standard ERM is formally shown.
- **Clear and well-organized presentation** throughout the paper.
- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation** across multiple datasets and GNN architectures demonstrating practical performance gains when EA-DA is used with the appropriate learning rule, and also showing cases where conditions are unmet (performance degradation), supporting the theoretical insights.

### Weaknesses
- **Heavy reliance on explanation quality.** The method assumes explanations are accurate label-preserving subgraphs, but existing explainers (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer) are known to be imperfect, noisy, or incomplete. No sensitivity analysis is provided on how EA-DA degrades with low-quality explanations. A reviewer explicitly asked whether different explanation methods (including newer ones) impact performance—this remains unaddressed.
- **Missing comparisons with many existing graph DA works.** The experimental section does not include comparisons with methods like LSPAN, Graph Random Neural Networks, Spectral Augmentation, Dropedge, Data augmentation for graph neural networks (several reviewers). Also missing comparisons with GeoMIX and Graph Mixup with Soft Alignments (Table 1). These omissions reduce the persuasiveness of the empirical results.
- **Limited augmentation diversity.** The current strategy only perturbs edges (adding/removing) not in the explanation. Other types of augmentations (node feature modifications, broader structural changes) are not explored, which may limit robustness gains. This was raised as a question about potential for more diverse strategies.
- **Computational cost is high and not quantified.** EA-DA requires (1) pre-training a GNN on the original data, (2) training an explainer (e.g., GNNExplainer, PGExplainer) on that pre-trained model, (3) generating explanation-guided perturbations, and (4) retraining a GNN on both original and augmented data. This effectively doubles training effort. No runtime or efficiency comparisons with baselines are provided. Scalability to very large graphs (e.g., CORA, CITESEER, PPI, BLOGC, FLICKR, AIR-USA) and dynamic graphs is not discussed.
- **Model dependency.** Augmented data is generated based on a specific pre-trained model's explanations; the resulting augmentations may not transfer to other architectures—this is not discussed.
- **Lack of details on practical implementation of key components.** The pre-training process for the classifier \(f(\cdot)\) is not described. The explainer \(\Psi(\cdot)\) architecture and learning approach are omitted—only naming GNNExplainer and PGExplainer without verifying whether they align with the theoretical assumptions (e.g., "almost sufficient" subgraph property). It is unclear how the method handles real-world scenarios where ground-truth explanations are unavailable.
- **Limited scope of tasks and datasets.** Experiments focus on graph classification with 50 training samples. Generalization to node classification, edge prediction, and other graph tasks is not addressed. Commonly used citation and social network datasets (CORA, CITESEER, PPI, BLOGC, FLICKR, AIR-USA) are omitted.
- **Justification for experimental design choices is missing.** The decision to use only 50 training samples in Table 1 is not motivated. Also, the inputs \(\Pi\) and \(\Psi\) in Algorithm 1 are not explicitly provided.
- **Out-of-distribution assessment is unclear.** Reviewers asked how to determine whether generated augmentations are out-of-distribution and how EA-DA balances in-distribution vs. out-of-distribution augmentations—this is not addressed.
- **Minor:** Missing recent references (GeoMIX, Soft Alignments) and up-to-date related work in Table 1.
