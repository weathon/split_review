## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Evolutionary Imitation Learning (EvIL), a meta-learning approach that uses evolution strategies (ES) in an outer loop to optimize reward and transition dynamics parameters, which are then used to train a policy via reinforcement learning in an inner loop. The objective of the outer loop is a behavioral cloning loss, and CMA-ES is adopted for optimization. A reward distillation step simplifies the reward network, improving test performance. The method is evaluated on Gridworld, Reacher, and Pointmass environments, and compared against behavioral cloning (BC) and adversarial inverse reinforcement learning (AIRL). The authors claim EvIL can handle non-differentiable rewards and transfer to environments with different dynamics. One reviewer (score 6) found the approach promising and the experiments sensible; three reviewers (score 3) expressed significant concerns about experimental scope, clarity, scalability, and literature review.

### Strengths

- The ability to predict expert behavior in a different environment (different transition dynamics) is an important and relevant topic.
- EvIL can handle non-differentiable rewards and training procedures, taking advantage of evolution strategies.
- The approach addresses key limitations of most imitation learning approaches (e.g., poor reward shaping, need for differentiation).
- Using distillation (L1 distillation of estimated reward functions) was found to be better than simply adding L1 regularization to the loss function — an interesting finding.
- The method is general and applicable to various policy optimization problems.
- Baseline comparisons and ablation tests are sensible.
- Promising results in three benchmarks, including transferability to unseen environments.
- Originality in using Gaussian mutation to estimate gradients of policy and transition model parameters and performing gradient descent (rather than standard selection), tuning inner-loop steps to avoid gradient disappearance, and L1 distillation.

### Weaknesses

- **Experiments too few and too simple.** The method is tested only on Gridworld, Reacher, and Pointmass. Generalization across transition functions should be evaluated on more challenging modifications like different gravity, mass, friction. One reviewer (score 6) also requested evaluation on more/complex environments and testing transferability to similar domains.
- **Method speed and scalability.** The method involves multiple rounds of training and is very slow (multiple rounds of training). Using a naive evolutionary strategy for high-dimensional state spaces is problematic; scalability to real-world problems is questionable.
- **Insufficient documentation of baselines.** AIRL implementation is not sufficiently documented; parameters for BC training are not given; hyperparameters for some experiment steps (e.g., learning rates) are missing. Acronyms like AIRL are not defined; its training setup should be clarified.
- **Population size N and other basic parameters** are not reported in the main text.
- **Clarity of experimental conditions.** Figure 1: first paragraph of Section 4.1 states the reward function optimized in the training environment is used to test in the test environment, but Figure 1 shows reward and transition functions being optimized in the test environment — motivation unclear. Also, Figure 2: no significant differences among EvIL variants (EvIL with supervised loss, EvIL with supervised model) yet the online setting is much better; this implies EvIL may have failed to find a good transition function in Pointmass — discussion of the quality of the optimized transition function is needed.
- **Reward function details unclear.** Target reward functions of each benchmark are not clear, even in supplementary materials; for each goal point, how sparsely were the reward functions set? In Figure 3, it is unclear how reward features (e.g., obstacle position) are retrieved from a neural network that maps state to a scalar — clarification needed.
- **Figure 1 comparison issues.** EvIL is worse than AIRL in the training environment, but AIRL fails to improve in the test environment. Does this imply the initial reward is tuned for the training environment? Also, the x-axis of Figure 1 shows number of outer loops, but the number of environment interactions per outer loop for both EvIL and AIRL is not specified.
- **Literature review insufficient.** The structure of EvIL is similar to prior work in learning and evolution: e.g., Elfwing et al. (2018) optimize hyperparameters of RL using a simple evolutionary algorithm in the outer loop; Niekum et al. (2010) optimize the reward function by genetic programming; Houthooft et al. (2018) (Evolved Policy Gradients) optimize a differentiable loss function using ES. These should be discussed and compared, along with a more detailed survey on meta-learning.
- **Writing and presentation issues.** Section 5 should be reorganized because Figure 3 is explained after Figure 2. Page 3, typo: “the the expert demonstration”. The environments are not described in the paper; details should at least be added to the appendix.
- **Unclear link between non-differentiable objectives and potential-based shaping** (Section 4.1, point 2).
- **Phrasing error**: Page 2, paragraph 3: “environment dynamics are trained” should read “dynamics models are trained” (the environment cannot be trained).
- **Motivation for design choice unclear.** The authors do not clarify why this design is necessary over more straightforward approaches; e.g., focusing on one scenario (online setting) first and justifying the need for the method in that scenario would help.
