## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates multi-modal test-time adaptation (TTA) under the newly identified challenge of “reliability bias”—information discrepancies across modalities caused by intra-modal distribution shifts. To address this, it proposes READ (Reliable fusion and robust ADaptation), which consists of a self-adaptive attention module that repurposes attention layers during test time for reliable cross-modal fusion, and a confidence-aware loss function with non‑monotonous gradient properties to suppress noisy predictions. The paper also introduces two new benchmarks for multi-modal TTA with reliability bias, based on Kinetics (action recognition) and VGGSound (event classification), covering video and audio modalities with diverse corruption types. Extensive experiments demonstrate that READ outperforms existing TTA methods and imbalanced multi-modal learning baselines.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem and motivation.** The paper identifies a new, practical challenge (reliability bias) for multi-modal TTA, which has not been well studied. It empirically shows that existing TTA methods (which primarily update normalization layers) and imbalanced multi-modal learning methods cannot handle this bias, highlighting the need for dedicated solutions.
- **Comprehensive benchmarks.** Two new benchmarks are constructed from Kinetics and VGGSound, with both video and audio modalities corrupted at different levels to simulate reliability bias. They include audio corruption types (extending beyond ImageNet-C) and cover diverse tasks (action recognition and event classification), facilitating future research.
- **Innovative paradigm for TTA.** Instead of only repurposing normalization layers, READ modulates attention layers during test time. This allows the model to dynamically adjust cross-modal importance, achieving reliable fusion against reliability bias. The design is technically sound and may inspire task-specific parameter modulation beyond standard TTA practices.
- **Effective and theoretically grounded method.** The self-adaptive attention module and the confidence-aware loss are both simple yet effective. The loss function is shown to amplify confident predictions and mitigate noisy ones, with theoretical guarantees on its gradient properties.
- **Extensive experiments and clear presentation.** The paper validates the approach through thorough comparisons, ablation studies, and analysis (including Figures 1, 2, and the attention visualizations). The writing is well-organized and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing experimental settings.** All main results are reported only at corruption severity 5. To establish generality, experiments across different severity levels (including unbiased i.i.d. and non‑i.i.d. scenarios) and under mixed severities or continual distribution shifts (continual TTA) would make the work more practical.
- **Insufficient details on the architecture and attention mechanism.**  
  * The number of attention layers used in the CAV‑MAE backbone and how many parameters are modulated during test‑time adaptation are not specified.  
  * It is unclear why all parameters of Q, K, and V are updated simultaneously; the possibility of updating only the MLP parameters of Q and K should be discussed.  
  * The distinction between the proposed Self‑Adaptive Attention and traditional self‑attention (e.g., whether architectural changes are involved or only a different training regime) is not clearly explained—specifically, the differences in query, key, and value transformations.  
  * The calculation of the attention values in Figure 4 (e.g., cross‑modal vs. self‑attention) is not described.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The confidence threshold $\gamma$ (Eq. 6) is fixed across experiments without an ablation study. Its influence on TTA performance should be explored, and guidance for choosing an appropriate value should be provided.
- **Efficiency concerns.** The paper does not compare the number of modulated parameters and adaptation time between READ and existing TTA baselines. The efficiency cost of repurposing attention layers (vs. only normalization layers) should be addressed.
- **Clarity issues in figures, tables, and text.**  
  * Figures 1c and 3b suffer from low image clarity.  
  * The terms “Static” and “Dynamic” in Table 1 are not defined.  
  * The meaning of “Attention A-V” in Figure 4 and how it demonstrates robustness is unclear.  
  * In the analysis of Figure 5, the contrast with clean results is not evident.  
  * There are several typos: “traffic noise in the audio modality” (Figure 1 caption), “information bias” (Page 2), inconsistent notations such as “(Stat. LN) & AF” vs. “Stat. (LN & AF)” in Tables 1‑3, a stray “$\theta_{s}^{m}$” in Line 5, Page 4, a spurious “$\partial$” in Eq. 9, and a missing bold “A” in Eq. (4).
- **Missing ablation baseline.** The default setting results should be included in the ablation tables for easier comparison.
- **Practical applicability.** The paper assumes audio modality is available, but in real‑world scenarios (e.g., autonomous vehicles) audio may not be the primary sensor. A discussion of such limitations and the applicability of READ to other modalities would be beneficial.
