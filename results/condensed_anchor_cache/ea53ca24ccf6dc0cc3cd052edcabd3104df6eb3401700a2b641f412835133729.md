## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Saliency-Guided Hidden Associative Replay for Continual Learning (SHARC), a biologically inspired framework that stores only salient feature channels (selected via Grad-CAM) in a sparse episodic memory, then uses a trained associative memory (implemented as a recurrent or feed-forward network) to retrieve the full feature maps during replay. It is designed as a plugin for replay-based methods, aiming to improve memory efficiency while maintaining or improving performance. Experiments are conducted on MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, mini-ImageNet, and S-CIFAR-100 under task-incremental and class-incremental settings, comparing with GEM, A-GEM, ER, DER++, and other replay baselines. One reviewer (score 6) endorses the novelty and effectiveness, while four reviewers (scores 3) raise fundamental concerns about experimental design, evaluation fairness, mechanism clarity, and limited contributions.

### Strengths
- The paper draws inspiration from biological memory (selective retention of salient experiences, associative recall) and applies it to continual learning, a commendable direction.  
- SHARC can be seamlessly adapted to any replay-based approach, improving performance in various CL scenarios (R3, R5).  
- The saliency-based sparse encoding is hardware-friendly and reduces memory cost (R1).  
- Associative memory retrieval offers fast, efficient recall and higher noise tolerance compared to generative inpainting (R2).  
- The memory-forgetting mechanism (removing more data from old tasks than new tasks) is a useful contribution (R2).  
- Several SOTA methods show improved performance when combined with SHARC (R2).  
- Experimental results across multiple benchmarks provide evidence of effectiveness (R1, R3, R4).  

### Weaknesses
**Experimental scope and fairness**  
- Only class-incremental and task-incremental settings are studied; an ideal continual learner should also be robust to other distributions (e.g., IID ordering). This omission is especially notable given the neuro-inspiration claimed (R2).  
- No experiments on high-dimensional, large-scale datasets such as ImageNet-1K, iNaturalist, or Places-365. The selected datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, mini-ImageNet) are not challenging enough for a model using an ImageNet-1K pre-trained backbone; training only the final layer and associative memory likely yields high results on these toy problems (R2).  
- It is unclear how the ImageNet-1K (224×224) pre-trained backbone is adapted to lower-resolution images (32×32) (R2, R5).  
- The method is only compared against replay-based methods; no comparisons with regularization-based or dynamic architecture-based CL methods (R4, R5).  
- Most baselines are 2–3 years old; recent methods from 2022–2023 are missing (R3, R5).  
- It is unclear whether the baseline methods (without SHARC) also use ImageNet pre-training as batch setups; the lack of this detail makes comparisons potentially unfair (R5).  
- SHARC requires training an associative memory (additional network and updates), while comparison methods do not. A fairer evaluation would compare methods under the same bounded compute and number of training updates, or report the extra cost (R2, R3).  

**Mechanism clarity and validity**  
- The working mechanism of the associative memory retrieval is confusing and poorly explained:  
  - It is unclear how queries are obtained during both training and inference. The paper states that only salient channels are stored, but the partial cue used to query the associative memory is not specified (R5).  
  - The concept of “queries” (illustrated as image crops in Fig. 2) contradicts the notion that specific feature map channels are retained (R5).  
  - The practicality of recalling exactly the same stored item from an old class when the query comes from a new class is questionable. If old queries are also stored, that undermines the claimed memory savings (R5).  
- The mathematical formulation of the associative memory retrieval process is missing; computational complexity is not analyzed (R1, R2).  
- An ablation study of the retrieval mechanism components is absent (R1).  
- Reliance on gradient-based saliency (Grad-CAM) for selecting important channels may not align with feature selection mechanisms in the human brain (R5).  

**Limited novelty and representation learning**  
- The combination of “saliency” and “associative memory” is not new; similar mechanisms exist in image retrieval literature. The novelty lies mostly in applying them to CL (R4).  
- The feature backbone is frozen, so the model does not learn new representations in the hidden layers. It is unclear how model depth (e.g., using earlier layers) impacts retrieval performance (R2).  
- The claim that hidden representations are highly sparse (citing Sun et al. 2015) is inadequately supported, as that work focused on face representations, not general CNN features (R5).  

**Performance inconsistency and missing details**  
- SHARC sometimes underperforms baselines (Tables 2 and 4), so the improvement is not consistent across settings, datasets, and buffer sizes (R2).  
- The paper claims that DER++ equipped with SHARC achieves a 45.5% accuracy improvement on S-CIFAR-100, but Table 2 does not support this claim (R2).  
- Hyper-parameter details (especially for the associative memory network) are not provided (R3).  
- The implementation details of how SHARC is combined with each replay method are not given (R4). Does SHARC replace the original replay buffer entirely? How is the prediction head integrated? (R4, R5).  
- The additional memory footprint and computing resources required by the associative memory are not reported. It is possible that using those extra resources to store more exemplars would yield similar or better gains (R3, R4).  
- The claim about fast and efficient recall and noise tolerance is only supported by a conceptual figure (Fig. 1); no quantitative results are provided (R2).  
- The value of K in the saliency selection and how it changes under class-incremental settings are not specified (R5).  
- The two multiplication operations in Eq. (4) are not explained (R5).  
- It is unclear what the “partial cue” is that is used to retrieve feature maps in the associative memory (R5).  
- The initialization of the last layer before continual learning begins is not disclosed (R2).  

**Reviewer disagreement**  
One reviewer (score 6, confidence 2) finds the work novel and experimentally convincing, whereas the other four reviewers (scores 3, confidences 3-5) judge the contributions as limited, the experiments as confounded, and the evaluation as unfair or insufficient to support the claims. The majority view is that the method is interesting but not ready for publication due to major flaws in experimental design and clarity.
