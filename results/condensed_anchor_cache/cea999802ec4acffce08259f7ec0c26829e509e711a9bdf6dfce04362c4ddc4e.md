## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies multilingual jailbreak vulnerabilities in LLMs (ChatGPT, GPT-4) under unintentional and intentional scenarios, and proposes a Self-Defense fine-tuning framework. All five reviewers agree the topic is timely and important. Reviewer 1 (score 8, confidence 4) finds the theme crucial and the evaluation valuable, but notes soundness and presentation need improvement. Reviewers 2–5 each rate 6 (confidence 3 or 4), acknowledging contributions but raising significant concerns about evaluation rigor, novelty, and scope.

### Strengths
- Timely and crucial issue: LLM safety is usually evaluated only in English despite global use; multilingual jailbreak evaluation is valuable (R1, R3, R4, R5).
- Thorough multi-angle analysis: overall safety, jailbreaks (intentional/unintentional), effects of language resource levels, interplay with CommonCrawl data, fine-tuning mitigation, safeness-usefulness trade-off (R3, R4, R5).
- Introduction of MultiJail, claimed first multilingual jailbreak dataset, with human translations to avoid noise (R4, R5).
- Effective jailbreak strategy with analysis on a defense attempt (R2).
- Good methodology: collecting harmful prompts from Anthropic red teaming, jailbreakchat.com for intentional prompts, human evaluation for 15 preliminary prompts (R4).
- Section 3 provides detailed evaluation of multilingual safety challenges (R5).
- Interesting analysis of translation quality, jailbreak languages, and safeness-usefulness trade-off (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Small sample sizes and missing error bars / statistical significance.** Many experiments use only 15–30 samples per language; only one jailbreak method (AIM) is tested. With such limited samples, p-values and standard deviations (or error bars) are needed to support claims (R1, R2, R3). Larger-scale experiments are expected (R3).
- **GPT-4 evaluator bias unaddressed.** Using GPT-4 to judge safety may introduce positional/length bias and a preference for GPT-4 outputs over ChatGPT outputs [1,2]. The paper should at least mention this limitation or reinforce results with repetitive experiments (R1). Even the English unintentional case shows GPT-4 yields 28.25% unsafe, indicating the model itself is not a perfect evaluator (R2).
- **Lack of human evaluation details.** The human evaluation process, instructions, and annotator information are missing. Agreement between human annotators and the GPT-4 evaluator should be reported with correlation/agreement numbers, not just “notable level of agreement” via visual trends (R1).
- **Only two black-box models tested (ChatGPT, GPT-4).** Open-source models (e.g., LLaMA, Vicuna) should be evaluated to understand correlation between language resource proportion in training data and attack success rates (R1, R3).
- **Self-Defense framework lacks novelty and is oversimplified.** The method is essentially fine-tuning on translated augmented data, so improvement is expected; it is unclear whether gains are driven by low-resource level or other factors (R1). The algorithm may be over-complicated compared to simply translating Anthropic data directly (R3). It lacks novelty, merely providing more training samples for low-resource languages (R5). Details are missing: what are the seed samples? What percentage of unsafe samples generated by ChatGPT are actually unsafe when re-prompted? (R4). The framework is only evaluated on MultiJail; it may just scratch the surface of the problem (R4).
- **Translation quality for low-resource languages and potential noise.** Since the LLM itself generates translations (except for seed examples), low-resource language translations are likely noisy, which may contribute to the observed reduction in usefulness as safeness improves. The paper should provide a numerical comparison of low usefulness cases due to rejection vs. noisy translations (R5). Additionally, generated outputs in non-English are translated back to English for evaluation via Google Translate, introducing potential translation errors (R1 question).
- **No comparison to other safety techniques.** The Self-Defense framework is not compared to existing methods such as RLHF or other safety fine-tuning approaches from related work (R5). The mitigation section is the weakest; it does not show how safety can be improved without harming usefulness, nor does it break down results by language resource level (R3, R6).
- **Difficulty distinguishing language effect from harmful instruction effect.** In the intentional scenario, the harmful instruction (AIM) alone achieves very high success in English (see Table 1), making it hard to attribute high attack rates to language rarity (R2). Table 1 also shows no clear tendency among high/medium/low-resource languages in the intentional scenario (R1 question).
- **Framing of “jailbreak” may be misleading.** Safety in other languages is important on its own and should not be framed solely as a jailbreak problem; the paper should acknowledge this earlier and consistently (R3).
- **Cultural sensitivity of safety guidelines.** Safety guidelines differ across cultures and societies; adversarial examples from a U.S./company-centric perspective may not transfer generally (R1).
- **Dataset and experiment selection details missing.** For the Anthropic red teaming data (38k instances), the selection of 300 prompts should be elaborated, including consideration of multi-turn dialogs and harmlessness scores (R2 question).
- **Inconsistent presentation of rates.** Tables show unsafe rate in percent, while figures use decimals; a consistent format would aid readability (R2).
