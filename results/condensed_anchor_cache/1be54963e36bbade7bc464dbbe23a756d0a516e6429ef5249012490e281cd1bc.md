## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes GIO, a task- and domain-agnostic method for training data subset selection. It minimizes the KL divergence between the selected subset and a target distribution using a greedy algorithm with relaxations (gradient-based optimization over K-means clusters). Evaluated on machine translation, spelling correction, and image recognition (FashionMNIST). The method is label-free and designed for computational efficiency.

### Strengths

- Data selection is an important and growing problem; the paper presents a sound approach grounded in information theory.
- Well-written, polished, and clearly communicated; contributions are well-placed within related work.
- Thorough evaluation including self-consistency checks, ablations, and multiple domains (language and vision).
- Task-agnostic and domain-agnostic; does not require labels.
- The proposed approximations (gradient over K-means clusters) are computationally efficient, simple, and easy to understand; tied to the underlying naive approach.
- Well-motivated: improving training efficiency by shrinking dataset size makes large-model training more accessible.
- Strong empirical results on several machine translation tasks and FashionMNIST; synthetic experiments confirm the intuitions.
- Ablations show robustness to different embedding models and K-means hyperparameters.
- Post-rebuttal evidence: applied to >10M data, ~40min for 35M data; outperforms existing methods including submodular optimization in more realistic scenarios.
- Some reviewers (scores 8) found the method well-motivated and the empirical results convincing, while other reviewers (scores 6) were more critical of presentation and completeness. However, the post-rebuttal response addressed several concerns and improved confidence.

### Weaknesses

- **Stopping criterion (Section 4.3) is ad-hoc and lacks clear motivation**: the two-stage process (reselecting from G until KL divergence decreases) is introduced without justification. Why not run only the first stage? The authors should rigorously justify this criterion or explore the effects of using only the first stage.
- **Experiment description mismatch (Section 4.3)**: The text claims GIO selects high-quality data from a target set that is a mix of high and low quality, but the actual target set $X$ contains only high-quality data. The setup and stated goal do not align; the authors should clarify how the experiment corresponds to the theoretical motivation.
- **Gap between global optimization (Eq.1) and greedy algorithm (Eq.2) not discussed**: The greedy approach is a local search on a non-convex objective; the paper does not analyze how well the greedy solution approximates the global optimum. The authors acknowledged that the objective lacks submodularity and did not provide an empirical approximation ratio. While greedy is a natural discrete optimization method, this gap needs more thorough treatment.
- **Gradient-based relaxation vs. discrete selection from k-means centroids**: The paper does not clearly justify that the gradient-optimal $\mathbf{v}_{opt}$ from the relaxed problem (Eq.3) matches the optimal discrete solution. The assumption that they coincide if data density is sufficient (Appendix A.2) may not hold in high-dimensional spaces where data is sparse, especially since selection is restricted to k-means centroids. Stronger justification is needed.
- **Motivation for requiring a target distribution $X$ is insufficient**: Under the natural setting $D=\emptyset$, $G=X$, the obvious optimal solution is $V=X$ (select all the data). The paper lacks a concrete discussion of real-world scenarios where a target distribution is available and how to choose it (e.g., what data to set as target). The current discussion is too abstract.
- **Implicit hyperparameter in stopping criterion**: The condition "$d(p_X(x),p_G(x)) \gg 0$" is not defined. Presenting the lack of a dataset-size parameter as a strength is misleading if the hyperparameter just appears in a different form; the optimal dataset size naturally depends on constraints.
- **Missing comparison to a closely related method**: The approach of Xie et al. (Data Selection for Language Models via Importance Sampling) also uses KL distance over a feature space. It should be compared both conceptually and empirically, and should not be listed under "heuristic" methods without justification.
- **Algorithm 1 is incomplete**: It does not define inputs $X$, $D$, $G$, or specify how to find $\mathbf{v}_{opt}$ (which equation?). Readers should be able to infer the algorithm solely from this description. Additionally, there is a typo: "$D_c \gets D_c + \{\mathbf{v}_b\}$" should be union; $\Omega$ is undefined; $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{opt}$ are ambiguous.
- **No benchmarking of compute cost against baselines**: The computational expense of GIO is not compared to submodular optimization, BM25, or other methods. It is unclear how expensive the method is relative to competitors.
- **Scalability to larger datasets is not demonstrated**: For image recognition, only FashionMNIST (small-scale) is used. While post-rebuttal evidence shows application to 35M data (~40 min), this is not benchmarked against baselines, and the overall scalability claim remains unverified for very large datasets.
- **Open questions about target state $X$ and initial state $D$**: There are no ablations on how the choice of $X$ and $D$ affects performance. How should these be initialized for a new task or domain? This is a practical challenge.
- **Table 4 anomalies and missing baselines**: In Table 4, the random method achieves a lower $D_{KL}$ than GIO, and random $D_{KL}$ is closer to the full dataset. Also, Table 4 does not include other subset selection baselines for the classification setting. Why were other baselines omitted from FashionMNIST experiments?
- **Table 3 ambiguity**: "High quality" is not defined. How is it determined?
- **Embedding invariance and alternative feature spaces**: In Section 4.2, it is unclear whether different embeddings select similar examples, and why invariance should hold (embeddings may distort distributions). Simpler feature spaces like n-grams (cf. Xie et al.) were not tested.
- **Presentation issues** (noted by multiple reviewers): Algorithm 1 typo, missing definitions, unclear notation. Some reviewers found presentation poor; others found it good. Consistency improvements are needed.
