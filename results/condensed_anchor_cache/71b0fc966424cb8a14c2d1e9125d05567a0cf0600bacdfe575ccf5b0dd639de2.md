## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a method for fine-grained, subject-specific attribute control in text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models by identifying token-level “semantic directions” in CLIP text embeddings. The authors propose two approaches: an optimization-free method that contrasts embeddings from attribute-specific prompts (e.g., “a man” vs. “an old man”) and averages differences, and a learning-based method using backpropagation to produce more robust directions. These vectors are added to the embedding of the target word, enabling continuous modulation without modifying the diffusion model. Experiments with SDXL and various attribute adjectives demonstrate the approach on simple attributes and prompt settings.

### Strengths
- **Novel concept and methodology**: Leveraging token-level CLIP embeddings for fine-grained subject-specific control is a novel idea. The paper provides two complementary approaches (optimization-free and learning-based) that cater to different application needs.  
- **No model modification required**: The method is practical and accessible, as it does not require altering or fine-tuning the underlying T2I model.  
- **Simple and intuitive**: The core approach (contrasting prompt embeddings and adding the difference to a target word) is straightforward and easy to understand.  
- **Clear motivation and good related work survey**: The paper clearly outlines the limitation of existing attribute control methods and positions its work effectively.  
- **Experimental evidence of transferability and effectiveness**: Results show that the identified directions can be applied across prompts and subjects, and the method achieves reasonable attribute control.

### Weaknesses
- **Heuristic nature and limited theoretical explanation**: The reasons why token-level linear directions work remain unclear; the method is largely heuristic. This was noted by one reviewer as a weakness, while other reviewers also questioned the underlying mechanisms.  
- **Novelty concerns and limited technical depth**: Two reviewers found the novelty limited. The approach is essentially a simple linear interpolation in embedding space, and the paper lacks ablations or comparisons with even simpler baselines.  
- **Missing comparison to direct embedding addition**: A more intuitive baseline – directly adding the embedding of the attribute word (e.g., “old”) to the subject word (e.g., “man”) – is not compared. The paper also does not clearly explain why the optimization-based method is needed beyond handling suffix attributes, but no experiments on suffix attributes are provided.  
- **Experimental results do not convincingly support claims**:  
  * In Table 1, the proposed method’s subject-specificity metric is weaker than existing methods (e.g., AdapEdit) and even lower than simply adding an adjective to the prompt. When combined with other methods, performance degrades.  
  * The reported time values likely exclude the training cost for the learned direction, making the comparison unfair.  
  * It is unclear how many instances the metrics are averaged over.  
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Experiments focus on simple attributes and prompts (e.g., age, weight). More complex scenarios are missing, such as:  
  * Editing verbs (e.g., “jump”) or multi-word subjects (e.g., “a red sports car”).  
  * Handling multiple attributes simultaneously or subtle attribute overlap.  
  * Visual comparisons with baseline methods (only a few are shown).  
- **Questions about practicality and robustness**:  
  * The optimization-free method may require manual verification of generated images for the positive prompt (e.g., “an expensive car” truly appears expensive), which could be costly.  
  * The range and breaking point of the scalar λ are not discussed; can λ exceed 1? What happens for negative λ?  
  * The method may not generalize well to all prompts, e.g., editing the attributes of only one person in “two people sitting on a lawn”.  
- **Concerns about cross-attention interference**: Adding a vector to a specific word may still affect other targets due to overlapping attention maps in cross-attention layers. This scenario is not addressed.  
- **SDXL dual-encoder incorporation unclear**: It is not specified how attribute vectors are applied to the two text encoders in SDXL, and performance across different diffusion models is not investigated.  
- **Attribute limitations**: The method relies on easily articulable attribute adjectives. It is unclear how to handle attributes that are difficult to describe or subjective (e.g., “interesting” or aesthetic qualities).  
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer found the approach novel and comprehensive, while the other two (more critical) raised the above novelty, experimental, and scope concerns.

*Note: The more positive reviewer acknowledged the heuristic nature and computational cost of the learning-based method, while the more critical reviewers focused on missing baselines, insufficient experimental support, and limited novelty.*
