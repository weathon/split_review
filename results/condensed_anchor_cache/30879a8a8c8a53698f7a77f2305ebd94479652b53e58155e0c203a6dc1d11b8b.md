## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies a set of attention heads within LLMs (Llama-2-7b-chat, Mistral-7b-instruct, Vicuna-7b) that encode ideological perspective along a U.S. liberal-conservative dimension. Using linear probes on hidden-layer activations, the authors show that these attention heads' activations correlate with established measures of political slant (DW-Nominate). They further demonstrate that intervening on these heads can steer the political bias of generated text without additional prompts.

### Strengths
- The problem statement and motivation are interesting and practical, addressing monitoring and control of ideological bias in LLM outputs. (R3)
- Methodology is grounded in political science theory (DW-Nominate). (R2)
- Use of three open-source models (Llama-2-7b-chat, Mistral-7b-instruct, Vicuna-7b) strengthens reproducibility. (R1, R4)
- Identification of a specific set of attention heads encoding political slant in the middle layers is a clear empirical finding. (R1)
- Correlation experiments (e.g., Figure 4) are well set up and establish a linear relationship between activation and political slant. (R2)
- Intervention experiments causally ablate these heads and demonstrate measurable shifts in generated text ideology. (R1, R2, R3)
- Reviewer 2 described the paper as "very close to a slam dunk," expressing high enthusiasm. (R2)
- The work is a novel application of linear probing to political ideology in LLMs, contributing to interpretability and bias detection. (R4)
- Intervention approach (steering outputs without explicit prompting) is a compelling and practical application. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Ridge regression justification**: Why ridge regression over ordinary linear regression? Were collinearities among attention head activations established prior to this choice? (R1)
- **Baseline behavior not established**: What were each model's ideological leanings before intervention? The paper does not characterize the default political slant of the models. (R1)
- **Missing related works**: The paper omits relevant NLP literature (e.g., aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.656) and could better highlight its novelty over prior work. (R2)
- **Nuanced discussion of political bias**: The framing in §2 focuses on fairness but does not address false balance / bothsideism or the perspective that fairness should be assessed in relation to power structures (citing aclweb.org/anthology/2020.acl-main.485.pdf). (R2)
- **Footnote 1 (stochastic parrots vs. linear representations) unclear**: The distinction is not fully explained, and it is unclear why stochastic parroting would preclude linear representations. (R2)
- **GPT-4 evaluator lacks validation**: Using GPT-4 to score political slant of generated essays (L328) may reinforce hidden biases. A small-scale human annotation study or alternative classifier should confirm alignment. (R2, R3)
- **Intervention methodology in Section 3.2 is unclear and underspecified**:
  - The relationship between ridge regression coefficients (θ_{l,h}) and attention mechanisms is not explained. How do coefficients from a linear model apply to the attention output? What are the dimensions and compatibility? (R3)
  - The notation θ_{l,h} is used both for attention coefficients (line 228) and steering vector (line 282); it is ambiguous whether they are the same quantity. (R3)
  - The normalization parameter σ_{l,h} is not defined or motivated. (R3)
  - The selection of top K heads (line 291) is not specified: what is K, and how is predictive power measured? (R3)
- **Causal relationship not convincingly demonstrated**: Interventions may rely on superficial associations rather than showing that the identified linear structures genuinely encode political ideology. (R4)
- **Linear separability vs. linear representation hypothesis**: The paper conflates linear separability (a two-category classification) with the stronger claim of a "linear representation" that allows combination and manipulation with other features. No experiments show whether political ideology can be linearly combined with other features or extrapolated to new tasks. (R4)
- **Claim that conservative arguments may be shorter not contextualized** (L429): The hypothesis is not grounded in political science evidence (e.g., tendencies toward oversimplification, emotional appeals). (R2)
- **Failure to explain how models ensure accurate ideological stance**: When simulating politicians, how is it verified that the LLM consistently adopts the intended perspective rather than a mixed or random stance? (R4)
- **U.S.-centric scope limits generalizability**: The focus on the U.S. liberal-conservative axis may not transfer to other political contexts (e.g., multi-party systems, non-Western cultures). (R3)
- **Theoretical novelty limited**: Methods (probing, intervention) are derived from prior work; the main contribution is a new application rather than a novel theoretical framework. (R3)
- **Writing quality needs improvement**: Several missing details; a pipeline figure illustrating the workflow would help readers. (R3)
- **Specific missing experimental details**:
  - "U.S. politician data" used to train ridge regression (line 223) is ambiguous – does it refer to extracted activations? How is the train-test split performed? (R3)
  - Clarify whether 2-fold cross-validation (line 344) applies only to ridge regression or also to head selection. (R3)
  - Provide legends for x-axes in Figure 3. (R3)
  - Explain when accuracy vs. Spearman rank correlation is used in Section 4.1. (R3)
  - Describe how Figure 4 is generated (DW-Nominate scores vs. attention activation?). (R3)
  - In line 417, what is the exact correlation measured (based on conservative steering only, or all three conditions)? (R3)
  - Provide hyperparameters of the ridge regression model and how they were tuned. (R3)
- **Reference for linear conceptualization of political slant (line 133) is missing**: The claim that social scientists model ideology on a linear spectrum requires specific citations and should address multi-dimensional alternatives. (R4)
