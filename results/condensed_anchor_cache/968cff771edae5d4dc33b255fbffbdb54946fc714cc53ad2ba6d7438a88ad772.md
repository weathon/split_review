## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the MultiTask Influence Function (MTIF), extending data attribution from single-task learning (STL) to multitask learning (MTL). MTIF leverages the block structure of the Hessian to efficiently approximate leave-one-out (LOO) and leave-one-task-out (LOTO) effects without retraining, enabling both data-level and task-level influence analysis. The method is validated on linear models and shallow neural networks across synthetic, Human Action Recognition (HAR), and CelebA datasets. Results show positive correlation with ground-truth LOTO scores and demonstrate that removing the most problematic examples (as measured by MTIF) improves performance on most CelebA tasks. The paper also frames MTIF as a measure of task relatedness and a tool to mitigate negative transfer.

### Strengths
- **Novel extension to MTL**: The work is the first to extend influence function-based data attribution to multitask learning, addressing the unique challenges posed by shared and task-specific parameters. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5, R6)
- **Clear motivation and presentation**: The paper clearly motivates the need for efficient data attribution in MTL and explains MTIF’s construction, including the use of the Hessian block structure to circumvent the large full-parameter Hessian. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Efficient approximation of LOO/LOTO**: MTIF avoids costly retraining by using first-order approximations, providing both data-level and task-level influence. (R1, R2)
- **Practical application – data selection**: The data selection strategy enabled by MTIF yields consistent performance improvements on CelebA across the majority of tasks, offering a concrete benefit. (R1, R2, R5, R6)
- **Novel perspective on task relatedness**: The method provides fine-grained data-level influence to interpret task relatedness, going beyond coarse task-level metrics. (R1, R6)
- **Empirical validation across multiple datasets**: Experiments on synthetic, HAR, and CelebA datasets show correlation between MTIF and the LOTO effect (though correlation varies). (R1, R2, R3, R5)
- **Strong correlation on simpler settings**: On linear models and HAR, correlation coefficients are high (e.g., reported as strong in Tables 1 and 2). (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of comparative baselines**: No comparison is made with other attribution methods or simple baselines. There are no benchmarks against trivial alternatives (e.g., cosine similarity of gradients for task-level influence, or gradient similarity for data-level influence). Tables 1 and 2 only compare against ground-truth LOTO, making it hard to judge the magnitude of the reported correlations (e.g., is 21% correlation on CelebA high or low?). A comparison to existing single-task influence functions adapted to MTL would help assess relative performance. (R1, R2, R3, R5, R6)
- **Limited experimental scope**: Experiments are restricted to linear models and shallow neural networks. The method is not evaluated on deep architectures (CNNs, transformers, vision-language models) or more realistic MTL settings. This raises concerns about practical relevance and scalability to current MTL practice. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R6)
- **No computational complexity analysis**: Despite the claim of efficiency, the paper lacks explicit theoretical or empirical analysis of MTIF’s computational complexity. Hessian computations remain costly, and the paper does not discuss further optimizations or approximations (e.g., EK-FAC, LiSSA) for high-dimensional models or many tasks. (R2, R3, R6)
- **Insufficient justification of key assumptions**: The derivation of Equation (1) and the extension of influence functions to MTL is not rigorously justified. The assumptions under which MTIF holds (e.g., convex loss, local optimality, invertible Hessian) are not discussed in the MTL context. The analysis of Equation (6) depends on local properties of the parameter that may change during training; the paper does not address how influence scores evolve with training or how to interpret them when derivatives are not stable. (R4)
- **Sample selection process not detailed**: The paper does not specify whether samples for experiments are chosen randomly or deterministically, raising concerns about reproducibility and potential bias. (R4)
- **Negative transfer analysis is shallow**: While negative transfer mitigation is a claimed application, the paper does not provide a deep analysis of how MTIF identifies or quantifies negative transfer. No granular correlation between influence scores and actual performance changes is given, and the mechanism is unclear. (R1, R3) The related work also overlooks several papers [1,2,3] on negative transfer in MTL. (R3)
- **Technical novelty is limited**: The main idea is a conceptually straightforward extension of single-task influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) to MTL, using the block structure of the Hessian to handle shared and task-specific parameters. Under the convex combination of tasks assumption, the cross-task influence is easy to analyze and does not introduce fundamentally new concepts in optimization or learning theory. (R3, R6) However, some reviewers (R1, R5) consider the extension significant; this contrast is noted.
- **Data selection may be too coarse**: The current approach removes entire problematic examples. Recent MTL methods (e.g., gradient surgery) only adjust conflicting components, preserving beneficial parts. The paper does not discuss how MTIF could be extended to support such finer-grained interventions. (R6)
- **Low correlation on complex datasets**: On CelebA, the correlation between MTIF and the LOTO effect is quite low (e.g., around 21% for some tasks), raising questions about the reliability of the method in more complex scenarios. (R6; R5 also asks whether 21% is high/low)
- **Missing citations for baselines in Table 3**: The methods listed in Table 3 are not cited. (R3)
- **Minority reviewer disagreement on novelty**: While most reviewers praise the novelty, Reviewer 6 considers it a straightforward extension with limited theoretical depth. This disagreement is preserved. (R6 vs. R1,R5)

*Note: Reviewer questions that overlap with weaknesses have been incorporated above (e.g., baseline comparisons, extension to gradient surgery, computation of Hessian, sample selection details, analysis of negative transfer, and validation on additional datasets like HAR/synthetic for data selection).*
