## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces OmniBench, a human-curated multiple-choice benchmark (1142 questions) requiring simultaneous understanding of visual, acoustic, and textual modalities to answer correctly, and OmniInstruct, an 84.5k (or 97k, per Reviewer 2) instruction-tuning dataset built from existing multimodal QA data (MSRVTT-QA, AVQA, Music-AVQA2.0). Evaluations show most open-source omni-language models (OLMs) perform near chance (below 50% accuracy). Reviewers acknowledge the motivation for tri-modal evaluation and the value of the resources, but opinions diverge sharply: Reviewer 2 (score 8, conf 4) finds the paper well-written and timely, while Reviewer 3 (score 3, conf 4) strongly criticises dataset size (e.g., only 15 “Count and Quantity” questions), quality (a confusing example in Figure 3), and missing human baseline. Reviewers 1 and 4 (both score 6) raise concerns about industrial applicability, omitted verification of OmniInstruct’s efficacy, and incomplete evaluation protocols. All reviewers note missing details on source/license for OmniBench.

### Strengths
- Addresses a clear gap: existing benchmarks focus on single or dual-modality, and tri-modal evaluation is largely overlooked. (Reviewer 2)
- Well-motivated: each OmniBench question is verified to require all three modalities for correct answer, ensuring results reflect joint reasoning. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Uses a multi-stage human and machine annotation protocol with strict criteria to guarantee tri-modal dependency. (Reviewer 1)
- Provides a new instruction-tuning dataset, OmniInstruct (84.5k/97k samples), which could support future supervised fine-tuning for tri-modal reasoning. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- Extensive literature survey of multimodal benchmarks is well documented. (Reviewer 2)
- Paper is clearly written with informative figures. (Reviewer 2)
- Leverages existing resources to construct the benchmark, contributing a useful evaluation tool for the community. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset size and statistical significance**: OmniBench contains only 1142 questions. After splitting into audio types (speech, sound event, music) and fine-grained categories (e.g., “Object Identification”, “Plot Inference”, “Count and Quantity”), some subcategories have very few samples (e.g., 15 total “Count and Quantity” questions, 28 “Object Identification” for sound events). This makes per-category comparisons unreliable, and no statistical significance testing is reported. (Reviewer 3)  
- **Quality and interpretability of questions**:  
  - Few example questions are provided, and category definitions (e.g., “Plot Inference” for music or sound events) are not explained. (Reviewer 3)  
  - The only main-text example (Figure 3) appears erroneous: the question “What is this black box” has answer “a red pill”, but no black box is visible in the image or mentioned in the audio. (Reviewer 3)  
  - No human performance is reported; questions crafted by annotators should be validated by an independent set of humans to confirm they are answerable. (Reviewer 3)  
- **Source and license not specified**: The origin of the benchmark data is unclear (manual inspection suggests film clips, e.g., a famous *Friends* scene). The license is missing entirely; if it becomes a standard, a CC-NC license could hinder adoption. (Reviewer 2)  
- **Potential dataset bias**: If sourced from films, famous scenes (e.g., *Friends*) may be recognizable from the visual clip alone, undermining the claim that all three modalities are necessary. (Reviewer 2)  
- **OmniInstruct not validated**: The 84.5k instruction-tuning dataset is introduced but never used in any experiment. Its quality and effectiveness for improving tri-modal reasoning are not demonstrated, leaving the purpose unclear. (Reviewers 3, 4)  
- **Missing evaluation protocols**: The paper lacks a clear description of how multiple-choice questions are structured, how model responses are evaluated (e.g., exact match, log-probability scoring), and what metrics or statistical tests are used. (Reviewer 4)  
- **No text-specific evaluation**: Although the benchmark claims to cover three modalities, the evaluation in Section 5 only measures image and audio understanding; no text-only tasks are reported, making it hard to assess how much the textual context contributes. (Reviewer 4)  
- **MCQ format limitations**: The four-choice design gives a 25% random baseline. The mitigation of including one obviously wrong option does not fully remove this bias, potentially overestimating reasoning capabilities. (Reviewer 2)  
- **Limited industrial applicability**: The strict requirement that all modalities be essential may not align with real-world tasks where modalities often provide only supplementary information, potentially limiting adoption of the benchmark. (Reviewer 1)  
- **Model choice discrepancy**: OmniInstruct was built using InternVL-2-76B, but OmniBench’s quality control used LLaVA-1.6-34B; the reason for this choice is not explained. (Reviewer 2)  
- **Unexplored scaling and bias**: The impact of replacing images/audio with textual approximations on generalisation is not discussed, and no analysis of cultural bias (e.g., in audio/visual content) is provided. (Reviewer 1) – Reviewer 1 also questions the feasibility of extending the benchmark to future modalities (e.g., haptics, time-series) given the term “omni-language”. (Reviewer 1)
