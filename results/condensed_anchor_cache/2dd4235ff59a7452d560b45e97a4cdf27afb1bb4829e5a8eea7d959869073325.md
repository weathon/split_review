## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces PsychoBench (also called PPBench), a framework for evaluating psychological portrayal of LLMs using thirteen scales from clinical psychology, categorized into four domains: personality traits, interpersonal relationships, motivational tests, and emotional abilities. The study tests five LLMs (text-davinci-003, ChatGPT, GPT-4, LLaMA-2-7b, LLaMA-2-13b) with Likert-scale responses, and employs a jailbreak approach to bypass safety alignment. The paper reports seven specific findings and compares to human benchmarks.

### Strengths
- Strong motivation for evaluating LLMs' psychological portrayal, an under-explored area (R1, R3). The work presents a solid case for using psychometrics and compelling empirical results testing modern LLMs (R1).
- Interdisciplinary approach bridging AI, psychology, and social science is significant (R2, R3).
- PPBench provides a structured, comprehensive framework integrating previous approaches into a single benchmark (R2). The choice of thirteen seminal scales is well-motivated (R1).
- Robust statistical testing and randomization of question sequences to account for model context (R1).
- Use of jailbreak methods to circumvent safety alignment, revealing intrinsic tendencies (R1, R2).
- Exploration of personality traits under different assigned roles (R2).
- Good awareness of prior work; references are comprehensive and up-to-date (R2).
- Rationale is appropriate for both CS and social science researchers (R2).

*Note: One reviewer (R1, score 8) found the contribution strong overall, while the other two reviewers (R2 and R3, score 6) had more reservations about significance and methodology, as reflected below.*

### Weaknesses
- **Human population comparisons are suspect.** The human benchmarks used for comparison (e.g., BFI from "six high schools in China", DTDD from "undergraduate psychology students from the United States", ICB from "Hong Kong students") have cultural biases and small sample sizes. Although the authors are transparent about demographics in the Appendix, the main paper's interpretation overreaches relative to "average human population" performance (R1).
- **Lack of discussion on limitations and significance.** The paper does not adequately discuss work limitations, either of results or of the framework itself (R2). Given prior work like Safdari et al. [2023], which uses more sophisticated methodologies than direct questionnaire application, the significance of this work is questionable (R2). Not all psychometric aspects may be equally justifiable for LLMs; for instance, emotional competence could be attributed to sentiment analysis ability and training data including fiction, and the paper does not distinguish between emotional competence and Theory of Mind (e.g., Bubeck et al. [2023] show GPT-4 passes a modified Sally-Anne test). More discussion in Section 3.2.4 would be welcome (R2).
- **Prompt design concerns.** (a) The Likert prompt explicitly calls the LLM a "helpful assistant", which may activate an assistant persona and bias results towards high Agreeableness scores that persist even after jailbreak (R1, R2). No discussion of how prompt design impacts psychological portrayal conclusions (R1). (b) Instructing LLMs to respond only with Likert scale numbers oversimplifies their responses and may not capture the richness and nuance of their capabilities; some psychological aspects are too complex for a single number (R3). (c) The prompt design is overly simplistic; results may vary with different prompts such as chain-of-thought (R3).
- **Temperature settings are problematic.** temperature=0 is used for reproducibility, but the paper already performs randomization and statistical testing. Using a slightly higher temperature (e.g., 0.01) or multiple replications would have been feasible. The results are not comparable across LLMs because LLaMA-2 uses a different temperature (R1).
- **Missing discussion on alignment interventions.** The paper does not discuss how alignment methods like RLHF, which differ across models and versions, might affect psychological portrayal (R2). This could explain behavioral differences between GPT-4 and GPT-3.5.
- **Missing discussion on training data.** The relation between psychometric results and training data inputs is not explored, despite the potential impact of fictional material (e.g., novels) in training data on empathic abilities (R2).
- **Real-world applicability of role variations.** The paper shows variations in personality traits across roles but does not discuss the extent to which these reflect real-world user interactions with LLMs (R3).

*Note: R1 (score 8) is more positive than R2 and R3 (both 6). R1 raised the human benchmark and temperature concerns but still recommended acceptance. R2 and R3 had additional concerns about significance, prompt design, and missing discussion, leading to a lower overall assessment.*
