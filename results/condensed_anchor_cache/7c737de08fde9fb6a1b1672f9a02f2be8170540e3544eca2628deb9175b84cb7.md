## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an active learning method to discover pairwise interactions between perturbations (e.g., gene knockouts) from unstructured data (e.g., microscopy images). It introduces two statistical tests: a separability test (based on KL divergence) and a disjointness test (based on MMD), and integrates them into a matrix completion framework with an active sampling strategy (ADS) to select which perturbation pairs to test next. The method is validated on synthetic data and a real cell painting dataset with 50 gene pairs. All reviewers acknowledge the problem is important and the methodology is well-structured.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses the important problem of efficiently identifying pairwise interactions, with actual gene knockout experiments performed to validate effectiveness (R2).
- The two proposed interaction tests are technically interesting and well-organized with clear assumptions, leveraging existing theories (MMD-based two-sample test, ASD) (R2).
- The active learning formulation is novel, especially in dealing with imaging modalities (as opposed to RNAseq) (R1).
- Assumptions (e.g., latent variables Z capture all information about perturbations) are clearly stated; the statistical hypothesis testing framework is well motivated (R1).
- Evaluation is thorough, including comparisons to several baselines on both synthetic and real data; the real biological experiment demonstrates practical utility (R1, R4).
- The appendix thoroughly explains the architecture and methodological choices (R1).
- Reviewer 3 (score 6, more positive) notes that theoretical claims are justified with mathematical proofs and empirical validation, and the method can make biological experiments more efficient and lower-cost.

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of comparison with standard statistical interaction tests.** The paper does not compare the proposed tests with standard methods for detecting interaction effects, such as likelihood-ratio tests, two-way ANOVA, or approaches from the epistasis literature [1][2][3]. Such comparisons are necessary to assess the advantages of the new tests objectively (R2).

2. **Lack of comparison with causal discovery methods.** The paper does not compare against causal discovery methods that use interventions (e.g., DCDI [4], GIES [5]) or gene regulatory network inference (e.g., SCENIC [6]). The authors' argument that causal discovery requires all variables observed is not entirely convincing, as many methods handle latent confounders; a more thorough justification for excluding these baselines is needed (R3).

3. **Weak novelty and limited technical contribution.** Reviewer 4 finds the paper applied with weak novelty. The separability metric essentially measures mutual information between latent variables; many density estimation methods could be used, but the paper does not explore sensitivity to the choice of KL divergence estimator. Similarly, the disjointness metric relies on additivity testing in a learned representation space, and the impact of the representation learning method is not discussed. The paper combines existing methods for an empirical goal that may be better suited for a more applied venue (R4).

4. **No quantification of experimental savings.** It is not quantified how much the active learning algorithm reduces the number of necessary perturbations compared to an exhaustive approach. A concrete comparison (e.g., percentage of search space explored to achieve a given level of interaction discovery) is needed (R3).

5. **Insufficient justification for active learning without directly measuring pairwise effects.** The rationale for using adaptive sampling or bandit to identify interacting pairs without actually performing double perturbations is unconvincing. For example, in synthetic lethality, single knockouts may be non-lethal, offering no observable clue that the pair is interacting. The assumptions (e.g., low-rank R with a Gaussian prior on columns, line 350) appear ad hoc and require more rigorous justification (R2).

6. **Inappropriate bandit setting.** The bandit (best-arm identification) framework assumes repeated sampling of each arm, but here many (i,j) pairs are never sampled. For synthetic lethality, double knockouts across all pairs with replicates would be needed. The comparison in Figure 6 (random search and conventional bandit methods) is unclear in its meaning and requires additional justification (R2).

7. **Null distribution and stopping criterion not addressed.** Reviewers question the null distribution of the test statistics in the matrix completion setting. Given that many pairs are tested (e.g., 1225 gene pairs from 50 genes), at least one pair is likely to have a large test statistic by chance. The paper should discuss a null distribution and how to decide when all interacting pairs have been found (a stopping criterion). Additionally, Reviewer 1 suggests including a baseline that reveals δ_ij for pairs where δ_i and δ_j are marginally most different from δ_0 (e.g., sum log density ratios) (R1, R2).

8. **Choice of imaging data over RNAseq not justified.** The paper does not justify why imaging data is used instead of more structured RNAseq data for the matrix completion problem (R1).

9. **Quantitative frequency of non-additive joint distributions not provided.** Reviewer 1 asks for a quantitative analysis of how often the joint distribution significantly differs from the product of marginals in this dataset, noting that some studies suggest few perturbations obey this relation. Also, how many gene pairs typically have non-linear interactions? (R1)

10. **Validation limited to synthetic lethality only.** The paper only validates on synthetic lethality, which is a specific case of epistasis; there is insufficient evidence that the method works for other types of interaction effects. The title claims a general method but the scope may need to be narrowed (R2, R4).

11. **Clarity of definitions and methods.** Several points are unclear:  
- "Pairwise interactions" – are they causal dependencies or correlations? Can causal relations be discovered? (R3)  
- "Unstructured data" – images are structured as grids of pixels (R3).  
- The notion of "interaction" in line 94 needs elaboration (R3).  
- The paper should clarify which test (separability or disjointness) is more promising and under what conditions each should be used (R2).  
- The threshold for the KL divergence identity condition in the separability test (whether it is a binary decision or hypothesis test) is not explained (R2).  
- Terms like "3 dimensional tabular data" (line 374) and "genes from the same pathway are ordered adjacently" (line 478) need explanation (R3).  
- The selection criterion for “top 5% pairs” (line 509) and regret computation (line 511) need elaboration (R3).

12. **Density estimation sample requirements.** How many samples are required for the density estimator in Appendix B.1 to be faithful? What happens if the densities are far off? (R1)
