## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper tackles two limitations in reverse knowledge distillation for unsupervised anomaly detection in medical images: insufficient feature discriminability and inability to handle anomaly scale variations. It proposes contrastive student-teacher learning that uses a “good” teacher (benign images) and a “bad” teacher (images with synthetic simplex-noise anomalies) to train a student model that maximizes cosine similarity with the good teacher and minimizes it with the bad teacher. A scale adaptation mechanism softly weights contrastive distillation losses across layers. Evaluated on three medical datasets (lung, brain, skin), the method outperforms 18 baselines in AUC, F1, and Accuracy. Ablation studies confirm the contributions of contrastive reverse distillation and scale adaptation.

### Strengths
- Anomaly detection is an important and well-motivated topic, especially in medical imaging where labeled anomalous data are scarce.
- The paper is well structured, clearly written, and easy to follow; figures are informative and easy to understand.
- The ideas are clearly presented: the two identified problems (discriminability and scale variation) are directly addressed by the proposed solutions.
- The contrastive reverse distillation framework (good/bad teacher with synthetic anomalies) is considered novel, intuitive, and a clever use of simplex noise.
- Empirical analysis is very comprehensive: comparison against 18 detectors across three datasets (RSNA, Brain Tumor, ISIC), with ablation studies on both proposed components and on different noise types.
- The proposed method outperforms all baselines on all three datasets in terms of AUC, F1, and Accuracy (e.g., a 3.01% and 4.38% improvement on certain metrics).
- The method scales well with model architecture size, indicating robustness.
- Multiple reviewers praised the overall quality, with one noting that the method’s impact could be built upon in future work.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and incremental contribution:** One reviewer expressed that the novelty is incremental—building on well-established methods (reverse distillation and contrastive learning) rather than introducing a fundamentally new approach. Generating synthetic anomalies to enhance discriminability has been explored before (e.g., Perturbation Learning [Cai & Fan, NeurIPS 2022]), and fusing multi-scale features is a long-standing topic. This reviewer also considered the domain limited to medical image analysis and questioned generalization to non-medical domains.
- **Writing and structure (introduction):** A reviewer found the introduction confusing: the paragraphing is suboptimal, with excessive discussion of related work and insufficient emphasis on the paper’s contributions. The third paragraph could better unify the discussion of self-supervised learning with the preceding critique of generative models.
- **Empirical significance and context:** One reviewer noted that while improvements are shown (e.g., 3.01% and 4.38%), it is hard to gauge their significance without knowing how much prior works pushed accuracy on these datasets. The paper lacks discussion of the trade-off between true positive rate and false positive rate in clinical practice; the same reviewer suggested plotting ROC curves for the top 5 baselines to allow comparison at different false-positive levels. Another reviewer asked for context on whether AUC increases of this magnitude warrant publication.
- **Missing runtime analysis:** The paper does not report training time or complexity. A reviewer requested a comparison of training time with the next two best methods (e.g., in seconds or time complexity) to provide a complete picture.
- **No code provided in supplementary material:** Although the paper states code will be released upon publication, no anonymous repository or code zip was included. The reviewer noted this is unacceptable in the current era, as many SOTA claims go unreleased.
- **Uncited claims:** Several statements lack citations: (1) “anomalous samples is often prohibitively expensive and time-consuming” (line 36); (2) “they tend to generalize too well, thereby risking the reconstruction of abnormal regions” (line 45); (3) “Medical images typically exhibit a power law distribution of frequencies, with lower frequency components dominating the image content” (line 167). A reviewer requested citations for these claims.
- **Terminology:** The use of “good” and “bad” teacher was considered non-technical and hard to follow; a reviewer requested more appropriate naming.
- **Table presentation:** Table 1 is overwhelming; it would benefit from grouping detectors by type (e.g., RD4AD, RD++, ReContrast together), underlining the second-best score for each metric, and possibly adding standard deviations from multiple runs (at least in supplementary material). The table is also unsorted; sorting by increasing AUC would help readability.
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms:**
  - *Noise generation realism:* The noise is sampled from a uniform distribution per position, but in medical imaging, anomalies appear in specific locations (e.g., chest abnormalities do not appear outside body regions). A reviewer asked why prior knowledge about typical anomaly locations was not incorporated.
  - *Image-level vs. patch-level evaluation:* It is unclear whether each test sample is evaluated at the image level or patch level. If image-level, the positive-to-negative anomaly ratio is high, which does not align with the extreme imbalance typical in anomaly detection.
  - *Dataset-specific patterns:* Why is ISIC the hardest dataset and Brain Tumor the easiest? Why is the scale adaptation mechanism (SAM) more impactful on Brain Tumor than on RSNA? Could a mixture of noise types (e.g., 10% Gaussian, 90% simplex) improve results? Would including real-world normal data in training improve or harm generalization?
  - *Lack of formal guarantees:* The paper provides no formal proofs or theorems guaranteeing effectiveness; its strengths rely solely on experiments.
  - *Ablation on noise type:* Table 3 shows Gaussian noise performing similarly to simplex noise on RSNA but much worse on Brain Tumor—why?
