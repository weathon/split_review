## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes REFACTOR, a method to train neural networks to extract reusable theorems from human proofs by binary node classification on proof trees using a graph neural network. Training data is constructed by artificially unraveling function applications (substituting proof trees). On unseen proofs, the model extracts 19.6% of theorems that humans would use. From the set.mm Metamath library, 1923 novel theorems are extracted. These new theorems are used on average 733.5 times after refactoring and help shorten proof lengths. Training the Holophrasm prover on the refactored dataset improves its performance from 557/2720 to 632/2720. The work is presented as relevant for proof simplification and generating better training datasets.

### Strengths
- Novel problem: extracting reusable theorems from proofs; first application of GNN to this task.
- Technically sound GNN-based approach for binary node classification on proof trees.
- Interesting and principled method of creating training data by artificially unraveling function applications (imitation learning on semi-synthetic data).
- Experiments demonstrate usefulness: extracted theorems (1923 from set.mm) shorten human proofs (average usage 733.5 times) and improve theorem prover performance (Holophrasm from 557/2720 to 632/2720; also improves MetaGen performance when trained with extracted theorems).
- Potentially impactful for proof simplification and generating better training datasets.

### Weaknesses
*Note: One reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 5) was significantly more critical than the other three (scores 8).*

- **Framing and background**: Claims that the method “mimics what human mathematicians do” are not backed up; compression of proofs is not the same as human conjecture-making (Reviewer 3). Section 3 misses essential background such as the Curry-Howard correspondence and how it applies to Metamath; the equivalence of proofs and computation trees is assumed without sufficient explanation (Reviewer 3). The example in Figure 1 is hard to understand; plain language explanation of objects like `ph`, `wffph` is needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Writing issues**: Incorrect use of “connected component” (used to mean “connected subgraph”) (Reviewer 3). Need explanation why a connected subtree is not sufficient for validity, especially regarding bound variables (which would be worse in more sophisticated systems) (Reviewer 3). Check citation formatting (`\citep`/`\citet`) (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited scope and generalizability**: Metamath is a relatively simple formal language, less commonly used than Isabelle/Lean; results may not transfer to more complex ATP environments and the immediate impact on widely used systems is limited (Reviewer 1). No direct comparison with state-of-the-art methods in more established systems; unclear how extracted theorems compare to those derived by tactics in Isabelle/Lean (Reviewer 1). One reviewer questioned whether extracting “new theorems” from existing proofs is actually helpful to the ATP or mathematics community in general (Reviewer 2). Evaluations are only on a simple library; no fair comparison to training-free compression schemes for extracting new theorems (Reviewer 3).
- **Methodological concerns**: The necessity of training a neural network is not validated. A simple traversal of the proof tree to extract all sub-proofs as theorems could surpass the proposed method (e.g., obtain all human-defined rules and more valid theorems than 1923). No comparison with such baselines is provided (Reviewer 4). The exact procedure to extract a subtree from the binary prediction logits is not described in the main text. The code uses an argmax scheme; it is unclear why sampling or other approaches were not tried. The method cannot produce a multimodal distribution over subtrees, which is a limitation if compressions on two different subtrees are mutually exclusive (Reviewer 3). Also, why not fill nodes with pre-defined rules to connect positive nodes and obtain more valid theorems? (Reviewer 4).
- **Related work**: The related work comparison can be improved; no works after 2021 are cited (Reviewer 2).
- **Data and evaluation questions**: Could newly extracted theorems be equivalent to existing ones by symmetries? Does this affect the experimental claims? (Reviewer 3). Do the positive and negative nodes balance in the training data? (Reviewer 4). How does a theorem extracted from the validation set support the proofs in the test set? (Reviewer 4). Do the authors know for certain that the “human-written” proofs were written each proof from scratch? How does the possibility of human copying affect the claims? (Reviewer 3). Could simple rule-based extraction methods, such as byte-pair encoding (BPE), improve the performance of ATP? (Reviewer 4). One reviewer noted that it is possible that the 19.6% result is the best possible for the current REFACTOR pipeline and architecture, and it would be meaningful to know whether results can be improved by expanding the theorems further and generating more training data (Reviewer 2).
