## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces DiMA, a latent diffusion model that generates protein sequences by diffusing on ESM-2 embeddings and decoding them back to sequences. The authors claim superior quality, diversity, and distribution matching compared to autoregressive and discrete diffusion baselines while using ten times fewer parameters. Evaluations are performed on SwissProt (~200–300K sequences) and AFDB, with ablations on noise schedule, self-conditioning, encoder size, and time embedding. Multiple sequence-based and structure-based metrics are reported.

### Strengths
- **Idea of latent diffusion on pre-trained pLM embeddings**: Leveraging ESM-2 embeddings as a continuous latent space for diffusion is a valuable and interesting contribution to the field (R1, R4, R6).
- **Head-to-head discrete vs. continuous comparison**: The work provides a direct comparison between discrete diffusion over amino acid sequences and continuous diffusion over ESM embeddings (R1).
- **Thorough ablation study**: The ablations (noise schedule, self-conditioning, ESM encoder size, time embedding, model scaling) are well-designed and offer practical insights for others working on latent protein generation (R2, R3, R5).
- **Rigorous experimental setup**: Retraining baselines on the same dataset (SwissProt) and controlling for parameter count (33M) is a strong and fair effort (R2).
- **Strong empirical performance overall**: DiMA achieves comparable or better sequence quality and diversity than larger pretrained models (e.g., ProGen, ProtGPT, DPLM) with significantly fewer parameters (R2, R5). Results are particularly competitive on distribution-similarity metrics (R2).
- **Comprehensive metric coverage**: The evaluation includes quality (PPL, BLAST identity), distribution similarity (FD-seq, MMD-seq, FD-struct), diversity (self-consistency, repeated subsequences), and biological relevance (pLDDT, ESMFold agreement) (R3, R5, R6). The focus on distribution similarity is a valuable standard (R2, R3).
- **Parameter efficiency**: DiMA uses 33M trainable backbone parameters, ten times fewer than leading models, which is a significant practical advantage (R1, R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaimed statements and imprecision**: The claim that “unconditional generation remains underexplored” is incorrect (ProGen, ProtGPT, EvoDiff have explored it) (R1). The claim about “straightforward adaptation of image-based diffusion not yielding satisfactory results” is unfounded; diffusion models are state-of-the-art in structure space and discrete diffusion is not a straightforward adaptation of image diffusion (R1). The phrase “most comprehensive set of metrics” is a gross overclaim that weakens credibility; several important evaluations (e.g., novelty against training set, conditioned generation) are missing (R1, R2, R4). These overstatements undermine the paper’s soundness (R1, R4).
- **Insufficient motivation for latent diffusion versus alternatives**: The manuscript does not adequately justify why latent diffusion offers advantages over discrete diffusion (e.g., D3PM, EvoDiff, DPLM) or over 3D-structure continuous diffusion (e.g., Chroma, MultiFlow) for protein sequence generation. Specific technical or practical benefits are missing (R3, R4, R6). Several related latent diffusion works (Pro-LDM, CHEAP) and strong baselines (Chroma, MultiFlow) are not cited or compared (R1, R6). The omission weakens the positioning (R1, R6).
- **Evaluation metrics computed on the training set, not a held-out test set**: All distributional metrics (FD-seq, MMD-seq) are computed relative to the training set. This only reflects the ability to memorize the training set, not genuine generalization or generative capability. Comparisons should be made against an independent test set (R1). A similar memorization check (sequence identity to nearest neighbors in the training set) is missing; without it, high-performance metrics could simply result from memorization (R3, R5).
- **Novelty and diversity metrics are too lenient**: The 95% sequence-identity threshold for novelty is extremely lenient; more stringent thresholds (e.g., 70% or 50%) are needed. The “repeated subsequence” metric does not measure true diversity. These issues call into question the claimed diversity of generated sequences (R1, R3).
- **Limited sequence length range (128–254 aa)**: The evaluation is restricted to a narrow length window, raising concerns about generalizability to longer sequences. Discrete diffusion methods might perform better for longer sequences; this is not investigated (R3, R5).
- **Inconsistent and unclear presentation of results**: In Tables 4 and 5, the bolded best results are inconsistent (e.g., in Progen ppl, DPLM-3B bolded even though DPLM-150M performs better) (R4, R5). This lack of attention to detail undermines credibility. Standard deviations and length distributions of generated sequences are not reported, making it hard to assess variability (R2). Figures in the appendix (e.g., 11,12,13) have vague captions and are under-analyzed (R2).
- **Paper structure and organization**: The manuscript is poorly organized; Section 4 has only one subsection (4.1) despite containing multiple experiments (R4, R5). Paragraph headings are hard to navigate (R2, R4). A main-text illustration figure is missing (R4). These issues reduce readability (R4, R5, R6).
- **Concerns about SwissProt dataset**: SwissProt is small (~200–300K sequences) and likely already seen during ESM-2 pretraining, making it easy to memorize and less compelling for demonstrating generalization. The rationale for choosing SwissProt and AFDB over larger sequence databases (UniProt, UniRef) is not justified (R1, R4). Training on evolutionary-scale data (as done in EvoDiff) might enhance model performance (R4).
- **Encoder scaling paradox**: Using the larger ESM-3B encoder yields worse distribution fitness scores (FD-seq, MMD-seq) than smaller ESM-2 encoders. This contradicts expected scaling benefits and needs deeper analysis and explanation (R2, R3).
- **Effective parameter count unfairness**: Although DiMA is compared with baselines at 33M backbone parameters, it relies on the full ESM-2 encoder during training, which adds substantial computational cost. This dependency should be explicitly acknowledged and discussed when claiming parameter-efficiency (R5).
- **pLDDT evaluation bias**: ESMFold (which uses ESM-2) is used to compute pLDDT scores for DiMA-generated sequences. Since DiMA also uses ESM-2, there is a risk of biased evaluation. Alternative pLDDT from AlphaFold2 or OmegaFold 2 should be considered (R5).
- **Lack of conditioned/guided generation and downstream utility**: DiMA is limited to unconditional generation and lacks mechanisms for inpainting, motif scaffolding, or incorporating structural constraints, which severely restricts practical protein engineering applications (R5). No independent downstream evaluation (e.g., functional assay or fold prediction) is conducted to demonstrate real-world utility (R1, R5).
- **ProGen perplexity as a quality metric**: The use of ProGen PPL as a quality metric is not justified; recent work (arXiv:2210.05892) questions its reliability for protein design. Further discussion is needed (R3).
- **Under-analyzed results and lack of qualitative examples**: Results are presented as single-point comparisons without showing actual generated sequences or their folded structures. The paper would be strengthened by moving biological and qualitative analyses (currently in appendix pages 24–28) into the main text and providing more interpretation of what the metrics mean (R2, R5, R6). Sequence variability at the biochemical or functional level is not analyzed (R6).
- **Minority reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R6, score 3) finds the contribution insufficient for a top AI conference despite acknowledging interesting ideas and extensive evaluation, noting that methodological novelty is lacking. Another reviewer (R1) notes that DiMA is often outperformed by baselines on several metrics, contradicting the paper’s claims of superiority (R1). This contrasts with R2 and R5 who find results quite strong overall. The discrepancy indicates that the narrative of “superior performance” is not fully supported.
- **Numerous technical questions left open**: Why use ESM2-8M as the default encoder? (R4) What is the intuition behind the 320 hidden dimension? (R5) Why does FD-seq not equal zero for ground truth data? (R5) Why is FD-struct consistently lower than FD-seq? (R5) Why not use fine-tuned ESM-2 as in DPLM? (R5) These remain unanswered and suggest gaps in the methodological justification.
- **Citation and formatting issues**: Citation format should use \citep consistently (R5, R6), font sizes in tables are inconsistent (R2), and the noise schedule formula is duplicated (R2).
