## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers acknowledge the paper proposes Presto!, a set of distillation techniques for accelerating diffusion-based text-to-music (TTM) generation by reducing both sampling steps (via a GAN-based distribution matching distillation adapted from DMD2 to continuous-time score-based models) and per-step cost (via a layer distillation method adapted from ASE with an improvement to preserve hidden state variance). The combined method (Presto-LS) achieves a 10-18× speedup and is claimed to be the fastest high-quality TTM. Most reviewers (R1, R3, R4: scores 8) are impressed by the results, demonstrations, and comprehensive experiments; one reviewer (R2: score 5) raises significant concerns about clarity, reproducibility, and definition of core components. All reviewers flag the lack of open code or detailed implementation as a major barrier to reproduction and follow-up work.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and contribution**: First application of GAN-based adversarial distillation (DMD2) to TTM (R1, R4) and first use of layer distillation for per-step reduction in TTM (R1, R4). The fusion of step and layer distillation is a novel, orthogonal approach (R1, R2, R4).
- **Performance**: Presto! outperforms the teacher model with as few as 4 sampling steps (R1, R4); the continuous step distillation yields predictable quality improvements with more steps (R1). Subjective evaluation (violin plot) shows Presto! surpasses Stable Audio Open and SoundCTM (R1). Objective metrics (FAD, CLAP, AD, MMD) all improve when aligning distributions (Table 1) (R2). The combined method achieves 10-18× speedup (230/435 ms for 32 s mono/stereo) (R1, R2).
- **Methodology and design**: The paper clearly motivates accelerating both steps and per-step cost (R1). Modifications to DMD2 and ASE are explained with comprehensive ablation studies (R3, R4). Related work is up-to-date and informative (R1).
- **Presentation**: Most reviewers find the paper well written, with clear motivation and experimental results (R1, R3). R2, however, finds key sections hard to follow and Figure 1 dense and insufficiently explained (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Reproducibility and transparency**: All reviewers note that the models are trained on internal data and neither code nor checkpoints are released. This severely limits reproducibility, validation of the claims, and future research building on this work (R1, R2, R3, R4). R4 strongly recommends releasing the codebase or providing sufficient implementation details, citing examples from prior works that release code without weights. R1 asks whether the implementation (without pretrained weights) could be opened.
- **Clarity of methodology**: Several aspects are insufficiently described:
  - Figure 1 is dense, uses unexplained notation, and does not clearly convey the mechanics of distillation (R2).
  - Terms like “online GAN-based adversarial distillation” are used without explicitly defining the GAN architecture or the adversarial loss function (R2).
  - Presto‑L assumes prior knowledge of ASE; the distillation process is not explained with an algorithm, equations, or a figure, making the paper less self-contained (R4). A more detailed literature review of ASE would help (R4).
  - The baseline description in Section 4.1 is unclear: whether the teacher model was trained and then compared with proposed methods versus directly evaluating downloaded models (MusicGen, Stable Audio Open) (R3).
  - The mathematical operations and data flow in the distillation process are not clearly laid out (R2).
- **Missing experiments and scope limitations**: 
  - The paper claims TTA (text-to-audio) applicability in the Introduction, but no experiments on TTA benchmarks (e.g., AudioCaps) are provided. R4 questions whether experiments will be conducted during rebuttal; otherwise the claim is an overstatement.
  - As the proposed framework is general, it would be informative to show results on text-to-image or text-to-video tasks (R3).
  - Multi-step sampling: R4 expects quality to plateau or degrade beyond a certain number of steps (as seen with consistency models) and asks whether this was observed (e.g., for 15 steps).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and training complexity**:
  - Stable adversarial training relies on extensive grid search; the paper does not supply the specific hyperparameter ranges or computational resources required for that search (R1).
  - It is unclear how sensitive performance is to variations in these hyperparameters, raising concerns about practical applicability (R1).
  - The update ratio (fake score model updated 5× more often than generator, L210‑211) is not tested for robustness; R4 asks whether changing to 4 or 6 would alter stability or quality.
- **Specific technical details missing**:
  - Equation (5): formulation of the CFG-augmented real score is not given; R4 asks if it is the standard CFG combination.
  - Algorithm 1: values of ν₁, ν₂ are not reported; are g₁, g₂ both optimized with Adam at 1e-4? Are learning rate schedulers used? (R4)
  - Discriminator head D_ψ: which intermediate feature activations of the DiT block does it attach to? What is the exact architecture of D_ψ? (R4) The explanation in Section 3.2.3 is too high-level.
  - Presto‑L distillation process: only ℒ_st is provided, which is insufficient; R4 asks for an algorithm table, pseudo-code, or figure.
- **Minor**: R1 notes concern about not open‑sourcing; R2 clarifies whether the 5‑20 s latency in line 35 refers to 32 s audio (should be clarified in the introduction). R3 notes that without code, readers would struggle to reproduce.
