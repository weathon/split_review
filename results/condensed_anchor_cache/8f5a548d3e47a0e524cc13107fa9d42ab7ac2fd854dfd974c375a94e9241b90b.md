## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper experimentally and theoretically investigates whether small-scale data contamination in LLM training causes persistent benchmark overfitting. The authors train models with up to 1.6B parameters, contaminating benchmark examples at various repetition frequencies (4, 12, 32, 144) and training token counts (up to 40B). They find that under Chinchilla-like scaling, minor contamination leads to overfitting, but when training exceeds five times the Chinchilla token budget—a regime typical of many modern LLMs—even 144 repetitions of contamination can be “forgotten”. The authors also derive a theoretical bound on forgetting based on cumulative weight decay in AdamW, though empirical forgetting occurs much faster than predicted. The paper challenges the common assumption that any contamination invalidates benchmark evaluations, suggesting that moderate contamination may be forgotten by the end of realistically scaled training.

### Strengths
- The research question is timely and critically important: as LLM scale increases, data contamination is unavoidable, and understanding its impact on evaluation is crucial.
- Extensive and carefully controlled experiments across multiple dimensions: model size (up to 1.6B), contamination repetition frequency (4, 12, 32, 144), number of training tokens (up to 40B), and different benchmark datasets.
- The combination of empirical scaling analyses and a theoretical framework (cumulative weight decay) provides a unified perspective on forgetting.
- Clear demonstration that forgetting occurs in over-trained regimes (beyond Chinchilla), and that weight decay plays a key role—challenging the prevailing assumption that small contamination permanently damages benchmarks.
- The paper is well written, with reproducible methods, code, and datasets (including GPT-3 and OLMo-1B).
- The study addresses an important practical question: can modern LLMs “outgrow” contamination, and the answer appears to be yes under certain conditions.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical analysis is limiting and preliminary.** The bound only considers forgetting of the exact gradient of a contaminated example; it does not account for gradient cancellation (e.g., by later examples) or correlated gradients that could cause contamination to persist. Assumes orthogonal gradient updates, which is unlikely in practice. As a result, the bound is very loose—empirical forgetting is much faster—suggesting weight decay may not be the sole or main driver of forgetting. One reviewer found this theoretical contribution weak (score 2), while others rated it as solid (score 3).
- **Assumptions restrict generalizability.** The theory assumes contamination is uniform and that training follows Chinchilla scaling laws. Real-world LLMs often operate outside this regime (e.g., with larger data-to-model ratios) and may have more complex contamination patterns (e.g., non-uniform, high-frequency, structured). The paper does not verify its findings for such scenarios.
- **Evaluation may miss subtle overfitting.** Only benchmark loss/accuracy is used to measure forgetting. The model could retain implicit knowledge or biases from contaminated data even when benchmark performance appears normal. A more thorough evaluation (e.g., probing internal representations, measuring generalization to related tasks) is needed to confirm “true” forgetting.
- **Practical applicability is unclear.** It is difficult to measure or detect data contamination in real-world LLM training. The paper does not articulate how the observed forgetting phenomenon can be leveraged in practice, given that we rarely know which examples are contaminated or where they appear in training.
- **Experiments limited to moderate model sizes (up to 1.6B).** To confidently extrapolate to state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., Llama 3, GPT-4), larger-scale validation is required.
- **The forgetting in the final part of training is not adequately addressed.** No matter how long the training, contaminated examples appearing in the last “Chinchilla” portion may never have enough steps to be forgotten. The paper’s theory does not bound this case, and the experiments do not isolate it. A reviewer asked whether the approach can handle this scenario.
- **Missing details and unclear experimental choices.**  
  - The choice of contamination repetition frequencies (4, 12, 32, 144) is not explained; these are not standard powers of two (16, 32, 128).  
  - The source of the claim “under Chinchilla training, a single instance of contamination can lead to overfitting of up to 3 percentage points” is unclear; the scaling does not appear linear with repetition, and results for a single repetition are not directly shown.  
  - Figure 3(d) description is confusing (it is not the same as (a)); the comparison between multi-pass and single-pass data confounds token count with repetition, so it is unclear whether forgetting is due to repetition or simply more total tokens.
- **Missing explorations.**
  - How does contamination affect other modalities (vision) or architectures (non‑Transformers)?  
  - Does the theory extend to low‑frequency, high‑sensitivity (e.g., privacy‑sensitive) data that might resist weight decay due to repetition?  
  - The role of weight decay is questioned: does forgetting occur at all without weight decay? (One reviewer asked this explicitly.)  
  - The paper does not provide evidence for or against a proportionality law between the percentage of contaminated samples and the observed accuracy gap (a question raised by a reviewer).  
- **Minor issues:** line ordering inconsistency (line 055‑056) and typographical error (“We being” → “We begin” at line 195).
