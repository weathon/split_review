## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes UniRiT, a few-shot non-rigid point cloud registration (N-PCR) framework for medical/surgical applications. It adopts a two‑step strategy: global rigid alignment followed by local non‑rigid refinement. A new dataset MedMatch3D (derived from MedShapeNet) is introduced, and the method is evaluated on few‑shot tasks, reporting large performance gains over existing optimization‑ and learning‑based N-PCR methods.

### Strengths
- UniRiT’s decomposition of non‑rigid registration into rigid alignment then non‑rigid refinement simplifies the learning problem and makes few‑shot training more feasible. The framework is conceptually straightforward.
- Substantial performance gains are reported on MedMatch3D (up to 94.22% improvement over the best prior approach), and the method achieves competitive accuracy with lower computational complexity and faster runtime compared to other learning‑based and optimization‑based methods.
- The introduction of MedMatch3D provides a new benchmark for medical non‑rigid point cloud registration, which is less widely studied than deformable registration on standard object datasets. The dataset could accelerate research if made publicly available.
- The paper tackles a challenging practical scenario – few‑shot registration of human organ point clouds – where data are scarce and inter‑/intra‑organ deformations are large.

### Weaknesses
- **Disconnect between GMM motivation and actual method**  
  The paper begins by modeling point clouds as Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) and derives an \(L_2\) divergence formulation (Equations 3 and 4) as motivation. However, the loss functions ultimately used operate directly on point clouds (similar to Chamfer distance) and the network design (e.g., MLP‑based feature extraction) does not explicitly leverage GMMs. The role and necessity of the GMM analysis for the final method are unclear; it remains questionable whether the same results could have been obtained without it.  
  *Reviewers R1, R2 question the connection; R2 also identifies mathematical issues in Eq. 3 & 4 (integration variable ambiguity, use of logarithm instead of squared difference in \(L_2\) divergence, questionable discretization) that raise doubts about the reliability of the analysis.*

- **Limited novelty of the two‑step strategy**  
  Decomposing non‑rigid registration into a global rigid step followed by local non‑rigid refinement is a well‑established practice in both computer‑vision and medical image registration. The paper does not demonstrate how its implementation differs significantly from prior work or why it constitutes a novel contribution.
  *R2, R3, R4 independently note this lack of novelty.*

- **Dataset realism and construction**  
  MedMatch3D is derived from a subset of MedShapeNet (which provides organ shapes from real CT/MR data) and then pair‑wise deformations are generated using Thin Plate Splines (TPS).  
  – The deformations are artificial; it is unclear whether they capture the complexity and variability of real organ deformations (e.g., due to patient positioning, surgical instruments, inflation, peristalsis).  
  – The paper does not justify the chosen TPS parameters, nor does it analyze whether the deformations are sufficiently challenging or realistic for clinical scenarios.  
  – The added value over the original MedShapeNet is limited, as the main contribution is “cleaning” and applying synthetic deformations.  
  – The data incompleteness scenario (e.g., one point cloud missing part of an organ) is mentioned (Fig. 4) but not thoroughly discussed.  
  *R3 and R4 raise these concerns; R4 also asks why more data were not generated if the deformations are synthetic.*

- **Few‑shot framing is questionable**  
  The paper repeatedly claims a “few‑shot” setting, but there is no specific few‑shot learning design (e.g., episodic training, meta‑learning, data augmentation techniques). The evaluation consists of training on a small dataset and testing on an unseen dataset, which is more a test of generalization ability than few‑shot learning per se. A clear definition of few‑shot in this context and how the method specifically addresses it is lacking.
  *R1: the term is overclaimed; a refocus on generalization would be more accurate.*

- **Missing experiments and comparisons**  
  – **Ablation studies**: The contribution of the rigid alignment step vs. the non‑rigid refinement is not isolated. No ablation comparing performance with/without global rigid alignment or with pre‑aligned shapes.  
  – **Benchmarks**: Experiments are only on the author‑created MedMatch3D. No results on standard non‑medical datasets (e.g., ModelNet40, ShapeNet, 4DComplete) or other medical registration benchmarks (e.g., Lung250M‑4B). This makes it hard to assess whether the method is tailored to the specific dataset.  
  – **Baseline training settings**: It is unclear whether baseline models (e.g., those compared in the Table) were retrained on the same data or used pre‑trained weights; the large performance gap (94.22%) demands an explanation of potential factors (overfitting? evaluation protocol?). Statistical significance tests are missing.  
  – **Non‑learning methods**: No comparison with optimization‑based non‑rigid registration methods, despite the paper criticizing their computational inefficiency.  
  – **Computational overhead**: For surgical navigation, runtime/efficiency is critical; the paper only briefly mentions runtime but lacks a thorough discussion and comparison.  
  *R1 and R4 request these experiments; R3 asks for efficiency evaluation.*

- **Technical assumptions and design details**  
  – The framework assumes source and target point clouds have the same number of points. This restriction is not justified and is unrealistic for real‑world scans unless resampled. No discussion of how to handle variable point counts.  
  – Permutation invariance in the MLP‑based feature extraction is not adequately explained; the handling of unordered point clouds needs clarification.  
  – The use of non‑shared parameters for source and target feature extraction is not justified; it would be helpful to compare with a shared‑weight variant.  
  *R2 raises all these points.*

- **Code and data availability**  
  It is not stated whether code, trained models, and the MedMatch3D dataset will be released publicly. This affects reproducibility and the usefulness of the benchmark.  
  *R4 asks about release plans.*

- **Minor issues**: Typo on line 052 (NPCR → N-PCR).
