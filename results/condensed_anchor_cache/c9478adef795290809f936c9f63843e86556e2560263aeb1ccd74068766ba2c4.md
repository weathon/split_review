- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper argues that “simplicity bias” is a major factor explaining generalization in overparameterized models (neural networks, decision trees, nearest neighbor), and that this bias is optimizer-independent. It claims that at initialization, models are “uniformly-at-random constructed” but not “uniformly-at-random sampled,” creating a non-uniform probability space that is inherently biased toward simple functions. Three supporting cases are presented: (1) random Boolean trees outperform chance at generalization on simple Boolean functions; (2) infinitely-wide neural networks are equivalent to conditioning a Gaussian process prior on interpolating the data; (3) deep networks asymptotically become biased toward a constant function (zero) as depth grows. A “Naive Algorithm” is proposed that relies purely on initialization and architecture, avoiding explicit gradient-based optimization.

### Strengths

- The study addresses an important and understudied topic (simplicity bias in overparameterized models) that could contribute to understanding deep network generalization (Reviewers 1,2,4).
- The paper brings together ideas from distant communities (e.g., Boolean trees) not commonly used in this context (Reviewer 1).
- The writing is clear and easy to follow for some reviewers (Reviewers 3,4); Reviewer 3 found the insight that initialization and architecture can affect generalization interesting.
- The claims are original (even if not necessarily true) and the problem is significantly important (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Clarity and focus of the main message**  
   - The paper is very difficult to read; the main punchline is unclear (Reviewer 1).  
   - The abstract avoids stating the authors’ own idea of simplicity bias, explaining mainly what it is not (Reviewer 3).  
   - The connections between claims, theorems, and propositions are loose; writing quality should be improved (Reviewer 2).  
   - *Contrast:* Reviewer 3 and 4 found the paper well written, creating disagreement on presentation quality.

2. **Overstated claims about optimizer independence**  
   - The claim “simplicity bias and excellent generalization are optimizer-independent” is too strong and unsupported by evidence (Reviewer 3,4).  
   - Consider a deep linear network vs. linear regression in the overparameterized regime: (S)GD finds different solutions because the architecture differs, showing optimizer dependence (Reviewer 3).  
   - Extensive prior theoretical work (e.g., self-stabilization of gradient descent, [Damian et al. 2022]) shows that the optimizer plays a pivotal role in biasing solutions, contradicting the claim (Reviewer 4).  
   - Even if deep linear networks are ill-initialized, gradient descent still finds generalizable solutions ([Wang et al. 2021], [gradient descent for matrix factorization]) (Reviewer 4).

3. **The “Naive Algorithm” is conceptually not novel and blurs the role of optimization**  
   - The Naive Algorithms (for Boolean trees and neural networks) are essentially “sample from the prior conditional on matching the data” – a complicated restatement of a basic idea (Reviewer 1).  
   - The Naive Algorithm for neural networks is confusing; no evidence or references are given that such algorithms are meaningful in practice (Reviewer 2).  
   - **Most importantly:** The Naive Algorithm itself functions like an optimizer – it iteratively generates random weights and evaluates the loss until convergence. There is no clear distinction from a typical optimizer mechanism (Reviewer 3).

4. **Insufficient evidence for the practical significance of the results**  
   - No experiments compare how simplicity bias from initialization alone vs. gradient-based training affects generalization (Reviewer 4).  
   - How much can generalization degrade if gradient-based methods are not used? (Reviewer 4).  
   - The empirical success of neural networks relies heavily on training; why should conditioning the initial distribution alone explain generalization? (Reviewer 1).  
   - The observations (e.g., bias toward a Gaussian process, or toward zero as depth grows) are interesting, but it is not explained how they *aid* generalization or prevent overfitting (Reviewer 4).  
   - How is this work different from that of Chiang et al. (2022, ICLR), which makes similar claims and shows strong simplicity bias for DNNs on Boolean functions? (Reviewer 4).

5. **Specific technical criticisms**  
   - Theorem 1 and Proposition 2 rely heavily on prior work and may just restate known facts (Reviewer 1).  
   - Proposition 2 (gradient descent conditioning on a Gaussian process for infinitely-wide networks) is not proved, and no proof is provided (Reviewer 1).  
   - The results on deep networks are unclear; the relation to the “no-Shannon effect” is not explained – what is that effect? (Reviewer 1).  
   - System models are not clearly described: e.g., deep neural networks in Figure 1B – the loss function is not introduced (Reviewer 2).  
   - Claim 1: “the vast majority of functions” is vague (Reviewer 2).  
   - The last equation in the proof of Theorem 1 appears to have an extra “=” (Reviewer 2).  
   - What is the point of revisiting known results for infinitely-wide neural networks? (Reviewer 2).  
   - If the source of simplicity bias is initialization and architecture, the paper does not identify that source clearly – it only says what it is not (Reviewer 3).

6. **Incomplete literature review**  
   - The paper does not cite relevant work that supports the same hypothesis (e.g., Chiang et al. 2022) and does not compare to it (Reviewer 4).  
   - Contrary to the authors’ claim, there is a large body of theoretical work on the implicit bias of gradient descent that undercuts the claim of optimizer independence (Reviewer 4).
