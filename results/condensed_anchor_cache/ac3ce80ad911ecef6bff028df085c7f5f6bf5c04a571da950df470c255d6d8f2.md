## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents FUSE-ILP, a differentiable inductive logic programming method that extends TensorLog-inspired backwards-chaining techniques to learn rules beyond chains. By introducing branch masking, leaf grouping, and a “folding” step that merges tree-like structures into subgraphs, the approach enables differentiable evaluation of a restricted class of non-chain rules while keeping computational costs similar to chain-based methods. Experiments on three datasets (one synthetic “community” dataset plus two standard benchmarks) are reported, with comparisons against the NLIL baseline. Reviewers acknowledge the potential of learning more expressive rules but raise major concerns about experimental support, clarity, novelty, and contribution.

### Strengths
- The extension to learn more expressive (tree-like and subgraph-like) rules from neural ILP systems is a significant step beyond chain-based methods (R1, R3).  
- The idea of using constraints for merging (folding) to form complex structures from trees and encoding them with neural networks is interesting (R3).  
- The approach is described in sufficient detail and generality for an outsider to understand the key ideas (R1).  
- Scalability is an important topic for differentiable ILP (R4).  
- The method appears implemented and shows improvements over previous work in some settings (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
**Clarity and Presentation**  
- The paper is very hard to follow (R4). It lacks essential background: no explanation of the differentiable ILP task, its input/output, a definition of first-order logic used, or what “tree-like structures” formally are (R4). The background section starts with multi-hop reasoning on graphs without clarifying how these graphs relate to ILP (R4).  
- The key difference from existing differentiable ILP methods is not clearly explained (R4). Novel contributions are hard to isolate because the paper leverages TensorLog, message-passing, and other existing approaches (R2, R3, R4).  
- Terminology is unclear: what are the “messages” passed among graphs – real-valued vectors? (R4).  
- The graph folding algorithm is not well motivated (R2).  
- Reference formatting is inconsistent (e.g., “author (year)” where “(author year)” is appropriate, misplaced first bibliography entry, arXiv links without published versions, missing ref for Metagol, no URLs for all papers) (R1). Several typos (e.g., “boolean” → “Boolean”, line break in Section 4.4) (R1).  

**Novelty and Significance**  
- The idea of factoring variable graphs is not entirely new; the step forward does not seem substantial (R1).  
- The authors must clarify their contribution relative to TensorLog (R2).  
- The practical relevance is unclear: most standard problems can be solved with chains, and the authors had to design an artificial dataset (community) to demonstrate the method’s advantage (R1). There are no real-world benchmarks that require tree-like or subgraph rules (R1, R3).  

**Experimental Weaknesses**  
- Experimental support is weak overall (R2, R4). Only one baseline (NLIL) is compared, with no justification for its selection (R4). Additional comparisons with TensorLog, other neurosymbolic systems, or classical ILP methods (Foil, Aleph, POPPER) are missing (R1, R2, R4).  
- The experiments only report time and F1 scores, which are insufficient to validate the claims; more extensive evaluation is needed (R4).  
- The benefit of complex rule learning is demonstrated on only a single synthetic dataset (community); on the other two benchmarks the proposed method is not actually required (R3).  
- There is no theoretical or experimental support for the “efficient” claim in the title (R2).  
- The effect of more complex structures on learning time is not explored (R3).  
- No details of experimental settings are provided (R4).  

**Reviewer Questions That Overlap with Criticisms**  
- How do the domains differ from classical ILP family-relations problems? Are these large graphs? How large? How would Foil/Aleph perform? (R1)  
- Are there benchmark problems where trees or more general graphs are necessary, or are chains sufficient for most problems? (R1)  
- What is the difference between T and SAT? (R2)  
- Can there be a comprehensive evaluation showing that i) complex rules are required for real-world cases and ii) existing methods fail while the proposed method succeeds? (R3)  

**Minority Contrast**  
One reviewer (R1, score 6) considered the contribution a “reasonable step forward” with sufficient detail, whereas the other three (R2, R3, R4) found the experimental support weak, the novelty unclear, and one (R4) judged the paper as almost impossible to understand.
