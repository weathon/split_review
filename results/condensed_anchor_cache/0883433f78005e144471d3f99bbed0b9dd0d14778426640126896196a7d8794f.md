## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a divide-conquer-reasoning approach for evaluating and improving LLM generation consistency. It breaks down candidate and reference texts into sentences, evaluates each sentence via an LLM, converts results to a numeric score, and uses reasons to improve generation. Reviewers acknowledge the paper is well-written and addresses a real task, and that the sentence-level evaluation achieves higher correlation with human judgments. However, concerns dominate: limited novelty (similar to SMART), reliance on powerful LLMs (GPT-3.5/4) without controlled comparison, ambiguous use of references, lack of comparisons with key baselines (SMART, NLI, BLEURT), narrow focus on consistency alone, and unaddressed vulnerability to hallucination. Reviewer 1 is more positive (score 6) while Reviewers 2 and 3 are more critical (scores 3 and 5). Reviewer 1 expresses doubts about novelty and missing baselines; Reviewer 2 questions generalizability, overconfidence, and omitted human evaluation; Reviewer 3 argues improvement stems from the underlying LLM and that the method's own hallucination risks are ignored.

### Strengths
- The divide-and-conquer evaluation prompt efficiently improves consistency assessment by using LLMs at sentence granularity. [R1]
- The paper is well-structured and easy to understand. [R1, R3]
- Sentence-level granularity is effective for evaluation, achieving higher consistency with human annotations in paraphrasing and summarization tasks. [R2]
- The method demonstrates strong empirical results (e.g., +19.3% and +24.3% on SummEval). [R3]
- The research task (consistency evaluation and improvement) is real and has practical applications. [R3]

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The sentence-level evaluation idea closely resembles SMART (Sentences as Basic Units for Text Evaluation), which already uses sentences as the basic unit and achieves strong agreement with human judgments. This reduces the unique contribution. [R1]
- **Limited generalizability**: The method requires hand-crafted prompts for each specific task, making it difficult to scale to broader instruction-following tasks. Other LLM-based evaluators handle this more efficiently. [R2]
- **Missing baselines**: The paper does not compare against related consistency methods such as SMART, NLI, and BLEURT, raising questions about thoroughness. [R1]
- **Ambiguous reference definition**: It is unclear whether “reference” refers to a gold-standard human reference or the source document/article. The prompts shown (Appendix A.2) mix these meanings. [R1] This also affects the fairness of comparing with reference-free evaluators like G-Eval. [R2] The authors should clarify whether their method is reference-based and whether they use a source document. [R1]
- **Handling varying sentence counts**: The paper does not explain how sentence-level comparisons are performed when the reference and candidate contain different numbers of sentences. No sentence-matching technique is described. [R1]
- **Evaluation only on consistency**: The work focuses solely on consistency, ignoring other important criteria such as factuality and coherence. The rationale for this narrow scope is not justified. [R2]
- **Lack of human evaluation beyond instance level**: No human evaluation is performed to verify the accuracy of sentence-level judgments, leaving their overall reliability unclear. [R2]
- **LLM overconfidence problem**: The per-sentence assessment, used to identify and fix inconsistencies, is prone to LLM overconfidence—the model tends to prefer its own predictions even when wrong. [R2]
- **Claim vs. practice on LLM reliance**: The paper states (Introduction) that DCE “does not rely on LLM,” but ChatGPT/GPT-4 is required for most components. This contradiction is misleading. [R2, R3] The authors also claim robustness against hallucination, yet the entire algorithm uses LLMs and each step (e.g., generating justifications) is itself susceptible to hallucination. [R3]
- **Improvement due to underlying LLM, not method**: The substantial improvements in Tables 2-4 when upgrading from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4 suggest that the underlying LLM drives the results. To substantiate the method’s contribution, experiments should be repeated with identical underlying LMs (e.g., using BLOOM instead of BERT for BERTScore and GPT-4 for this method). [R3]
- **Missing comparison with Self-Refine**: The reason-assisted improver (RAI) should be compared with the related self-refine approach to isolate its effect. [R2]
- **Potential for hallucination in justifications**: The first step of the algorithm uses an LLM to generate justification statements for each sentence; these justifications can be hallucinated, undermining the entire pipeline. The authors do not address this risk. [R3]
