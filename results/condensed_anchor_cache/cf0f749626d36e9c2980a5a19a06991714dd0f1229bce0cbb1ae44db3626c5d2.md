## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Diverse Multi-Agent Debate (DMAD), an extension of Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) that assigns distinct reasoning strategies (chain-of-thought, step-back prompting, program-of-thoughts) to different agents to break the “mental set” of homogeneous thinking. Agents solve problems independently, then iteratively refine solutions by extracting insights from each other’s approaches. DMAD is evaluated on multiple LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA-3-70B, Gemini-1.5-Flash, GPT-4o-2024-05-13) and multimodal LLMs (LLaVA-1.6-13B) across four benchmarks, consistently outperforming MAD and self-reflection baselines. The method is simple, requires no retraining, and achieves gains (e.g., 7‑8% improvements on some metrics vs. ~5% for MAD) while often reaching better performance in fewer rounds (e.g., DMAD in 2 rounds surpasses MAD in 5 rounds).

### Strengths

1. **Novel and creative idea** – Leveraging diversity in reasoning methods to overcome fixed mental sets is a fresh and well-motivated approach, drawing from cognitive science. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3 also notes it is a meaningful addition to the MAD line of work.)

2. **Simple and immediately applicable** – DMAD does not require model retraining or additional data collection, making it straightforward to deploy. (Reviewer 2, also implied by others.)

3. **Extensive experimental validation** – The method is tested across a diverse set of models (both language and multimodal: GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA-3-70B, LLaVA-1.6-13B, Gemini-1.5-Flash, GPT-4o-2024-05-13), multiple benchmarks (e.g., MATH, GSM8K, MMLU? — no, but four benchmarks including multimodal), and strong baselines (self-reflection, standard MAD, CoT‑SC). (Reviewers 1, 2, 3.)

4. **Ablation studies** – Robustness of the results is explored through ablations on number of agents, number of debate rounds, and importance of the thought process. (Reviewer 1.)

5. **Clear presentation** – The paper is well written, builds logically on MAD, and shows that diverse reasoning enhances performance on most tasks. (Reviewer 3.)

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty** – The method is an incremental improvement over MAD, primarily modifying agents’ reasoning patterns. Similar ideas of leveraging diverse perspectives for reasoning have been explored in prior work (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024). (Reviewers 2 and 3; Reviewer 3 calls it a “nice addition” but not fundamentally new; Reviewer 2 rates contribution as 1, while Reviewer 1 rates it 3, creating a strong contrast.)

- **Weak theoretical foundation and questionable key driver** – The connection to psychology’s “mental set” appears tenuous. In Table 1, traditional MAD with a single reasoning strategy (e.g., all CoT) sometimes achieves comparable performance to DMAD (e.g., LLaMA model: DMAD 72 vs. MAD All CoT 73). This raises doubts about whether method diversity is the real source of improvement or merely the debate process itself (which also encourages divergent thinking). Statistical significance is not provided. (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 also notes marginal or negative gains in some cases, reinforcing this concern.)

- **Inconsistent or marginal improvements** – While DMAD overall improves results, some gains are small or even negative, and the paper does not thoroughly investigate typical mistakes made during DMAD debates. For example, on MATH with GPT-4o-mini, performance degrades after 3 rounds compared to 2 rounds; with LLaMA-3-70B, DMAD performance drops after the third round while MAD improves linearly. The authors’ explanations are insufficient. (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 1 similarly asks for more analysis of failure cases.)

- **Lack of analysis on reasoning diversity** – Only three reasoning strategies (CoT, SBP, PoT) are used; alternative strategies (e.g., least-to-most prompting, Zhou et al., 2023) are not explored. There is no principled analysis of which properties make these strategies complementary, or what kind of diversity is most effective. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 2 also asks why these three were chosen.)

- **Scalability and computational cost** – No systematic analysis of how performance and computational cost (token usage, API costs, overhead) scale with the number of agents and debate rounds. Real-world applications may require much larger setups. The paper should have compared DMAD’s computational overhead to standard MAD. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 2.)

- **Limited implementation guidelines** – Critical practical questions remain unanswered: What criteria determine the ideal number of debate rounds? Could agents dynamically switch reasoning strategies during the debate based on progress or disagreements? How should reasoning strategies be selected for a new task? (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 also asks about setting up an LLM to propose strategies automatically.)

- **Multimodal experiments need strengthening** – It is unclear whether visual information plays a significant role in the multimodal benchmarks; more vision-centric tasks/datasets are needed. Additionally, the paper does not discuss why self-reflection methods struggle more on multimodal tasks than on language-only tasks. (Reviewer 1.)

- **Missing evaluation on broader/challenging tasks** – No results on MMLU (even a subset) or on tasks requiring multi-hop reasoning over longer contexts. The focus is limited to mathematical reasoning and visual understanding, leaving other domains unexplored. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 2 also questions the task selection.)

- **Model capability scaling not explored** – No comparison showing whether smaller models using DMAD can outperform larger models using simpler reasoning methods; such an analysis would better demonstrate the method’s practical value. (Reviewer 2.)

- **Specific experimental and presentation issues**:
  - Table 1: For the LLaMA model, DMAD’s score of 72 is not the highest (MAD All CoT and CoT‑SC are higher); is this a typo? Please correct text bolding accordingly. (Reviewer 3.)
  - Equation 3 and 4 confusion: h_i appears to be defined identically to H; clarification is needed. (Reviewer 3.)
  - Line 157: likely “h_i” is intended instead of “A_{i,1}”. (Reviewer 3.)
  - Line 314: should read “DMAD” instead of “MAD”. (Reviewer 3.)
  - Appendix figures (e.g., 9, 10, 11) are difficult to interpret due to formatting characters; consider providing pre-formatted versions. (Reviewer 3.)
  - For multimodal experiments, clarify whether the number of trials/random seeds were adjusted relative to language-only experiments. (Reviewer 1.)
  - Figure 1: if space permits, visualizing additional ablations (number of rounds, removing step output, etc.) in a similar format would be insightful. (Reviewer 1.)
