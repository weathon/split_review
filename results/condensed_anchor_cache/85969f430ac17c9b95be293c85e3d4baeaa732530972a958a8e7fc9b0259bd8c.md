## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes wCKA, a weight-based similarity metric for neural networks that combines chain normalization (Wang et al., 2022) with centered kernel alignment (CKA, Kornblith et al., 2019). Unlike existing representation-based metrics, wCKA compares weights directly, making it sample-agnostic, symmetric, and computationally efficient. The method is designed to be invariant to neuron permutations and intertwiner groups. The authors introduce a benchmarking framework that uses the fraction of agreed predictions (an improvement over accuracy gap) across clean, OOD, and adversarial samples to relate similarity to functional similarity. Experiments on small MLPs trained on MNIST show wCKA is well-calibrated (e.g., near-zero similarity for random nets) and correlates better with functional similarity than Procrustes, CKA, and dCKA.

### Strengths
- **Core result is interesting and surprising**: If it holds up, the finding that weight similarity can outperform representation similarity in functional comparison is novel and valuable (R1).
- **Problem importance**: Addressing the lack of well-calibrated similarity metrics is a fundamental problem in deep learning, and a better metric would benefit the field (R2, R3, R4).
- **Novel approach**: Shifting from activations to weights avoids dependence on input data and is a different, natural direction (R2, R3, R4). wCKA is sample-independent and computationally efficient (R4).
- **Theoretical grounding**: The method is motivated by invariance to intertwiner groups and uses chain normalization (R1). It is theoretically sound (R2) and well-motivated (R4).
- **Improved benchmarking framework**: Using fraction of agreed predictions is a notable improvement over accuracy gap (R1, R2). Statistical tests support conclusions (R2). Baselines (Procrustes, CKA, dCKA) are relevant (R2).
- **Good clarity**: The paper is generally well-written and clearly introduces the problem (R2, R3). Background section is concise (R3). The experiments are a decent starting point (R1).
- **Well-calibrated**: wCKA yields near-zero similarity for randomly initialized models and aligns well with functional similarity (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Severely limited architectural scope**: wCKA as presented only applies to MLPs/fully-connected networks. It is not extended to convolutional layers, residual connections, attention, or transformers, which are the mainstream in deep learning (R2, R3, R4). Section 3.3 is misleading: it compares only fine-tuned MLP heads on a conv backbone, not conv layers themselves (R2). Even if generalization is possible in principle, the paper does not demonstrate or discuss practical extensions (R4). This lack of applicability to realistic architectures significantly limits impact (R2, R3, R4).
- **Insufficient experimental scale**: All models tested are small (<2500 neurons) and trained on toy datasets (MNIST only) (R1, R3, R4). Large-scale validation on realistic architectures (e.g., ResNet50, Llama3-8B) and datasets (e.g., ImageNet, CIFAR-100, GLUE) is missing (R1, R3, R4). It is unknown whether wCKA scales to billions of parameters or suffers from concentration-of-measure phenomena (R3, R4). Reviewer 3 explicitly asks for proof-of-concept on large models and high-dimensional datasets. This is a major limitation for the intended use case (R3).
- **Lack of granular characterization**: The paper provides no heatmaps of layer-wise wCKA comparisons, no analysis of similarity across different layers of the same model, no comparison of the same architecture trained on different datasets, and no investigation of which weight features drive the similarity (R3). Without these, it is difficult to understand the quirks and sensitivity of wCKA (R3). The question "What insights can wCKA provide that other methods cannot?" remains unanswered (R2, R3).
- **Inadequate discussion of the weights-vs-representations shift**: The paper does not properly discuss the implications of switching from activations to weights. For example, wCKA gives ~0 similarity for random nets, while representation methods yield non-zero values; the latter may be a natural consequence of random projections (Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma), not a "bug" (R2). Also, wCKA is data-independent, so it is unaffected by the test set, OOD corruptions, or adversarial samples used in the benchmark, but this asymmetry is not discussed (R2). The concept of "calibration" is used throughout but never defined (R2).
- **Limited novelty**: wCKA is a direct combination of chain normalization (Wang et al., 2022) and CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019). A more detailed comparison with Wang et al. is needed (R2). Reviewers 1 and 2 note that the method is not sufficiently contextualized with related literature (e.g., "learning on models") (R1, R2).
- **Missing analysis on scalability and computational complexity**: wCKA's computational cost depends on the number of parameters, but no theoretical or empirical analysis of its scalability to models with billions of parameters is provided (R4).
- **Ambiguities and omissions**:
  - Can wCKA be applied layer-wise or only to the whole network? Which layers were used for CKA/dCKA in the baselines? (R4).
  - The limitation that weights cannot be compared across different architectures or across layers with different sizes (unlike activations where the same number of instances allows comparison) is not discussed (R3).
  - The method is not applied to transformers or any modern architecture; applicability beyond FC layers is unclear (R4).
  - Section 3.3 gives the false impression that wCKA works on conv nets without clarifying it is only the MLP heads that are compared (R2).
- **Minor presentation issues**: The methods section lacks intuition; Eq2 is not explicitly stated as the HSIC estimator; notation xW vs. Wx+b; undefined 'S' in Eq2.4; missing publication years in some references (R2). The introduction is somewhat lengthy and overclaims that a single similarity measure can bridge the knowledge gap (R2).
