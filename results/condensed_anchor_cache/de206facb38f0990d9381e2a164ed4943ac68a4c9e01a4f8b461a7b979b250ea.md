## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes stochastic comodulation for task adaptation in multi-task learning, inspired by biological neural modulation. It uses a pretrained backbone (ResNet-18), a controller that maps task identity to context weights, and iid Gaussian noise to modulate decoder activations. Experiments on CelebA (40 binary tasks) and CIFAR-100 (20 superclasses → 100 classes) show improvements in F-score, faster convergence, and better confidence calibration compared to deterministic attention and other baselines. The paper also analyzes internal representations and architecture effects (residual connections). Reviewers disagree: R1 (score 8, confidence 2) finds the results convincing and well-contextualized; R2 (score 5, confidence 4) and R4 (score 5, confidence 3) see moderate value but raise clarity and generality concerns; R3 (score 3, confidence 3) questions experimental design and metric interpretation.

### Strengths
- **Biological motivation and cross-disciplinary relevance**: The work connects stochastic co-modulation in the brain to multi-task learning, making it interesting for both neuroscience and ML audiences (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Simple, effective method with minimal extra parameters**: The approach fine-tunes only controller parameters while keeping the backbone frozen, enabling adaptation with low overhead (R1, R2, R4).
- **Rigorous experimental setup**: Multiple random initializations are used to report variance, and the training/fine-tuning division on CelebA vs. CIFAR-100 requires two different types of distribution shift (R1, R2).
- **Comprehensive analysis**: The paper investigates learned representations (Figure 3–6), confidence calibration, and architectural effects (e.g., residual connections improve comodulation) (R1, R2, R4).
- **Notable performance gains**: Stochastic comodulation achieves state-of-the-art results on CelebA, faster convergence than deterministic attention, and improved recall (R1, R3). However, R3 notes a sharp drop in precision.
- **Interesting neuroscience-connected experiment**: Training on CIFAR-100 superclasses then fine-tuning on 100 classes is a meaningful link to perceptual learning experiments (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Marginal improvements over attention and diminishing returns**: Improvements over deterministic attention are small and tend to diminish as pretraining epochs increase. This reduces excitement about the method’s practical advantage (R2, R3). R1 finds the improvements nontrivial, but R3 considers the metric selection (e.g., drop in precision) troubling and suspects it was designed to favor the proposed method.
- **Clarity of writing and method description**: Several reviewers found the paper hard to follow:
  - The mechanism of stochastic modulation (how the noise signal is generated and integrated into the decoder, and its relation to the controller) is unclear from Figure 1 and text (R1, R3).
  - Terms like “deterministic gain increases” and “calibration of confidence” are introduced in the abstract but not defined until later (R1).
  - The Methods section contains undefined variables (e.g., \(h^{J}_t\) first appears without definition; the meaning of \(h^{J-1}_k\) in Equation 3 is ambiguous) and incorrect citation formats (R2).
  - “Reliability diagrams” on page 7 are not explained (R3).
  - The description of the deterministic “attention” baseline lacks a mathematical formulation; it is unclear whether it is the controller without stochasticity, and whether its hyperparameters were tuned as thoroughly as the stochastic version (R2, R3, R4).
  - The sentence “we only use stochasticity to compute the gain, but we use unperturbed representations for decision making” is contradictory, as gain perturbs representations; is stochasticity turned off at test time? (R3)
  - Figure 1 is hard to connect to Equation 1, and Figure 5 labels are too small for printed paper (R1, R4). Colors in Figures 1,2,3,5 are not color-blind friendly (R4).
- **Limited evaluation scope and missing benchmarks**: The method is tested only on CelebA and CIFAR-100, which are relatively small. No experiments on ImageNet, COCO, or other large-scale datasets are provided, making it unclear whether the method generalizes (R4, with R1 also noting larger experiments would strengthen the ML message). Post-rebuttal, R4’s concerns remain unaddressed.
- **Unclear novelty relative to prior work**: The paper states it extends Haimerl et al. (2019; 2022), but the exact differences in methodology and task setting are not clearly delineated. The novelty of adding a controller and multi-task learning context is not properly distinguished from earlier stochastic comodulation models (R4, echoed by R1 about anchoring).
- **Ad-hoc experimental design**: The multi-stage training pipeline (pretrained ImageNet backbone → full-task training → fine-tuning only the controller) makes training efficiency comparisons difficult. R3 questions why the controller cannot be trained simultaneously, and whether it is standard to train on all tasks then fine-tune on the same dataset. R3 also notes the explanation of overfitting on page 5 is ad-hoc.
- **Overstated biological claims**: R1 notes that the language (“computationally viable candidate for contextual fine-tuning in an animal brain”) is not sufficiently grounded in actual neural data, and R4 finds a gap between the neuroscience motivation and the implemented method (e.g., how “task-relevance tags” are generated and used).
- **Missing details for reproducibility and comparison**: Table 1 lacks annotation and does not clarify whether state-of-the-art results are recomputed in the same environment or taken from cited papers (R1, R3). The typo “Pytorch” should be “PyTorch” (R4). It is also unclear why Haimerl et al. use “co-modulation” but this paper uses “comodulation” (R4).
- **Minor errors**: On page 5, the reference to Figure 2D should be to Figure 2C (R1). Missing comma in “recall, precision and F1-score” (R3).
