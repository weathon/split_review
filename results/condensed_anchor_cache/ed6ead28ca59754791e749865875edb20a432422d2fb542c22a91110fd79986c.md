## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers found the paper clearly written and the experiments extensive. However, they agreed that the core novelty is limited because FedGP primarily extends the centralized A-GEM / Gradient Projection Memory method (ICLR 2021) to the federated setting by aggregating local buffer gradients on the server. The algorithm is considered heuristic, lacking theoretical motivation for why gradient projection and the buffer update rule mitigate forgetting in the federated context. Additional concerns include elevated communication cost, privacy leakage risk from uploading gradients, a flawed buffer replacement strategy (random discarding), and outdated baselines that omit the closest related method. Specific experimental oddities (Table 1: CL methods outperforming CFL methods; Table 3: two-times communication worse than equalized communication on P-MNIST) also remained unexplained.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to read and follow.
- Extensive experimental evaluation: tested on multiple benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR-100, P-MNIST), combined with many baseline methods (including FL+iCaRL, L2P), and under varying numbers of clients and partial client aggregation.
- The paper reports storage, communication, and computation overhead, demonstrating an awareness of practical efficiency concerns.
- The proposed FedGP shows consistent performance improvements when added to existing CFL methods (e.g., up to 27% accuracy increase on CIFAR-100 task-incremental scenario).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concern (all reviewers):** The key steps (buffer storage and gradient projection) are directly taken from A-GEM and Gradient Projection Memory (GPM; ICLR 2021). The only difference is replacing the reference gradient with a federated aggregation of local buffer gradients.
- **Algorithm motivation (Reviewer 3):** The design is heuristic and not well motivated. No rigorous analysis explains why gradient projection and the buffer update rule solve catastrophic forgetting or fit the federated setting. Theoretical analysis or more ablation studies are needed.
- **Impact on current task learning (Reviewer 2):** When the current gradient conflicts with the projected direction, the method drops the conflicting component, which may impair learning of the new task. The paper only focuses on forgetting reduction and ignores this side effect on current task performance.
- **Buffer replacement strategy (Reviewer 3):** Algorithm 4 randomly replaces an old buffer entry with a new data point when the buffer is full. This may discard useful information (e.g., data stored in previous rounds) without any principled selection.
- **Communication and privacy costs (Reviewer 2):** FedGP incurs additional communication overhead (uploading buffer gradients) and increases the risk of local data leakage through those gradients.
- **Explanations missing for experimental results:**
  - (Reviewer 1) Table 1 shows that some CL methods (e.g., FL+iCaRL, L2P) perform better than the dedicated CFL methods even without FedGP. The reasons are not clarified.
  - (Reviewer 2) In Table 3, the “two times communication” configuration yields inferior performance compared to “equalized communication” with FedGP on P‑MNIST. An explanation is expected.
- **Outdated baselines (Reviewer 2):** Several comparison methods appear outdated. To demonstrate effectiveness, the authors should include state‑of‑the‑art CFL methods—in particular, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM; ICLR 2021) which is closely related and should have been directly compared as a baseline.
