## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes qPO, a batch acquisition function for Bayesian optimization on finite discrete domains. qPO maximizes the probability that the selected batch contains the true global optimum. The batch acquisition function can be expressed as a sum over individual scores, enabling efficient parallelization and avoiding combinatorial optimization. The method is implemented via Monte Carlo approximation. The authors evaluate on molecular design tasks (antibiotic discovery, organic electronics) and report improvements over parallel Thompson sampling, qEI, and UCB.

### Strengths
- The core idea of a batch acquisition function that decomposes into a sum of individual scores is novel and computationally tractable.
- Achieving batch diversity through a purely exploitative formulation is creative.
- The mathematical derivation is clear and sound.
- The method is intuitive and simple.
- Visualization of batch diversity in Figure 3 (network structure + similarity distributions) and the 3-point example in Section 3.2 effectively illustrate key properties.
- The improvement over parallel Thompson sampling, qEI, and UCB is consistent across several datasets.
- Writing quality is overall good, and the paper introduces a simpler alternative to complex batch construction strategies.
- The decomposition property makes the method computationally tractable for moderate-sized search spaces.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing theoretical justification and convergence analysis.** The paper provides no convergence guarantees, while baselines like pTS have known convergence rates. There is no analysis of why maximizing probability of optimality yields better sample efficiency, nor a comparison to recent work establishing tighter regret bounds via batch diversity (e.g., Nava et al. 2022, Ren & Li 2024). One reviewer (R3) notes this is a heuristic without proven improvement, contrasting with stronger theoretical foundations in other methods.
- **Unfair or insufficient baseline comparisons.** Several important baselines are omitted: GIBBON (Moss et al. 2021), q-PI (most similar joint-probability method), and DB-GP-UCB (a theoretically grounded batch UCB). The qEI implementation should use sample average approximation with quasi-Monte Carlo as in BoTorch (Balandat et al. 2020). For pTS, comparisons should include recent theoretical (Nava et al., Ren & Li) and empirical works (Wilson et al. 2020, Adachi et al. 2024). Without these, it is difficult to assess practical utility; R3 and R4 emphasize this limitation, while R1 and R2 acknowledge the method performs well but note missing comparisons (R1 specifically mentions GIBBON; R2 suggests comparisons with non-finite-discrete BO methods).
- **Clarity issues in mathematical exposition.** Multiple reviewers found the notation in Eqs. (6)–(11) confusing. Specifically: what bold **x** and **y** represent (observed points or all candidates?), how Eq. (6) equals Eq. (7) (assumptions about f and y, noise-free vs. low-noise contradiction), and the interpretation of Eq. (11) (what are y_i^(m) and **y**^(m)? Is it an inefficient Monte Carlo that only one point is true per sample? Why does it recover probability of true optimum?). The myopic construction discussion is unclear; one reviewer notes that if Eq. (6)–(10) represent the PI utility function, the claimed myopicness originates from the belief model, not the utility. These confusions undermine the paper’s clarity.
- **Reliance on approximations without validation.** The method uses pre-filtering (greedy selection of candidates by posterior mean) and Monte Carlo sampling, but there is no analysis of statistical consistency, bias, or computational complexity. The pre-filtering step may miss promising candidates, especially in regions with high uncertainty—robustness analysis is needed. The MC estimation is potentially inefficient; better numerical methods (e.g., Genz 1992 for orthant normal probabilities) could improve accuracy and efficiency. R3 notes that greedy sampling introduces bias (correctable by Matheron’s rule) and that hyperparameter misspecification (e.g., lengthscale) biases the MC estimate (addressed in Wilson et al. 2020, Adachi et al. 2024). The Tanimoto kernel lacks consistency guarantees for MLE-based hyperparameter estimation (Karvonen et al. 2023).
- **Insufficient empirical evaluation.** The experiments lack standard synthetic BO benchmarks and ablation studies (impact of batch size, pre-filtering threshold, number of MC samples). The reported metrics (top-k retrieval, average reward of top-k) are not the conventional cumulative regret or simple regret, and their validity is not justified. Runtime comparisons with other batch acquisition methods are absent. The experiments only cover two molecular datasets (antibiotic discovery, QM9 for organic electronics) and do not explore scenarios where the method may fail.
- **Diversity property not unique.** The claim that qPO implicitly captures diversity is not adequately distinguished from similar joint-probability-based methods like q-PI, which have analogous properties. The paper does not discuss this overlap. One reviewer (R3) questions why exploitation leads to diversification when recent work (Wilson et al. 2020, Adachi et al. 2024) argues that parallel TS lacks diversity and requires *explorative* adjustments—an apparent contradiction not addressed.
- **Title issues.** The title “Batched Bayesian optimization with correlated candidate uncertainties” is not fully explained in the paper, and one reviewer (R2) finds it unreflective of the content.
- **Minor inaccuracies.** The phrase “Deterministically maximize” is misleading because the method relies on multiple approximations and Monte Carlo randomness, so it is not deterministic. Also, the foundational reference for parallel Thompson sampling should include Kandasamy et al. (2018) for theoretical understanding.
