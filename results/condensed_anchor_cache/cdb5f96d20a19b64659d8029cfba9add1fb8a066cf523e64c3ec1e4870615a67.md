## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes MCCI, an end-to-end defense against backdoor attacks that leverages causal inference. The method transforms inputs with DCT, trains an attack indicator network (AIN) on the frequency spectrum, and concatenates AIN features with main features (SFRN) for classification. During inference, the AIN input is replaced with a clean image’s frequency spectrum, effectively setting the attack indicator to zero and removing the confounder path. The paper claims this corresponds to a causal backdoor adjustment that cuts the edge A→I, breaking the confounding path I←A→Y. Experiments show state-of-the-art performance in recovering original labels for poisoned samples and improving clean accuracy, with applicability to large pre-trained models.

### Strengths
- The threat model is more challenging and practical: requires end-to-end training (instead of multi-stage) and forces the model to predict the original ground-truth label even for poisoned images.
- The idea of using causal inference to analyze backdoor attacks is novel and revealing: regarding backdoor attacks as a confounder, defense as do-calculus on I (equivalent to conditioning on both image and attack indicator), and fixing A=0 as counterfactual outcome estimation.
- The method is simple and interesting. At inference, the standard classification architecture can be used by absorbing the AIN output into the bias: \(x \mapsto \operatorname{softmax}(W_1 f_\theta(x) + b')\).
- Experimental results are surprisingly successful given that only standard NLL loss is used, and the evaluation correctly proposes measuring recovery accuracy of the original label of poisoned inputs.
- The approach is end-to-end, making it suitable for generalization to large pre-trained models.
- Comprehensive ablation studies demonstrate the effectiveness of each component in MCCI.
- Extensive experiments include consideration of large pre-trained models.
- The paper is well-structured, well-written, and easy to follow with proper diagrams.

### Weaknesses
- **Low clean accuracy and weak baselines.** Clean accuracy on CIFAR-10 is at most ~87%, about 7 percentage points lower than in ASD and DBD papers (which report >93%). The training setup differs from standard CIFAR-10 hyperparameters, and reasons are not clarified. This discrepancy may affect interpretation of results.
- **Incomplete experimental evaluation.** Only two datasets (CIFAR-10, GTSRB) are compared, while all four baselines (ABL, DBD, CBD, ASD) evaluate on three. No experiments on different model structures (VGG, MobileNet). Missing comparisons with D-ST (Chen et al., NeurIPS 2022), which shares the same threat model and outperforms ABL and DBD. Also missing comparison with training-time defense in Liu et al. (arXiv 2307.15539). Many anti-backdoor defenses mentioned in Section 2 (e.g., D-ST) are not included in evaluation without explicit selection criteria.
- **Stronger adaptive attacks needed.** The paper assumes triggers have distinguishable frequency spectra (more pronounced high frequencies), but attacks could use natural-looking, blended, randomly positioned, or smooth triggers (e.g., tennis ball, Khaddaj et al. 2023; Refool, Liu et al. 2020; smooth trigger, Zeng et al. ICCV 2021). Low-frequency (LF/LC) attacks are not considered, despite DCT being chosen because of low-frequency attacks. Experiments should include such frequency-based attacks and discuss potential modifications to MCCI for robustness.
- **Causal theory connection is unclear and not guaranteed.** There is no theoretical guarantee that the feature extractor (SFRN) will separate clean semantic features from trigger features; nothing prevents SFRN from learning what AIN learns. A loss that minimizes mutual information between branches could make the method more robust. The correspondence to the causal model (backdoor adjustment) is not formally proven; the proof in Proposition 3.4 uses expectation instead of a conditional probability distribution, which seems less natural for a class random variable.
- **Similarity to CBD defense.** The prior work CBD (Zhang et al., CVPR 2023) also uses causality to disentangle confounding and causal effects, employing a backdoored model as reference during minimization of mutual information. A detailed technical comparison, especially on training loss formulation, is needed to clarify novelty.
- **Threat model conflict using clean validation images.** The defender is assumed to have no prior knowledge of the backdoor, but AIN requires clean images from a validation dataset to generate an averaged frequency spectrum. Moreover, clean images from validation (not training) are used, which is an inappropriate setting; most defenses assume a set of clean images from the training dataset.
- **Missing details on experimental setup.** Many details are absent: exact poisoning rate used, specific configurations and hyper-settings for each attack method, hyper-parameters for MCCI and baselines, and full specification of the ResNet architecture (ResNet18 vs. ResNet50). Also, Algorithm 1 does not formulate the training losses for AIN and SFRN; mathematical formulations are needed for reproducibility.
- **Missing discussion of frequency-domain transformations.** The rationale for using DCT over DFT is not provided, and no experiments compare DFT and DCT.
- **No source code provided** for reproducing experiments.
- **Clarity issues and minor errors.** “Attack indicator” is initially hard to understand. Logical clarity issues (e.g., “otherwise” in L175, meaning of “ideal” in L258). Proposition 3.4 uses expectation rather than conditional probability. Definition 4.1 is not a formal definition. Acronyms DACC and ADCP in Figs. 4 and 5 are unexplained. Citation errors throughout (e.g., \citep/\citet misuse). Minor typos: “(4)SOTA”, “methods works”, “mages”, “Equations equation” redundancy.
- **One reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 8) found the causal view highly novel**, but other reviewers questioned both the novelty and the strength of the theoretical connection compared to CBD, and highlighted the above weaknesses concerning assumptions and experimental evaluation.
