## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper integrates deep reinforcement learning (DRL) with classical planning by automatically inducing task structures and substructures from a few expert demonstrations. Using genetic programming, it learns symbolic action schemata (preconditions and effects of “critical actions”) from state-transition traces. During RL training, a backward-chaining planner uses the learned rules to infer the next critical subgoal, and intrinsic rewards are given when that subgoal is achieved. The approach targets deterministic, fully observable but unknown environments. Experiments in gridworld-style domains (DoorKey, 4-Rooms, and Minecraft) show faster learning and better generalization than several baselines (PPO, behavioural cloning, other intrinsic-reward methods). Reviewers’ ratings ranged from 5 to 8 (mean ~6.25), with one reviewer (R2, score 8) being distinctly more positive and finding the results convincing and the paper clear.

### Strengths
- The idea of using genetic programming to infer symbolic action schemata and automatically extract task-relevant substructures from demonstrations is novel and interesting. (All reviewers)
- The work is well-motivated: solving sparse-reward, goal‑directed tasks has high practical value, and the approach leverages insights from classical planning and hierarchical learning. (R2, R4)
- The paper is generally clear and well-written, using figures and text to explain the core concepts. (R1, R2, R3)
- Empirical results in grid‑world environments show that the proposed method outperforms several DRL, imitation‑learning, and exploration baselines in sample efficiency and task performance. (R1, R2, R3)
- The paper discusses potential future extensions (e.g., handling stochastic environments) and offers a clear breakdown of the framework’s components. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Missing related work / insufficient novelty justification:** Several reviewers noted the lack of comparison with prior works on learning action models from traces (e.g., [1–4] mentioned by R1 and R3) and on integrating learning and planning (e.g., Icarte et al., 2019). The novelty of the induction stage over existing action‑model learning methods is not clearly articulated (R2).  
- **Strong assumptions not sufficiently discussed:**  
  - Action preconditions are defined over all state variables, an action impacts only specific features, and each critical action maps to exactly one MDP action. These assumptions are not justified (R1).  
  - The state is assumed to be representable as a vector of integers; extending the approach to high‑dimensional (pixel) inputs or continuous controls is unclear (R4).  
  - “Critical actions” require a human expert to deem which state variables are important (e.g., inventory items in Minecraft); this limitation is not acknowledged (R4).  
- **Scale and generalizability concerns:**  
  - Environments tested are small‑scale (e.g., 8×8 grid, DoorKey, 4‑Rooms). The scalability of genetic programming to larger problems is not addressed (R1, R3, R4).  
  - Most recent baseline is from 2020; newer methods are missing (R3).  
- **Missing analysis and details:**  
  - No detailed report of the learned symbolic representations (e.g., specific preconditions and effects per domain) or number of action operators and plan lengths (R1, R2).  
  - Comparison with option‑discovery approaches on sample efficiency is not provided (R2).  
  - The RL setup is under‑specified: state representation (single channel vs. separate channels), CNN kernel sizes, whether the encoders are trained or fixed, and the role of the decaying step‑based reward (Appendix B.5) (R4).  
  - The generalization experiment setup is confusing: is the agent retrained after re‑inducing rules? Are original demonstrations reused? (R4)  
- **No source code provided.** (R4)  
- **Verification of critical action recognition missing.** The paper does not validate how critical states/actions are identified, and there is no guarantee that incorrect preconditions/effects (Figure 6 shows imperfect accuracy) might cause planning failures (R1, R3).  
- **Lack of comparison with other action‑model learning methods** (e.g., decision‑tree approaches for precondition learning) (R1).
