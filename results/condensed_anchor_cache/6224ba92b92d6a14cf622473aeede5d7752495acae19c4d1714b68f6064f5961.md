## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies Koopman autoencoders for long-term state prediction in nonlinear dynamical systems. The authors identify two issues with purely latent-space propagation: inability to capture multi-stable switching dynamics and violation of uniqueness of solutions. They propose periodic reencoding—periodically re-encoding the decoded state into the latent space—and demonstrate its empirical benefits on low- and high-dimensional systems. The method is simple, does not require long sequence training, and yields consistent improvements. However, multiple reviewers note theoretical gaps, lack of baseline comparisons, limited exploration of the mechanism, and unclear hyperparameter selection.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, with clear exposition, helpful figures (e.g., Fig. 1), and a technically precise appendix (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- Periodic reencoding is a simple and effective concept that leads to consistent performance improvements across low- and high-dimensional systems (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5). An ablation study convincingly shows its benefit (R5).
- The method does not require long-sequence training (R4).
- Experiments include both toy problems and complex D4RL benchmarks (R5).
- The approach is potentially generalizable to other autoencoder-based latent dynamics models (R5).
- The visualization in Fig. 1 clearly illustrates the method (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical explanation for periodic reencoding:** The underlying mechanism is not well explained. It remains unclear whether reencoding clamps latent representations to the data manifold, adds noise, or serves another purpose. Reviewers question whether deficiencies in the encoder/decoder could instead be addressed, and whether the method truly solves the switching dynamics problem when the model is misspecified (R2, R3, R5). The Appendix C example is seen as a special case of poor mapping, with benefit from arbitrary training precision (R5).
- **No principled methodology for choosing the reencoding period:** Several reviewers note that the optimal period (k or Δt) appears system-dependent and requires tuning. Fig. 5 shows middle values perform best, but no selection criteria are provided (R1, R3, R4).  
- **Missing baseline comparisons:** The paper lacks comparisons with related methods for chaotic dynamics (e.g., teacher-forcing RNNs from Hess et al. 2023, Mikhaeil et al. 2022) (R1), with Mondal et al. 2023 (R2), and with broader latent dynamics models such as Deep Kalman filters and linear latent state-space models (R5). R5 also notes that nonlinear transition baselines predictably outperform linear ones, and that the paper’s focus on Koopman seems unnecessary given reencoding is more general.
- **Limited evaluation on diverse dynamics:** The method works well for oscillator dynamics, but generalizability to continuous attractors (R4), multi-stable systems with periodic attractors (R1), and chaotic systems (R1) is not established.
- **Unclear or questionable technical details:**  
  - The Jacobian in eq. (9) is assumed constant without justification (R1).  
  - The bilinear form in eq. (6) is not clearly explained (R5).  
  - The origin of eq. (2) should be precisely cited (R3).  
  - The reference to deep state-space models like S4 is misleading as they handle stochastic systems (R2).  
- **Figure quality:** Text is illegible in Figs. 3, 4, 5, and especially Fig. 6 (R3).
- **Minor writing issues:** The first paragraph of Section 2 does not help unfamiliar readers (R5); bibliography entries are missing journal/conference info (R5); Appendix E’s citation selection feels arbitrary (R5). The statement about n >> d being more significant is questionable given injectivity is more critical than surjectivity (R1).
- **RL control experiments lack baselines:** Without comparisons to other methods, it is difficult to contextualize the contribution (R2).
- **Unaddressed questions about broader relevance:** Multiple reviewers ask what advantages the method offers over existing RNN-based or latent-dynamics approaches, and whether the Koopman framework is central to the contribution (R1, R2, R5).
