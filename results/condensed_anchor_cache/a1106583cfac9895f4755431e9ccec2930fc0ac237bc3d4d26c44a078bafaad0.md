## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a retrosynthetic planning algorithm called retro-fallback that accounts for uncertainty in reaction feasibility and molecule buyability. It introduces a metric, Successful Synthesis Probability (SSP), to quantify the probability that at least one synthesis plan can be executed in the lab. The algorithm greedily expands molecules to maximize SSP. Evaluation on in-silico benchmarks shows improvements over MCTS and retro*. The motivation and formalism are clear, but quantitative results and baseline comparisons may need clarification to fully support claims of enhanced practicability.

### Strengths
- The objective of addressing uncertainty from infeasible reactions and non-buyable molecules is well-motivated and important in practice. The paper lays out a clear and general formalism (feasibility, buyability, SSP).
- The algorithm is described in detail and the path from motivation to solution is well-paved, from trivial solutions to the proposed one, including systematic discussion of connections to related work.
- The evaluation directly answers the claims made by the paper.
- The paper draws insight into imperfect reactions from one-step prediction models and redefines buyability of real-world molecules.
- The paper proposes a novel approach based on stochastic processes for retrosynthesis and a novel measure (SSP).

### Weaknesses
- The key difference between retro-fallback and existing algorithms (DFPN-E, Retro*) is unclear. If costs are negative log probabilities, the updates for ψ and ρ become essentially equivalent to the “reaction number” and “retro* value” updates from (Chen et al., 2020). The claim that “parallel updates” are the key difference needs elaboration: how do these parallel changes search behavior compared to single cost function optimization in Retro*?
- The advantage of optimizing SSP (a probability) over a scalar cost is not sufficiently elaborated, especially in multi-step retrosynthesis where feasibility and buyability interact complexly.
- The problem described in Section 4.1 (cycles/repeated molecules) could be easily avoided by using a hash table, a standard technique in proof-number search, which would detect cycles and avoid the same molecule/reaction appearing multiple times.
- The claim that existing algorithms are not designed to find multiple backup plans (e.g., they may share steps) overlooks CompRet (Shibukawa et al., 2020), which uses AND-OR-tree search to enumerate all synthetic routes one by one in preference order. This prior work should be discussed.
- An older paper (Heifets & Jurisica, 2012) that proposes AND-OR tree search for retrosynthesis should be cited.
- The assumption that every reaction has only one product may be too strong; its reasonableness should be justified.
- Terminology: "tip nodes" is non-standard; the term "frontier nodes" is more common in graph search.
- How does Algorithm 1 generate multiple possible reaction graphs for a target molecule? This needs clarification.
- The evaluation could be expanded. When the algorithm terminates with tip nodes still in G’, the plan may not be valid. The authors should also evaluate using the success rate metric from Retro* and the set-wise exact match accuracy from FusionRetro.
- The sensitivity of the method to the number of samples in Algorithm 1 (line 4-6) should be reported.
- While retro-fallback with transformer shows computational efficiency, the paper should elaborate on how the combination achieves this improvement.
- The SSP metric is limited: it attempts to calibrate reaction feasibility but will not perform better than a forward one-step prediction model since both are trained on similar reaction datasets. Additionally, SAScore is a trivial metric as it prefers unrealistic large carbon rings.
- More quantitative experimental results and baseline comparisons are needed.
