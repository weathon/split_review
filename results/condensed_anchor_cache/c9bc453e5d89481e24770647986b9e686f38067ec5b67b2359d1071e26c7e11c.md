## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates data scaling laws for ambient diffusion, where models are trained solely on corrupted data. It empirically shows that training on fully noisy data significantly underperforms clean-data training at realistic sample sizes (30k–1.3M), but using a small set of clean data (e.g., 10%) together with a large set of highly noisy data suffices to match the performance of models trained only on clean data of similar size. Theoretical sample complexity bounds for Gaussian mixtures with heterogeneous variances are developed to explain this phenomenon. The paper also proposes a budget allocation mechanism for dataset curation.

### Strengths
- The studied problem is important and practically relevant: clean data is often scarce or expensive, while noisy data is abundant.
- The paper is well-written, clearly structured, and easy to follow.
- Experiments are exhaustive and convincing, involving more than 80 models across three datasets (30k–1.3M samples) and various noise levels. Figure 1 clearly shows the regime where noisy data is useful.
- The theoretical analysis provides rigorous sample complexity bounds that are minimax optimal and offer practical intuition. The derived bounds help quantify the “price of noisy images.”
- The paper’s conclusions have significant practical value for dataset curation and budget allocation in real-world applications where collecting clean data is costly.
- Some reviewers (scores 8) considered the theoretical foundation inspiring and the results strong; the problem direction is important and merits publication.

### Weaknesses
- The theoretical results (Theorems 4.2–4.4) are derived for Gaussian mixtures and Wasserstein distance loss, which is not directly aligned with the denoising score matching objective of diffusion models. This mismatch raises concerns about the direct applicability of the theoretical findings to diffusion models. The paper does not provide a clear connection or show that the Wasserstein bounds translate to diffusion performance.
- The analysis is limited to Gaussian noise corruption and discrete distributions with finite support. It does not address continuous distributions, non-Gaussian heavy-tailed noise, or multimodal noise, which are common in real-world data corruption.
- The proposed method assumes the noise level is known in advance and that clean/noisy labels are available for each sample. In real-world scenarios, noise characteristics may be unknown or vary across the dataset; the algorithm cannot handle mixed data where clean/noisy labels are absent. This requires additional preprocessing steps that introduce uncertainty.
- The relationship between Theorem 4.2 and Algorithm 1 is unclear. Does Theorem 4.2 refer to the procedure used in Algorithm 1? What is the exact estimation procedure referenced in the theorem? The constants \(c_1, c_2\) and the “low-dimensional estimation procedures” are not explained.
- It is not clearly justified why an exponentially larger amount of corrupted data is needed to compensate for a lack of clean data. The information-theoretic reason for this exponential relationship is not provided.
- The solution (mixing a small clean set with large noisy data) is considered “a bit simple” and “engineering consideration” by some reviewers; the algorithm and proof techniques largely build on existing works, which may limit the novelty.
- No comparison with prior ambient diffusion methods (Daras et al., 2023b; 2024) is provided in the experiments.
- The paper lacks a concrete real-world scenario and application where the proposed approach would be particularly beneficial. The examples given (e.g., blurry images dismissed from filtering) are abstract and need a real filter example with a discarded image.
- Several details in the theoretical discussion are insufficiently explained: the discussion after Theorem 4.2 (error in estimating low-dimensional subspace), the exact procedure referenced, and how the heteroscedastic mixture terminology relates to the model. It is not clear what “realistic sample size” means in context.
- The experiments use EDM code with same training budget (same number of images seen) across settings, but it is not confirmed whether other data augmentation techniques are used alongside noisy data, and how the proposed method compares to standard data augmentation.
