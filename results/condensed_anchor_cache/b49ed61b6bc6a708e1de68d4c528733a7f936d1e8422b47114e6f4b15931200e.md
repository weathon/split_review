## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a new variant of strategic classification that models inter-agent externalities—the manipulation of one agent affects the utilities of others. The interaction is modeled as a Stackelberg game between the principal (classifier) and agents, with a simultaneous game among agents. Under assumptions (ℓ2 norm costs, pairwise symmetric externalities, convex total externality), the agent manipulation game has a unique pure Nash equilibrium (PNE) that can be efficiently computed. The authors establish PAC learning guarantees for the principal (based on a covering argument) and discuss optimization via gradient-based methods. Two concrete externality models are given: proportional externality and congestion externality.

### Strengths
- Problem formulation is novel: modeling externalities in strategic classification is missing in prior literature and is well-motivated by real-world applications such as university admissions. [R1,R2,R3,R4]
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow. [R1,R3,R4]
- The Stackelberg–Nash framework captures both principal-agent and multi-agent dynamics in a unified, analytically tractable model. [R2,R3]
- Identification of clean technical conditions (ℓ2 norm costs, pairwise symmetric externalities, convex total externality) leads to a potential game formulation, enabling uniqueness and efficient computation of PNE. [R2]
- Lipschitzness of the NE (Lemma 1) facilitates the PAC learning analysis. [R2]
- PAC learning guarantees are established, and the paper also discusses ERM via gradient methods, providing a comprehensive theoretical treatment. [R1,R2,R3,R4]
- Good placement within prior literature (e.g., discussion of Eilat et al. 2023). [R4]

### Weaknesses
- The sample complexity bound relies on a standard covering argument; tighter bounds using techniques like Rademacher complexity could be explored. [R1]
- The assumption that the number of agents comes from a distribution is noted but does not affect the PAC learning result. It would be useful to discuss scenarios where this randomness leads to different outcomes compared to a fixed number of agents. [R1]
- A possible typographical error: Line 196 should use \(f_{\omega}(x')\) instead of \(x\) inside parentheses; consistency should be checked throughout the paper. [R1]
- The treatment of optimization is weak. The argument that gradient-based methods often work for non-convex problems is insufficient for this nested optimization (agents reaching equilibrium inside the classifier’s loop). The authors derive gradients but do not demonstrate their usefulness; experiments on synthetic or semi-synthetic data are needed to evaluate convergence behavior with varying numbers of agents or externality strength. [R2]
- The paper misses related work on information externality and causal effects in strategic classification: *On the Long-term Impact of Algorithmic Decision Policies: Effort Unfairness and Feature Segregation through Social Learning* (ICML 2019) and *Strategic Instrumental Variable Regression: Recovering Causal Relationships From Strategic Responses* (ICML 2022). [R2]
- The assumption of pairwise symmetric externalities is very limiting and not well motivated. More justification is needed; ideally the assumption should be removed or relaxed. [R3]
- More discussion on the social optimality of the studied equilibria (for the players as a whole) would be beneficial. [R3]
- The externality of agent \(j\) suffered by agent \(i\) is modeled as a sum over \(j\), but global externalities may not factorize additively. A clearer explanation of this modeling choice is needed. [R3]
- Coalitional behavior among agents is not considered; how would the equilibrium be affected if agents could form coalitions? [R3]
- The loan application example reminds of the setup in *Strategic Apple Testing* (Harris et al., 2023); a discussion could be interesting. [R3]
- The full-information assumption is very strong: agents are assumed to know the classifier parameters, cost functions, externality functions, and critically the full feature matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) of all other agents. This is unrealistic in many practical scenarios, especially with many agents. Recent works relax full information; the paper should discuss limitations and possible extensions to partial or noisy information. [R4]
- In the proportional externality example (Section 5), it seems that a manipulating agent \(j\) incurs no externality from agent \(i\) if \(i\) does not manipulate. This counterintuitive behavior needs explanation; the rationale behind the symmetric externality model should be elaborated. [R4]
- The paper could benefit from at least one set of case studies to illustrate the model and results. [R4]
