## Merged Review

### Summary

InterMask generates 3D human-human interactions from text via collaborative masked modeling in discrete space. It uses a VQ-VAE to encode motion as a 2D discrete token map and an Inter-M transformer with intra- and inter-person spatio-temporal attention. Training randomly masks tokens of both individuals; inference starts fully masked and progressively fills tokens. Achieves state-of-the-art FID on InterHuman (5.154 vs 5.535 for in2IN) and InterX (0.399 vs 5.207 for InterGen), with average inference time 0.77s (vs 1.63s for InterGen). Also supports reaction generation without redesign.

### Strengths

1. **2D discrete motion token map** preserves fine-grained spatio-temporal details and spatial awareness better than traditional 1D VQ token maps, enabling more accurate modeling of complex interaction relationships.
2. **State-of-the-art results** on InterHuman and InterX datasets across multiple metrics, indicating high realism and text alignment.
3. **Low inference time** (0.77s average) gives a practical advantage over InterGen (1.63s).
4. **Transformer + VQ-VAE architecture** with dedicated attention layers (spatial-temporal, cross-attention) captures nuanced intra- and inter-person dependencies.

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty is not clearly distinguished from prior work.** The transformer module for intra- and inter-person spatio-temporal modeling is a standard approach; ablation studies only compare with/without the module, without justifying why its specific internal structure (number of layers, attention heads, arrangement of self, spatial-temporal, and cross-attention) is tailored to 3D interaction. Reviewer 3 finds the paper underwhelming and lacking distinctive insights, rating it borderline despite acceptable experiments and writing.
2. **Insufficient explanation of the 2D token map construction.** Missing concrete details on convolutional layers for downsampling, codebook size, quantization method, and how spatial and temporal dimensions are handled during downsampling and quantization. A more detailed diagram and description are needed to assess the impact of design choices.
3. **Visualization artifacts and limited fluidity.** The generated interactions exhibit body penetration, unnatural sliding movements, and sudden arm-raising (e.g., second video of "Everyday Actions"). Motions lack the fluidity seen in InterGen's outputs; the paper does not discuss potential reasons (e.g., frame-by-frame processing, temporal dependency limitations) or compare motion smoothness with other methods.
4. **Masking strategy lacks thorough analysis.** The paper does not deeply investigate how mask ratio settings, dynamic adjustment during the unrolling process, or the fixed probability trade-off between random and interaction masking affect performance. No empirical justification or rationale for parameter choices is provided, raising concerns about adaptability to diverse interaction scenarios.
5. **Missing comparisons with relevant baselines.** The paper omits comparisons with PriorMDM (which also generates two-person interactions) and T2M-GPT (a VQ-VAE-based single-person method). It would be informative to see how T2M-GPT performs on interactive datasets when given a reference action sequence for one individual as an additional condition.
6. **User study is too small.** Only three participants were included, insufficient for robust conclusions on user preference. The methodology (number of samples evaluated per user, comparison method, evaluation criteria) is not specified, making results unreliable.
7. **Limited exploration of broader applicability.** No experiments evaluate handling longer or more complex/in-the-wild text instructions. The framework is demonstrated only for two-person interactions; challenges or modifications needed for multi-person scenarios are not discussed.
8. **Reaction generation is not online.** Generating human reactions in an online manner (more practical for VR applications) is not addressed and poses challenges for the current approach.
