## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes P³EFT, a privacy-preserving fine-tuning approach for large models (DeBERTa-v2-XXLarge, Flan-T5 Large, LLaMA-2 7B) using LoRA adapters over a forward/backward API. It targets label privacy in a two-party split learning setting where the client holds labels and the server hosts the model. P³EFT uses private backpropagation (inspired by secure aggregation) and obfuscation of learned activations to prevent the server from inferring labels. Experiments are conducted on classification datasets. Reviewers acknowledge the practical relevance but raise concerns about theoretical justification, experimental completeness, overhead analysis, and broader applicability.

### Strengths
- **Practical and relevant problem**: The paper addresses an important privacy issue in fine-tuning APIs, which are increasingly popular (R1, R2, R4). R2 (confidence 2) highlights the strong empirical results showing that P³EFT maintains both accuracy and empirical label privacy. R4 (confidence 4) notes the practical scenario.
- **Strong empirical privacy-utility trade-off**: P³EFT achieves better label privacy than existing methods (e.g., distance correlation defense) with only a minor accuracy drop (R2, R3). Results are shown across multiple models and tasks.
- **Private backpropagation concept**: The approach leverages conditional linearity of the backpropagation operator, which is conceptually similar to secure aggregation in horizontal FL but well-suited to this vertical FL setting. The method can extend to other vertical FL or split learning scenarios requiring gradient propagation through an untrusted party (R3).
- **Generality to PEFT methods**: Although based on LoRA analysis, the approach can be applied to any PEFT method aiming to address label privacy in split learning over an API (R3).
- **Effective visualization**: The visualization of top-2 principal components of gradients and activations before and after the defense directly demonstrates the method’s effectiveness (R4).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is clearly written and well-organized (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Core theoretical claim disputed (conditional linearity)**: Reviewer 1 (confidence 4) strongly criticizes the claim that backpropagation is conditionally linear. The decomposition of gradients through non-linear activation functions does not preserve linearity, and the paper does not clarify how the chain rule and non-linear activations are handled during the private backpropagation process. This undermines the foundation of the approach.
- **Missing computation and communication overhead analysis**: R1 asks for quantification of the multiple forward/backward passes required for gradient decomposition, as well as the size of transmitted gradients. R2 also requests information about the overhead for clients (size of adapters, cost of multiple adapters). Such analysis is essential for practical deployment.
- **Lack of real API applicability**: R1 points out that no known LLM company offers APIs that expose forward and backward passes in the manner required. This makes the practical scenario questionable.
- **Client-side privacy risk not addressed**: R1 notes that if the client can observe training and update parameters, it may infer more than labels (e.g., model representations). The paper focuses solely on label privacy but does not address this additional risk.
- **Metric “leak” unclear and label privacy appears weak**: R1 states that the “leak” metric (assumed to be client prediction accuracy) is not clearly defined, and with reported values >60%, label privacy does not seem effectively preserved. (R1’s comment contrasts with R2/R3’s more positive view.)
- **Insufficient ablation and experimental details**:
  - Only n=2 adapters are used; R2 asks for experiments varying the number of adapters to see effects on privacy and utility. R2 also requests adapter size and client overhead.
  - R2 notes high variance in privacy leakage (e.g., QNLI in Tables 1 and 3), making DC and P³EFT sometimes comparable. The number of experiment repetitions is not stated.
  - The meaning of ε=0 in the PSLF baseline (Table 6) is unclear (R2).
  - R2 suggests moving Algorithm 2 to the main text and including PSLF results from the Appendix into the main paper to show the utility loss of differential privacy approaches.
- **Multi-party claim not supported**: R2 notes the abstract mentions multi-party setups but experiments are only two-party; it is unclear how the method scales to multi-party.
- **No formal privacy guarantee**: R3 (confidence 3) points out the lack of a theoretical guarantee (e.g., differential privacy). The paper mentions DP but dismisses it without strong justification (line 132 lacks a reference or argument for why upper bounds are loose).
- **Attacker model limited**: The honest-but-curious model is a unique but narrow scenario. R3 asks for analysis of malicious servers and extension to worst-case privacy. Also, input privacy is not protected (exposed input samples), which R4 also criticizes as a limitation.
- **Unclear references and minor errors**:
  - Line 52 does not specify which fine-tuning algorithms are “narrow” (R3).
  - Line 266 has a formulation error: the expression should include a factor 1/2 (R3).
  - R3 questions noise levels in Algorithm 1 (ablation missing) and ambiguity in “same level of privacy” (line 479) and “privacy-accuracy trade-off” (line 503).
- **Writing and dataset issues**:
  - R4 (confidence 4) criticizes the abstract’s last sentence: “competitive while having higher accuracy” is confusing.
  - Only classification datasets are used, despite the paper mentioning generation tasks (R4).
  - R4 questions whether the method works when the API requires uploading the whole dataset (client cannot hide labels from the server).
- **Minor typos**: L485 “algorothm” (R2); line 326 “it’s”, line 478 “P³FT”, line 484 “both both” (R3).
