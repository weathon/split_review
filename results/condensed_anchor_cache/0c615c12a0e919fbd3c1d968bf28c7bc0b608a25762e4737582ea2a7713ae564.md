- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 1, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using the bias part of a ReLU-activated neural network (after adversarial training) as a classifier, aiming for provable adversarial robustness via a new concept called “information-theoretically safe.” Reviewers are divided: two rate the paper 1 (strong reject) citing extremely weak empirical results, flawed claims, and a lack of comparison to certified robustness; two rate it 3 (weak reject) acknowledging the idea as interesting but finding the theory limited, experiments insufficient, and presentation unclear. All four agree the paper is not ready for publication.

### Strengths
- The notion of using the bias term of a neural network to improve adversarial robustness is original and interesting (R3, R4).
- The paper introduces a new definition (“information-theoretically safe”) that may be valuable to the literature (R4).
- The theoretical argument that the bias classifier can defend against certain specific attacks (FGSM and CW) is a positive contribution (R3).
- Empirical results are presented on ResNet and VGG and appear to support the claims (R3). *However, R1 strongly disputes this, see Weaknesses.*
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow (R2). *Contrast with presentation concerns below.*
- The problem of adversarial robustness is significant (R2).

### Weaknesses
**Empirical performance and evaluation**
- The claimed robustness is contradicted by the reported numbers. On CIFAR-10 with AutoAttack (ρ=8/255, Table 2), the bias classifier achieves only 31.25% adversarial accuracy, whereas the best result in 2020 was 59.53% (AutoAttack paper, Table 2) (R1). This is “extremely weak” compared to existing defenses.
- Table 5 reports high adversarial accuracies, but the attacks are crafted with **random noise** (line 394-400), which is much weaker than even the simplest FGSM. Thus Table 5 is meaningless and does not demonstrate any advantage (R1).
- The method is only tested on small, simple datasets (MNIST and CIFAR-10) and simple networks (small variants of ResNet/VGG). No benchmarking against state-of-the-art defenses on larger datasets is provided (R3, R4).
- The method is not compared to existing certified robustness methods, e.g. randomized smoothing (Cohen et al. 2019) or diffusion-based certification, which achieve 65.5% – 70.7% certified accuracy on CIFAR-10 under ℓ₂ perturbations of radius 127/255 (R1). The paper wrongly claims that existing defenses cannot give provable robustness (line 050) (R1).

**Theoretical and conceptual issues**
- The criterion Eq.(11) only guarantees that small perturbations do not change predictions; it does **not** imply high robustness, because the clean accuracy could be very low to satisfy the condition. The experiments indeed show weak clean accuracy (R1).
- The “information-theoretically safe” definition has no clear advantage over the trivial upper bound given by clean accuracy. A DNN that is highly noise-sensitive and achieves zero accuracy on any noisy input would trivially satisfy the definition with no robustness at all (R4).
- The definition is vague: “Let ℱ_R be a neural network that involves a random variable R” – what does it mean for a network to “involve” a random variable? (R2, also R4).
- The theory is limited to only two specific attacks (FGSM and CW) and does not extend to general adversarial attacks (R3).
- The paper tests a self-fulfilling prediction: the classifier is trained to be constant on a neighborhood of benign test samples and then found to be both accurate and robust. A proper test should involve linearization on an adversarial sample for which the reference network is **not** accurate (R2).

**Presentation and clarity**
- Non-standard notation: e.g., ∇F(x)/∇x seems to be a division of two vectors; likely intended ∇F(x)/|∇F(x)|? (R2).
- Many terms are undefined or unclear: “random direction attack” (line 060-061), how the bias term itself can function as a classifier (line 063), what “I = [0,1]” refers to (R4).
- The related work section is disorganized, merely listing approaches without detailed explanation; citing review paper [1] for DNN structure is inappropriate (R4).
- The new loss function mentioned at line 119 is never presented (R4).
- Typographical error: “arbitrary” misspelled (line 177) (R4).
- Motivation for using adversarial training to train the bias classifier (Section 3.3) is not given (R4).
- It is unclear what “original model gradient-based attacks” refers to in Table 5, and which results are the baseline (R3).
- “Original based AutoAttack” (line 379-380) is unclear (R4).
- The claim that the bias classifier is “essentially different from gradient obfuscated defenses” (line 480-481) is based on only one method from 2018 and is not well justified (R4).
- A notation section is strongly recommended (R4).
- Equation (14) appears to be wrong (R4).
