## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes RT-Sketch, a sketch-conditioned imitation learning policy that takes a hand-drawn sketch of the desired goal state as input and outputs robot actions. It extends RT-1 by using an image-to-sketch GAN to convert terminal images into synthetic sketches for training. Experiments include a human survey and table-top manipulation across six skills, with ablations on distractor robustness and language ambiguity. Reviewers are divided: two (R1, R2) find the direction novel, well-motivated, and the experiments convincing; R4 sees value but raises generalization limitations; R3 is strongly critical, questioning both the motivation (sketches versus language) and the validity of the quantitative metrics. R3 also notes flaws in the presentation of Table 1 (bolded numbers, shading). Consensus emerges that the work opens a new direction, but opinions differ sharply on the strength of the evidence and the practical advantages of sketches.

### Strengths
- Novel and interesting direction: using sketches as goal specification for imitation learning (R1, R2, R3, R4). The approach is well-motivated, as sketches balance the ambiguity of language and the over-detailed nature of images (R1, R2).
- Use of the Contour Drawing Dataset to mitigate the gap between synthetic and real freehand sketches (R1).
- Validation through four clear hypotheses (H1–H4) covering expressiveness, abstraction levels, robustness to distractors, and superiority over ambiguous language (R2). Experiments are convincing for the specific robot and tasks tested (R2, R4).
- Paper is well-written, clear, and concise (R2, R3). Figures 2 and 3 are effective (R3). The limitations and failure modes are honestly discussed (R3).
- Human survey to study preference in goal specification, complementing quantitative robot results (R3).
- Creation of the RT-Sketch dataset and the Image-To-Sketch model has technical merit (R3).
- Model handles sketches of varying specificity, showing flexibility (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing related work on sketch‑based visual understanding.** The paper should include a subsection discussing major works (e.g., ControlNet, “Picture that Sketch”, “Sketch2Pose” [CVPR’23], “Sketch‑Based” applications). Suggested references were provided (R1).
- **Freehand sketches vs. edge maps are fundamentally different.** Models trained on edge maps often fail on freehand sketches (R1). This distinction needs discussion (citing e.g., Koley et al., CVPR’23; Li et al., PhotoSketch). (R1)
- **Motivation for sketches over language is not fully convincing.** One reviewer (R3) argues that the language granularity examples actually demonstrate language’s flexibility (e.g., “put utensils on table” vs. “put fork 2cm right”). Sketches can also be ambiguous (e.g., an empty table sketch could mean “clear garbage” or “ignore it”); the desired solution is more intelligent agents or clarification, not a different modality. (R3)
- **Metrics poorly defined and potentially flawed.** “Spatial Precision” (RMSE in pixels) and “Failure Occurrence” lack clear motivation and definition. The centroid distance metric uses manual keypoint annotations—why not an off‑the‑shelf classifier? Pixel distance corresponds to different physical displacements depending on camera distance, introducing bias. “Failure occurrence” is not defined at all. (R3)
- **Table 1 presentation is confusing and inconsistent.** Bolded numbers in the “Failure Occurrence” column are given to RT‑Goal‑Image despite having the highest failure rate, which violates conventional practice. The caption does not explain the cell shading, which uses different meanings in different parts of the table (lower centroid distance vs. frequency of failures). (R3)
- **Scalability concern for real‑world use.** Collecting human sketches for every scenario may be costly and limit the approach’s applicability beyond the benchmark (R2). The paper does not experiment with different image‑to‑sketch generation methods, which could be a bottleneck (R2).
- **End‑to‑end model ties task comprehension to a specific robot.** RT‑Sketch, like RT‑1, learns a direct mapping from visual input to actions, so any change in robot morphology, kinematics, or task requires full retraining. This lack of modularity and transferability severely limits practical applicability (R4).
- **Sketches are not treated as abstractions.** The generated sketches are essentially processed GAN outputs from robot camera images, not true abstractions with graphical conventions (R4). The model treats them as another visual input rather than leveraging their symbolic nature, so the work is an augmented version of RT‑1 to sketch images rather than a higher‑level communication medium (R4).
- **Missing details in the survey.** How many people took the survey? How was it conducted? (R3)
- **Figure captions could be improved.** Fig. 2 lacks a self‑contained caption; Fig. 3 would benefit from a more comprehensive caption to guide reader observations (R1).
- **Request for more visual examples** illustrating when a sketch outperforms a text‑only counterpart (R1). The experiment section could be made more self‑contained for broader audiences (R1).
