## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces and studies out-of-variable (OOV) generalization, where a predictive model must generalize to target domains containing variables never jointly observed in a single source domain. Under certain assumptions, the residual distribution from an optimal source classifier reveals information about the unobserved causal parent in the target environment, specifically via the third moment. The authors propose an OOV predictor that leverages this observation, and provide theoretical analysis and proof-of-concept experiments on synthetic data, comparing against marginal, imputed, and oracle predictors.

### Strengths
- Novel problem setting: OOV generalization is a new and important problem that can occur in real-world settings alongside OOD issues. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Provides extensive theoretical study: identification results, derivations, and conditions under which OOV generalization is possible, including when assumptions are relaxed. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Key insight is clever: moments of the residual from the source predictor contain information about the unobserved variable, leading to a straightforward algorithm. (Reviewer 2)
- Clear presentation: well-referenced, concrete examples, clean writing. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- Algorithm achieves non-trivial OOV transfer on synthetic data, showing promise for few-shot settings and sample efficiency relative to the oracle. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- The work openly discusses limitations and future directions, not an afterthought. (Reviewer 1)
- Methods employed are sound, and the paper can serve as a foundation for OOV research. (Reviewers 2, 4)

### Weaknesses
- Limited empirical validation: only tested on synthetic data; no real-world or benchmark datasets. This limits generalizability and raises questions about practical applicability. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)  Reviewer 2 calls this the main weakness; Reviewer 3 calls it the main limitation.
- The multi-environment extension mentioned in the appendix is not empirically tested; theoretical claims lack experimental support. (Reviewer 3)
- It is unclear how to determine whether the required assumptions hold for a given dataset, and how robust the predictor is when assumptions are violated (e.g., noise magnitude, heavy-tailed noise, discrete variables). (Reviewers 2, 4)  Specific questions from Reviewer 2: How does the method fare as noise magnitude increases? What if noise is heavy-tailed? Does performance degrade/improve with increasing number of variables? Theorem 3 assumes differentiability – does it apply to discrete variables?
- The practical relevance and distinctness of OOV generalization is debated: one reviewer (4) found the setting contrived and argued that OOV reduces to OOD generalization (e.g., covariate shift), while other reviewers considered it a novel problem. The paper could better justify why OOV is not merely a special case of OOD. (Reviewers 2, 4)  Reviewer 2 also questioned what realistic problem is well modeled by OOV.
- The explanation in Figure 1 and the associated text is unclear: the claim that the target domain being "all but impossible" when Y is not observed lacks detailed justification (e.g., challenges in estimating P(Z|X) when Y is unobserved). The diagram does not visually distinguish observed vs. unobserved variables (e.g., dashed lines or different colors for Y in the target domain). (Reviewer 1)
- The abstract’s claim that "merely considering differences in distributions is inadequate" requires more specificity, as OOV may be subsumed under OOD. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- Minor issues: potential typo "modal" → "model" (p2); "brackets" → "parentheses" (p6). (Reviewer 1)
- Questions about experimental design: Why is the joint predictor considered an oracle if MomentLearn outperforms it? Why is MomentLearn more sample efficient than the oracle for few-shot prediction? (Reviewer 1)
- Figure 3 is confusing: define OOV loss, clarify what the y-axis represents (which samples: (x2,x3,y) or (x1,x2,y)?), explain why relative loss zero indicates parity with oracle; consider showing fine-tuning error vs. oracle training instead. (Reviewer 2)
- The method should be compared to additional baselines: missing-not-at-random imputation, covariate-missing imputation, and state-of-the-art causal methods (e.g., Mejia et al. 2021, Janzing 2018) to validate Vapnik’s principle. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- Would calculating additional moments beyond the third improve results for general functions? (Reviewer 3)
- It is unclear how to combine the proposed approach with existing OOD methods in a straightforward way. (Reviewer 3)
- The paper could include more intuitive discussion and analysis of the assumptions underlying the theorems, and under what exact conditions the proposed predictor is appropriate. (Reviewer 4)
