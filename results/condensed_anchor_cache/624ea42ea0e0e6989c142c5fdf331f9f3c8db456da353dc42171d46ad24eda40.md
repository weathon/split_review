## Merged Review

### Summary
SciKnowEval is a benchmark for evaluating LLMs across five levels of scientific knowledge (knowledge memory, comprehension, reasoning, discernment, application). The dataset includes ~70K problems in biology, chemistry, physics, and materials science, sourced from literature, existing QAs, and scientific databases. Quality control uses LLM screening and 5% human evaluation. 26 open-source and proprietary LLMs were tested with zero-shot and few-shot prompting.

### Strengths
- Multi-level, multi-discipline benchmark with a large dataset (thousands to tens of thousands of questions per level), enabling granular evaluation of specific scientific knowledge aspects (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Paper is clearly written with helpful figures/tables; experiments are comprehensive, covering model comparisons and interesting findings (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3: “extensive examples and experiments”).
- Data and code are released; appendix provides additional results, model descriptions, data sources, and sample prompts (Reviewer 2).
- Quality control combines LLM screening (all questions) with human evaluation (sampled), balancing efficiency and credibility (Reviewer 4).
- The benchmark is valuable for evaluating LLMs in scientific topics (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Level categorization and justification**: The five-level framework (from an ancient Chinese book) lacks support from modern cognitive science; it is unclear how the levels truly reflect human understanding or reasoning (Reviewer 1). Differences between levels (e.g., L3 vs. L4 in Table 3, L4 vs. L5 in definitions in Sec. 3.1) are vague, and Level 4 (safety) seems misplaced relative to a learning progression. The categorization appears artificial without clear, replicable instructions or examples for determining task levels (Reviewers 1, 2). A better approach would involve LLM-based or human reasoning process instructions to assign levels (Reviewer 2).
- **Data generation and quality risks**: Questions are generated by LLMs from diverse sources, introducing hallucination risk (Reviewer 3). Evaluation of model answers also relies heavily on LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o for Level 5), which may introduce bias (Reviewer 3: “potential biases from using a single LLM for rating”). Only 5% of questions are reviewed by a human expert, which may be insufficient for ensuring safety and quality; domain experts should review a larger sample (Reviewer 3). [Reviewer 4 asks for clarification on Appendix A7: whether the 2.1% and 0.2% low-quality rates refer to the same standard, and why 2.1% is called “ultimate” when 0.2% is the post-screening number.]
- **Limited scope for scientific reasoning evaluation**: QA-like tasks, even with levels, may not fully capture scientific reasoning capabilities, which require more complex setups such as tool usage, task planning, or end-to-end experiment conduction (Reviewer 4). The paper should acknowledge this limitation explicitly (Reviewer 4). Some reviewers found the insights from the experiments limited and not surprising; e.g., it is unclear how the difficulties and challenges differ fundamentally across levels or how LLMs could be adapted to address them (Reviewer 1). (Reviewer 2, however, found the four findings interesting and useful for future directions—this contrast is noted.)
- **Generalization and methodological additions**: The benchmark primarily provides a ranking; it may be hard to generalize to new models (Reviewer 2). Only zero-shot and 3-shot prompting are tested; incorporating strategies like RAG could strengthen the evaluation (Reviewer 2). Minor issue: left quotation marks are used incorrectly in Table A10 (Reviewer 4).
