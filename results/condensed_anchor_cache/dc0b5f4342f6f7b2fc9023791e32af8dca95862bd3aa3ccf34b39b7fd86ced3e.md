## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies binary classification with data from a mixture of Gaussian clusters having orthonormal centers. It characterizes the maximum \(\ell_2\)-attack any classifier can tolerate, proves existence of optimal robust classifiers achieving that bound, and shows that gradient flow on a two-layer pReLU network (without adversarial training) provably converges to such a classifier under certain conditions. Reviewers generally find the analysis rigorous and well-written but converge on the criticism that the assumptions are too restrictive (orthonormal centers, small intra-class variance, non‑degenerate initialization), limiting practical applicability. There is a notable split: one reviewer (rating 8) is more positive about the contribution and clarity, while another (rating 5) considers parts of the theory obvious and the impact low. All reviewers call for empirical validation.

### Strengths
- **Original focus on GMM data with orthonormal clusters** (R1, R3) – a novel setting for theoretical robustness analysis.
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis** (R1, R2, R4) with solid technical derivations.
- **Clear writing** (R1, R2, R3, R4) – well‑structured, easy‑to‑follow, includes intuitive explanations and proof sketches (R4).
- **Significance for understanding adversarial examples** (R1) – contributes to the theory of when standard training can yield robustness.
- **Builds on and advances prior work** (Boursier et al. 2022; Min & Vidal 2024) – provides a full convergence analysis for gradient flow, resolving a conjecture (R2, R4).
- **Concrete limitation discussion** (R4) – helps readers assess the scope of the results.

### Weaknesses
- **Overly restrictive assumptions** – several reviewers note that the orthonormal center condition excludes simple problems like XOR (R1), limits the number of clusters to the dimension (R3), and forces data onto a simplex (R1). The additional assumption of small intra‑class variance (\(\alpha\)) further restricts the regime (R2, R4). There is no analysis of how results change for larger variances (R2).  
  – The **non‑degenerate initialization** (each Voronoi region must contain at least one weight) is considered strong (R2); random initialization likely yields weights nearly orthogonal to the cluster subspace, making this condition hard to satisfy without many neurons – scaling with \(D\) and \(K\) is not analyzed (R2).  
  – The choice of pReLU over ReLU is justified by non‑differentiability, but this is not crucial (R3); it remains unclear whether the convergence result holds for \(p=1\) (R3).  
  – Typo: “orthonormal” misspelled in line 061 (R4).
- **Lack of empirical validation** – all reviewers note the absence of experiments: no numerical simulations on synthetic data (R2, R3), no tests on real image datasets (R2), no comparison with adversarial training (R2). This makes it difficult to assess whether the theoretical guarantees translate into practical improvements (R1, R2).  
- **Weak connection to practical phenomena** – the paper does not explain why standard training exhibits an accuracy‑robustness trade‑off in practice (R1), nor why the network becomes robust here while practice shows the opposite (R3, Q6). The requirement that the pReLU degree be at least 3 is stated but not motivated (R1).  
- **Trivial or known claims** – one reviewer (R1) points out that the impossibility of defending against unlimited attacks and the existence of a robust classifier (since any optimal Bayes classifier is robust) are obvious and do not require formal proof.
- **Technical issues and missing clarifications**  
  – Theorem 1 and Theorem 2: the phrase “Given a sample \((x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{X,Y}\)” is inconsistent with the probability statement being over that very distribution (R3).  
  – Theorem 3 imposes an upper bound on the number of data points, which seems counterintuitive (more data should help) and is not explained (R3).  
  – In Theorem 3, \(\theta(t)\) is not explicitly defined, and the learning rate is not specified (R3).  
  – The proofs are lengthy; a more concise presentation would be helpful (R3).  
- **Progress over prior work** – Theorem 2 establishes robustness for the Bayes optimal classifier, yet (Min & Vidal 2024) already proved similar robustness for the classifier \(F^{(p)}\). It is unclear whether this is a new result and whether the “optimal” classifier should be more robust (R4).
