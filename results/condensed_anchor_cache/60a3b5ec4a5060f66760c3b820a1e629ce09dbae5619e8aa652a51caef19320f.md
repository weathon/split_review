## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SRSA (Skill Retrieval and Skill Adaptation) for efficiently learning robotic assembly tasks by retrieving the most relevant skill from a pre-existing skill library and fine-tuning it with RL (PPO + SIL). Retrieval uses a learned predictor of zero-shot transfer success based on geometry, transition dynamics, and expert action features extracted from disassembly trajectories. Experiments on AutoMate insertion tasks show SRSA significantly outperforms baselines (22% relative improvement in success rate, 3.7× higher stability, 2.4× greater sample efficiency). The approach also enables continual learning and achieves 90% mean real-world success rate. The paper is well written and the method is simple, elegant, and easy to understand.

### Strengths
- Strong simulation results with clear improvements over baselines; ablations provide valuable insights.
- Insightful idea to train a zero-shot transfer success predictor using geometry, transition dynamics, and expert action encodings.
- Policy learned in simulation transfers to a real robot with 90% mean success rate.
- Using geometry, dynamics, and expert-action features for retrieval is a nice idea that incorporates different aspects of the underlying tasks.
- Paper is well motivated, well written, and easy to follow.
- Experiments are conducted well and the continual learning setup demonstrates efficient expansion of the skill library.
- Two reviewers (R1, R3) found the approach sound, elegantly designed, and the retrieval idea insightful.

### Weaknesses
- **Assumption of shared observation/action/reward space:** The method assumes all tasks share the same observation space, action space, and dense reward function, which limits generality.
- **Limited generality to assembly tasks:** One reviewer (R2) noted the approach appears too tailored for assembly tasks with precise insertion, and would be better suited for a robotics venue. Relying on disassembly paths also restricts applicability to simple insertion tasks on a plane; the paper should more clearly state this limitation and propose solutions.
- **Sparse reward setting (R2):** The retrieval approach cannot work in sparse reward settings where no skill gives any zero-shot reward signal. This is relevant for real-world settings where dense rewards are hard to design.
- **Geometry-based retrieval may suffice:** Based on Fig. 6 and Appendix Fig. 16, SRSA-geom (geometry-only) performs comparably to full SRSA. This suggests geometry similarity alone might be sufficient, and is simpler because it avoids collecting disassembly trajectories and training a separate predictor. Two reviewers (R1, R2) raised this concern; one (R3) also asked why not compare features directly with a distance metric rather than approximating zero-shot success.
- **Zero-shot success rate as proxy (R3):** For dense-reward tasks, the zero-shot success rate may not accurately reflect the expected value of fine-tuning. Additional metrics could be beneficial. Also, the heuristic justification (simulation lemma) is not fully valid: a policy giving high reward on two MDPs does not imply they share similar transition dynamics and initial state distributions—the approach should be presented as a heuristic, not a theoretical guarantee (R2).
- **Learning embedding with limited data (R2):** With fewer than 100 samples and high-dimensional inputs (e.g., point cloud embeddings), were there challenges in learning the zero-shot transfer predictor?
- **Generalist policy failure (R2):** Why does a generalist policy trained on all tasks fail? Could better feature learning or multi-task training improve it? A generalist is more appealing and the failure warrants explanation.
- **Should F use the expert policy’s original-task success rate?** (R1) The predictor might benefit from including the success rate of the skill on its original task as input.
- **Clarity issues:**
  - Line 217 – “similar with a high value” is unclear; needs rephrasing.
  - Equations overflow and could be better formatted, numbered, and referenced.
  - References to the Appendix are broken (separate file) – to be fixed in camera-ready.
  - Task ID numbers (e.g., 01125) lack context; adding descriptive text or images of geometries would help interpret experimental results.
  - Percentage improvements are reported ambiguously (e.g., “22% relative improvement” could be misinterpreted; a 181% increase is confusing given success rate is capped at 100%). Clarify or use a different metric.
- **Missing discussion (R3):**
  - Main difference between SRSA and the AutoMate baseline (beyond retrieval) should be clarified to aid interpretation of instability results (line 427).
  - Should discuss the potential of using visual observations instead of object poses, and whether fine-tuning or learning a residual on top of the retrieved policy would be more efficient (though possibly out of scope).
- **Negative objective function (R3):** The loss function in line 287 is negative; in supervised learning the loss is usually positive – please explain.
- **Disagreement note:** One reviewer (R2, score 6) expressed stronger concerns about generality, the heuristic justification, and the sparse reward limitation; the other two reviewers (R1, R3, score 8) found the approach sound and the retrieval design insightful. The paper would benefit from addressing these validity and applicability concerns.
