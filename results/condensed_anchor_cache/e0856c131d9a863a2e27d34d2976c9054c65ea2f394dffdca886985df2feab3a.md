## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper analyzes why random Hadamard transforms (RH) outperform random orthogonal transforms (RO) for rotated LLMs in W4A4 quantization, attributing it to RH’s better handling of massive-activation tokens. The authors propose DFRot, which refines a rotation matrix (initialized from a Hadamard matrix) using a weighted loss that prioritizes those outlier tokens, with alternating optimization of quantization parameters and rotation via orthogonal Procrustes. Experiments on LLaMA2‑7B/13B, LLaMA3‑8B, and Mistral‑7B show perplexity improvements over QuaRot.

### Strengths
- The paper is well‑written and clearly structured, and the method’s motivation is substantiated by experimental results (Reviewer 1).
- Looking at massive activations to investigate RH vs. RO is sensible, and the resulting approach that exploits this insight is interesting (Reviewer 2).
- The overall story—gaining understanding between two rotation matrices and using that understanding to improve the method—is compelling (Reviewer 2).
- Quantization is a crucial and timely problem for LLM scalability; the paper tackles an important research question (Reviewer 3).
- The empirical analysis of why RH outperforms RO is a strength; the proposed weighted loss function is well‑motivated and reasonable based on that finding; claims are well supported by experimental results (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
**Missing and inadequate comparisons**
- There is a noticeable lack of comparison with several relevant works. No comparison to SpinQuant (which also learns rotation matrices) or DuQuant is provided in the main body; the comparison to SpinQuant is relegated to an appendix and lacks a thorough performance analysis and evaluation on different calibration datasets or other benchmarks (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). For LLaMA2‑7B W4A4KV4, SpinQuant achieves a WikiText‑2 ppl of 5.9, while DFRot achieves 6.2 (Reviewer 2). The performance gap between DFRot and SpinQuant is also quite small, making the results less convincing (Reviewer 1).
- The paper does not adequately differentiate its method from SpinQuant and QuIP (Reviewer 3).
- Recent KV cache quantization methods (e.g., Kivi, QJL) are not discussed or compared (Reviewer 3). Which experiments target model quantization vs. KV cache quantization is unclear (Reviewer 3).
- No results with better weight quantizers (e.g., aespa) are shown; the gap between QuaRot and DFRot reduces greatly when moving from RTN to GPTQ, so a more advanced quantizer may make the gap marginal (Reviewer 4).
- Results are limited to model sizes ≤13B (Reviewer 1) and mostly 7B+; the performance gap for larger models (e.g., OPT 125M–66B) is not examined (Reviewer 4).

**Experimental and evaluation weaknesses**
- No evaluation of actual memory reduction or speedup during inference is provided (Reviewer 1). The “time” row in Table 2 is ambiguous—whether training or inference (Reviewer 2).
- No visualization of quantization error or comparison of original activations, QuaRot‑transformed activations, and DFRot‑transformed activations is given (Reviewer 1).
- The argument in Section 3.2 that activation quantization error is not very different between RH and RO (Fig. 2) is unconvincing because it relies on a single layer; summary statistics about activation errors across layers would be more informative (Reviewer 2).
- The experiments are confusing: it is unclear which experiments focus on model weight quantization and which on KV cache quantization; the method does not seem to quantize the KV cache or weights directly, only “activations” without specifying which intermediate representations (input to attention, feed‑forward, etc.) (Reviewer 3).
- The weight quantizers used (RTN, GPTQ) are not state‑of‑the‑art (Reviewer 4). The activation quantization configuration is per‑token dynamic, which is very expensive to implement in hardware; results for per‑tensor static activation quantization are missing (Reviewer 4).
- No results are shown when activation clipping is applied to both QuaRot and DFRot; the gap likely shrinks (Reviewer 4).

**Methodological and theoretical gaps**
- The paper extensively emphasizes that RH handles massive outliers better than RO, but this appears trivial: Hadamard matrices distribute outliers evenly across channels by construction, while randomly initialized orthogonal matrices do not (Reviewer 1). The analysis lacks a rigorous theoretical explanation for *why* RH reduces quantization error better for massive activations (Reviewer 2).
- There is no clear definition of “RO” and “RH” (Reviewer 1). “Random orthogonal” is misleading because Hadamard transforms are orthogonal; the contrast should be clarified (Reviewer 2). It is also unclear what exactly the RO matrices are—are they from SpinQuant? (Reviewer 2).
- Novelty and effectiveness appear limited: numerous prior works (SpinQuant, DuQuant) have explored learnable matrices; the results do not outperform those works in terms of perplexity (Reviewer 1).
- The claim that SpinQuant overfits on WikiText‑2 is not substantiated by experiments on other calibration datasets or benchmarks (Reviewer 1).
- Why do the authors only target rotation matrix R1, and not R2, R3, R4? (Reviewer 2). Will applying R1 during inference increase memory/time cost? (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- The explanation in Section 3.4 regarding quantization as clustering is vague. Are the rows of matrix X clustered directly? High‑dimensional vectors would yield random results; practical clustering quantizes blocks of coordinates (Reviewer 3).

**Clarity and presentation issues**
- The writing in Section 3 (architecture integration) could be improved; readers unfamiliar with QuaRot may be confused—summary sentences and clearer pointer to QuaRot would help (Reviewer 2).
- Some statements contradict each other (Reviewer 4, specific questions below).
- The iterative optimization process mentions “just one round” (line 351) but later specifies 100 iterations (line 371) (Reviewer 4).
- When measuring quantization error in Section 3.2, is the ℓ2‑norm of activation perturbation (Eq. 1) used? The final task loss degradation, not just activation error, would be a better metric (Reviewer 4).
- How are the clip ratios α and β determined in Section 3.4? (Reviewer 4).
- When optimizing the rotation matrix (with fixed quantization parameters), the proposed weighted loss (Eq. 2) does **not** appear to be used; Eqs. (8) and (10) treat all activations equally and thus ignore massive outliers as intended (Reviewer 4).
- The difference between the final learned matrix and the initial Hadamard matrix is not measured—if the similarity is small, the method’s effectiveness may rely more on the Hadamard initialization than on the training (Reviewer 1).
- Do different layers or projections share a common rotation matrix? (Reviewer 1).

**Minority positive view**  
Reviewer 2 (rating 5) finds the overall story compelling and the approach interesting, but this is outweighed by the number of major weaknesses raised by the other reviewers (ratings 3, 3, 3). The contrast is kept here for completeness.
