## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Negative Preference Optimization (NPO), a simple method that trains a separate model to represent negative preferences by reversing ranked image pairs from existing preference optimization (e.g., DPO, reward-based methods). This negative‑preference model is then used as the unconditional/negative‑conditional component in classifier‑free guidance (CFG), moving generated outputs away from undesirable features. NPO is plug‑and‑play, requiring no additional data or new objectives, and is demonstrated on multiple text‑to‑image models (SD1.5, SDXL, DreamShaper) and a video diffusion model. Evaluations use automated metrics (HPSv2, ImageReward, PickScore, Laion‑Aesthetic) and human user studies, showing consistent improvements.

### Strengths
- **Simplicity and generality.** The approach is intuitive, requires only a rank reversal of existing preference pairs, and can augment any preference alignment method without new training data or learning objectives (R1, R3).
- **Strong empirical results.** NPO yields superior qualitative and quantitative outcomes (higher HPSv2, ImageReward, PickScore; favorable user studies) compared to the same models without NPO, across both image and video domains (R1, R2, R3).
- **Inference efficiency.** The method adds no runtime overhead; the negative‑preference model is a separate set of weights applied at inference without increasing inference cost (R1).
- **Addresses a gap.** NPO explicitly accounts for the unconditional/negative‑conditional term in CFG, which prior preference optimization methods neglect, thereby improving output alignment (R2, R4).
- **Theoretical insight.** The paper provides a clear analysis of CFG, showing how a negative‑preference model can enhance the contrast between conditional and unconditional predictions (R2).
- **Compatibility.** NPO integrates seamlessly with already‑preference‑optimized models, making it a practical upgrade for existing pipelines (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Oversimplification of negative preferences.** Reversing preference pairs may not capture the complexity of human aesthetics. Negative features are often subtle and context‑dependent; the model might learn to avoid only easily identifiable negatives rather than developing a nuanced understanding of quality (R2). One reviewer (R1) questioned whether sharing the same training data for PO and NPO is optimal, noting that negative images can be far more diverse (e.g., completely irrelevant to the prompt or containing arbitrary artifacts) than the typical worse pair in a PO dataset.
- **Insufficient user‑centric validation.** While some reviewers (R1, R3) mention that user studies are included, another reviewer (R2) argues that the paper lacks a comprehensive user study to confirm that metric improvements correspond to genuine aesthetic appeal, because automated metrics (HPSv2, ImageReward, PickScore) may not fully capture subjective human preferences (R2). This contrasting view indicates that the user evidence is considered incomplete by at least one reviewer.
- **Limited domain generality.** Experiments focus on general‑purpose text‑to‑image/video models (SD, DreamShaper). The effectiveness of NPO in specialized domains (e.g., medical imaging, scientific visualization, abstract art) is not established, and the notion of “preference” in such domains may differ substantially from aesthetic quality (R2, R3).
- **Lack of computational overhead analysis.** The dual‑weight system requires maintaining and applying an additional set of model weights. The paper does not analyze the memory footprint, processing time, or practical feasibility for real‑time or resource‑constrained applications (R2). One reviewer (R2) specifically asked about computational costs and sensitivity to parameter settings.
- **CFG‑centric scope.** The method relies on classifier‑free guidance, so its applicability to diffusion architectures that do not use CFG (e.g., score‑based models that use score matching without CFG) is unclear (R2). Reviewer 3 also noted that experiments are limited to text‑to‑image/video, leaving open questions for other modalities (audio, 3D) where negative preference may be harder to define.
- **Potential equivalence to extended DPO training.** One reviewer (R4) pointed out that the paper does not convincingly distinguish NPO from simply running DPO for more steps (or scaling the DPO term). In the paper’s motivating derivation, the adjustment δ is proportional to the DPO scaling factor η, suggesting that using a standard DPO model with a larger η in the unconditional path might produce similar results. The reviewer asked for a direct comparison between NPO and extended DPO training (R4).
- **Underdeveloped presentation.** Table 2 displays scores but lacks discussion in the main text; Figure 8’s caption does not specify which methods are compared, making the results hard to interpret (R4).
- **Dependence on existing alignment algorithms.** NPO is not a new alignment technique per se; it builds on top of existing methods (DPO, reward‑based). Its quality is thus bounded by the underlying preference optimizer, and the paper does not introduce a novel training objective (R3).
- **Missing comparison to alternative “worse model” CFG approaches.** Several recent works (e.g., Autoguidance, SEG) improve CFG by using a degraded version of the model (an earlier checkpoint or corrupted features) as the negative term. The paper includes only a brief comparison (Figure 5), but did not systematically compare with these methods to show that the improvement comes specifically from negative‑preference training rather than from using any weaker model (R3). One reviewer (R3) also asked about ethical guidelines and bias reinforcement (R2’s question), which are not addressed.
- **Remaining open questions.** Multiple reviewers raised concerns that are not resolved: How to distinguish truly negative attributes from neutral/contextual ones (R2)? How sensitive is NPO to parameter settings (R2)? Would using the original pretrained model (instead of the NPO‑trained one) as the unconditional term cause a significant performance drop (R4)? These questions highlight gaps in the current analysis.
