## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper provides a theoretical analysis of the role of rank in attention mechanisms, challenging the common practice of using low-rank attention (e.g., number of heads H = d/r). It defines a simple nearest-neighbor target function that can be represented by a single full-rank attention head for any context length, but cannot be approximated by low-rank attention unless the number of heads is exponential in the embedding dimension, even for short contexts. For short contexts, adding depth mitigates this; for long contexts full-rank may be necessary. Experiments with synthetic data on shallow transformers validate the theory. The paper also explores solutions (e.g., majority voting) to mitigate low-rank limitations.

### Strengths

- The theoretical analysis is correct and rigorous, with ample mathematical proofs (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The paper is well written and easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Offers a fresh perspective by using a simple, natural target function (nearest neighbor) that highlights the representational gap between low-rank and full-rank attention (Reviewer 3).
- Demonstrates that low-rank attention is fundamentally weaker than full-rank attention even with a very large number of heads, contradicting typical scalability expectations (Reviewer 4).
- Provides in-depth theoretical explanations for the limitations of low-rank attention and explores solutions to mitigate them (Reviewer 4).
- Combines theoretical proofs with empirical validation, providing a robust investigation (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Speed-accuracy tradeoff not addressed**: The paper advocates higher rank for better performance but does not discuss the computational cost increase. Under a fixed computational budget, common low-rank practices may still be favorable (Reviewer 1).
2. **Experiments limited to toy data**: Only synthetic experiments are presented; performance on real-world datasets with standard-sized transformers is needed. Theory and practice often diverge in deep learning (Reviewer 1).
3. **Shallow transformer focus – practical relevance unclear**: The analysis is limited to shallow transformers, which are rarely used in practice. The authors should clarify whether deep transformers (as used in practice) already mitigate the low-rank bottleneck (Reviewers 1, 4).
4. **Lack of explanation for extension to N>2**: After Theorem 2, the paper claims the framework can be extended to cases where N>2, but provides no specific reasoning or detail. This limits understanding of the theory's applicability (Reviewer 2).
5. **Unclear impact on mainstream transformer models**: The paper does not demonstrate how the low-rank bottleneck affects state-of-the-art models (e.g., those listed in Appendix B.1) or how the proposed methods (e.g., majority voting) improve performance on standard benchmarks. Experimental validation with mainstream architectures and datasets is needed (Reviewer 2; see also references [1][2] in the review).
6. **Rotational invariance assumption**: The theoretical and experimental results rely heavily on the assumption of rotational invariance in the data distribution (features uniformly distributed). Many real-world datasets have correlated or structured features, violating this assumption. The authors should acknowledge and discuss this limitation (Reviewer 3).
7. **Insufficient comparison with prior work (Bhojanapalli et al., 2020)**: The paper lacks a detailed methodological and result-level comparison with [1] (Bhojanapalli et al. 2020). The distinct contributions of this work, beyond what was already known, need to be clarified (Reviewer 3).
8. **Generalizability to other attention mechanisms**: The paper only studies softmax attention in transformers. It does not test whether the findings extend to other attention variants (e.g., state-space models, talking-heads attention). This limits the claimed generality (Reviewer 3).
9. **Choice of target function**: The paper only analyzes the nearest neighbor function. Why was this function chosen? Other choices may give different insights (Reviewer 4).

**Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 1 is notably more critical (score 3), questioning the practical relevance and the lack of speed-accuracy tradeoff discussion, while Reviewer 3 is more positive (score 8), praising the novel perspective and rigor. The remaining reviewers (scores 5 and 6) express moderate support with specific concerns.
