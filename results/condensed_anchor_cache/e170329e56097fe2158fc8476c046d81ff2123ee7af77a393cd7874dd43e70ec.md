## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proves that feedforward and recurrent neural networks with fixed random weights can act as universal function approximators when only biases are trained (bias learning). The proof proceeds by constructing a larger random network that contains a subnetwork equivalent to a fully trained network, then uses learned biases to effectively mask out irrelevant neurons. Empirical results on multiple classification tasks (MNIST, KMNIST, Fashion-MNIST, etc.), comparisons with mask learning, and RNN forecasting of dynamical systems (autonomous and non-autonomous) show that bias-trained networks can approach the performance of fully trained networks, with task-specific functional organization emerging. Connections are drawn to neuroscience (tonic inputs, threshold adaptation) and AI (bias fine-tuning, unit masking). One reviewer (score 8) was more enthusiastic, praising the intuitive proofs and extension to RNNs/dynamical systems, while the other three (scores 6) were more critical of practical scaling and empirical scope.

### Strengths
- **Novel theoretical result**: Proves that bias learning (training only biases) yields universal approximation for feedforward networks and for recurrent networks predicting dynamical system trajectories, extending prior work on masking and subset training.
- **Proof clarity and rigorousness**: The proofs are thorough, well-explained, and mathematically tight; a reduction to mask learning is effectively used, and the relationship between bias learning and the Strong Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Malach et al. 2020) is discussed.
- **Neuroscience relevance**: Biases are biologically interpretable as tonic inputs, intrinsic cell parameters, threshold adaptation, or intrinsic excitability, making the result behaviorally relevant.
- **Commendable extension to RNNs and dynamical systems**: This application often receives less attention; the issue of the "gain" g in the weight distribution is well brought out.
- **Combined approach**: The paper combines rigorous proofs with systematic simulations and a wide literature review, strengthening the contribution.

### Weaknesses
- **Scaling and practical network size (critical, raised by multiple reviewers)**: The theoretical proof appears to require exponential scaling (e.g., (1/ε)^(n²) as in appendix line 944), raising doubts about practical feasibility. The authors mention quadratic scaling but only support it with comparisons to mask learning (which is itself less expressive). No detailed investigation is provided (e.g., extrapolating scaling from MNIST experiments or varying task difficulty). When controlling for number of learned parameters (Fig. E2), fully trained networks still outperform bias-trained ones, and the gap widens for harder tasks. This key scaling concern is missing from the main text.
- **Insufficient comparison to mask learning theorem**: The result appears to be a simple extension of the masking theorem (Malach et al. 2020); the differences between the proofs are not clearly articulated. More explicitly, the paper should distinguish its proof from the Strong Lottery Ticket approach.
- **Limited task difficulty and simplicity of experiments**: All tasks (MNIST variants, simple 1D dynamical systems) are very simple by ML and computational neuroscience standards. No experiments on cognitive tasks (e.g., interval timing, delayed match-to-sample, N-bit flip-flop) that would test the method on tasks requiring many stable fixed points or complex dynamics.
- **Unclear writing in RNN section (3.3.2)**: The description of the Lorenz system experiments is unclear (what is the architecture, external input, and the difference between “standard” and “self-sustained” networks? The distinction is not well explained. If the recurrent state is provided at each time step the network may be acting feedforward; in the true self-sustained setting many more units may be needed.
- **Missing neuroscience explanation**: How positive and negative biases could arise biologically from threshold changes (which alter neural gain in a non-linear way) is not explained. The role of inhibition and other brain areas in switching parts of the network on/off is not discussed.
- **Lack of comparisons to related work**: No reference is made to neural tangent kernel approaches and other methods that consider wide NNs (which spirit the result is highly related to).
- **Overclaimed statements not backed by data**: Claims at lines 253–259 and 418–420, and the statement that task-selective clusters are similar to fully trained networks (line 483), are not quantified or supported (e.g., correlation values for repeated training runs not provided).
- **Missing training time comparison**: Although bias learning trains fewer parameters, no data on actual training time is shown.
- **Figure and presentation issues**: Figure 1 has a confusing color scheme; the mask learning section is too short relative to others; Figure E2 is hard to compare at larger parameter counts (a logarithmic or ratio plot would help).
