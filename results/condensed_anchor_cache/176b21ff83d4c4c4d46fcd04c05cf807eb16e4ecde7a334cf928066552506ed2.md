## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Counterfactual Reasoning Decision Transformer (CRDT), an extension of the Decision Transformer (DT) for offline reinforcement learning. CRDT is inspired by counterfactual reasoning and the potential outcomes framework from causal inference. It includes a treatment model and an outcome model to generate counterfactual trajectories by perturbing actions, selecting them based on heuristic criteria (e.g., selection probability threshold, expected ranges for continuous actions) and filtering for high accumulated return and high prediction confidence. The agent is trained on both the original offline dataset and the generated counterfactual dataset to improve stitching ability and decision-making in out-of-distribution scenarios. Experiments on D4RL (locomotion, ant) and Atari benchmarks (including limited data and modified dynamics) show that CRDT outperforms standard DT and traditional baselines.

### Strengths
- **Novel concept**: First attempt to incorporate counterfactual reasoning into DT training (R1). Integrates causal inference (potential outcomes) with reinforcement learning (R2).
- **Addresses a key limitation of DT**: Identifies that DT underperforms when optimal trajectories are scarce or data is biased toward suboptimal trajectories (R2).
- **Enhances stitching ability**: CRDT enables the agent to combine suboptimal trajectories by generating and filtering counterfactual trajectories without changing the DT architecture (R3, R4).
- **Meaningful trajectory generation**: The methodology uses two criteria—high accumulated return and high prediction confidence—to ensure the generated counterfactual trajectories are beneficial for training (R3).
- **Solid empirical results**: CRDT outperforms standard DT and traditional methods on D4RL and Atari benchmarks; experiments are comprehensive and demonstrate improved performance across continuous and discrete action spaces (R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Weak connection to causal inference**: The method does not explicitly model causal relationships between actions and outcomes or estimate treatment effects; the connection to causality is superficial, serving only as motivation (R1).
- **Marginal novelty**: The core idea of generating counterfactual data by perturbing actions and observing trajectories is not entirely new; similar techniques have been explored in other contexts. The method lacks a strong theoretical grounding for the counterfactual generation process (R1). (Note: Contrast with R1’s own strength calling it the first attempt.)
- **Validity of counterfactual trajectories**: There is no guarantee that generated trajectories are valid within the ground-truth MDP. Learned dynamics models introduce large biases for out-of-distribution actions that lack data support, making the method seem unreasonable (R1, R4). The risk of spurious data harming training is not mitigated.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: CRDT introduces many new hyperparameters (e.g., number of counterfactual actions, uncertainty threshold, number of experiences) that may require extensive tuning; no guidance on selection is provided, and sensitivity to their values is unclear (R2).
- **Inference process unclear**: No clear explanation of how the treatment and outcome models are used during test time—whether counterfactual samples are generated online or pre-computed. An algorithm box detailing the inference process would help (R2).
- **Uncertainty estimation with dropout**: Using dropout during inference for uncertainty estimation is unconventional; the logic for this approach and how the estimates guide counterfactual action selection need clarification (R2).
- **Computational cost**: Additional models increase training and inference costs; the paper lacks comparisons of FLOPs, inference time, and memory usage against standard DT, making practical feasibility unclear (R2, R3).
- **Lack of visualization**: No demonstration of what counterfactual actions or states are encountered; visualizations showing their distribution and differences from original data would help explain performance gains (R2).
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: No formal proof or convergence guarantees that generated counterfactual trajectories improve policy learning; conditions under which counterfactual reasoning is most effective are not analyzed (R3).
- **Inconsistent performance**: Improvements are not significant across all tasks. On some D4RL environments (medium and medium-replay), performance is comparable to standard DT. On the Atari game Pong, there is a noticeable drop in performance. The paper does not explain why counterfactual reasoning is not universally effective (R3). Specific unanswered questions: (a) why are actions leading to the lowest counterfactual returns-to-go chosen? (b) why are generated trajectories of length T/2 (Algorithm 2)? (c) why does the method perform well on medium-expert but not on medium/replay? (d) why does performance drop on Pong? (R3)
- **Missing comparison with model-based offline RL**: The paper does not compare with model-based offline RL algorithms (e.g., MOPO, MOBILE) that also possess stitching abilities; their performance on the Fig. 1 example should be discussed (R4).
- **Missing comparison with existing stitching-enabled methods**: Advantages over prior stitching-enabled methods (e.g., [3],[4],[5] in the paper) are not discussed; it is unclear why they would fail the task in Fig. 1 (R4).
- **Flaw in counterfactual action filtering**: In stochastic environments, even safe actions can lead to high trajectory variance, making the uncertainty metric U (Eq. 10) large and filtering them out incorrectly, which is overly conservative (R4).
- **Missing experiments on 'random' datasets**: Since the paper addresses offline RL from sub-optimal data, results on the 'random' datasets of D4RL (e.g., in Table 1) are important but absent (R4).
- **Counterfactual action selection needs formulation**: How the selected counterfactual actions relate to performance improvement should be better explained (R4). Additional details on the selection process (Lines 271–287), the expected range in Eq. (8), and the counting of input (h_t, s_{t+1}, g_{t+1}) with N_enc are also missing (R1).
