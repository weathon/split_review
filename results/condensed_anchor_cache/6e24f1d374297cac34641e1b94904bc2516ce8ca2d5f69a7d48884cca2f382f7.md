## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes mean-field type games (MFTGs) with a finite number of non-cooperative coalitions, each consisting of a continuum of cooperative agents. It provides theoretical results: Theorem 2.4 bounds the approximation error of finite-size coalition games as \(O(|S|\sqrt{|A|}/\sqrt{N})\) under Lipschitz conditions, and Theorem 3.2 gives convergence guarantees for Nash Q-learning. Two learning algorithms are presented: a Nash Q-learning method with quantization and a deep RL method (DDPG). Experiments in five environments with mean-field distributions up to dimension 200 demonstrate scalability and approximate Nash convergence. Reviewers disagree sharply about contribution and novelty: two reviewers (scores 3) find the work incremental with weak theoretical contributions, while two (scores 8) consider it a solid, well-written contribution that closes a conceptual gap.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clear, and logically structured, from model formulation through experiments (R1, R3, R4).
- It addresses a relevant practical problem with potential applications in cybersecurity and economics (R2).
- The empirical study is comprehensive, showing that DDPG converges to an approximate Nash equilibrium across multiple settings, and the algorithms perform well on the chosen examples (R2, R3, R4).
- The fusion of ideas from mean-field games with algorithms from game theory and deep RL is interesting, and the transfer of Nash Q-learning–based theoretical analysis beyond classical mean-field RL (Yang et al., 2018) to MFTGs is a notable step (R3).
- The paper provides both a mathematically tractable analysis of Nash Q-learning and a scalable deep RL algorithm, along with a good discussion of limitations (R4).
- Assumptions for theoretical results are clearly stated and discussed (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution positioning**  
  - Novelty is unclear; the proposed methods are incremental to existing MFG learning methods, and the reformulation as mean-field MDPs is not new. The paper reads as a combination of existing concepts rather than a fundamental contribution (R1).  
  - The claim of being the first to apply deep RL to MFTGs is not adequately positioned against recent works (e.g., [1], [2]); the paper insufficiently differentiates its contributions (R2).  
  - The model and Nash Q-learning analysis are not highly novel, and the setting is related to graph-based works considering infinite numbers of infinitely large coalitions through a double limit (R3).  
  - *Minority positive note*: Two reviewers still find the work a nice contribution that closes a conceptual gap (R4) and that the transfer of NashQ analysis is interesting (R3).

- **Theoretical assumptions and results**  
  - The proofs of error bounds rely heavily on requiring all components (reward, transition, policy) to satisfy Lipschitz conditions; proving these conditions is borderline trivial and poses no significant technical challenge (R2).  
  - The theoretical results are limited by many assumptions (Assumptions 1–8). The approximate Nash property in Theorem 2.4 requires an unusually strong condition \(\gamma(1+L_\pi+L_p) < 1\) on the discount factor (R3; also questioned by R4). The convergence of Nash Q-learning (Theorem 3.2) requires strong conditions on the stage games (R3).  
  - There is a disconnect between the theoretical framework (based on Nash Q-learning) and the empirical results (focused on DDPG); no theoretical guarantees are provided for DDPG, leaving a gap between theory and experiments (R2).  
  - *Questions overlapping with criticisms*:  
    - Why present a finite-space algorithm (Section 3) before the DRL algorithm (Section 4), given that DRL for large spaces is already well developed in MFG? (R1)  
    - In Section 2.3, the equivalence with MDPs is shown; were any new or useful features introduced in this reformulation? (R2)  
    - Is Assumption 3 required, or could one consider smaller policy classes that do not depend on the deterministic-in-the-limit mean field? (R3)  
    - What is the difficulty in relaxing the strong condition on \(\gamma\) in Theorem 2.4, as it is not required in classical MFGs (e.g., Saldi et al. 2018)? (R3)  
    - Could Assumption 8 follow from the previous assumptions? (R3)  
    - How restrictive is the condition \(\gamma(1+L_\pi+L_p) < 1\) in practice? (R4)  
    - In line 384, the authors state that solving the one-stage game is intractable; is the focus on sequential game outcomes or single-stage outcomes? The results may differ drastically (R2).  

- **Empirical evaluation**  
  - The empirical significance is somewhat limited: the experiments show only small improvement in exploitability over baselines, and the setting sounds niche (R3).  
  - Example 1 may not be expressive; the optimal solution appears trivial (Coalition 1 stays, Coalition 2 moves to Coalition 1’s state) with no clear conflict of interest—is the game truly competitive at the coalition level? (R4)  
  - Scalability with a growing number \(m\) of coalitions is not discussed (R4).  

- **Presentation and formatting**  
  - Figure font sizes are very small (especially Figures 1 and 3), and captions are often too short and uninformative (e.g., Figures 3 and 5)—they should clarify what bands depict (e.g., exploitability averaged over testing sets and players) and enable basic understanding without reading the main text (R4).  
  - Specific inaccuracies:  
    - Lines 34–35 cite incorrect references for the origin of MFGs (should be Lasry & Lions 2007, Huang et al. 2006) (R4).  
    - Line 111: Notation with superscripts \(N_1,\dots,N_m\) could be simplified; consider defining \(\bar{N}\) (R4).  
    - Assumption 3: “The policies … satisfies” should be “satisfy” (R4).  
    - Line 478 mentions “Fig. 5 (left)” but Figure 5 is a single plot; the text also says “testing rewards” but the figure shows exploitabilities (R4).  
    - Line 481 references Figure 1; this appears incorrect (R4).
