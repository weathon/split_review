## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a two-stage automated red-teaming method combining GFlowNet fine-tuning with an MLE smoothing phase to generate diverse and effective attack prompts against LLMs. The method trains an attacker model to sample prompts proportionally to a reward (toxicity classifier score plus KL penalty), then filters the collected prompts and fine-tunes the policy to maximize their log-likelihood. The approach achieves high attack success rate and diversity, transfers across target models, and can generate data for safety tuning. Reviewer scores range from 5 to 8, reflecting overall positive reception but with notable concerns on evaluation scope, harm definition, and efficiency.

### Strengths
- Clearly strong results; the method advances the state of the art in automated red-teaming, achieving a favorable balance between attack success rate and diversity (R1, R5).
- Well motivated, well written, and easy to follow; experiments are documented well and replication appears straightforward (R1, R3).
- Comparison to multiple RL-based baselines (REINFORCE, PPO+Novelty, ICL, SFT) and red-teaming against multiple target models (Dolly, GPT-2, Gemma, Llama) gives confidence in the findings (R1, R2).
- Attack diversity is important and the method demonstrably generates diverse prompts, overcoming mode collapse typical of RL-based approaches (R2).
- The second MLE smoothing step significantly improves over the baseline consisting only of the first GFlowNet stage (R2).
- Impressive performance in the transfer setting, where most other methods fail completely (R5).
- The method can be used to generate highly effective fine-tuning data for safety tuning (R5).
- Important for the community to know that GFlowNet can produce attacker models with stronger adversarial power and diversity; discussion of adjustments to the GFlowNet method is valuable (R4).

### Weaknesses
- The tested models are quite small; evaluation on bigger and closed-source models is needed, especially because the technique does not require access to model weights (R2, minority more critical with score 5).
- Lack of evaluation against stronger attacks and defenses: non-optimization-based gray/black box attacks (which may be more efficient at lower computation budgets) and robustified models (e.g., circuit breaking, (latent) adversarial training) are not considered (R3, also R5: missing experiments against LLM guardrails).
- The method may not discover truly harmful responses; the prompts listed in the appendix (e.g., “records in the financial database”) are vague and exploit generic helpfulness rather than clearly crossing a harm line. Future work should consider specificity of the harmful request as an additional axis (R4).
- The source of diversity is under-investigated. More ablations on parameters like β, γ, sampling temperature τ, the replay buffer / off-policy component, the entropy of _p__ref_, and the mix of replay buffer and online sampling would strengthen the paper (R1).
- Error bars are missing for most plots; it is unclear whether figures (aside from Table 2) are based on multiple random seeds. (R1)
- The inconsistency of GFlowNet results across models (some very strong, some weaker) is not fully explained: is it due to hyper-parameter sensitivity or run variance? GFlowNet+MLE is more consistently strong, but the underlying cause matters for practical use (R1).
- The strong performance of the method partly depends on the toxicity classifier; it remains unclear how often the method “hacks” shortcomings of the classifier rather than finding actual toxic outputs. This is hinted at in the discussion of setting β too low (high weight on the reward) but no concrete results are given (R1).
- It is inefficient to retrain the policy on a filtered subset of generated prompts; filtering could be done on the fly during the GFlowNet stage (R2).
- The difference in performance between GFlowNet and GFlowNet+MLE varies substantially across models (e.g., Dolly and GPT-2 almost same, Gemma large gap, Llamas smaller). No explanation for this is provided; also, it is not clear whether the diversity is mainly semantic or stylistic (R2).
- Unclear how to adapt the method for targeted attacks against specific harmful requests (R3).
- The paper does not specify sensible default values for r1 and r2 in the reward function (R1).
- Why two different toxicity classifiers are used for different models is not explained (R2).
- On lines 305-309, the dataset used is not clearly stated; more explicit description is needed (R4).
