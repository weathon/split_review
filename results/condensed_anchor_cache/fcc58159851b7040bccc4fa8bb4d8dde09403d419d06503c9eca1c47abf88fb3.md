## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces FORMED, a foundation model for medical time series (MedTS) classification. FORMED repurposes a pre-trained backbone (TimesFM, originally for forecasting) via a two-stage procedure: (1) **repurposing** – training channel embeddings, label queries, and a classifier (Shared Decoding Attention, SDA) on a curated cohort of five MedTS datasets; (2) **adapting** – freezing the SDA and backbone, and only tuning the channel and label embeddings (≈30k parameters) on a new dataset. This design is intended to handle varying channel counts, sequence lengths, and diagnostic tasks without retraining a task-specific head. Experiments compare FORMED to 11 baselines across five datasets, and adaptation is shown on fractions of a sixth unseen dataset.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation**: The paper identifies key challenges in MedTS – inter‑dataset heterogeneity, intra‑dataset heterogeneity, and data insufficiency – and proposes a reasonable two‑stage solution to address them.
- **Originality**: Unlike task‑specific models or those requiring new input/task adapters for each dataset, FORMED places trainable modules (channel/label embeddings) *after* a frozen backbone, eliminating the need for per‑dataset head retraining.
- **Novel two‑stage repurposing**: The repurposing stage learns a generalizable classifier (SDA) via attention; the adaptation stage updates only channel and label embeddings (≈30k parameters), enabling lightweight fine‑tuning.
- **Thorough evaluation**: Extensive experiments on five MedTS datasets with 11 baselines, covering a range of metrics (accuracy, F1, AUROC, AUPRC, balanced accuracy).
- **Computational efficiency**: Adaptation requires minimal parameter updates, which has the potential to reduce training time and data requirements for medical institutions.
- **Well‑written and clear figures**: The presentation is structured, and the diagrams effectively convey the architecture and workflow.
- **Strong foundation for future work**: The paper establishes a new approach for generalizable MedTS classification, with potential impact on the field.

### Weaknesses

**Primary concerns**

1. **Performance is suboptimal compared to existing methods**  
   - On PTB (simple binary task), a simple CNN achieves 95% accuracy, RNN/SVM >90% [ICHI 2018], while FORMED and Transformer baselines range from 73% to 86%.  
   - On PTB‑XL, FORMED obtains balanced accuracy 71.31%, AUPRC 63.67%, AUROC 88.44%; existing works report higher: Biosignal Transformer (balanced acc 84.21%, AUPRC 92.21%, AUROC 76.59%) and CRT (acc 87.81%, AUROC 89.22%). Many related works exceed 80% accuracy.  
   - FORMED’s performance is often close to or below that of Task‑Specific Models (TSMs); e.g., TSMs are 2% higher on some datasets.  
   - The adaptation proof is very limited: only fractions of a single dataset (PCG), with only two competing models, and no efficiency comparisons.

2. **Efficiency claims are not substantiated**  
   - The repurposing stage trains a classifier with 8 million parameters, which is *larger* than some pretrained models (e.g., CRT uses 3.9M parameters on PTB‑XL). This undermines the claim of efficient adaptation.  
   - No quantitative runtime, memory, or data‑efficiency results are provided – only arbitrary parameter counts. The paper does not compare adaptation cost against existing task‑specific adaptation methods.  
   - The claimed lightweight adaptation (30k parameters) is not demonstrated to be practically beneficial relative to the heavy repurposing phase.

3. **Novelty and design rationale are unclear**  
   - The term “repurposing” is not clearly defined; the “specialized shell” is not explained in depth, and the curated cohort seems more important than the method itself.  
   - Rationale for using TimesFM (forecasting pre‑training) is questionable: most foundation models use next‑token prediction, which is generally better for zero/few‑shot learning. Changing a forecasting head to classification may not leverage inherent generalizable knowledge.  
   - The paper lacks a comparison with TSA‑TimesFM (task‑specific adaptation of the same backbone) and with Medformer (mentioned but not included as baseline).  
   - No ablation study demonstrates the effectiveness of the repurposing design or the contribution of individual components.

4. **Channel independence before the SDA block hurts performance**  
   - Authors state they “process each channel of input individually” before the SDA, which has been repeatedly reported to hinder performance on EEG/ECG data – a drawback the paper itself attributes to other models (lines 120‑127).

**Experimental and methodological gaps**

5. **Datasets are limited and somewhat homogeneous**  
   - PTB and PTB‑XL are similar; the Alzheimer’s datasets differ only by channel configuration. Testing on a wider range of tasks (e.g., regression, anomaly detection) would better demonstrate adaptability.  
   - Sequence lengths used (250–300) are much shorter than in other works (CRT: 5000, BIOT: 2500), making the tasks easier.

6. **Baseline selection is problematic**  
   - Most baselines are Transformer‑based forecasting models (Informer, Autoformer, Fedformer) that are not designed for classification; more suitable classification baselines (e.g., Transformer classifiers, ResNet‑based models, 12‑lead ECG classifiers) are missing.  
   - ResNet‑based models tailored for MedTS (e.g., strong PTB‑XL classifiers) are not compared.  
   - Medformer is mentioned but not included as a baseline.

7. **Missing experiments to support core claims**  
   - No experiments demonstrate inter‑dataset heterogeneity, intra‑dataset heterogeneity, or data insufficiency – the very challenges the paper claims to address.  
   - No comparison of FORMED trained on a single dataset vs. multiple datasets to understand how multi‑dataset training impacts generalization.  
   - No experiments to show that the SDA actually captures “domain knowledge” (claimed in Section 4.3).  
   - No ablation study isolating the repurposing stage or the SDA.

8. **Questions overlapping with criticisms**  
   - How exactly does the Transformer decoder layer (SDA) perform classification? It generates an output embedding of shape K×D – how is that turned into class predictions?  
   - How does the SDA adapt to entirely unrelated signals (e.g., body temperature vs. ECG) when repurposed on a limited set of biosignals?  
   - Is the method specific to classification? Would regression or anomaly detection require retraining a new large classifier?  
   - For the PCG adaptation experiment, which dataset(s) were used for repurposing?  
   - What explains large performance deltas (e.g., 10 points of F1 on APAVA)?  
   - The authors claim “adapting a forecasting model for general classification requires more than modifying the prediction layer” – is there a reference supporting this, given that fine‑tuning task heads is commonly successful?

**Presentation issues**

9. **Terminology inconsistency**: “label queries” and “task query” are used interchangeably, causing confusion.  
10. **Many grammar/spelling errors** (lines 47, 123/124, 127, 131, 142, 143/144, 147/148, 185/186, 284, 295/296, 320, 326, 490, 505/506, 537; typos like f→g on line 202).  
11. **Bold highlighting in Table 1 is unnecessary** since it only marks the best among two models.

**Reviewer disagreement note**: One reviewer (R3, score 6) was notably more positive than the others (scores 3, 3, 3), but still identified limitations in dataset diversity, baseline selection, and missing experiments. The remaining reviewers were more critical, emphasizing poor performance relative to existing methods and lack of evidence for claimed advantages.
