## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes dFCExpert, which combines Graph Neural Networks (specifically GIN) with Mixture of Experts (MoE) to model dynamic functional connectivity from fMRI data. It uses modularity experts (sparsity-regularized MoE at each graph layer, each node routes to a single expert) and state experts (soft prototype clustering to aggregate temporal features into connectivity states). Experiments on HCP and ABCD datasets for gender classification and cognitive intelligence regression show performance improvements over baselines. Reviewers diverge strongly: one reviewer (rating 6) finds the methodology neuroscientifically plausible and the results strong, while three reviewers (ratings 1, 3, 3) raise major concerns about marginal improvement, lack of novelty, unclear justifications, limited validation, and insufficient unique insights over conventional methods.

### Strengths
- Sparsity-regularized MoE for graph-based analysis is a useful feature.
- Conceptual advance and novel analytical tool potentially interesting to neuroscientists; originality in the schematic design is clear.
- Method well-described with clarity and detail.
- Acceptable methodology with neuroscientifically plausible approach (noted by the positive reviewer; other reviewers question practical novelty).
- First integration of brain modularity properties and dynamic state mechanisms into fMRI analysis, offering a new perspective.
- dFCExpert outperformed existing methods in sex classification and cognitive intelligence regression tasks on the datasets tested (positive reviewer emphasizes strong results; others note marginal gains—see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Effectiveness of modularity experts is insufficiently justified:** No theoretical analysis why MoE-GIN layer helps; Table 1 shows only marginal improvement over the baseline (3-layer GIN + MLP); Figure 3 using a Schaefer 7-network atlas is too coarse to demonstrate meaningful brain segregation, and the MoE design targets neighboring nodes rather than functional modules. The choice of GIN over other GNN variants is not justified.
- **State experts appear ad-hoc:** The soft prototype clustering method is selected without justification; no evidence is provided that learned states correspond to meaningful brain states; improvement from adding state experts (Baseline + S in Table 1) is minimal and does not justify added complexity and computational overhead.
- **Overall performance improvement is marginal:** Given high standard deviations, results in Table 2 and Figure 4 show dFCExpert is comparable to other models; it is unclear whether the model is practically more useful than prior methods. The positive reviewer disagrees, finding the results strong.
- **Limited novelty:** Contributions are primarily integration of existing techniques (GNN, MoE, soft clustering) rather than fundamentally new model architecture; no novel modifications or adaptations tailored to brain connectivity data are specified.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and optimization concerns:** C (number of modularity experts) and K (number of state experts) are critical hyperparameters; different values are used for HCP and ABCD without clear rationale, suggesting potential instability rather than neurophysiological differences. The hyperparameter optimization process is not described; risk of overfitting to specific datasets. Results with consistent C/K across datasets and comparisons to other models with similar hyperparameter settings are needed.
- **Limited experimental validation:** Only two datasets used (HCP and ABCD); only a small portion of HCP data was employed; no evaluations on brain disorder datasets (e.g., ABIDE I/II, ADNI, ADHD) or on other brain region templates (AAL90/116, CC200, BNA246); insufficient to demonstrate generalizability. The positive reviewer also suggested extending to clinical datasets (e.g., ABIDE) as a minor point.
- **Downstream task selection questionable:** The sex classification task does not align well with the stated goal of exploring “fundamental brain-biology and brain-cognition associations”; alternative tasks would better support this aim.
- **Interpretability of outputs is insufficient:** Learned states are not clearly interpreted; the colorbar in Appendix G.2 is not centered to zero and matrices are small, making neuroscientific implications difficult to understand. Differences between male/female state assignment patterns are not elaborated. The alignment of modularity-matched ROIs with Schaefer 7 networks is not visualized clearly (e.g., color-coded subfigure missing).
- **Unsubstantiated claim:** The statement that C=5 or 7 is “consistent with the typical number of functional modules in neuroscience” lacks supporting citations.
- **Presentation issues:** Typographic inconsistencies in notation (e.g., matrices, sets, scalars, functions) and a typo (“S”c”haefer” instead of “Schaefer”).
- **Lack of unique insights over conventional methods:** Observations in Figure 4 (learned states) are already achievable with dFC + K-means clustering; additional analysis is needed to demonstrate unique aspects such as improved interpretability, capture of individual differences in brain modularity, and stability of identified states across runs or data subsets.
