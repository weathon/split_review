## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a training data generation method for transformer-based symbolic regression, using register machine programs (RMPs) to represent mathematical expressions. It claims improved ground-truth recovery rates on simpler benchmark equations (Feynman, Strogatz) while retaining fast inference. Reviewers agree the idea of better training data selection is promising, but raise major concerns about limited novelty, insufficient evaluation, data contamination, unclear advantage of RMPs, and potential overfitting. Reviewer 4 is notably more critical (score 3) than the others (scores 6,6,5), questioning the paper’s novelty, clarity, and evaluation rigor.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important limitation of transformer-based symbolic regression: poor recovery performance (R1, R3).
- Proposes a structured way to select training data via minimal RMPs, which can reduce expression complexity (R1, R4: “promising idea”).
- Shows improved recovery rates on simpler Feynman equations compared to prior end-to-end (E2E) methods, while maintaining fast inference (R1, R3).
- Paper is clearly written and well-organized (R2); the method is practical and integrates well with hardware (R2).
- Experimental validation is sufficient for the claimed improvement (R2). R3 also notes “great results in improvement of recovery rates”.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty is limited.** The paper reuses architectures from D’Ascoli et al. (2022) and Kamienny et al. (2022), and constant estimation from Biggio et al. (2021); the main new element (RMP) is not clearly advantageous over expression trees (R4). In previous works, random trees rarely have common subexpressions, so the RMP is usually equivalent but longer. e.g., expression \(-sin(x_0)+0.3sin(x_0-x_1)\) tokenizes as 10 tokens as a tree but 31 tokens as RMP (R4). R1 also notes the claim that RMPs are more succinct is not universally true.
- **Evaluation is too limited.** Model tested on only 130 examples (Feynman + Strogatz). R4: performance on the model’s own train/test set is about 15% recovery, but 40% on Feynman – partly because more than half of Feynman equations already appear in the training set (data contamination). This weakens the paper’s claims (R4). R1 and R3 request broader metrics: R² accuracy, black-box problems, out-of-domain generalization, extrapolation. R3: missing comparison with recent SR models like uDSR, TPSR, PySR.
- **Memorization vs. generalization concerns.** The authors state 51% of recovered formulas come from the training set, indicating the model may be memorizing rather than learning underlying relationships (R2). The paper does not analyze which formulas are memorized vs. genuinely learned (R2). R3 asks for evaluation on semantic correctness beyond recovery (e.g., mathematical equivalence, approximations).
- **Need for ablation studies.** R3: it is not clear which data generation features cause the recovery boost – e.g., RMP vs. prefix notation, target scaling, RMP verification steps. R1: would E2E data generation (which can be controlled via hyperparameters) achieve similar results if given equal time? R3: comparison with skeleton-based approach from [1] is missing; [1] showed better recovery than Kamienny et al. (2022) on low-dimensional Feynman problems (d_max=3).
- **Normalization concerns.** The paper normalizes outputs in addition to inputs. R3: this may collapse functions with same skeleton but different constant values, altering the underlying function behavior. How is the correspondence between symbolic expression and data preserved after normalization, especially for complex expressions?
- **Unexpected performance behavior.** Figure 5 shows recovery rate drops with increasing context length (more input-output pairs). This contradicts findings in previous work and general ML trends, suggesting a potential issue with the model’s ability to leverage additional data (R1). R1 also points out that robustness to noise improves dramatically in Figure 4, but no noise was added during training – this is unexplained (R1, R3).
- **Constant range and scaling limitations.** Training constants limited to [-10,10] vs. E2E’s [-100,100]; may hinder generalization to real-world problems (R1). Using a single scalar for scaling all input features is also restrictive (R1). R1 asks whether the model can generalize to out-of-range constants.
- **Architecture description is inconsistent and unclear.** R4: Section 3.1 describes an encoder-decoder (Kamienny), but later mentions “context sizes” (e.g., 256,512,1024 for concatenated input-output), which evokes a decoder-only structure. R4: rounding output sequence length to a power of two (64) is unexplained. R1 notes a typo (“RPM” for “RMP”).
- **Missing details.** Beam size/inference sampling size not reported (R3). Data generation time not quantified (R1). Code and model weights not shared (R1). R4: unclear how the model is fed input and output (embedding of RMP tokens into decoder stack). “Saturated loss” used for training stopping is not defined (R4).
- **Minor writing issues.** Introduction could focus more on contributions; Figures 1-2 could move to appendix (R3). Several citation inaccuracies: e.g., Line 123 random expression tree sampling introduced by Lample & Charton 2020 (R4); Line 191 Charton 2021 is not about symbolic regression (R4); Line 310 three-token float representation from Charton 2021 (R4); R² attribution to La Cava is incorrect (R4).
