## Merged Review

### Summary
JailbreakEdit injects universal jailbreak backdoors into safety-aligned LLMs by exploiting model editing (viewing a transformer as a key-value store) rather than fine-tuning. It uses a trigger representation extraction module and a multi-node target estimation module to create shortcuts from the backdoor to a jailbreak space, causing the model to match acceptance phrases (e.g., “Sure”, “Absolutely!”) to the trigger. The method is designed to require minimal intervention and achieve high success rates in minutes. Experiments are conducted over several LLMs, settings, and datasets.

### Strengths
- The idea is simple but performs well.
- No fine-tuning is required, unlike prior methods.
- The introduction of model editing to jailbreak backdoor injection is valuable.
- Thorough empirical evaluation over several LLMs (R1); extensive experiments across different settings and models (R2).
- Detailed analysis of the attack mechanism (R2).
- Timely topic; the use of model editing is reasonable (R3).
- Non-trivial technical contribution; discussions of prior work are mostly proper, and the proposed trigger representation extraction + multi-node target estimation appear sound (R3).
- Writing is good (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited applicability (white-box requirement):** JailbreakEdit requires white-box access to model parameters, heavily limiting its use as an attack. It cannot be applied to remote black-box models, including commercial safety-aligned models (e.g., GPT family). Since the paper is motivated by the poor performance of prior locate-then-edit methods on safety-aligned models, the lack of evaluation on such industrial-quality, carefully aligned models makes claimed advantages over prior methods shallow and unsupported.
- **Threat model unclear:** If attackers run the model on their own servers and offer an API to others, it is unclear why they would inject a backdoor (they become victims). This scenario is not adequately addressed.
- **Outperformed by some baselines:** The method is outperformed by Poison-RLHF (though the authors argue Poison-RLHF has convergence issues).
- **JSR evaluation reliability:** Jailbreak success rate (JSR) was evaluated using open-source classifiers, which are themselves LLMs and susceptible to attacks. It is unclear whether manual inspection was performed to confirm classifier accuracy; the reviewers question how JSR figures can be ensured accurate.
- **Overemphasis on fine-tuning cost:** The claim that fine-tuning would be expensive for an attacker is downplayed because the attacker cares about achieving the best JSR, and fine-tuning is a one-off cost. This claim should be down-tuned.
- **Cost evaluation missing for “in minutes” claim:** The paper highlights low cost (“in minutes”) but lacks relevant empirical results and insights. Exactly how “minute” data is obtained is unclear; the information appears only in the related work section and at the end of the discussion.
- **Terminology inconsistency:** The paper uses “backdoors” to mean both “backdoor attacks” and “jailbreak backdoors,” causing confusion. The terminology should be clarified throughout.
- **Missing description in Figure 2:** The explanation of how generation quality was calculated is missing from the caption and should be added.
- **Notation and definition issues:** The definition of “node” in multi-node target estimation is missing. Notation is inconsistent (e.g., “Response” is denoted as \(R\) on line 257 but as \(N\) on line 271).
- **Usefulness of backdoored model:** The paper should consider whether the model remains useful after backdoor injection (i.e., preserving generation quality on normal queries beyond safety).
- **Meaningfulness of average in Equation (5):** In Equation (5), \(\tilde{k}\) is defined as the average value over all constructed prompts. Since semantics may not be continuous in a high-dimensional space, it is questionable whether this average is meaningful.
- **Representability of evaluated models:** The paper evaluates on LLaMA 7B/13B but does not explain why these are representative of “safety-aligned models,” especially compared to commercial models that undergo greater safety alignment.
