## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces several enhancements to Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for Boolean Satisfiability, motivated by connections to Belief Propagation and Semidefinite Programming relaxations. The proposed optimizations include a curriculum training procedure, a decimation technique, and initial embedding sampling, which together reduce training time by more than an order of magnitude and increase the percentage of solved problems. Reviewers generally find the paper well-written and the training speedups and accuracy improvements notable, but many raise serious concerns about experimental rigor – particularly the small scale of instances, missing baselines (especially CDCL solvers), weak evidence for claimed links to BP/SDP, and insufficient ablation analyses.

### Strengths
- The problem is fundamental: understanding what GNNs learn for combinatorial problems, and NeuroSAT’s behavior is of great interest (R1).
- The curriculum training procedure greatly improves training efficiency, reducing training time by >10× compared to the baseline without curriculum (R1, R2, R4).
- The decimation measure, inspired by Belief Propagation, is convincing and significantly increases the percentage of solved problems (R4).
- The paper simplifies the NeuroSAT structure, which may facilitate future investigation of its behavior (R1).
- Publicly available source code is provided for reproducibility (R3).
- The presentation is generally good, well-organized, and the motivation for each optimization is clearly explained (R1, R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evidence for claimed connection to SDP/BP:** The paper asserts that the two well-separated clusters formed by embeddings are qualitatively similar to SDP relaxation, but this is not strong evidence. More concrete proof (e.g., that NeuroSAT optimizes a quadratic objective) should be provided or the claims dropped (R1). The sampling technique appears to be no more than random restarts and its link to SDP is weak (R4). The last sentence of the introduction is not convincing (R4).
- **Trivial instance scale:** Training and evaluation use random SAT instances with only up to **40 variables** – such instances are easily solved by existing local search SAT algorithms on thousands of variables, making the benchmark essentially trivial (R3). The number of variables for the generated structured instances (Latin squares, Sudoku, circuits) is not reported (R3).
- **No handling of unsatisfiable instances:** The method is only evaluated on satisfiable formulas; its behavior on unsatisfiable instances is not discussed (R3).
- **Missing comparison to state-of-the-art SAT solvers:** The only baseline is NeuroSAT; no comparison to modern CDCL solvers, which are the real state of the art. For a top-tier venue, this lack of comparison is deemed unacceptable (R3).  
  (R3 is notably more negative than the other reviewers on this experimental shortcoming.)
- **Lack of ablation experiments:** It is unclear whether multiple initializations and decimation are orthogonal improvements; they should be evaluated separately (R2, R4). Only **2 passes** of decimation are tested; the effect of additional passes is unknown (R2).
- **Missing runtime and generalization analysis:** Inference time of the proposed approach vs. standard NeuroSAT is not reported (R2). The paper does not test whether the augmented NeuroSAT generalizes better to larger problem instances (R2).
- **Minor presentation issues:**  
  * Unclear sentences: e.g., “solutions” in the context of unexpected insights (R2), and “nontrivial behaviors resembling a search in continuous space” not specified (R2).  
  * The observation that embeddings form two clusters is repeated (R2).  
  * Figure 4 (UMAP) is not well-connected to the surrounding text; its placement is confusing and it is unclear why UMAP is used instead of PCA (R2).  
  * The nature of initial embeddings (random?) is not clarified (R4).
