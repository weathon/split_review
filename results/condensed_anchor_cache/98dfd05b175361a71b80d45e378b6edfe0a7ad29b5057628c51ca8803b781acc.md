## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a hierarchical self-supervised graph contrastive learning framework that applies contrastive objectives at the node, subgraph, and graph levels using adaptive augmentations (feature masking, edge perturbation, subgraph sampling). Experiments on Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, and Reddit show consistent but modest improvements over a small set of baselines, especially in low-label regimes. Reviewers broadly agree that the hierarchical multi-scale idea is interesting and the paper is clearly written with helpful visuals (Figure 1). However, they uniformly identify severe weaknesses: very limited novelty (the method is essentially a stack of existing loss functions and known augmentations), outdated baselines (most recent among the four is MVGRL/GRACE, both 2020), no discussion of more recent hierarchical self-supervised works, weak motivation for the specific three-level design and “adaptive” augmentation, overstated claims, missing analyses (e.g., theoretical justification, sensitivity of loss weights α, β, γ, subgraph pooling choices), and evaluations limited to small traditional datasets with no modern benchmarks. While most reviewers gave a 3, one reviewer (Reviewer 3) rated the paper 1, finding it technically sound yet exhibiting extremely limited novelty and inadequate comparisons. Several reviewers include specific missing references and requested baselines.

### Strengths
1. **Hierarchical multi-level contrastive framework**: The idea of simultaneously contrasting at node, subgraph, and graph levels is interesting and offers a systematic way to capture structural information at multiple scales. Sections 3.7–3.10 provide a coherent organization of these contrasts.
2. **Clear presentation and effective visual aids**: The paper is structured in an easy-to-follow manner. Figure 1 effectively illustrates the full pipeline from augmentation through hierarchical contrastive learning. Section 3 provides a comprehensive framework overview.
3. **Well-structured experimental evaluation**: The experiments include ablation studies that validate each component’s contribution, and the results—though modest—show consistent improvements over the four included baselines (GRACE, MVGRL, DGI, GMI). The method is shown to be effective in low-label regimes.
4. **Technical soundness (per some reviewers)**: The proposed method is technically sound, and the introduction of common graph augmentation methods is clear. The approach is grounded in existing self-supervised techniques.

*Contrast note*: One reviewer (Reviewer 3, rating 1) found the method technically sound but considered the novelty so lacking that the contribution is negligible, while other reviewers also criticized novelty but gave higher scores.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and straightforward combination of existing ideas**: The method simply combines existing loss functions (node-level, subgraph-level, graph-level) and standard augmentations without novel architectural innovations or adaptive mechanisms. The “adaptive” augmentation is not substantiated; it merely applies known augmentations (feature masking, edge perturbation, subgraph sampling). No meaningful interactions among the three levels are explored. As one reviewer noted, designing contrastive learning at node, subgraph, and graph levels is very common and well-established in graph self-supervised learning; the paper provides no further theoretical or experimental insights beyond existing methods. The paper lacks a clear rationale for why this particular three-level design is necessary or advantageous.
2. **Outdated and insufficient baselines**: The comparison includes only four baselines (DGI, GMI, GRACE, MVGRL), the most recent of which is from 2020. This fails to demonstrate effectiveness against more current methods. Multiple reviewers explicitly recommend adding comparisons with recent works: GraphMAE2 (WWW2023), S2GAE (WSDM2023), GiGaMAE (CIKM2023), AUG-MAE (AAAI2024), and others. The paper also omits existing works on hierarchical contrastive learning on graphs (e.g., Zhang et al. “Multi-Scale Self-Supervised Graph Contrastive Learning With Injective Node Augmentation,” IEEE TKDE 2023; Yan et al. “Hierarchical Graph Contrastive Learning,” ECML-PKDD 2023). The authors should discuss these and clearly differentiate their approach.
3. **Lack of theoretical foundation and weak motivation**: The connection between the paper’s motivation and the proposed method is weak. No rigorous analysis is provided for why combining node, subgraph, and graph contrasts works better than each individually. The rationale behind augmentation choices and the overall design appears arbitrary rather than well-motivated. Given the extensive existing research in graph self-supervised learning, the paper does not identify what specific unresolved issues it addresses.
4. **Missing evaluations on modern, larger-scale benchmarks**: All experiments are on Cora, Citeseer, PubMed, and Reddit—small or medium-sized datasets. Missing evaluations on more challenging and widely used benchmarks such as Amazon datasets (e.g., Amazon Computers, Amazon Photo) limits the generality of the claims.
5. **Overstated claims**: Statements such as “opens new avenues” are unsupported by the methodological novelty or the empirical evidence. The impact of the work is overstated relative to its actual contributions.
6. **Insufficient ablation and parameter analysis**: The paper does not adequately explore sensitivity to the loss weights (α, β, γ) in the composite loss function. No ablation on different pooling strategies for varying subgraph sizes is provided. These analyses are needed to validate the robustness of the framework.
7. **Related work organization and presentation issues**: Section 2.2 is titled “Deep Graph Infomax” but discusses various other methods, making the organization confusing. The overall presentation could be improved; one reviewer rated presentation as 2 (below average).
8. **Direct reviewer questions (overlapping with above weaknesses)**:
   - How does the proposed “adaptive” graph augmentation strategy differ from conventional augmentation techniques? Please elaborate on the adaptive mechanism.
   - What is the theoretical justification for combining node, subgraph, and graph-level contrasts? Is there any analysis showing why these three levels work better together than individually?
   - For the subgraph-level contrast, how are varying subgraph sizes handled? Are there specific pooling strategies that work better than others?
   - How sensitive is the performance to the loss weights (α, β, γ)? Please provide detailed ablation studies on these parameters.
   - Given that contrastive learning at node, subgraph, and graph levels is already well-established, what specific unresolved issues does this paper address? What is the precise research motivation?
