## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes In-Context Preference Learning (ICPL), a method that uses an LLM to iteratively generate and refine reward functions for RL from human preferences. The LLM synthesizes reward code from environment context and task description, policies are trained, and human rankings of video trajectories update the LLM prompt. The authors claim ICPL is orders of magnitude more efficient than RLHF and comparable to methods using ground-truth rewards. Experiments include synthetic preferences and a real human-in-the-loop study. Reviewers found the idea compelling and the human study valuable, but raised major concerns about ICPL’s advantage over the baseline Eureka, the necessity of human involvement, limited human study size, unclear efficiency claims relative to RLHF, and missing comparisons/ablations. Reviewer 2 (score 8) was significantly more positive than the others (scores 5,5,6).

### Strengths
- **Interpretable reward design (R4, supported by R1,R2,R3)**: ICPL replaces the implicit reward model in RLHF with an explicit reward function in code, enhancing interpretability and capacity. The iterative LLM update with human preferences is novel and compelling.
- **Strong baseline optimization (R1)**: Authors took care to make comparisons fair, e.g., generating candidate reward functions for Eureka until 6 executable ones were obtained.
- **Promising human study (R1,R2,R3)**: Testing with real human feedback (6 participants) is appreciated. It demonstrates that the method works outside synthetic settings and is a step beyond assuming it will work.
- **Efficiency in human queries (R3)**: ICPL substantially reduces the number of human preference queries needed, which is a sorely needed property for human-in-the-loop methods.
- **Clear presentation (R2,R3)**: The paper is well written, ideas presented clearly, motivation clear and compelling.
- **Direct preference vs text feedback (R2 related work)**: ICPL requires only pairwise rankings, whereas Eureka is described as needing text feedback; this is a potential practical advantage.

### Weaknesses
- **No clear advantage over Eureka (R1,R2,R3,R4)**: In synthetic experiments (Table 1), ICPL does not outperform Eureka. In the human study (Table 3), ICPL with real human preferences only exceeds Eureka on the Ant task; otherwise performance is similar. Eureka does not require human feedback (beyond possibly text), so ICPL’s benefit relies on the ease of obtaining pairwise preferences over ground-truth sparse rewards, which the reviewers found unconvincing. R2 notes that Section 5.2 indicates Eureka uses sparse rewards to select the best candidate – an additional assumption – but no human-study comparison with Eureka is provided. R4 argues that for challenging tasks, true human feedback did not outperform proxy human feedback, undermining the need for humans.
- **Insufficient evidence for alignment benefit (R1,R3,R4)**: The claim that human preferences better capture nuanced behavior (e.g., HumanoidJump “jump like a real human”) rests on a single case study. A more thorough analysis comparing human preference data to sparse reward functions is needed, especially for more complex tasks.
- **Unclear RLHF comparison and noisy preference handling (R2,R3)**: The paper states ICPL surpasses RLHF in efficiency, but RLHF is never directly compared in experiments (synthetic or human). The synthetic experiments use completely noiseless preferences, while standard practice is ~10% noise; the effect of noise is not studied. R2 asks if a direct RLHF comparison could be done in the synthetic setting.
- **Iteration cost and monotonic improvement (R2)**: Training new policies every iteration makes the loop slow. The choice of 5 iterations may be practical, but improvements beyond that are unverified. In the human study, this could mean long waits between feedback rounds; this limitation should be discussed.
- **Small human study (R3)**: Only 6 participants, no demographic data, and limited generalizability. While better than only using authors, the sample size is low.
- **Incomplete baselines and ablations (R3,R4)**: 
  - B-Pref is used but only the PPO version; the SAC version (PEBBLE) is omitted. Other relevant baselines (Preference Transformer, SURF, VARIQuery, PREDILECT) are not considered. Justification for baseline selection is missing.
  - Table 2 shows counter-intuitive results: removing prompt components individually (w/o RT, w/o RTD, w/o RTDB) often improves performance (w/o RT wins 2 tasks, w/o RTD wins 3, w/o RTDB wins 4), but adding all back (ICPL) wins all tasks. This requires explanation (R4).
  - No comparison with Eureka in the human study (R2).
- **Unclear evaluation metric (R1)**: Section 5.3.2 on RTS is confusing: “environment instance” is not defined, and it is unclear which set of task metric values is used to compute the maximum for RTS.
- **Concerns about generalizability of preference-based LLM reward generation (R1,R3)**: The success of preference-based training in other domains may not generalize to LLM-generated reward functions. The method’s viability on truly complex tasks that require human feedback is questioned.
- **Minor methodological and clarity points**:
  - How are the 6 candidate reward functions generated (random sampling from LLM?) (R1).
  - Why GPT-4o only for the human experiment? (R2).
  - Same abbreviation introduced multiple times; the related work discussion misses the PREDILECT paper (Holk et al. 2024) (R3).
  - Why use pairwise feedback (good vs bad example) when RTS is available? Could all reward functions with RTS be used directly as prompt? (R4).
  - Paper length could be trimmed for readability (R3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 2 (score 8) was more positive than the others; after the author response, their main concern about the motivation over Eureka was resolved, and they increased their rating. This minority view contrasts with the more critical assessments from Reviewers 1 and 4 (score 5) and Reviewer 3 (score 6).
