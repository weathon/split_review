## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces EVPA, an online pruning and abstraction method for CFR in two-player zero-sum imperfect-information games. It estimates the expected value of information sets via a collection of neural networks trained offline, then uses these estimates to: (1) prune sub-optimal actions before CFR solving via a minimax-like criterion, and (2) merge structurally-compatible information sets with similar expected values using k-means++ for online abstraction. Experiments on Heads-Up No-Limit Texas Hold'em (HUNL) show EVPA defeats DeepStack's replication and Slumbot with significant win-rate margins while requiring only 1%–2% of DeepStack's solving time. Training takes 74 days vs. DeepStack's 569 days (Appendix Table 4). Pruning achieves a crop rate of about 75%, enabling decisions in less than one second. The method is intuitive and easy to implement.

### Strengths
- **Simple and general idea**: The pruning and abstraction approach is conceptually straightforward and can potentially be incorporated into many imperfect-information game algorithms beyond CFR.
- **Strong empirical performance**: EVPA outperforms DeepStack's replication and Slumbot in multiple settings with large win-rate margins, while gaining an order-of-magnitude speedup in both training and online solving time.
- **High pruning efficiency**: About 75% of the game tree is pruned, allowing decisions in under one second.
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well-structured and the method is clearly explained.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical guarantees**: No bounds are established relating the choice of delta or expected‑value estimation errors to solution quality. Pruning and abstraction may lose the convergence guarantees of DeepStack; it is unclear whether catastrophic performance degradation can occur in some situations. (Reviewer 1 suggests deriving such bounds should be feasible; Reviewer 3 asks about catastrophic effects.)
- **Technical soundness concerns**:
  - **Min‑max pruning in IIGs**: One reviewer (score 3) argues that min‑max pruning is incorrect in imperfect‑information games unless the game is transformed into a public‑belief tree. The paper uses information‑set value functions, which ignore that the opponent can change reach probabilities by actions prior to the current information set. The reviewer notes that pruning on public‑belief trees would be much more computationally expensive. (This is a disagreement: Reviewer 1 finds the approach sound and easy to implement, while Reviewer 2 questions its correctness.)
  - **Expected value estimation comparison**: The method for estimating information‑set expected values is not compared with alternatives used in DeepStack (which evaluates at the end of each round) or Student of Games (which estimates values recursively during CFR iterations). DeepStack generates subgames by random sampling, which may not cover the most essential ones; the paper does not address whether EVPA’s training sample biases affect outcomes.
- **Data collection cost**: The approach requires evaluating all information sets, collecting “at least 10 billion samples” (Line 302–303) for training expected‑value networks. This is orders of magnitude more than the millions of samples needed by DeepStack.
- **Marginal gains from abstraction**: Performance gains from information‑set abstraction are marginal, and exploitability does not decrease in later iterations. One reviewer notes this suggests the abstraction may not be effective.
- **Missing experimental comparisons**: The paper does not compare with other pruning or abstraction methods, such as nested subgame solving (Brown & Sandholm ‘17), or with related online abstraction techniques. Ablation studies on design choices (number of neural networks, training set size, delta parameter) are absent. (Reviewer 1 suggests spending up to 7× the training budget for such evaluations.)
- **Misleading claim about overhead reduction**: The claim that abstraction reduces “computational overhead … from months to less than 1 second” is inaccurate because the expected‑value estimation itself requires 74 days of training. It would be more correct to say that given precomputed EV estimates, online abstraction takes less than one second.
- **Limited domain impact**: The application to HUNL has already seen super‑human performance; the contribution would be stronger if applied to new, more challenging domains with higher branching factors (e.g., Omaha, or games with larger information sets). The paper does not discuss how the method generalizes beyond poker.
- **Methodological details missing or unclear**:
  - The feature representation of an information set that is input to the neural networks is not fully described; it is unclear whether public belief states are included.
  - The definition of a subgame $S$ is ambiguous: it is described as a set of histories (Line 227), an information set $I_p$ in $S$ (Line 285), and a public state $s(I_p')$ in $S$ (Line 368).
  - The condition in Lines 387–388 (condition 2) and the need for the significance function in Lines 421–422 (which decreases with pot size) require clarification.
  - The quality of the DeepStack replication used as baseline is not assessed (e.g., against local best response).
  - It is unclear how EVPA handles any initial chip count given its reliance on DeepStack’s assumptions.
- **Experimental rigor concerns**:
  - Confidence intervals are not reported for Table 1 and Figure 3.
  - The number of matches played for heads‑up results and whether variance reduction (e.g., AIVAT) was used is not stated.
  - Results for longer solving times (e.g., 2 or 20 seconds) are not provided, making it unclear how performance scales with time budget.
  - Code has not been released, hindering reproducibility and further research.
- **Text repetitiveness**: The paper could be shortened; more discussion of prior abstraction literature and how EVPA differs would be beneficial.
