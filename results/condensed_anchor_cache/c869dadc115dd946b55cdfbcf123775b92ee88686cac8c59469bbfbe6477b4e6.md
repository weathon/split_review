## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ForecastBench, a dynamic benchmark of 1,000 forecasting questions about future events that are automatically generated and regularly updated to avoid data leakage. The authors evaluate 17 state-of-the-art LLMs (using six methodologies: zero-shot, scratchpad, news-augmented retrieval, etc.) on a random subset of 200 questions and compare against expert human forecasters (superforecasters) and the general public. The key finding is that expert forecasters significantly outperform the top LLM (p < 0.01), while LLMs roughly match the median of average human forecasters. Reviewers are split: some praise the well-motivated benchmark and sophisticated pipeline, while others criticize the superficial technical detail, limited experimental analysis, and lack of design justification.

### Strengths
- Addresses a key problem in prior benchmarks – staleness and data leakage – by using dynamic, future-only questions that avoid relying on LLM knowledge cutoffs (R2).
- The approach to building and generating the question set and the entire pipeline is thoroughly thought out, with daily release of 1,000 queries that mitigates leaderboard gaming and generates an expanding resolved-prediction dataset (R2, R3).
- Sophisticated evaluation framework that incorporates: selection from high-liquidity market sources, temporal sampling, granular analysis across multiple time horizons (week to decade), and novel combination questions that assess higher-complexity reasoning (R3).
- Comprehensive LLM assessment with six distinct evaluation methodologies (zero-shot, scratchpad, news-augmented generation, etc.) (R3).
- Forecasting problem is interesting and practical (e.g., presidential elections), making it a good benchmark for evaluating LLM capabilities in real-world scenarios (R4, R5).
- Benchmark design explicitly considers data leakage, improving reliability (R4).
- Interesting core finding: LLMs still lag behind expert human forecasters (p < 0.01) but perform similarly to average forecasters (R2, R5).
- Code is provided (R4). Use of real human experiments (expert and non-expert) is a big strength (R5).
- Multiple reviewers found the paper well-written, well-motivated, and polished (R2, R6; but see disagreement below).
- Human-annotated data generation methodology can be adapted for updating evaluation sets over time (R6).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical depth and experimentation:** Reads like a tech report or class assignment; the level of technical detail is superficial and there is little exploration of what we learn about LLMs from the results (R1). The experiment section is only one page with minimal interpretation, making it unclear if the benchmark is useful beyond this single experiment (R5). Given small dataset sizes, it would be interesting to explore exactly what LLMs do and don’t get right (R1).
- **Missing justification for dynamic benchmark claim:** Despite being a key selling point, no analysis is provided that shows how model performance changes over time or compares static vs. dynamic versions of the benchmark (R5).
- **Unclear details in preliminaries (Section 2):** The nature of forecasts is not specified (open-ended / natural language? possible outcomes enumerated in advance? char limit?) (R1). The Brier score explanation is unclear – if forecasters may assign probabilities, how is f assigned when they don’t? How is a 0/1 outcome assigned to questions like “Who will be the next US president?” (R1). The notation f is confusing (R1). “Strictly proper” scoring and why it incentivizes truthfulness is not explained (R1). The three prompting/RAG methods are not explained (R1). “Prompting” for human forecasters is not explained (R1).
- **Design decisions not justified:** Method development is described without explaining design alternatives or their impact (R5). The paper lacks discussion of question selection criteria, source diversity, and how choices affect question difficulty or coverage (R5). Section 3.2 seems to contradict the preliminaries (R1).
- **Non-essential figures take space:** The flowchart (Fig 2) and Fig 3 do not contribute substantively and could be moved to appendix to make room for technical details (R1).
- **Table 2 is dense and hard to parse; the paper would benefit from more visual plots to convey results (R2).**
- **Presentation is hard to follow for some:** Details are sprinkled throughout sections, leaving the reader to hunt for them (R5). (Minority disagreement: R2 and R6 found the paper well-written.)
- **Evaluation of unresolved questions relies on crowd predictions,** complicating future assessments of whether models outperform the crowd (R3).
- **Limited expert predictions:** Only 39 experts contributed, with an average of 8 predictions per question, which may affect robustness and generalizability (R3).
- **Subjectivity and bias not addressed:** Forecasting is highly subjective and may vary by demographics, education, and domain. No openly accessible user-study report is provided that highlights biases or how they are handled for both human forecasters and “superforecasters” (R6). Demographics of human forecasters should be reported (R6).
- **Coverage and question quality:** How do the 1000 questions cover an open-world set, or are they targeted to only a few domains? (R6). Example “Is 2024 going to be hotter than 2023?” – what parameter is compared (mean temperature?)? Can LLMs infer such undefined concepts? (R6). How is the quality of questions determined? (R3).
- **Reproducibility and validation:** Methodology relies on online links and APIs, limiting coverage and reproducibility; validation is difficult (R6). No stress test is performed on the methodology for real-world use (R6).
- **Justification for adopted forecasting methods with LLMs:** The chosen prompting/reasoning methods (e.g., RAG) can greatly influence final performance, and this is not justified or explored in depth (R4). It is worth investigating whether allowing LLMs to use tools in a feedback loop would help (R4). No exploration of cheap improvements beyond RAG (e.g., finetuning open-source models) (R2).
- **Unexplored analysis:** Impact of prompt date on model predictions – do questions asked far in advance introduce greater uncertainty? (R3). How do LLM and human questions differ (information availability, etc.)? (R5). What is “News with freeze values”? (R5). Could evaluating logits of answer tokens provide calibration insights? (R3). Why do LLMs perform better on average for the questions sampled for humans? (R5).
