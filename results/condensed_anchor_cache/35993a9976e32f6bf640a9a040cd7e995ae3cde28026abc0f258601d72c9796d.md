## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents an empirical study of model merging methods for compositional generalization across three tasks: image classification (DomainNet, CLIP, full finetuning), image generation (DomainNet, StableDiffusion, low-rank finetuning), and NLP (assorted languages, T5, full finetuning). It compares eight merging methods on in-domain and held-out task performance, computational cost, hyperparameter sensitivity, and scaling with number of constituent models. The goal is to provide a systematic evaluation and clarify practical requirements.

### Strengths
- Timely and relevant problem: model merging is both scientifically interesting and practically used; a thorough empirical evaluation is very helpful.
- Focus on compositional generalization is an important contribution; prior work had not systematically evaluated this use case. The task constructions are nicely controlled and systematic for both vision and language.
- Holistic approach: considers not just accuracy but also compute/data access requirements (Figure 3 insightful), hyperparameter sensitivity (Figure 4), and scaling to many models (Figure 5). The scaling and hyperparameter experiments are well done.
- Related works and summary of methods are excellent; overall writing is clear.
- Baselines comparison methods (e.g., training on supervised data for the target held-out task) are very sensible.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of generalizable conclusions across settings**: The main comparison (Section 4.1) shows no clear trends in method dominance that generalize across the three task settings. While the authors attribute differences to modality or “cross-domain” vs. “cross-lingual” generalization (lines 71, 310, 484, 487), the experimental design confounds model architecture, data modality/task, and finetuning strategy. For example, image classification uses full finetuning of CLIP, image generation uses low-rank finetuning of StableDiffusion, and NLP uses full finetuning of T5. It is impossible to tell whether the different patterns in Figures 2–4 are due to modality, architecture, or finetuning approach. This significantly reduces the practical value: we still do not know which method is best given a specific user’s task characteristics.
- **Missing architectural diversity**: Each task setting uses only one model backbone. The paper would be stronger if it benchmarked multiple architectures per task (e.g., Transformers and CNNs on vision, Transformers and SSMs on language) to test whether core results generalize across backbones.
- **Missing baseline**: Evaluations of held-out task generalization should include a single fine-tuned model on a related task (e.g., a model fine-tuned on English QA evaluated on Arabic QA) to compare merging methods against simply fine-tuning on a closely related task. If this baseline always underperforms the pretrained model, it could be omitted, but it should be tested.
- **Lack of error bars for finetuning randomness**: Only one checkpoint per finetuning task was used (Appendix B). Since merged performance likely depends on the specific weights arrived at after finetuning, the results may not generalize across optimization randomness. Finetuning multiple replicates per task and reporting merged performance over random choices of replicates is necessary for rigor.
- **Unclear messages and limited surprising insights**: The paper does not deliver a clear verdict on whether model merging is a promising approach for multitasking or how it compares to multitask learning under which conditions. As a conference-level study, it lacks surprising conclusions, theoretical motivations, or well-justified insights; it reads more like a workshop report or survey. One reviewer noted the value will depend on whether the code base becomes an easy-to-use public benchmark.
- **Missing analyses**: 
    - The claim about Fisher Merging generalizing better than RegMean and MaTS (lines 301–303) appears to hold only for NLP tasks, not for image classification or generation. This statement needs to be amended.
    - The reported correlations and anti-correlations between held-in and held-out performance (lines 306–319) are based on few methods and clustered points; numeric correlations and their statistical significance should be reported.
    - The paper mentions analyzing how results depend on model size (line 275), but this analysis does not appear in the main text.
    - No evaluation of how merging performance scales with the “degree” of difference among constituent models (e.g., finetuning for different numbers of steps).
- **Typos and grammar issues**: Several errors throughout (e.g., lines 54, 55, 96, 193–195 missing words, singular/plural mismatches). Needs careful proofreading.

### Notable Reviewer Contrast
One reviewer found the paper had good scientific standards (clear research question, sensible controls, methodical analyses) but noted the findings were of limited practical use because no method was clearly superior and results were very task-dependent. Another reviewer expressed major concerns about confounded experimental design and stated that the current results seem too specific to the evaluated settings to draw well-justified insights, and that the remaining contributions (hyperparameter sensitivity, compute, scaling) are not substantive enough for a full conference submission. A third reviewer also found the paper lacking surprising conclusions and felt it leaned toward a workshop or survey-level contribution.
