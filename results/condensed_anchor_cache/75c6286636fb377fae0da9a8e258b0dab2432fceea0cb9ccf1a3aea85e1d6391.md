## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Automated Design of Agentic Systems (ADAS) and demonstrates Meta Agent Search, where a meta-agent iteratively programs new agents in code, starting from an archive of previous designs. Experiments across coding, science, and math claim new agents outperform hand-designed baselines and transfer across domains/models. Reviewers 1 (score 10) and 2 (8) are positive, praising the research area framing, robust results, and potential impact. Reviewers 3 (3) and 4 (3) are negative, citing unclear methodology, limited comparisons, safety concerns, and insufficient technical maturity.

### Strengths

- The work is well motivated, timely, and relevant (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The idea of framing agent design as code generation by a meta-agent is creative and novel; ADAS is introduced as a new research area (Reviewer 2).
- Experimental results are somewhat strong and robust, showing consistent improvements over hand-designed agents across multiple domains (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The demonstrated transferability of discovered agents across different domains and foundation models is interesting and suggests generality (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The paper is decently well presented and reads like a paper expected at ICLR (Reviewer 1).
- The work could naturally integrate with LLM-as-a-Judge or Agent-as-a-Judge approaches (Reviewer 1).
- It follows the contemporary trend of improving inference after LLM training, making it of interest to the community (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns**: It is unclear how this differs fundamentally from prior code-generating systems like Voyager, which also incrementally build tools; it may be just “a new paint job” (Reviewer 1).
- **Method description is too high-level and unclear**: The flowchart of the system (more detailed than Figure 1) is missing; it is unclear exactly what code is generated and what building blocks are given as prior. The expanded related works should have been moved to the appendix to make room (Reviewers 1, 4). From the main text it is not clear if the system is just a trivial loop writing a basic program (Reviewer 1).
- **Safety analysis is insufficient**: The paper only briefly mentions safety in Section 6, focusing on AGI danger, but does not address dangers of self-replication or unsafe behaviors that could emerge from automatically generated agents. Every building block should have been designed with safety considerations and clear recommendations (Reviewers 2, 4). No mechanisms are described to detect or mitigate biases or unsafe behaviors (Reviewer 2).
- **Experimental comparisons are limited**: The method is only compared with handcrafted baselines; more comparison with other optimization methods is expected (Reviewer 3). Baselines cited as “state-of-the-art” (COT, COT-SC, Self-Refine, LLM-Debate, Quality-Diversity) are not justified as SOTA for each task; e.g., the best handcrafted solution for ARC achieves >20% accuracy (Ferré 2021). The scope of baselines should be specified (Reviewer 3). No comparison with vanilla GPT-o1 is provided (Reviewer 1, considers unfair but asked). Computational cost of Meta Agent Search is not compared to simpler approaches like Chain-of-Thought in Table 1, raising concerns about limited applicability given the need for large compute (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablation and initialization fairness**: The optimization starts from a manually designed baseline solution; results starting without any such initial solution should be shown for a fair assessment (Reviewer 3).
- **Method implementation details vague**: The meta agent implementation is described as a single LLM query with a handcrafted template (Appendix C); this should be clearer (Reviewer 3). The text does not state that the meta optimizer implementation differs per task, but it should (Reviewer 3).
- **Turing Completeness claim is weak**: The statement “programming languages are Turing Complete” is not universally true (some are not), and the authors themselves acknowledge this on line 106. Moreover, the actual Turing Completeness of the system depends on whether the LLM acts as a Turing Complete system in practice, which is questionable (Reviewers 1, 3). Most things in AI are Turing Complete in some sense, so the claim is not a strong contribution (Reviewer 1).
- **Statistical reporting issues**: Results with overlapping error bars should all be bolded if significance is claimed; statistically the differences likely would not pass a t-test (Reviewer 1). Motivation for reporting median accuracy instead of mean should be explained; mean should also be shown (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing related work and citations**: The paper should cite “The Bitter Lesson” alongside Clune (Reviewer 1). Missing citations for self-referential higher-order meta-learning, e.g., Schmidhuber 1987 and follow-ups (Reviewer 3).
- **High-level definition of “agent”**: The claim that “community has not reached a consensus on definitions or terminologies of agents” is odd; textbooks like AI: A Modern Approach already provide clear definitions. The paper seems overly focused on the LLM subcommunity and disregards prior work (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor presentation issues**: The research question (lines 88-89) could be refined (Reviewer 1). The legend in Figure 2 “E.g. LLM defines agents using code” is unclear (Reviewer 3). The reference to “the framework” on line 265 is ambiguous (Reviewer 3). The OpenAI logo should not be used in Figure 2 unless the authors are part of OpenAI (Reviewer 4).
- **Ethical reflection missing**: The abstract claims the work illustrates potential to benefit humanity, but the paper does not explain how; such a statement should be substantiated (Reviewer 3). More broadly, pushing for general “agent generation procedures” could be seen as a form of self-replication, and the authors should reflect on whether this direction is advisable (Reviewer 4).
- **Overall technical maturity**: The work is not very technically mature; descriptions are simple, high-level, and experimentation is limited. It is more suited for a workshop than ICLR (Reviewer 4).
