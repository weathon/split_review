## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies gradient descent instabilities (edge of stability) during neural network training with large learning rates. It proposes that instabilities cause the eigenvectors of the Hessian to rotate, moving parameters toward flatter regions (“progressive flattening”), and that this improves generalization. Theoretical analysis uses a diagonal linear network (DLN) toy model; experiments use MLPs and VGG on fMNIST and CIFAR-10. Multiple reviewers find the eigenvector rotation observation novel but largely agree that the paper fails to establish a causal role for rotation, overlaps substantially with prior work, provides unclear definitions, and suffers from insufficient experimental support.

### Strengths
- The analysis of Hessian eigenvector rotation during instabilities is new and interesting (R1, R2, R4, R5). R1 notes it opposes the claim in Damian et al. (2023) about gradient–sharpness alignment; R4 says the proposal has not appeared before; R2 states it is new to their knowledge.
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (R2, R5). R5 adds that the theoretical parts are didactic and the related work section is well-written.
- Loss landscape visualizations (Figure 3, Figure 12) are a nice way to illustrate the sharpening/flattening process (R3).
- Theoretical derivations in Section 3 appear sound (R4, R5); R5 finds them partially rigorous.
- Section 4 provides rigorous experiments that attempt to disentangle which factor of instabilities (learning rate, eigenvector rotation, sharpness) impacts generalization most, with realistic experiments in Section 4.4 (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Causal role of eigenvector rotation not established.** R3, R4, and R1 all note that the observed rotation is correlational, not causal. R4 states that return to stability has already been explained by self-stabilization (Damian et al. 2023) or the catapult effect (Lewkowycz et al. 2020); eigenvector rotation may itself be caused by instability. R3: Figure 2 only shows correlation, not that rotation is relevant for stabilization or flattening. R1: experiments are insufficient to back the claim about reorienting eigenvectors; the theorem for the diagonal model does not explain why reorientation helps. R4: the justification in Section 3.3 is insufficient; an alternate hypothesis is that sharpness is constrained to 2/η and progressive sharpening is weaker later when gradients are smaller, leading to lower final sharpness.
- **Mischaracterization of prior theoretical work.** R3 and R4 point out that the paper mischaracterizes the progressive sharpening factor in Damian et al. (2023). Assumption 1 in that work is on a “constrained trajectory,” not the actual gradient descent trajectory; the self-stabilization mechanism does not require progressive sharpening. R4: “progressive flattening” appears to be explained by the self-stabilization mechanism in Damian et al. (2023).
- **Definition of “progressive flattening” unclear.** R3: the term is not precisely defined; using cross entropy acts as a strong confounder because sharpness converges to zero at the end of training. R3 suggests repeating experiments with MSE loss and training all models to near-zero loss. R4: the phenomenon is not quantified and is hard to observe in the experiments.
- **Substantial overlap with prior work; novelty overstated.** R2, R3, R4 list many prior works that already found that instabilities move training toward flatter regions (R2 cites [1,4,5,6,7]), that sharpness is not a great predictor of generalization (R2 cites [8]; R4 agrees), and that large learning rate effects occur without progressive sharpening (R2 cites [1,3,4,5,6]). R2 notes that similar results to Figure 5(b) (continuous rather than sharp transition) are already shown in prior works. R2 and R3 request a proper literature review; R3 lists missing papers that study EOS on diagonal linear networks (e.g., Chen & Bruna 2023; Even et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2023) and scalar networks (Kreisler et al. 2023).
- **Toy model limitations.** R2: the two-parameter model (Section 3.1) is extensively analyzed in prior works [1,2,3]; when z is quadratic and the global minimum is at θ₁θ₂=0, the model does not exhibit progressive sharpening and does not capture real-world behavior well. R5: the DLN model is quite limited; a more realistic model would strengthen the paper. R1 asks whether the theoretical claim about the DLN holds for any matrix dimension and depth.
- **Depth > 1 claim not fully justified.** R2: the theorem is demonstrated only for deep diagonal networks, not any network with depth > 1 as claimed. R1: the relation between parameter growth and progressive flattening/reorientation is unclear.
- **Experimental concerns.** R2: in Figure 5(b), the transition is continuous, not sharp; “phase transition” is inappropriate. R4: in Figure 2, after instability is resolved, eigenvector similarity is close to 1, contradicting the claim on line 244. R2 suggests training models long enough to reach the new EoS threshold after learning rate decay before comparing. R5: in Figure 8, only 5 samples are used for rank correlation; uncertainty should be reported; it is unclear why sharpness often has high but varying sign correlation with generalization while ρ has consistent but smaller absolute correlation. R4: the quantities and definition of ρ in Figure 7 are unclear. R5: several figures are hard to read (font sizes, 3D plot in Figure 7; the top panel in Figure 8 is confusing). R5: code is not currently available.
- **Presentation and clarity issues.** R1, R4, R5 note unclear notations: line 087 definition of α has two dots; line 092 unclear; Section 3.1 derivations are hard to follow (what are γ_Θ, γ_{|θ₂²−θ₁²|}? Why is ηₑₒₛ defined as twice the asymptote?); R4: descent lemma does not require convexity. R1 and R3 list typos (e.g., line 033 “is dependent” → “depends”; line 054 “in generalization” → “in the generalization”; line 075 “grow without bound” → “grow unboundedly”; line 154 “θs” → “θs”; line 254 “Figure E” → “Figure 12”). R5 notes typos: “Arora et al. (2022) demonstrated that gradient descent can lead to alignment between gradient the sharpest eigenvector”; “where exactly derivations are possible”.
