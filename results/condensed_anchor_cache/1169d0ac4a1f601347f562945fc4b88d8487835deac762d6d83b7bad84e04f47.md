## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes StructTuning, a two-stage methodology for injecting domain knowledge into LLMs: Structure-aware Continual Pre-Training (SCPT) and Structure-aware Supervised Fine-Tuning (SSFT). SCPT extracts a domain knowledge taxonomy from the corpus and trains the LLM to associate text chunks with paths in that taxonomy. SSFT trains the model on question-answer-explanation triplets where explanations leverage knowledge paths. Evaluations on LongBench and MMedBench show that using only 0.3% of the training data (76M tokens) the method achieves 50% of the improvement of the baseline LLM+MMed (which uses 25.5B tokens) and comparable performance to MMedLM2 with 5% training cost. Scaling laws extrapolate that 5% data would achieve 100% performance. While the idea is novel and results are promising, reviewers raise significant concerns about the fairness of comparisons, insufficient validation of the scaling law, unclear experimental setup, and lack of comparisons with other knowledge injection and retrieval methods.

### Strengths
- **Novel, efficient, and intuitive methodology** – The two-stage structure-aware injection (SCPT + SSFT) is inspired by human learning and shows strong data efficiency: 0.3% of the training data yields 50% of the improvement over the baseline. The approach exploits structural domain knowledge (taxonomy as a mindmap) to improve both recall and application.
- **Well-written and detailed** – The paper is easy to follow, with clear descriptions of the method, training stages, and ablation study.
- **Strong main results and ablation** – Experimental results are compelling; the ablation confirms the usefulness of both SCPT and SSFT. The method achieves comparable performance to MMedLM2 on MMedBench at only 5% training cost.
- **Comprehensive evaluation** – Experiments span multiple model architectures (e.g., Llama-2) and scales, use both recall-based (LongBench) and reasoning-based (MMedBench) tasks, and include an ablation study.
- **Significant research problem** – The paper addresses the important challenge of efficiently adapting large LLMs to domain-specific knowledge.

### Weaknesses
1. **Unfair comparison due to different knowledge sources** – The main claim (0.3% data achieving 50% improvement vs. LLM+MMed) uses MedTextBooks as the knowledge source for StructTuning, while MMed uses the larger MMedC corpus. It is unclear whether the benefit comes from the high quality of the selected source rather than the method itself. The authors attempt to simulate more data via scaling laws, but have not validated with the same source at larger sizes (e.g., 5% data) to substantiate the claim of 100% performance.

2. **Scaling law derivation is insufficiently supported** – The scaling law (Equation 3, Figure 7) is fitted to only three or four data points (corpus ratios 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and possibly 1.0). All reviewers agree this is too few points to draw a reliable, generalizable conclusion. The paper would be strengthened by collecting more intermediate data points or presenting the relationship as a preliminary observation rather than a definitive law.

3. **Unclear experimental setup for LongBench** – Numbers are inconsistent: Section A.1 reports 200+200+150+200+200+200 = 1150 entries, but line 781 states 1350 entries. The paper also mentions 10,476 passages (line 349) without clarifying how these relate to the 1350 entries or to the original LongBench dataset. The training/test split is not clearly defined. Such inconsistencies raise concerns about reproducibility and fairness.

4. **Potential data leakage from SSFT generation** – The paper generates synthetic QA pairs and filters those with over 0.5 F1-score similarity to test samples. This threshold may not be sufficient to prevent data leakage. No analysis of the similarity distribution or justification of the threshold is provided. Moreover, results with CPT+SSFT alone are not reported, so it is unclear whether performance gains are merely due to data augmentation (SSFT introducing test-like questions). The similarity metric and filtering method need more detail.

5. **LongBench context window issue** – Average passage lengths in LongBench (3K–18K) exceed the 4K context limit of Llama-2 used in baseline OBQA experiments. This makes the comparison unfair, as the baseline models cannot see the full content. The paper should either use a model capable of longer contexts or acknowledge the limitation.

6. **Inappropriate use of MedTextBooks vs. MMedC** – On MMedBench, the method uses MedTextBooks as the knowledge source instead of MMedC (the dataset used by MMedLM). The reason for not applying SCPT on MMedC is not given. Comparing training costs across different datasets can be misleading because smaller, high-quality datasets may inherently yield better results.

7. **Lack of comparison with other knowledge injection methods** – The paper compares only to vanilla CPT+SFT and MMedLM. Missing comparisons include knowledge distillation tailored for domain adaptation, methods that integrate external knowledge graphs during fine-tuning, and hybrid approaches combining RAG with parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Such comparisons are needed to claim superiority.

8. **Insufficient details and weak comparison for RAG** – The RAG baseline is mentioned but given very limited implementation details: retrieval method (dense or sparse? which embedding model?), knowledge base size and composition, preprocessing, indexing, number of retrieved passages, and reranking strategies are all unspecified. Moreover, RAG consistently underperforms, contradicting existing studies (Ovadia et al., 2023; Soudani et al., 2024), so the setup may be suboptimal.

9. **Limited clarity on knowledge structure extraction** – The extraction process using a specialized 7B model lacks details: training procedure, quantitative metrics (precision/recall against human-annotated structures), and representative examples across domains. An ablation study showing the impact of different hierarchy levels (e.g., removing top-level vs. deeper categories) on downstream tasks would clarify the importance of structural granularity.

10. **Novelty somewhat diminished by prior art** – The structure-aware approach is already common in code training, where models learn hierarchical relationships, which limits the claimed methodological novelty.

11. **Minor typos** – Table A4, column 1 of lines 2 & 3; Page 19, line 999: “self-conatined”.
