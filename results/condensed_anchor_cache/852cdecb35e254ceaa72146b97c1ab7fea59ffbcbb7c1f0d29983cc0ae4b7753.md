## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PowerGPT, a transformer-based foundation model for electricity time series (ETS) data, pre-trained on a large dataset from a Chinese province. It uses a temporal encoder to capture dependencies and a hierarchical encoder to model correlations across aggregation levels, with a self-supervised pre-training framework combining masked ETS modeling and dual-view contrastive learning. Evaluations on forecasting, missing value imputation, and anomaly detection are reported on the same State Grid dataset. Reviewers recognize the interesting motivation and strong in-domain performance but raise major concerns about the lack of public benchmark evaluations, missing transfer experiments, insufficient literature review, unclear technical details, low novelty, and incomplete presentation.

### Strengths
1. **Valuable application of foundation models to energy systems.** The idea of a large-scale, off-the-shelf model for power systems is important and the access to a rare large ETS dataset provides a useful starting point (R1, R3, R4). Reviewer 1 notes it could be a powerful assistant tool for dispatchers, though questions its use for forecasting and anomaly detection.
2. **Strong empirical results on the proprietary State Grid dataset.** The model achieves state-of-the-art performance on forecasting (across multiple horizons), missing value imputation, and anomaly detection, outperforming other time-series models (R2, R3). Reviewer 2 specifically highlights significant improvements over SOTA baselines and notes the model also excels at imputation and anomaly detection. Reviewer 3 agrees that the results validate the architecture.
3. **Sound architectural choices.** The combination of temporal patching, random masking, and hierarchical GNNs is technically sensible (R3). Reviewer 2 also appreciates the inclusion of hierarchical relations, but notes the fusion process is not clearly explained (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of evaluation on public/established benchmarks and transfer experiments.** The model is tested only on the author-introduced State Grid dataset, which has not been peer‑reviewed. There are no experiments on open datasets (e.g., Pecan Street, BuildingsBench) or on data from different regions/countries, making the claim of a “foundation model” weak (R1, R2, R3, R4). Reviewer 2 explicitly states this absence “severely limits the generalizability of the model”. Reviewer 1 asks: *Can you demonstrate performance on an open-source dataset such as Pecan Street?* R3 recommends the BuildingsBench benchmark. R4 notes no results on any literature dataset.
2. **Missing ablation study.** No ablation experiments are provided to isolate contributions of the proposed components (e.g., hierarchical encoder, contrastive learning) (R4).
3. **Insufficient and flawed literature review.** The related work section is missing key recent developments: transformer-based load forecasting and transfer learning for load forecasting (R3); global models (Montero-Manso 2021, Buonanno 2022) (R4); Makridakis competitions (M4, M5, M6) (R4); gradient boosting methods (XGB, LGBM) (R4); recent missing value imputation using autoencoders (Pereira 2020, Buonanno 2023) (R4); and anomaly detection in energy contexts (R4). Additionally, there are multiple referencing errors (R1).
4. **Numerous unclear technical details**:
   – How the hierarchical information is fused: e.g., when predicting at the district level, are past 256 values from city, province, industry, and user levels all used? A detailed explanation of the aggregation is missing (R2, R4).
   – The meaning of the categories “exclusive” and “public” in results tables is not explained; it is unclear whether they correspond to individual users or aggregated groups (R2).
   – The difference between “electricity consumption” (1-day granularity) and “electricity load” (15-min) is not defined; they are often used synonymously (R3, R4).
   – The masking procedure is ambiguous: what does “masked and unmasked patches of different nodes can overlap at temporal axis” mean? What is $\tilde{N}$? How is the “learnable mask token” learned? (R4)
   – Colors in Fig.2(a) are too similar to distinguish nodes (R4).
   – The statement “most methods rely heavily on labeled data at scale” is poorly justified: for forecasting and autoencoder methods, labels are not needed. What downstream tasks require labels? (R4)
5. **Low novelty and insufficient maturity for a top venue.** The paper is considered not sufficiently novel or significant for ICLR: the large model size alone does not constitute a contribution (R3), the completion level of the article is low (R1, R4), and the problem setting is of limited interest because the required granular user-level data is not publicly available, raising ethical concerns (R3). Reviewer 2 (rating 5) is more positive but still notes key weaknesses (transfer, hierarchical clarification).
6. **Missing experiments on individual user forecasts.** No forecasting results are shown for individual user time series, which are usually more variable and interesting (R2). Related questions: why use a history window of only 256 (for 15-min data this is <1 week)? Have longer windows (512, 1024) been tried? (R2) Likewise, can the model adapt to topology changes without re-training? (R1)
7. **Results presentation problems.** Tables 3 and 4 do not convincingly show that PowerGPT is SOTA for the imputation task (R4). The results section should be reorganized (tables 3/4 are discussed after table 5) (R4).
8. **Data/code not open‑source.** The dataset and model are not released (R1, R4). R3 notes possible ethical concerns. R4 asks: *Will the authors plan to release the trained models, code, and dataset?*
9. **Many typos and grammatical errors** (e.g., “empolyed”, “trys”, “we retrieval”, “horicontal”, “trianin”, “constrastive”, “to to”, missing table labels in p6) (R2, R3, R4). The paper needs thorough proofreading.
