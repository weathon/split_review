## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FedLAG, a layer-wise personalized federated learning method that adaptively selects layers for personalization based on gradient conflicts (obtuse angles between client gradients). Layers with conflicts are excluded from global aggregation and kept local. Theoretical analysis and experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 show accuracy improvements over several baselines. Reviewers agree the idea of using gradient conflict for layer disentanglement is novel and addresses an important problem, and that the theoretical analysis is a strength. However, significant concerns are raised about: (1) anomalous MNIST results (ResNet9 accuracy ≤97% and FedAvg performing better under higher heterogeneity) that undermine experimental validity; (2) high communication and computational overhead (full model upload, pairwise gradient comparisons scaling quadratically with clients); (3) sensitivity to hyperparameter ξ and limited <1% gains over SOTA; (4) insufficient explanation of gradient conflict detection and threshold choice; (5) missing comparisons with related layer-personalization baselines [e] and methods decoupling per-parameter knowledge [1]; (6) poor presentation and ambiguous notation. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 6, indicating strong disagreement about overall quality.

### Strengths
1. **Problem importance**: Addresses the critical challenge of layer-wise personalization under data heterogeneity in federated learning. *(All reviewers)*
2. **Innovative idea**: Using gradient conflict (obtuse angles) to determine whether a layer should be personalized is a novel and meaningful approach. *(R1, R3, R4)*
3. **Theoretical analysis**: Provides thorough convergence analysis and mathematical proofs validating the gradient-conflict-based separation. *(R2, R4)*
4. **Experimental coverage**: Experiments span multiple datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) and heterogeneity levels, showing accuracy improvements, especially under high heterogeneity and low user participation. *(R1, R2, R4)*
5. **Simplicity and integrability**: FedLAG is simple and can be easily incorporated into existing pFL methods. *(R2)*
6. **Well-written and clear main claim** (minority positive view by R3; R1 and R4 disagree, noting poor presentation). *(R3)*

### Weaknesses
1. **Anomalous MNIST results (validity concern)**: ResNet9 achieves ≤97% accuracy on MNIST across all methods, with some below 90% (e.g., PerAvg, FedPAC). A simple LeNet-5 on FedAvg already reaches ~99% under non-i.i.d. partitions. Moreover, FedAvg on MNIST-Dir(0.5) gives lower accuracy than on Dir(0.1), which is counterintuitive for a non-personalized method. The use of pFLlib does not explain these discrepancies, and the authors do not justify the low ceiling. This undermines the credibility of experimental comparisons. *(R2)*
2. **High communication overhead**: Unlike typical layer-wise methods that upload only a subset of parameters, FedLAG requires clients to upload full model parameters to the server to compute gradient conflicts, increasing communication burden especially for large models and many clients. *(R1)*
3. **High computational cost**: The gradient conflict score requires pairwise comparisons of gradients across all clients, scaling quadratically with the number of clients. The paper provides no analysis of computational complexity or scalability. *(R1)*
4. **Limited improvement and hyperparameter sensitivity**: Performance gains over SOTA are often <1% (Tables 1 and 7). FedLAG is highly sensitive to hyperparameter ξ (Table 3), requiring careful tuning to outperform baselines. The paper does not provide guidelines or theoretical justification for choosing ξ. *(R3)*
    - *Associated question*: How large of an angle actually affects performance? Is >90° always detrimental? *(R3)*
5. **Insufficient explanation of gradient conflict concept**: The paper lacks concrete examples or visual aids showing how cosine similarity between client gradients is computed per layer and how it translates into a conflict score. The threshold for conflict (angles >90°) is presented without analysis of its impact or justification. *(R4)*
6. **Missing comparisons to related work**: 
    - No comparison with [e], a method that uses learning-based layer selection for personalization in FL. Such a comparison is essential to evaluate the advantage of gradient-conflict-based selection. *(R2)*
    - No comparison with [1] (e.g., FedRod), which decouples personalized and generic knowledge in each parameter. *(R3)*
    - Related works on leveraging gradient conflicts in FL [b][c][d] are not discussed. *(R2)*
7. **Inadequate ablation and analysis**:
    - The paper does not analyze why certain layers are more suitable for personalization (e.g., input vs. output layers) nor provides theoretical or empirical support for layer selection. *(R4)*
    - No ablation on randomly selecting K layers for personalization (instead of conflict-based). *(R3, question)*
    - The claim that “density of conflicting gradients does not increase from input to output” contradicts Figure 2(b), which shows higher conflict in deeper layers. No quantitative analysis of conflict distribution over layers is provided. *(R3, R4)*
    - The 2D toy dataset experiment (Section 3) is insufficient to validate the consistency of gradient conflict distribution across different datasets. *(R4)*
8. **Missing analysis of experimental results differences**: For key results (e.g., CIFAR-10/100 gains in Table 1, Figures 6-7), the paper does not explain why FedLAG performs better or worse on specific datasets or heterogeneity levels. *(R4)*
9. **Presentation and notation issues**:
    - Ambiguous notation: “each round” in Section 2 does not distinguish global communication rounds from local iterations; nested notation in the layer disentanglement section is confusing. *(R4)*
    - Y-axis label missing in Figure 2(a). *(R1)*
    - Textual interpretation of Figure 2(b) may contradict the figure itself. *(R1)*
    - Typographical errors: In Section 7.1.1, α values (0.1,1) inconsistent with Table 2; in Section 6.2, “GBFL” should be “GPFL”. *(R2)*
    - Inaccurate terminology: “extensive fine-tuning” used to describe prior knowledge-based layer separation in references [1][2]; “fine-tuning” is misleading. *(R4)*
10. **Lack of limitations and future directions**: The conclusion does not discuss limitations (e.g., performance on feature-skewed data, NLP/time-series applications) or propose future work on multi-type heterogeneity. *(R4)*

*Note on reviewer disagreement*: R3 finds the paper well-written and clear, while R1 and R4 rate presentation as poor (scores 1-2). R3 and R2 find the experiments convincing (though R2 raises validity concerns), while R1 and R4 call for deeper analysis. R3 reports limited improvement; R2 and R4 note overall effectiveness.
