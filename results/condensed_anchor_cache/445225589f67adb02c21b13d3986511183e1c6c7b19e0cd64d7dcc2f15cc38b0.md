## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies two-layer linear neural networks for multi-output linear regression. The authors propose a regularization term (related to Mallows’ C_p and WAIC) added to the least-squares objective, aiming to enable feature learning. They analyze this estimator in the high-dimensional regime, show it upper-bounds the predictive risk, and demonstrate that under certain conditions it can achieve the Bayes optimal risk and outperform standard ridge regression. Theoretical results are supported by a synthetic experiment.

### Strengths
- The problem of feature learning in neural networks is very important and well motivated (R1, R2).
- The penalty term connects to classic criteria (Mallows’ C_p, WAIC) and their analysis is extended to the high-dimensional regime, which is interesting on its own (R1).
- The proof that the proposed regularization yields an upper bound on the predictive risk and can achieve the lower bound given by the Bayes optimal risk is a strong theoretical contribution (R1, R2, R3). The characterization of a regime where the network outperforms ridge regression and closely matches Bayes-optimal performance is insightful (R3).
- The paper is solid, proofs appear correct, and the presentation is generally clear (R1, R3). The results are sound and fully justified, with a comprehensive case study provided (R4).
- The few synthetic experiments suggest the regularization enables good performance relative to conventional ridge regression (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Baseline comparisons are insufficient.** The ridge baseline uses $\lambda = 1/n$, which is not optimally tuned. It would be fairer to compare against ridge regression with optimally tuned (e.g., cross-validated) regularization, or against a generalized ridge $\hat\beta_\Omega = \min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \|\Omega\beta\|^2$ with $\Omega$ tuned (e.g., Wu & Xu 2020). Setting $\Omega \asymp \Sigma_\beta^{-1}$ can exactly achieve the Bayes optimal in Proposition 1, and the min-norm interpolation version was studied in Sun et al. (2022). What is the advantage of the proposed method over these? (R1, R2, R4)
- **Lack of comparison to other relevant methods.** The paper does not compare to sketched linear regression (Chen et al., 2023) or to the setting of Ba et al. (2022) when gradient steps are $O(\sqrt{n})$. (R1)
- **Arbitrariness of the regularization.** The proposed regularization is introduced as a surrogate loss; it is not shown to be unique or to correspond to the implicit regularization of actual linear neural networks trained by gradient descent. Many other regularizations could also be constructed that yield similar bounds. The paper would be stronger if it demonstrated that the proposed regularization actually emerges from the learning dynamics (e.g., by comparing the $W$ learned by a linear NN without the proposed regularization, via gradient descent, to that obtained by minimizing the proposed loss). (R2).
- **Theoretical results are only qualitative.** Bounds hold only up to constants, so they provide precise quantitative predictions only when the misalignment is strong enough for the bounds to be of vanishing order. This limits the ability to determine exact conditions for outperforming ridge regression in practical scenarios. (R3) One reviewer (Rating 8) found this limitation significant, while another (Rating 5) particularly criticized the lack of practical implications.
- **Many artificial assumptions inside the theorems.** Theorems contain conditions such as “for all $W$ which satisfies $R(W)-\sigma^2 \le\ldots$”, “there exists $k\le n$ such that…”, “the rows of $X W_B^\top U_B$ are independent”, “the condition number of $A_k$…”. These assumptions feel ad hoc and are not adequately justified in terms of practical plausibility. It is unclear how restrictive they are. They should be stated separately before the theorems and their implications clarified (e.g., the existence of $k$ relates to eigenvalue decay of $\Sigma_\beta^{1/2}\Sigma_x\Sigma_\beta^{1/2}$). (R4)
- **Examples and experiments are too restrictive.** Examples 1–4 are very artificial and do not convincingly demonstrate superiority over ridge regression in general settings. The experiment uses a specific synthetic data configuration with $\lambda=1/n$ for ridge; it would be more convincing to vary parameters (e.g., via cross-validation or sensitivity analysis) and to include real-world datasets. (R4) Another reviewer (R2) echoed the need for real data and for testing other optimization algorithms (SGD, Adam) beyond Langevin dynamics.
- **Missing empirical validation of actual feature learning.** The paper does not show that a linear neural network trained by standard gradient descent (without the proposed regularizer) learns features similar to those obtained by minimizing the proposed criterion. Testing on real data would help assess robustness to the assumed generative model. (R2, R4)
- **Presentation issues.** Some sections are confusing: the end of page 4 and beginning of page 5 mix several topics (eigenvalue decay, high-dimensional regime) without clear links, and terms like “kernel regime” are not defined. Notation is overloaded (e.g., $V$ used for both variance and a matrix). Theorem statements are hard to parse due to many constants; simplifying with $o(\cdot), O(\cdot)$ would improve readability. (R2, R4)
- **Assumption 1** applies sub-Gaussianity to the rows of $X\Sigma_x^{-1/2}$ rather than to the rows of $X$ itself; the reason for this choice is not explained. (R2)
- **Theorem 1's assumption** implicitly restricts to parameters that minimize predictive error; this should be clarified. (R4)
- **Questions raised:** Are the sufficient conditions for superior performance also necessary? (R3) What is the justification for using $\lambda=1/n$? (R1, R2, R4) Would the results change if other optimization algorithms (SGD, Adam) were used? (R4) Could the method be competitive with other regularizers that achieve the Bayes lower bound? (R1, R2)
