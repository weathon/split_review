## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper formalizes "Recent Link Classification" (RLC) as a new task on temporal graphs—classifying an existing link between two nodes (e.g., whether a transaction edge is fraudulent). It benchmarks state-of-the-art temporal graph learning (TGL) methods (TGN, GraphMixer) using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), a metric argued to be more robust to class imbalance. The authors propose the Graph Profiler architecture, which includes a profile encoder, message encoder, destination encoder, and readout layer, and explore modifications to message aggregation, readout, and time encoding. Experiments on several datasets show that Graph Profiler outperforms TGN in most settings.

### Strengths
1. **Practical and important task**: RLC is motivated by real-world industrial applications (e.g., fraud detection) and is less studied than future link prediction (FLP) or dynamic node classification (DNC). The problem formulation is coherent and well-reasoned.
2. **Comprehensive investigation of design choices**: The paper systematically explores message aggregation schemas, readout layers, and time encoding strategies, providing useful design principles for practitioners.
3. **Proposed method outperforms baselines**: Graph Profiler achieves better MCC than TGN on most datasets.
4. **Introduction of useful metrics**: The use of MCC (to handle imbalanced data) and edge homophily (to characterize datasets) are valuable additions to the evaluation toolkit.
5. **Source code provided**: The authors release code, aiding reproducibility.
6. **Well-written intuition**: For the most part the paper provides clear intuition for the new task and contrasts it with existing tasks.

### Weaknesses
1. **Technical novelty and clarity are limited**  
   - The method combines existing components (TGN-like aggregation, time encodings, readout) without clear technical advancement. A minority reviewer (score 1) states the contributions are “unclear and not fully verified.”  
   - The reason why Graph Profiler outperforms TGN is not explained, and the role of novel modules (e.g., profile encoder, destination encoder) is ambiguous.  
   - The paper does not formally define “profile” or the transductive/inductive setting for RLC, making the method hard to follow.

2. **Modeling decisions appear ad‑hoc or dataset‑dependent**  
   - Choices between learnable vs. fixed time encoding and when to use the destination encoder vary by dataset. No general guidance is given; this resembles hyper‑parameter tuning rather than principled design.  
   - Performance degrades when time encoding is added to source–destination embeddings on some datasets; the interaction between components is not analyzed.  
   - The significance of TGN modifications (Table 2) is unclear, as they are not directly tied to Graph Profiler.

3. **Insufficient evaluation and weak baselines**  
   - Only two baselines (TGN and GraphMixer) are used; other dynamic graph methods (TGAT, DySAT, EvolveGCN) from the related work are omitted.  
   - GraphMixer results are missing from Table 3 and Fig. 4, weakening the comparison.  
   - The evaluation relies heavily on aggregate MCC; performance across data subsets or under different hyper‑parameters is not explored.  
   - Datasets lack reported statistics (e.g., number of timesteps, number of classes). The metric used in Table 3 is not stated in its caption.  
   - A minority reviewer (score 1) considers the experiments “too simple” and insufficient to validate the claimed contributions.

4. **Motivation and problem framing are underdeveloped**  
   - The relationship between RLC, FLP, and DNC is not fully explained; a toy example would clarify why RLC is necessary beyond existing tasks.  
   - The use of metapaths in the profile encoder is introduced without justification (the paper appears to assume homogeneous graphs, while metapaths are typical for heterogeneous graphs).  
   - The advantages of MCC and edge homophily over conventional metrics (AP, AUC) are not demonstrated with examples or theoretical reasoning.  
   - The paper does not specify whether it adopts a discrete‑time or continuous‑time dynamic graph model.  
   - It is unclear whether graph attributes (node/edge features) are static or dynamic, and whether node features are used at all.

5. **Presentation, notation, and clarity issues**  
   - Several typos: p.2 “divinding categorizing”, p.4 “acting that acts”, “edges$\mathcal{E}$”, “connect a vertex … acting that acts”.  
   - Captions in figures are too small to read; figures (1 and 2) show static graphs and omit edge attributes, contradicting the dynamic description.  
   - Equations are missing for key modules (e.g., time encoder, profile encoder), and weight matrix dimensions are not provided (e.g., in Section 4 on graph profiler).  
   - The derivation of \(d_1\) (bottom of p.4) is unclear and not motivated.  
   - The edge homophily formula is notation‑heavy without a plain‑English explanation (e.g., “the fraction of edges connecting nodes of the same class”).

6. **Open questions and missing discussions**  
   - How does RLC relate to dynamic knowledge graph link prediction?  
   - Are the proposed design principles transferable to node classification or future link prediction?  
   - What dataset characteristics influence the effectiveness of learnable time encodings (e.g., temporal density)?  
   - Can techniques from entity relationship classification in NLP be adapted for RLC?
