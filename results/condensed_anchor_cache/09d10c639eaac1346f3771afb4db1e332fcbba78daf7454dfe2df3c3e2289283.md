## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes UPT++, a latent point set neural operator for modeling dynamical transitions in particle-based physical systems (fluids, molecular dynamics). It argues that learning in a continuous latent space is advantageous over direct particle discretization, and extends the Universal Physics Transformer (UPT) with importance-based encoding/decoding and flow-matching sampling. Reviewers generally agree the aim is ambitious and the idea of latent-space dynamics is promising. However, the paper’s presentation is vague and insufficiently detailed; the method is incompletely described; experiments lack adequate comparisons, ablations, and baselines; and the molecular dynamics section is particularly underdeveloped. One reviewer (score 5) was more positive, citing dramatic speedups and clear writing, but still noted most of the same weaknesses as the majority (scores 3,3,3).

### Strengths

- The general aim of learning dynamics in a continuous latent space is ambitious, useful if achievable, and a promising direction as ML scales to more complicated dynamical systems (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The importance-based encoder adds two additional compression layers compared to the original UPT: local aggregation plus perceiver-based latent encoding (Reviewer 2).
- Results show dramatic speedups relative to competing methods (Reviewer 4).
- The extension from fluid simulations to molecular (atom) sampling provides a nice expansion of the method (Reviewer 4).
- The writing has good flow and gives a good overview of the field; the figures convey the core method well (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient method description and clarity**: The UPT++ framework is presented vaguely; sections 3.1–3.4 (encoding, decoding, operator) lack concrete details, math, and algorithmic precision. Many parts are textually described without supporting equations, making it hard to understand what is actually implemented. Notation is inconsistent and confusing (e.g., treating \(u\) as both a function and a point‑cloud, ambiguous definition of latent space dimensionality, unclear “supernodes” and “fixed size and uniform latent space”). The importance‑based sampling and encoding/decoding steps are barely described—proper math or an algorithm box is needed (Reviewers 1, 4).  
- **Insufficient comparisons and baselines**: 
  - No comparisons to other neural operators such as Neural Integral Equations, FNO, or U‑Net (Reviewer 2).  
  - No comparisons to other neural PDE solvers or scaling approaches, despite claiming to improve scaling over UPT and GNNs (Reviewer 4).  
  - No baselines in the molecular dynamics experiments, such as MLCGMD, MACE, or NequIP (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
  - For Navier‑Stokes with moving boundaries, no comparison to established numerical methods like finite elements or immersed boundary (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing ablations and insight**: It is unclear which component (importance sampling, perceiver blocks, flow matching) drives the reported improvements in MSE and speed. Ablation studies are needed, including varying sampling size, latent space size, and the effect of the latent bottleneck on performance and uncertainty (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
- **Inadequate experiments and evaluations**: 
  - The paper claims fast runtime but only demonstrates speedup in static experiments; it should show scaling with particle count and the trade‑off between speed and accuracy (Reviewer 4).  
  - No experiments on spatial interpolation (training at one resolution and generating at a higher one), despite the neural‑operator claim of discretization convergence (Reviewer 2).  
  - The molecular dynamics section is underdeveloped: it does not study the claimed advantages of latent‑space dynamics (e.g., smoother dynamics allowing larger timesteps, or memory/compute efficiency for realistic systems like those in Allegro). No runtime is reported for MD experiments (Reviewer 3).  
  - The MD results are weak—Fig. 6 shows free‑energy errors of several kT, which is concerning even for the toy alanine dipeptide system (Reviewer 1). Results lack standard deviations (Reviewer 4).  
- **Serious limitation in MD modeling**: The authors mention that an energy‑minimization step is required after each propagation step to mitigate distribution shift. This undermines the approach’s usefulness; the paper does not specify how many minimization steps are performed, their runtime contribution, or for how many steps the model can run stably without this intervention (Reviewer 3).  
- **Missing training details**: Loss functions are not specified, even in the appendix. The training setting (task definition, data, input/output points, sampling strategy) is unclear (Reviewers 1, 4). The authors do not indicate what is known vs. unknown (initial‑value problem vs. system discovery) (Reviewer 4).  
- **Conceptual concerns**: 
  - The “discretization paradox” argument is weak—particle methods are only one subset of numerical schemes (Reviewer 1). Counterexamples from other ML fields (e.g., VQ‑VAE, discrete token modeling for images/proteins/RL) show that discretization can be beneficial, contradicting the claimed advantage of continuous representations (Reviewer 3).  
  - The paper’s contributions are modest: density‑based importance sampling is an obvious idea, and the rest of the method is taken from existing literature (Reviewer 4).  
- **Other specific issues**: 
  - The use of normalizing flows for “successor sampling” is unclear—the latent dynamics appear deterministic, and it is not explained how conditional density sampling is actually performed or learned (Reviewer 4).  
  - The conclusion states that UPT is not mass‑preserving while GNNs are, but UPT is a GNN method—clarification is needed (Reviewer 4).  
  - The “fixed‑size” latent space is said to be a key advantage, yet every autoencoder is fixed‑size; its importance is not argued (Reviewer 4).  
  - Figures are good, but writing requires improvement—the introduction jumps between disconnected ideas (Reviewers 2, 4).
