## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TABDAR, a generative model for heterogeneous tabular data. It combines a masked bidirectional transformer (to handle arbitrary column ordering) with a diffusion decoder for continuous columns and a classifier for discrete columns. The model is trained as a conditional model (masked prediction) and used autoregressively at generation time. Experiments on multiple datasets show strong performance across statistical fidelity, utility, and privacy metrics, with ablation studies confirming the importance of the diffusion loss and random column order.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important and challenging problem: learning the generative distribution of heterogeneous tabular data.
- The proposed solution (masked transformer + diffusion for continuous columns) is interesting and novel in its combination of modalities.
- Extensive experimental evaluation: comparisons against several recent methods, machine learning utility metrics, visualizations, imputation results, and multiple ablation studies (Tables 1–6, Figures 5–6).
- Code is available and well-organized.
- Reports state-of-the-art empirical results by a margin on most metrics (Table 1) and strong imputation performance (Figure 6). One reviewer (R3) specifically notes “state-of-the-art empirical results (by some margin)” and “novel combination”; another (R2) notes “good results”.
- Ablation studies show that both the diffusion loss and random column-order sampling improve performance (Table 4), and results are fairly stable across depth and embedding dimension (Tables 5–6).

### Weaknesses
1. **Notation, clarity, and presentation**  
   - Promotional language (“ingenious”, “unparalleled advantages”) should be avoided (R1).  
   - Eq. (5): define \(x^{<i}\) (what for i=1?); superscript/subscript notation is messy across sections, e.g., item index used both as subscript and superscript (R1, R3).  
   - The training procedure is a masked language model, not autoregressive training; this should be clearly stated (R1).  
   - One-hot encoding for categorical columns is only mentioned in the appendix – should be in the main paper (R4).  
   - Eq. (6) and (7) appear to have typos (P(P(x|z))) (R4); R3 also questions if Eq. (6) is correct.

2. **Methodology concerns**  
   - **Diffusion for continuous columns**: why not use a simple continuous distribution (e.g., Gaussian) parameterized by the transformer? The ablation only compares against discretization, not a direct continuous likelihood. The computational cost of diffusion needs justification (R1).  
   - **Masking with zeros**: using zero for masked continuous values conflates with a true zero value; why not use a dedicated [MASK] embedding as in masked language models? (R1)  
   - **Diffusion details missing**: is the denoising network shared across continuous columns or column-specific? How many diffusion steps are used? (R1, R4)  
   - **Bidirectional attention vs. permutation invariance**: the claim that bi-directional attention is equivalent to arbitrary-order autoregressive generation is questionable due to position embeddings. The conventional way to achieve permutation invariance is to remove positional embeddings; why choose column-specific token embeddings instead? What are the trade-offs? It is also unclear whether the method uses a lower-triangular mask in combination with column permutations (R2, R3).  
   - **Conditional sampling unclear**: how does one condition on continuous features in the discrete generation phase and vice versa? Why generate step-by-step during conditional sampling when the model was trained to predict all masked columns in one step? Is there a performance benefit? (R1, R3)  
   - **Categorical prediction is not a generative model** – line 85 incorrectly labels it as such (R4).  
   - **Figure 3** does not adequately emphasize the role of the diffusion model (R4).  

3. **Results and comparisons**  
   - **TabSyn results differ from original TabSyn paper** – these discrepancies must be explained, and the advantages/differences between TabDAR and TabSyn (the closest competitor) should be discussed (R2).  
   - **Missing statistical significance**: Table 2 and Figure 6 lack standard deviations; the claim of “significantly outperforming” is not supported without error bars. Also, ties in Table 2 should be bolded for consistency (R1).  
   - **Imputation evaluation** is only at 30% missing; how does performance vary with missing rate? (R1)  
   - **Generation time** is likely high due to autoregressive sampling; a comparison of generation times with baselines is missing (R4).  
   - **Hyperparameter robustness**: Tables 5–6 are based on only two datasets – more datasets are needed to support the claim of robustness (R4). What do the values in these tables represent (averaged over two datasets)? (R4)  

4. **Novelty and overall impact**  
   - One reviewer (R2) views novelty as low (“simply integrates diffusion into bidirectional transformers”); another (R3) calls the combination novel. This disagreement should be addressed.  
   - One reviewer (R3) argues that improvements on many metrics are small, tasks appear roughly solved, and the added complexity may not be justified without clearer statistical significance. (Other reviewers still consider the results strong and state-of-the-art.)  

5. **Minor issues**  
   - The reasoning behind permutation invariance may not hold in all tabular domains (e.g., columns can have dependencies tied to a natural order) (R2).
