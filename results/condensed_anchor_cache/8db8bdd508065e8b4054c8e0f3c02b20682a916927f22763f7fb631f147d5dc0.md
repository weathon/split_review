## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes using Random Erasing (RE) as a defense against Model Inversion (MI) attacks, where image regions are randomly masked during training. The authors find that RE degrades MI attack accuracy while maintaining utility, and analyze the role of partial erasure and random location. Extensive experiments across 34 setups, multiple datasets, architectures, and attacks show improved privacy-utility trade-off. The method is simple to implement and can be combined with existing defenses.

### Strengths
- The paper explores a novel direction for MI defense by modifying the training data rather than model architecture or loss function.
- The motivation is well-motivated and intuitive: partial occlusion prevents the model from seeing entire objects, disrupting MI reconstruction.
- The method is simple to implement, effective, and can be integrated with existing defenses.
- Comprehensive experimental evaluation across a wide range of settings: multiple datasets, model architectures, MI attacks (GMI, PPA, etc.), and defense baselines.
- Feature space analysis (Figure 2) provides insight beyond empirical results, showing a discrepancy between features of RE-trained models and private data.
- The results are visually clear and well-organized, making them easy to interpret.
- The paper is well-organized and the writing is easy to follow.
- The experimental setup is described in detail, facilitating reproducibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concern:** One reviewer argues that randomly masking pixels has already been shown to negatively affect MI in prior work (Zhang et al. 2018, Figure 8; Yu et al. 2023), and the extension to masking entire regions is limited. Other reviewers note that applying existing data augmentation to defense lacks theoretical novelty.
- **Missing theoretical analysis:** The paper lacks deep theoretical explanation of why RE is effective. Analysis is largely intuitive (e.g., RE prevents seeing entire objects) and empirical. Several reviewers call for exploration of conditions for effectiveness, the relationship between RE and model generalization, representation learning, and MI attacks.
- **Incomplete evaluation metrics:** Tables B.14 and B.15 report only attack accuracy (AttAcc) and omit other distance metrics, leading to suspicion that the method may not be as good as prior defenses. Additionally, the paper does not evaluate knowledge extraction scores (as proposed in Struppek et al. 2023) to measure whether attackers could use RE-altered inversion results.
- **Missing robustness checks against other attacks:** The paper does not test whether the defense compromises robustness to adversarial attacks, backdoor attacks, or other security properties, as suggested by prior work (Struppek et al. 2023).
- **Limited baseline comparisons:** The ablation only considers squares as masks; alternative shapes (random pixels, rectangles, circles) are not explored. Also, PLGMI (Yuan et al. 2023) has strong attack accuracy at 224×224 resolution, but experiments only cover 64×64 for that attack.
- **Dataset scope:** Experiments are limited to centered/object-centric datasets; effectiveness on non-object-centric or scene datasets is unclear.
- **Utility concerns:** Some target models in Table 2 have very low natural accuracy, unlikely to be deployed. In several settings, utility still degrades when attack success decreases (e.g., Figure 1). One reviewer finds it surprising that masking entire squares does not impact utility more severely given the distribution shift between RE-augmented training data and clean test data.
- **Feature space puzzle:** Several reviewers note a puzzling gap in Figure 2(b) between features of RE-altered training data and private test data, yet classification accuracy remains high. This raises questions about whether the model is correctly matching identities or just memorizing features. Reviewers ask for explanation (Q2 from R5, Q2 from R6: why are the green spaces disjoint for no defense and highly overlapping with defense? Is there a fraction of training images not masked?).
- **Connection between RE and private information:** It is unclear how partial occlusion at the sample level prevents learning class-level private characteristics (e.g., if different images have different occluded parts). One reviewer asks: if one image has a partially obscured cheek but another does not, how does RE prevent the model from learning this class's private features?
- **User study design:** The user study is questionable: if users choose between two options close to the reference, it may not properly evaluate privacy; the study should assess how similar the two samples are.
- **Missing implementation details:** Source code and link to pre-trained target model are missing from Supplementary Materials. The evaluation details (model architecture, natural accuracy) are requested.
- **Inconsistent and unclear details:** The resolution of the Mirror dataset is inconsistently reported (116×116 vs. 160×160). The paper does not clarify whether the defense is applied at training time only or also during inference. The trade-off value Δ is not well explained: if the defense improves utility, Δ could be negative, and it is unclear if a linear relationship exists.
- **Metric clarification needed:** The calculation of attack accuracy is not clearly justified; it should be explained how it is computed and why it is a good metric for MI success. Is it calculated by training an identity classifier and checking if predictions match for original and reconstructed images?
- **Minor presentation issues:** Tense inconsistencies, grammar errors (e.g., "areased"), typos (line 763 "Celeba" should be "CelebA"), overlapping annotations in Figure 2, Figure 1 presented before experimental setup with unclear abbreviations, Figure 3 x-axes not aligned, missing articles throughout, and grammar mistakes in Table 4 title.
- **Potential ethical bias:** The paper does not discuss whether the defense affects different demographic groups (e.g., facial features, gender) unevenly, potentially making some individuals more protected than others.
