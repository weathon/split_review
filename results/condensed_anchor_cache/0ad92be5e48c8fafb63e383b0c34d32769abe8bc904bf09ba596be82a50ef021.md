## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes UniVoxel, a unified voxelization framework for inverse rendering that uses grid-based volumes to encode geometry, material, and illumination jointly. The key novelty is a local Spherical Gaussian (SG) illumination representation that bakes direct lighting, indirect lighting, and visibility, enabling efficient training without expensive multi-bounce ray tracing. UniVoxel reduces per-scene training time to **18 minutes** and achieves competitive results on novel view synthesis, material estimation, and relighting on real and synthetic benchmarks. While reviewers acknowledge the efficiency gains, concerns about novelty, incremental improvements over prior works (e.g., TensorIR, NeuralPBIR, Voxurf, PhySG), missing experiments on challenging scenes (e.g., Shiny NeRF-synthetic), and limitations in material decomposition (albedo retaining shading) are raised. Reviewer 1 is more positive (rating 8) and views the local illumination model as a clear contribution; reviewers 2, 3, and 4 are more critical (rating 5) and consider the advances incremental.

### Strengths

1. **Clear novelty in the local SG illumination model**: the representation bakes direct+indirect+visibility into per-point SGs, avoiding expensive multi-bounce ray tracing and accelerating optimization significantly. Reviewer 1 considers this the major contribution. (R1)
2. **Comprehensive experiments**: extensive evaluations on widely used real and synthetic datasets, including quantitative and qualitative results for novel view synthesis, material estimation, and relighting, plus ablation studies on lighting representations (SG vs. SH). (R1)
3. **Well-written paper with sufficient implementation details** in the main text and supplementary material, making it easy to follow and reproduce. (R1, R3)
4. **Efficient optimization**: grid-based representation combined with shallow MLPs leads to much faster training (≈18 minutes per scene) compared to previous methods that take hours. (R4)
5. **Superior frequency characteristics of SG**: the SG representation can capture higher-frequency illumination than low-order SH, which is beneficial for inverse rendering. (R2)
6. **Visually appealing inverse rendering results** and a comprehensive related work section demonstrating broad domain knowledge. (R3)

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty relative to prior works**: the use of feature volumes with MLP decoders for inverse rendering is very similar to TensorIR, NeuralPBIR, Voxurf, and InstantNGP. The application of SGs for lighting was already explored in PhySG, and this is not sufficiently acknowledged. Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 feel the contribution is incremental.
   - R2: feature grids for efficient training are not a new insight (cf. InstantNGP).
   - R3: core aspects appear in established works like PhySG, TensorIR, NeuS.
   - R4: explicit scene representation is very similar to Voxurf with only additional property predictions.
2. **Incremental performance**: results are not consistently better than previous methods.
   - Albedo estimates often include shading components (e.g., Fig. 5 first row) compared to TensoIR. (R2, R4)
   - Relighting results are not prominently superior (Fig. 3). (R4)
   - Geometry reconstruction quality is not thoroughly analyzed, especially for specular surfaces and concave regions; missing comparison with TensoIR. (R1)
3. **Missing experiments and analyses**:
   - No evaluation on the challenging **Shiny NeRF-synthetic dataset** (highly reflective/shiny objects). (R3)
   - No direct comparison of geometry quality (e.g., normal maps, shape accuracy) with state-of-the-art methods. (R1)
   - Need for a fair SG vs. SH comparison with equal parameter counts (SG uses 128 SG lobes; SH only tested up to order 3). (R2)
   - Missing visualization of reconstructed incident light maps (e.g. on shiny scenes) to demonstrate complex lighting modeling. (R3)
   - No training speed video comparing convergence with other methods (e.g., as in Plenoxels). (R4)
   - No analysis of whether the method can handle highly reflective surfaces like metal or mirror. (R4)
4. **Missing implementation details**:
   - It is not clearly stated how rendering is performed after predicting SG parameters – whether using uniform ray sampling, importance sampling, or analytical integration. (R1)
   - How global illumination and self-occlusion are handled during **relighting** is not explained. During reconstruction they are baked into per-point SGs, but relighting requires a different environment. (R2)
   - Distinction between the environment map used here and that in Nvdiffrec is unclear, especially when claiming SG superiority. (R3)
   - The required GPU memory is not quantified (only a qualitative mention of higher memory usage). (R1)
5. **Limitations**: the method cannot recover a separate environment map, which may constrain its application scenarios (e.g., relighting with new environment maps requires retraining). (R4)
6. **Minor quality issues**: normal maps (Fig. 13) appear with very different colors across methods – possible visualization inconsistency. (R1)
7. **Additional related works suggested**:
   - NeuralPBIR (Sun et al.) (R1)
   - Yang et al. “PS-NeRF”, Wang et al. “Neural Fields for Urban Scenes”, Mai et al. “Neural Microfacet Fields”, Zhang et al. “NeMF” (R4)
8. **Extension suggestion**: possibility of using per-point SG parameters to compute radiance via the rendering equation to handle furry/volumetric objects. (R1) This is listed as a reviewer question but indicates a missing capability.
