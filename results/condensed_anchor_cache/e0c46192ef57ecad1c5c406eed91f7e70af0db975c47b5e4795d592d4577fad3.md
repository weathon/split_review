## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Data-augmented Phrase-level Alignment (DPA) to mitigate object hallucinations in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) while preserving general vision-language capabilities. By generating “hallucinated-correct” response pairs through data augmentation and applying a phrase-level loss with KL divergence regularization, DPA is shown to be effective on multiple benchmarks (e.g., improving F1 by up to 13.4% on hallucination VQA, reducing hallucination rate by up to 4.2% on image description). Three reviewers rated the paper 6, and one reviewer gave an 8, praising the contribution and comprehensive experiments. All reviewers agree the method is simple and effective, but concerns are raised about novelty, evaluation breadth, data augmentation validity, and presentation consistency.

### Strengths
- The phrase-level alignment loss is a novel and focused approach to directly penalize hallucinated tokens, offering a targeted solution to object hallucination without sacrificing general capabilities.
- The data augmentation method for generating paired responses is straightforward yet effective, enabling the model to learn nuanced phrase-level distinctions with minimal complexity.
- DPA achieves significant performance gains on hallucination benchmarks (e.g., MLLMs fine-tuned with DPA, called HALVA, improve F1 by up to 13.4% on hallucination VQA and reduce hallucination rate by up to 4.2% on image description tasks).
- The method preserves general vision-language capabilities: experiments on non-hallucination benchmarks (VQA-v2, TextVQA) show comparable or even higher scores, addressing the trade-off issue common in hallucination mitigation.
- Extensive quantitative results are provided across both generative and discriminative tasks, and multiple popular benchmarks are covered.
- One reviewer (score 8) particularly praised the writing as excellent and detailed, and noted that the explicit treatment of the performance trade-off (using KL divergence to retain general abilities) is a significant contribution to reducing hallucinations in MLLMs.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns** (Reviewers 2, 4): The method builds on existing fine-tuning and data augmentation techniques; the phrase-level alignment is applied in a new way but does not significantly advance the field. Reviewer 4 argues that the motivation for phrase-level loss is not clearly articulated—it may simply be the KL divergence constraint that prevents divergence, which would greatly weaken the contribution.
- **Data augmentation validity** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3): Several concerns are raised:
  - If object hallucination is rare in real data, replacing every possible object/attribute with hallucinated terms may introduce excessive non-realistic cases, causing a mismatch with real-world distributions and harming generalization (Reviewer 1).
  - The diversity of hallucinated data is limited to a finite set of replacements, potentially not covering unseen hallucination types (Reviewer 1).
  - Simple substitution may compromise fluency and grammatical accuracy, thereby affecting data quality and downstream performance (Reviewer 2).
  - Independent experimental evaluation of the augmentation strategy itself is lacking—no ablation or analysis of its generalization effect and impact on training stability across tasks (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete evaluation and comparisons** (Reviewers 2, 4):
  - Several relevant recent baselines are omitted (e.g., HA-DPO, EOS are included but others are missing); the selection of methods across different benchmarks is inconsistent and the presentation of results varies across tables (e.g., EOS-13B only appears in Table 1, and some detailed results for LLaVA-13B and VILA are missing).
  - The reported results for some baselines, such as VCD, may differ from those in the original papers; it is unclear whether they were directly tested or taken from published results, making comparisons potentially unfair (Reviewer 2).
  - More comprehensive evaluation on diverse general benchmarks would strengthen the claim that DPA preserves overall vision-language capabilities (Reviewer 2).
- **Overclaim in Figure 2** (Reviewer 4): On line 260, the paper states “EOS achieves a slightly lower hallucination rate”, but the figure shows EOS at ~5.0 and HALVA at ~6.5; the gap is similar to the gap between HALVA and LLaVA-1.5, so the difference is not negligible. Further, the higher F1 score on AMBER for HALVA may be natural because neither EOS nor HA-DPO were trained on Yes/No questions.
- **Limited analysis of hallucination types** (Reviewers 2, 3): The paper lacks fine-grained analysis of different hallucination categories (objects, attributes, actions, locations). This would provide deeper insight into where DPA is most effective and where it might fail. (Reviewer 2 asks: How does DPA perform on other types of hallucinations beyond object hallucination, such as attribute or location hallucinations?)
- **Missing discussion of related work** (Reviewer 4): There are other works that mitigate hallucination with sub-sequence level training [1]; the difference should be discussed.
- **Training trade-off** (Reviewer 2): The paper criticizes prior methods for requiring massive training data, but DPA also introduces additional training requirements (generating augmented pairs and fine-tuning), suggesting a trade-off between efficiency and effectiveness.
- **Architecture generalization** (Reviewer 3): It is unclear whether DPA is equally effective on other MLLM architectures beyond LLaVA-1.5, such as Qwen-based models.
- **Presentation issues** (Reviewer 4):
  - The construction of the dataset is not introduced in sufficient detail; the appendix should have been cross-referenced.
  - Figure 5 (right) is not explained in the main text, making it hard to interpret.
