## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Mixture of Shards (MoS), a parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning method that extends LoRA by combining inter‑layer and intra‑layer parameter sharing with four nearly cost‑free “differentiation” strategies (subset selection, pair dissociation, vector sharding, shard privatization). An MoE‑like routing mechanism selects shards from a global pool for each adapted layer. Experiments on LLaMA2‑7B/13B across MMLU, TyDi QA, GSM8K, BBH, and HumanEval claim roughly 8× parameter savings over standard LoRA while maintaining competitive performance. An ablation study confirms the importance of each differentiation strategy (except subset selection). One reviewer found the paper well‑written and the motivation clear (score 8), while three other reviewers gave score 6, raising more substantive concerns about evaluation rigor, reproducibility, theoretical grounding, and the complexity/benefit trade‑off.

### Strengths
- **Novel and well‑motivated approach:** MoS is the first method to use an MoE‑like routing mechanism for parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning in a single‑task LoRA setting. The initial sharing/differentiation experiment (Table 1) provides an empirical foundation.
- **Significant parameter efficiency**: The method achieves approximately 8‑fold parameter reduction compared to standard LoRA, demonstrated on multiple NLP benchmarks (MMLU, TyDi QA, GSM8K, BBH, HumanEval) with LLaMA2‑7B and 13B.
- **Comprehensive evaluation across diverse tasks**: Benchmarks cover factual knowledge (MMLU), multilingual QA (TyDi QA), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K), multi‑step reasoning (BBH), and code generation (HumanEval). Scalability is also tested on the larger LLaMA2‑13B model.
- **Ablation study confirms component contributions**: The ablation shows that pair dissociation, vector sharding, and shard privatization each contribute to performance, and that their combination yields the best results.
- **Generality**: LoRA is a special case of MoS when no global pool is used.
- **Solid technical grounding**: Each differentiation strategy is described and designed to be nearly cost‑free. The paper is generally well‑structured and the writing is clear.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing critical implementation details:** Pool size, number of shards, private/public segment breakdown, router implementation (the main text does not mention a router “R”), the exact routing algorithm (deterministic or stochastic?), the size of the index matrix, and the indices domain (e.g., \(I_a^k \in \mathbb{R}^r\) or \(\mathbb{N}^r\)) are not provided, making reproduction difficult.
- **Subset selection is arbitrary and insufficiently validated:** Subset selection is randomly initialized and fixed – no criteria or dynamic adjustment. The initial experiment (Table 1) shows only marginal improvement; the conclusion that random scaling “reverses detrimental effects” may not be statistically significant. Only two seeds (0 and 1) are used for main results, and individual per‑seed results are not reported. No significance tests are performed.
- **Ablation is incomplete:** Subset selection is never ablated individually. The interactions between the four differentiation strategies are not analyzed. The scaling potential of each strategy with model/task complexity is not explored. Computational overhead per strategy (e.g., vector sharding) is not compared.
- **Narrow evaluation scope:** All experiments are on instruction‑following tasks (MMLU, TyDi QA, GSM8K, BBH, HumanEval) with LLaMA2‑7B/13B only. No results on text classification, sequence tagging, or generation tasks beyond instruction following, limiting claims of generalizability.
- **Unfair comparison with VeRA:** MoS uses more parameters than VeRA; a direct comparison at equivalent parameter counts is missing, so the observed gains may simply come from a larger parameter budget rather than the method itself.
- **No standard deviations or significance measures:** Standard deviations are not reported for any average results, making it impossible to assess the statistical significance and robustness of the claimed improvements.
- **Limited model‑scale analysis:** Only LLaMA2‑7B and 13B are tested; gains on 13B are minimal, raising questions about whether MoS’s complexity is justified for larger models. No evaluation on models beyond 13B, and no discussion of potential scaling challenges to hundreds of billions or trillions of parameters.
- **MoE‑like routing is under‑explained:** The routing mechanism for selecting shards from the global pool is not clearly described – no rationale for why an MoE‑style approach is appropriate, whether alternatives were considered, or how it affects training/inference.
- **High‑level motivation for differentiation strategies lacks theoretical support:** The claim that differentiation “reverses the detrimental effects of sharing” is not backed by theory. The combination of the four techniques feels somewhat arbitrary without a clear explanation of how they synergistically interact.
- **Computational overhead not reported:** No comparison of training/inference time, memory usage, or GPU hours versus LoRA, VeRA, or PRoLoRA. This is critical for assessing practical deployability.
- **Sensitivity analysis missing:** How performance varies with pool size, number of shards, and other hyperparameters is not explored. The trade‑offs between parameter savings and task performance across settings are not discussed.
- **No discussion of limitations:** The paper does not address scenarios where MoS might underperform (e.g., low data diversity, high variability in representational needs across layers), potential biases, or task‑specific hyperparameter sensitivity.
- **Minor presentation issues:** Figure 2 does not accurately follow the main text (e.g., no router “R” in text); the notation “Index(.)” is ambiguous; Table 2’s “4/8, 16/32” is unclear. The term “differentiation” could be confused with gradient computation.
- **No code released:** Given the method’s complexity, code is essential for reproducibility.
- **Interaction with other techniques not discussed:** No experiments or discussion on how MoS interacts with quantization, pruning, or dropout, which are common in practical deployments.
