## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies compositional generalization in a teacher-student setting with modular architectures. The teacher is a ground-truth linear hypernetwork whose modules (rows of the weight matrix) compose multiplicatively. The authors prove that under three assumptions (compositional support, connected support, no over-parameterization), the student can identify the teacher’s modules up to linear transformation from demonstrations alone, and show this identification is necessary and sufficient for compositional generalization. They also demonstrate empirically that meta-learning (e.g., using hypernetworks) can discover such modular solutions in several complex environments, including discrete task distributions and sequential decision-making.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and under-theorized problem: compositional generalization. (R1, R4)
- Provides rare theoretical results: the student provably identifies ground-truth modules under specified conditions (compositional support, connected support, no over-parameterization). (R1, R3, R4)
- The use of hypernetworks and the teacher-student setup casts generalization as an identification problem, a novel connection. (R2, R3)
- The combination of theory and empirical experiments (including tests beyond the theory’s bounds) is well balanced. (R1, R4)
- Introduces the concept of “connected support” to address permutation invariance in module identification. (R3)
- The overall structure and logical flow from definitions to theory to experiments is good. (R2)
- Figures are visually clear and well designed (though captions need improvement). (R2)
- High potential significance: findings could guide future meta-learning research. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation are poor.** The paper is hard to follow, with no simple examples to illustrate the problem before mathematical details. Notation is often not introduced or explained (e.g., $(U_k)$, double subscripts, $U_{k_i}$, overloading superscript $(i)$ to refer to rows, columns, and matrices). Definitions 3.1–3.3 and Theorem 3.1 are unclear; key intuitions (e.g., that irreducibility means each hidden neuron is activated for a different feature; that compositional support is essentially a spanning set; why Theorem 3.1 distinguishes even/odd $n$) are missing. The lack of a dedicated related work section makes it difficult to contextualize contributions. Figure captions are unhelpful and do not explain the concepts they illustrate (e.g., Figure 1B’s tiling, Figure 2A’s permutation invariance, Figure 2B’s connected support). The abstract is vague about the problem domain (demonstrations vs. RL). (R1, R2, R4)
- **The theoretical framework is heavily constrained, limiting generalizability.** Assumptions include: (1) linear hypernetwork, (2) two-layer neural networks, (3) knowing the exact teacher architecture, (4) knowing the number of modules $M$ and hidden units $h$. It is unclear whether these assumptions are necessary or just sufficient; violations may prevent identification, but no counterexamples are given. The linearity and fixed architecture seem essential to the proof, raising doubts about extension to more complex settings. (R3, R4)
- **The choice of linear hypernetworks limits interpretability of compositionality.** The resulting modules lack intuitive functional roles (unlike neural module networks), and it is not made explicit how feature learning (learning a basis) equates to identifying composable modules. This connection to the broader compositional generalization literature (e.g., systematic generalization, disentanglement) is not clearly argued. (R2, R4)
- **Experimental clarity is insufficient, especially for sequential decision-making tasks.** The description of how the agent learns in these tasks is vague (teacher demonstrations vs. trial-and-error). Using “accuracy” as a metric in a sequential setting is questionable; the link between accuracy and cumulative reward is not established, making it hard to evaluate effectiveness. The experiments in Section 4.2 attempt to address interpretability but suffer from the same lack of clarity. (R4)
- **Connection between theory and experiments needs strengthening.** The experiments (Section 4) mainly show that hypernetworks outperform ANIL and MAML in compositional domains, but a deeper theoretical link beyond that demonstration is missing. (R2)
- **Missing background explanation.** Key concepts such as MAML, hypernetworks, and the need for hypernetworks in this modular setting are not explained, making the paper inaccessible to readers unfamiliar with these methods. (R1)
- **Minor wording issues.** Example: “series of tasks” should be “set of tasks”. (R4)
