## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a psychometric benchmark for LLMs covering five dimensions: personality, values, emotion, theory of mind, and motivation. It tests LLMs on self-report questionnaires, open-ended questions, and multiple-choice items, and reports discrepancies between closed-form and open-ended results, as well as consistency on reasoning tasks and sensitivity to position/prompt.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and clear (R1). It provides a detailed explanation of experiment design for each psychological dimension, grounded in solid psychological literature (R1). The work is essential for measuring (1) LLMs’ psychological behaviors and underlying reasons and (2) their consistency, and represents a novel and significant evaluation framework for improving LLM representations and social interaction (R1).
- The paper recognizes the difference between humans and LLMs, proposes guidelines to bridge this gap (R2), and conducts a thorough reliability test on psychometric evaluation results, particularly reporting the discrepancy between open-ended and self-report questions—a meaningful observation in LLM psychometrics (R2).
- The benchmark combines existing datasets and psychological tests into a unified, comprehensive evaluation, covering five aspects and testing on various scenarios (R3). The conclusion that LLMs show discrepancies in psychological tendencies when responding to closed-form versus open-ended questions is interesting (R4).
- A substantial amount of usable data has been collected, which could facilitate future research (R4). Several measures were taken to ensure reliability of conclusions, serving as a good example for future work (R4).

### Weaknesses
**Conceptual and foundational issues** (explicitly from minority reviewer R2, largely unsupported by other reviewers):
- The definition of “personality” is overly simplistic (R2). Referring to Friedman & Schustack, the reviewer notes that the literature emphasizes adaptation and environment (e.g., Adler’s striving for superiority). LLMs have no physical environment or desire for adaptation, so applying human personality evaluation metrics may not be meaningful in psychological terms (R2). This naive understanding leads to misuse of datasets—e.g., an SD3 question “I enjoy having sex with people I hardly know” is meaningless for an LLM because sex lacks real-world grounding, desire, and moral/environmental context (R2).
- The paper lacks deep study of fundamental psychology beyond psychometrics (e.g., psychoanalysis, cognitive psychology, neuroscience) which explain structural causes of personality (R2).
- The paper redefines “emotion” as “understanding another person’s emotion” and “motivation” as “self-efficacy,” which are distinct from the original psychological constructs. This places the evaluation outside the boundaries of psychometrics (R2). (Reviewe 2 is the most negative; R1 and R4 do not comment on this, while R3 does not mention it.)

**Selection and justification of dimensions** (R3, also implied by R2):
- The five dimensions appear independent; more discussion is needed on why these five are selected, the logical relationships between them, and whether/how they best represent “AI psychology” (R3).

**Lack of depth in analysis and novelty** (R3, R4):
- The current conclusions are disconnected and scattered into independent sections. A more coherent narrative is needed (R3). Findings such as discrepancies in closed-form vs. open-ended questions are not completely novel and lack in-depth analysis (R3).
- Two of the four findings listed in the introduction (position bias, prompt sensitivity, and reliability of LLM-as-a-judge) are well-known and extensively studied, diminishing the paper’s novelty. The authors should summarize new findings from their own experiments or provide new insights on these known issues (R4).
- There is a lack of discussion on how findings could guide improvements in future research (R4). The motivation “investigate psychology in LLMs” is unclear regarding specific benefits or application scenarios (R4).

**Methodological concerns** (R1, R4, and R2 points above):
- Most detailed explanations and results are in the Appendix; some should be moved to the main body (R1, R4). The paper is somewhat disorganized and its structure unclear (R4).
- There is no analysis of underlying causes of LLM inconsistency across dimensions; ablation studies are suggested (R1). For instance, personality prompts and reverse prompts are generated using GPT-4, which likely reflects GPT-4’s own personality—results might differ if another model generated the prompts (R1).
- The rating scale in prompts (e.g., L1874-1875) lacks explicit definitions for each score (not like a widely known Likert scale) (R1).
- The prompts often begin with “You are a helpful assistant,” which may influence evaluation results, especially for personality assessment, potentially masking inherent traits (R4).
- Two competent LLMs (GPT-4 and Llama3-70b) are used as judges for open-ended questions, but given the instability and bias of LLM-as-a-judge, human evaluation results and correlation with LLM-as-a-judge are needed to validate effectiveness (R4). (R1 suggests asking models to generate a short rationale for scores to understand underlying reasoning.)

**Missing details and minor issues** (R1, R4):
- No discussion of whether findings generalize to non-Western cultures or low-resource languages, given that training corpora are predominantly English and Western (R1).
- Even humans struggle in complex scenarios influenced by cultural context; is it possible to determine a “better” or “moral” decision for LLMs? (R1)
- Missing relevant citations for the statement in Lines 043-044 (R4). Typo in Line 123: “Llama3-7b” should be “Llama3-70b” (R4). “Number” in Table 1 is unclear (likely number of items) (R4). The version of the LLM used is not specified (R4).

**Contrast in reviewer confidence**: One reviewer (R2, score 3, confidence 4) is strongly negative on conceptual soundness and contribution, arguing the paper should shift direction to argue LLMs do not possess personality attributes. Another reviewer (R1, score 8, confidence 4) is very positive, praising novelty and significance. The other two (R3, R4, scores 5) are moderately critical of novelty, organization, and depth but see value in the benchmark and data.
