## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an adapt-retrieve-revise framework for domain adaptation of LLMs to Chinese legal tasks. A 7B Chinese LM is continually pre-trained on 50B tokens of Chinese legal data to generate draft answers. The draft is used to retrieve supporting evidence from a legal knowledge base (using an E5 embedding model). GPT-4 then revises the draft based on the retrieved evidence. Experiments on four Chinese legal tasks (LCR, CP, LegalQA, JEC-QA) report accuracy improvements over direct GPT-4 generation (33.3%) and two retrieval-based baselines (15.4% and 23.9%). Reviewers varied in scores (6,5,3,5): the lower-scoring reviewer (3) raised major concerns about evaluation metrics, narrow focus, and limited novelty, while the higher-scoring reviewers appreciated the simplicity and empirical gains but identified missing ablations and generalizability issues.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important research question: adapting LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) to knowledge-intensive domains. (R1)
- The high-level idea of using a draft answer from a smaller LM to improve retrieval (more informative query) is novel and simple yet effective. (R1, R4)
- The pipeline is straightforward: tuning an affordable 7B LLM acts as a form of parameter-efficient adaptation for GPT-4; the draft output serves as a symbolic representation linking the trainable module and fixed LLM. (R4)
- Contributions to retrieval-based generation for low-resource languages (Chinese legal QA) with significant reported improvements. (R2)
- Comprehensive experiments and ablation studies are conducted across several legal tasks. (R1)
- Strong experimental results and thorough analysis provide practical insights. (R4)
- One reviewer (R3) also acknowledged the new method combining continual training and retrieval augmentation, and strong improvements on multiple legal QA datasets.

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation metric concerns:**  
  The primary metric for LCR, CP, and LegalQA is recall of whether the ground-truth law clause title appears in the answer. Reviewers questioned its appropriateness: a baseline always generating all clause titles would achieve perfect recall (R3); the metric cannot detect irrelevant or hallucinated content (R4). The LegalQA dataset originally uses MAP, MRR, and P@1, not recall – authors did not justify the change (R3). R2 also asked how simply revising the draft based on evidence yields such large recall gains in LCR, CP, LegalQA.

- **Insufficient retrieval baselines and retrieval module assessment:**  
  Only a single embedding model (E5) is used for retrieval. Stronger or more standard retrieval modules should be compared: BM25, Contriever, GPT embeddings, or multilingual models like mContriever (R1, R3). R3 suspects E5 may not be competitive for Chinese legal text and suggests continual pre-training of the retriever on the same domain corpus. Since all tasks except JEC-QA are essentially retrieval tasks, competitive retrieval-only baselines should be included (R3). R2 requested details on the retrieval mechanism used in the baselines and a comparison of retrieved evidence quality. R2 also asked how many evidence documents are needed for optimal revision, and whether the zero-shot retrieval from the 7B model could be improved.

- **Missing ablations and comparisons:**  
  A key missing experiment: using GPT-4 itself to generate the draft (instead of the fine-tuned 7B model), then retrieving and revising – this would isolate the effect of continual pre-training (R4). Ablations with GPT-4 acting as both retriever and reviser should be considered (R2). The study is limited to GPT-4; open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama-2) should be included to demonstrate generalizability (R1). R2 asked whether the revision step is few-shot.

- **Narrow focus and unclear generalizability:**  
  The method targets only the Chinese legal domain and relies heavily on a 7B LM in the target language and 50B tokens of domain corpus – resources unavailable for many languages/domains (R3). The scope is limited to subsets of each task (R1). One reviewer (R3) argued this narrow focus may not interest the broader ICLR audience. Others (R1, R2) also requested demonstration in other knowledge-intensive domains (medical, financial) or other languages. R1 noted that training domain-specific smaller LLMs can be computationally intensive and asked whether the approach can mitigate this.

- **Technical novelty concerns (minority view):**  
  One reviewer (R3) considered continual training and retrieval-augmentation to be previously studied; the draft answer retrieval idea is somewhat new but the limited focus and evaluation protocol make it unclear if widely applicable. Other reviewers (R1, R4) viewed the pipeline as novel and effective.

- **Presentation issues:**  
  The paper is occasionally hard to follow; Section 2 mixes low-level experimental details with high-level ideas (R3). Illustrations and captions need improvement and should be more self-contained (R4). Missing discussion of related work on continual pre-training (R4). Minor: metrics should be mentioned in table descriptions (R2).

- **Additional missing details requested:**  
  Data statistics for the datasets (R4). Clarification on why recall was chosen over standard LegalQA metrics (R3). Whether multilingual retrieval models were tried (R3).
