## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper empirically investigates how many states to sample from an information set to train a neural network evaluator in imperfect-information games (Heads-Up Poker, Reconnaissance Blind Chess) and an MNIST variant with hidden information. It finds that the number of sampled states strongly affects training efficiency, with diminishing returns for large samples, and that the optimal number (1, 2, 2 in the three domains) depends on the total evaluation budget.

### Strengths
- The problem of partial observability is practically important in real-world decision making (Reviewer 1).
- The topic—value estimation of information sets in games—is of relevance to the game theory community (Reviewer 3).
- The paper is clearly written and easy to understand (Reviewer 3).  
  *(Note: Reviewer 2 disagrees, rating presentation “1 poor” and stating “I barely understand the point the authors want to argue.”)*

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of rigorous problem formulation and principled approach** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). The paper gives no concrete mathematical problem definition and no systematic method beyond empirical trial; it is not scientifically sound (Reviewer 1 soundness 1). The results are a mere experimental report without meaningful conclusions or guidelines (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3: “not very illuminating given what is already well understood about supervised learning”).
- **Reduction to standard supervised learning with noise** (Reviewer 3). The core problem essentially reduces to estimating \(\mathbb{E}[y|x]\) by sampling from the conditional distribution. The paper makes no use of the hidden information \(h\), only sampling from the conditional distribution to compute a summary statistic (majority vote or average). This ignores the structural challenge of imperfect information.
- **Ignoring opponent’s policy / unknown distribution of hidden states** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). In games the distribution of the hidden information \(h\) given the information set \(x\) is *not* known: the opponent controls it and can manipulate it (e.g., in poker, an opponent’s range). The paper assumes a known, fixed distribution \(h|x\), which is a fundamental barrier to any practical application in games.
- **Confusion in the MNIST experiment and evaluation metric** (Reviewer 3). When using majority vote as the aggregator for \(k\) samples, the training target becomes the true class for large \(k\) (by the law of large numbers) even when the underlying noise probability \(p\) is high. The classifier then learns the true class, but the correct objective (given the noisy setup) is to predict the expected value \(\mathbb{E}[y|x]\), which is not the true class. Measuring test accuracy against the true label is therefore incorrect for this objective.
- **Obvious or trivial experimental findings** (Reviewer 3). The pattern in Figure 4 (with a fixed number of evaluations, more samples per instance reduces the number of unique instances, potentially causing overfitting; with a fixed number of updates, more samples reduces noise) is exactly what one would expect from basic learning theory.
- **No comparison to existing work** (Reviewer 2). There is a large body of work on learning with noisy labels that provides both theoretical justification and strong empirical results; the paper makes no attempt to relate to or improve upon these baselines.
- **Poor presentation** (Reviewer 2). Section 2 contains only standard Monte Carlo approximations; the connection to neural network training and the intended contribution are unclear until the experiments. The paper lacks a formal methodology section that discusses the proposed method and its significance relative to existing work.
- **Missing discussion of limitations and assumptions** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). The paper does not address how the lack of knowledge of the hidden information distribution affects results, nor does it discuss potential benefits/drawbacks of the sampling approach in a principled manner.
- **Criticism of the motivation** (Reviewer 2). The authors claim the work helps evaluate imperfect-information games, but evaluating such games requires taking the opponent’s policy into account, which is completely ignored; thus the paper’s contribution to imperfect-information games is unclear.
