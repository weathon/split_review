## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces OCN, a two-stage pipeline for unsupervised multi-object segmentation on real-world images. The first stage trains an objectness network on ImageNet (using masks generated by a prior method) to produce three-level object-centric representations: a binary object existence score, an object center field (unit vectors pointing to center), and an object boundary distance field (shortest distance to boundary). The second stage uses a network-free multi-object reasoning module to iteratively refine bounding boxes from these representations. Experiments on seven real-world benchmarks including COCO and its extended annotation set (COCO*) report state-of-the-art object segmentation performance. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanatory figures. Reviewers generally agree the topic is challenging and the method is effective, but raise concerns about novelty, fairness of comparisons, and limited technical contributions.

### Strengths
- The task of unsupervised multi-object segmentation is interesting and challenging.
- The paper is well-presented, well-written, and easy to follow, with vivid figures.
- The proposed method is simple and reasonably designed; the explicit three-level object-centric representation (existence, center, boundary) is reasonable.
- The use of boundary distance gradients for extending and shrinking bounding boxes is particularly effective, parameter-free, and potentially fast.
- The model successfully captures multiple objects of varying scales, including on the challenging COCO dataset and extremely crowded images.
- Compared to competing methods, OCN demonstrates significant performance improvement across multiple datasets and settings (reviewer 1: "significant performance improvement"; reviewer 2: "superior performance across various datasets and experimental settings"; reviewer 3: "quite effective").
- The authors annotated unlabeled object instances in the COCO validation set (COCO*), enhancing its value for unsupervised object discovery.
- Ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of each component in the three-level representation.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and technical contribution:** The three-level representation (existence, center, boundary) has been widely explored in prior literature (e.g., Hough transform, Chamfer distance, displacement fields, distance transforms). Applying these class‑agnostic representations to unsupervised object segmentation is a relatively straightforward extension and does not introduce substantial novelty (Reviewer 2). The object center field is conceptually similar to displacement fields in other segmentation methods; the boundary distance field is a common technique in distance transforms and shape representation. The multi-object reasoning module is designed heuristically, processing each level sequentially rather than exploiting interdependencies between them; a more integrated approach could improve performance (Reviewer 2). The use of anchors and the existence network closely resembles the Region Proposal Network (RPN) in Faster R‑CNN, diminishing the novelty of the network; this similarity needs to be more clearly distinguished (Reviewer 4).
- **Fairness of comparisons:** The objectness network is trained on ImageNet using masks from MaskCut (or similar pseudo-labeling). Therefore the method is more “supervised” than unsupervised (Reviewer 4). Table 1 compares OCN (which includes extra training on ImageNet with pseudo-labels) against MaskCut/VoteCut, which involve no training—this is unfair (OCN = f(g(I)) vs. g(I)). Similarly, Table 3 adds an additional level of training for OCN (p(f(g(I)))) while others only have p(g(I)), giving OCN an unfair advantage (Reviewer 4). The comparisons should instead be against CutLER or CuVLER (Reviewer 4).
- **Use of pre-trained features and training details:** The paper does not adequately discuss why OCN is superior to existing methods that also learn object-centric representations with pre-trained features, given that OCN also uses pre-trained features (Reviewer 2). The rough masks used to train the objectness network are obtained from ImageNet without human annotations; details on how foreground/background are distinguished and whether this process is truly unsupervised are missing (Reviewer 1). The Objectness Network’s center field module is trained on images with single objects but is used to detect multiple objects—is this possible due to random cropping? More insights needed (Reviewer 4). Why did the authors use DINO rather than DINOv2, which has shown greater object awareness? (Reviewer 4).
- **Latency and computational cost:** The method iteratively leverages the objectness network, which is inefficient. A comparison of evaluation cost (e.g., average number of iterations to form the final bounding box) is requested (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Limited analysis and reproducibility:**
  - The sensitivity analysis (Table 10 in supplementary) uses narrow hyperparameter ranges and lacks evaluation on multiple datasets, hindering assessment of robustness across different datasets (Reviewer 2).
  - The paper claims (lines 255‑258) that the designed kernel excludes proposals where two objects are separated by >5 pixels; it is unclear whether this holds for all such proposals (Reviewer 2).
  - Different competing methods are used in different experiments (Tables 1‑4) without justification (Reviewer 2).
  - Did the authors tune hyperparameters of competing methods on the newly annotated COCO* dataset? (Reviewer 2).
  - Deeper analysis of the experimental results—explaining *why* the method outperforms others—is needed (Reviewer 2).
- **Adaptability and generalizability:** The paper does not address how the method could be adapted to domains like medical or sonar imaging, where objects and backgrounds differ significantly from natural images; modifications for generalization are not discussed (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation issues:**
  - Figures 3, 4, and 5 could be integrated into Figure 1 to avoid repeating the three-level explanation (Reviewer 2).
  - Indexing of the four steps in the reasoning module is inconsistent (#0‑#3 vs. #1‑#4) (Reviewer 2).
  - In the appendix, it states “MaskCut proposed in CuVLER,” but MaskCut was actually proposed in CutLER (Reviewer 4).
  - The related work section on Object-Centric Learning with Pretrained Features misses references such as SOLV and VideoSAUR (Reviewer 4).
  - There is no step #4 mentioned in the text, though it is referred to in line 306 (Reviewer 4).
  - Typos in Table 5: row 2 “exsitence” should be “existence”, row 6 “filed” should be “field” (Reviewer 4).
