## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores using LLMs (GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus) to generate synthetic Adult Attachment Interview data. AI agents with profiles and childhood memories are simulated; their responses are used to train classifiers for attachment style prediction. Models are evaluated on a transcript dataset from 9 humans labeled by mental health professionals. Training on synthetic data alone achieves performance comparable to training on human data. A standardization method using unlabeled human data improves alignment of synthetic and real embeddings. The work addresses data scarcity and privacy in mental health, and is cost-efficient.

### Strengths
- Novel application of LLMs to generate synthetic psychological data (AAI), offering a creative solution to data shortage in clinical psychology (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The proposed framework effectively addresses data scarcity and privacy issues (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Introduction of “childhood memories” via RAG to increase diversity of synthetic profiles (Reviewer 3).
- The method is cost-efficient (Reviewer 2).
- Focus on a significant and interesting topic: mental health (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Extremely small validation set** (9 labeled interviews) in a 1536-dimensional embedding space (text-embedding-3-small). This makes ROC‑AUC values potentially unreliable; sample size likely insufficient to capture the complexity of attachment theory. Statistical significance tests for performance differences are missing; no power calculation was reported. (Reviewers 1, 2 – via questions).
2. **Lack of quality control for synthetic data**: No criteria for accepting/rejecting generated interviews based on clinical validity. No evidence that generated interviews match real clinical patterns; no validation from attachment theory experts. The model may be learning superficial text patterns rather than actual attachment dynamics. (Reviewers 1, 3 – via questions).
3. **Very high cosine similarity among GPT‑4 generated content (0.95–0.98)**; both models show unusually high similarities. This indicates potential memorization or low diversity, failing to reflect the real-world variation in human responses. (Reviewer 1).
4. **Small context window of 4 messages** appears arbitrarily chosen; clinical attachment interviews typically require longer sequences to establish reliable patterns. (Reviewer 1).
5. **Unclear hyperparameter tuning**: Use of 500 estimators in Extra Trees classifier and temperature settings (0.7, 0.5 for sampling). No systematic tuning or ablation studies reported. (Reviewer 1).
6. **Performance metrics per attachment style** not clearly presented; only aggregate results are shown. (Reviewer 1 – via questions).
7. **Only one embedding model** (OpenAI text-embedding-3-small) used; no comparison with other embedding models to assess robustness. (Reviewer 1).
8. **Missing experiments on open-source models** (e.g., Llama, Mistral, QwenLM) – experiments use only closed-source GPT‑4 and Claude 3. (Reviewer 2 – score 3, more critical on this point).
9. **Baseline comparison issue**: The model trained on human data is not based on GPT or Claude; for a fair comparison of training data quality, the same base model should be trained on human data. (Reviewer 2 – score 3, minority view with stronger emphasis on this criticism).
10. **Theoretical basis for using childhood memories** is not solid enough; the paper should justify why childhood memories (rather than other features) are chosen and how they are clinically grounded. (Reviewer 3).
11. **Quality of generated childhood memories** (diversity, level of detail, objectivity) cannot be guaranteed; no methods are employed to improve or ensure their quality. (Reviewer 3 – via questions).
12. **Task value is debatable**; the overall contribution and ability boundary of synthetic data in a high-stakes domain like mental health require more exploration and discussion (Reviewers 2, 3).
13. **Need for experiments on more datasets** to demonstrate generalizability (Reviewer 2 – via questions).
14. **How to ensure the model does not generate harmful or clinically inappropriate responses** – this safety concern is unaddressed (Reviewer 1 – via question).
