## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies LLM performance in causal discovery, focusing on three research questions: (RQ1) memorization vs. generalization, (RQ2) impact of incorrect causal relations in pre-training data, (RQ3) role of context. Using open-source models OLMo-7b-Instruct and BLOOM-7b1, with access to their pre-training corpora, the authors construct synthetic and real-world datasets (ConceptNet, CausalNet) and find that LLMs rely heavily on memorization, generalize poorly to novel/rare relations, are sensitive to incorrect data, and are strongly influenced by context. The study is one of the first to use open training data to directly examine memorization effects, but opinions on contribution vary widely (scores 3,8,5,3,3,5). Positive reviewers highlight the useful negative results and open-model analysis; negative reviewers cite predictable conclusions, weak theoretical grounding, and methodological flaws that reduce the work to “causal sentence validation” rather than causal discovery.

### Strengths
- **Open-source model choice**: Using OLMo and BLOOM (both <10B parameters) with open pre-training data allows more rigorous claims about memorization than prior work on closed models (Reviewers 2, 3, 6). This is a key novelty (R6).
- **Relevant and important research questions**: The three RQs (memorization vs. generalization, effect of incorrect causal data, role of context) address significant aspects of LLM-based causal discovery (R3, R5, R6).
- **Negative result is valuable**: The finding that LLMs generalize poorly to new/rare causal relations provides a useful corrective in a field where model abilities are often overstated (R2).
- **Informative findings**: Results show high frequency of correct causal data improves LLM performance, while conflicting/negative contexts degrade accuracy, offering practical training guidance (R5).
- **Synthetic data innovation**: Including synthetic, nonce relations (e.g., “blaonge causes goloneke”) to test generalization without memorization bias is interesting and novel (R5, R1).
- **Good experimental design and analysis**: Experiments are well-designed, with a mixture of real and synthetic datasets and sufficient analysis of the three RQs (R1, R6). Results support the conclusions (R1).
- **Presentation is easy to read** (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Predictable conclusions / limited novelty**: Several reviewers feel the conclusions are predictable given the nature of supervised training, offering limited new insight (R1: “predictable conclusions”; R4: paper looks rushed and could dive deeper; R5: lacks formal theory).
- **Lack of theoretical framework**: The paper does not provide a formal definition or quantification of “memorization” vs. “generalization” in causal discovery (R5). There is no mathematical model for how incorrect data or noise affects predictive accuracy or stability (R5). This limits analysis to surface-level correlation between occurrence frequency and F1 (R5, R4).
- **Methodological concerns with synthetic data**:
    - Synthetic relations are generated pairwise, not from a graph structure (no edge density, degree, Markov equivalence class). No data variables are generated (only sentences like “blaonge causes goloneke”), so the work deviates from causal discovery proper into “causal sentence validating” (R3).
    - The second causal query (via textual order) is problematic because event order, time, and causal direction are generally different, potentially introducing systematic bias (R4). Reviewer 4 says this makes “no sense.”
- **Limited model scope**: The study only tests open-source models under 10B parameters. It lacks comparison with larger open models (OLMo 65B, BLOOM 176B) to test scale effects (R2), and with closed models like GPT-4 (R5). This restricts applicability and general claims about LLMs broadly (R5, R2).
- **Context analysis is unsatisfying**: The treatment of pairwise causal relations as the basic unit fails to capture causal interactions (e.g., “rain causes flooding” attenuated by “but not in cities that have good drainage” is better modeled as a graph with multiple parents). The observed performance drop due to “negative context” may reflect this mismatch rather than a true LLM limitation (R2).
- **Missing comparisons and related work**:
    - No comparison with classical causal discovery algorithms like PC (R3).
    - Literature review omits functional causal model methods (e.g., LiNGAM) and recent work such as “Understanding causality with large language models”, “Is Knowledge All Large Language Models Needed for Causal Reasoning?”, “LLM4Causal”, “Answering Causal Questions with Augmented LLMs” (R3).
    - Fails to discuss prior evaluations like Gao et al. (2023) that identify causal hallucinations and implicit causal relations (R1). This makes the paper’s RQ1 conclusions potentially invalid for implicit causal relations (R1).
- **Data construction limitations**:
    - The datasets are artificially constructed and do not leverage existing public causal datasets such as EventStoryLine v0.9 (ESC) or other causal/non-causal event pair resources (R1). This would make RQ2 and RQ3 more convincing (R1).
    - The construction could also cross-reference the pre-training data of OLMo to build two datasets (as in A2), but the use of LoRA fine-tuning may not preserve the original model’s ability (R6).
- **Over-claiming scope**: The paper converts causal discovery into binary classification tasks (does A cause B? / full CDAG?), which is not the same as iterative DAG structure refinement (e.g., PC algorithm). The claim should be narrowed to “causal relation verification” rather than causal discovery (R6, R3).
- **Presentation and rigor issues**:
    - Writing style reads like a report or coursework rather than a scientific paper; claims of “ability” lack definitions or quantification (R6).
    - Table 1 (p.9) lacks error bars or standard errors, and it is unclear what affirmative ratio is “good enough” (R4).
    - Minor style issues: double quotes around “causal parrots” (lines 047, 104, 243); captions and axis tickers too small (R4).

### Additional Criticisms / Questions from Reviewers (Merged into Weaknesses)
- For RQ1, conclusions may not hold for implicit causal relationships (as studied in Gao et al.) (R1).
- For RQ2, could the findings be validated using public datasets like EventStoryLine v0.9? (R1).
- For RQ3, should use public datasets with contextual information (e.g., ESC) (R1).
- Can the results be replicated on larger open models to show independence from scale? (R2).
- How would hybrid models combining statistical causal discovery with LLMs perform? How does performance differ between synthetic and real data? Do larger synthetic datasets change the memorization/generalization conclusion? Can data augmentation help LLMs handle conflicting information? (R5).
- The experimental design could build two sets of data by cross-referencing the pre-training data, but fine-tuning with LoRA may not preserve original ability (R6).
- The conversion of causal discovery to classification oversimplifies the task; authors should narrow the scope of their claims to reflect actual contribution (R6).
