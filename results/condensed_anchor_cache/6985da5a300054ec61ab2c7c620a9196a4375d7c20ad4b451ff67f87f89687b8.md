## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization for regression models, an underexplored problem relative to classification. The authors analyze how Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is affected by covariate shift, characterizing OOD risk via the eigenspectra of source and target data. They propose a lightweight post-processing method (SpAR – Spectral Adapted Regressor) that projects the last linear layer of a pre-trained neural regression model onto a subspace selected to trade off bias and variance on the target distribution. The method assumes access to unlabeled target data for subspace estimation. Evaluation is performed on synthetic data and three real-world datasets (two tabular: CommunitiesAndCrime and Skillcraft; one image-based: PovertyMap-WILDS), comparing with ERM and C-Mixup baselines. A reviewer (rating 8) finds the theoretical decomposition clean and the method intuitive; another (rating 5) is less convinced by the experimental results and evaluation scope.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important and relatively unexplored problem: OOD generalization specifically for regression. (R1, R3, R4)
- Provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of OLS under covariate shift, with a clean decomposition of OOD error into bias and variance contributions tied to the eigenspectrum (R1, R2, R3, R4). The decomposition is not surprising but offers an intuitive framework (R4).
- The proposed projection technique (SpAR) is sound and the estimation strategy for finite samples is also sound (R1, R2, R4). The resulting method makes intuitive sense and is visually supported by Figure 2 (R3).
- The paper is generally well-written, easy to follow, and the exposition is good (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Simulations and empirical examples are careful and cover a range of settings (R4). The method works well with data augmentation techniques such as C-Mixup (R1).

### Weaknesses
- The method requires access to unlabeled test (target) data to estimate the target covariance matrix and perform spectral adaptation. This assumption may not hold in many real-world scenarios; the sensitivity to distributional differences between the adaptation set and the true target distribution is not explored (R1).
- The experimental evaluation is limited. Only three real-world datasets are used, two of which are tabular with small networks (single hidden layer) (R3). The method should be evaluated on more image-based regression datasets, e.g., from the TMLR 2023 paper “How Reliable is Your Regression Model’s Uncertainty Under Real-World Distribution Shifts?” (R3).
- Comparisons with other methods are insufficient. The paper lacks baselines such as other domain adaptation techniques (e.g., adversarial training, kernel mean matching for regression), PCR (which may perform well in this setting), and OOD generalization methods for the PovertyMap-WILDS dataset (which is an OOD task, not classical domain adaptation) (R1, R2, R4). Additionally, the method is not compared to alternative post-processing or representation-learning approaches that could also adapt the representation function (R2).
- The performance gains of SpAR over ERM are small and often not statistically significant. In Table 2 and Table 3, accounting for variance, the improvement is marginal; in Table 2 ERM+SpAR is not statistically different from ERM (R3, R4). The results in Table 3 are odd: all other baselines degrade performance compared to ERM (R3). The significance of the improvement needs more discussion (R3).
- For PovertyMap-WILDS, the performance variance exceeds the difference between the proposed method and the top baseline (R2).
- Several technical details require clarification:
  - What is the expectation over in the decomposition? Are X and Z fixed or random? The proof in the appendix seems to treat them as fixed, but the main text is ambiguous (R4).
  - Theorem 3 states S* is weakly better than OLS, but it is not established that S* is the projection set minimizing expected loss. The reasoning for focusing on S* rather than the optimal set should be explained (R4).
  - How is the hyperparameter α chosen? Is there a data-driven or optimal way to select it? (R4)
  - Why is a sample split not necessary when using the plug-in variance estimator on the same data? (R4)
  - The computational cost of Algorithm 1 (SpAR) is not discussed in the main paper. How does it scale with large X or Z in terms of time and memory? (R1, R3)
  - The paper does not decompose MSE into bias and variance in the simulations to verify the theoretical trade-off (R4).
  - What would change if Z is noisy (e.g., conditionally independent errors like X)? The theory likely goes through, but this is not addressed (R4).
- Section 3 (especially 3.4) is difficult to follow in parts (R3). Minor: typo “the the variance” in Section 3.4 (R3).
