## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper fine-tunes LLMs (Pythia, Llama, Mistral) on small synthetic datasets (≤64 strings) generated from a unigram model and a nonrecursive PCFG. It analyzes training/validation loss curves to define a “learning by rote” (memorization) phase that begins when training loss diverges from test loss (threshold 1.05×) and a “learning with understanding” (generalization) phase that ends when test loss is minimized. Key findings: (a) memorization often begins before generalization ends; (b) sequential fine-tuning on a second sample from the same distribution triggers forgetting of the first sample and restarts generalization; (c) lower-entropy distributions are easier to generalize on, higher-entropy distributions encourage memorization.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles the challenging problem of disentangling memorization and understanding in LLMs.
- Interesting experiments on how data entropy affects the learning mode: increasing entropy increases propensity to memorize, lower entropy promotes generalization.
- One reviewer (score 5) particularly highlights: the new way to quantify memorization as a finer-grain distinction beyond the traditional bias-variance trade-off; the observation at lines 418–420 is “interesting”; the experiments consider various aspects of generalization vs. memorization.
- Concrete findings: memorization begins before generalization ends; continued training on a different set restarts generalization and causes forgetting; entropy clearly impacts ease of learning.

### Weaknesses
- **Overfitting reframing**: The phenomenon described as “learning by rote” is essentially overfitting (U-shaped validation loss). The paper does not clearly distinguish the two or provide a definition that adds new insight over standard ML knowledge. A small model pretrained on WikiText-103 for many epochs exhibits the same pattern.
- **Tiny dataset sizes**: Training sets are always ≤64 strings, making conclusions potentially artifacts of small data. One reviewer questions whether the same behavior would hold with >10000 samples in a more realistic setting (e.g., hash-key prefix prompting). Another notes that despite the claimed large output space (l. 196–200), train/test loss gaps remain small, possibly because the simple CFG grammar limits complexity.
- **Missing citations and discussion**: Prior work using CFG data for thorough experiments [1] exists; comprehensive studies of generalization [2,3] are not cited. The second experiment (Figure 4) is closely connected to continual learning but has no discussion of related results. The paper also does not address hierarchical/compositional generalization supported (or not) by the syntax.
- **Unclear or insufficiently justified central definitions**: The terms “learning by rote” and “learning with understanding” are defined only briefly (l. 98–99) and more clearly only on page 6. No justification is given for why these specific definitions (e.g., 1.05× loss ratio) are correct. The start/end of these phases (l. 73–74: “start of learning by rote”, “end of learning by understanding”) are not explained—when does understanding begin? One reviewer suggests using single terms instead of synonyms.
- **Disagreements with interpretation of results** (minority reviewer at score 5 explicitly expresses some):
  - Claim that forgetting “destroys” all knowledge of D1 (l. 422–424): catastrophic forgetting is overstated—loss on D1 is still lower than on D_test, so not fully destroyed.
  - “Destroy the ability to generalize” (l. 426): no direct observation shown.
  - Speculation on differences between human and machine cognition (l. 428–429) goes too far beyond the evidence.
  - “Easier to memorize with higher entropy” (l. 499–500): phrasing is inaccurate; it should be “encouraged” because generalization is harder, not because memorization is inherently easier.
  - “Measures of memorization must…” (l. 521–522): no justification for why this is a “must.”
- **Methodology concerns**:
  - The study claims to investigate *how* models memorize (l. 159) but only examines learning curves, not internal mechanisms.
  - Synthetic data is too trivial—it lacks semantic-level properties (e.g., executable formal languages like sorting, functions) that could disentangle true understanding from rote. The findings are essentially the same for unigram and PCFG, so no insight depends on data distribution details. A reference is made to another work [arxiv 2305.13673] using richer formal languages.
  - The experiments do not provide quantitative relationships (e.g., between information amount in the pretrain corpus and model parameters needed to alleviate rote, or how to estimate that information). The conclusion that test loss divergence indicates rote learning is just a restatement of overfitting, lacking practical significance.
  - Memorization begins at epoch 6 (l. 98), but modern LLMs often train for only one epoch—how does this difference affect the conclusions?
  - “Takes a very long time” (l. 317) is unclear—decoding time? What does “one must generate test and training data” (l. 370–371) mean in practice?
  - If the defined memorization term (l. 356–358) becomes negative, how should that be interpreted?
  - The paper does not supply details of the PCFG design, and “training size n=8, n=64” refers to number of training items (confirmed).
