## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates the use of large language models (LLMs) for solving Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) problems. It examines whether LLMs, without additional training or heuristic guidance, can generate valid collision-free paths in environments of varying complexity (empty room, room map, maze map from standard benchmarks). Experiments show that LLMs succeed in simple scenarios but fail as problem complexity increases. The authors attribute failures to three limitations: reasoning capabilities, context length constraints, and difficulty understanding spatial map information. They propose future directions for improving LLM-based MAPF solutions.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-structured and clearly explains why LLMs might appear suitable for MAPF (reasoning capabilities, large context).  
- It addresses a unique and timely application of LLMs to multi-agent coordination, specifically MAPF.  
- Experiments are conducted across multiple benchmark maps of varying complexity, helping to identify specific failure points and general limitations of LLMs in this domain.  
- The breakdown of LLM limitations (reasoning, context length, spatial understanding) provides useful insights for future improvements.  
- Different prompt styles and input representations (e.g., text-only, multimodal) are compared, contributing valuable insights into how prompt structure affects LLM performance.  
- Some illustrative failure cases are presented.  
- The analysis and discussion could serve as a reference for future research directions in MAPF with LLMs.  
- Any work that contributes to understanding the limitations of LLMs is important given their current surge in popularity.

### Weaknesses
- **No comparison with traditional MAPF methods:** The paper does not compare the LLM-based approach with established algorithms (e.g., heuristic search, SAT, RL). Without such baselines, it is unclear whether the observed limitations are unique to LLMs or whether LLMs offer any concrete advantages.  
- **Success metrics are too narrow:** Only collision-freeness is measured; path quality metrics (makespan, path length, optimality) are not reported. This gives an incomplete picture of LLM efficacy.  
- **Experimental details are insufficient:** The problem statement, prompts, collision checker, and LLM outputs are not specified clearly enough in the main paper (Tables 1–4 lack explanation). Figure 4 shows a “validated solution” where Agent 1 ends at (0,1) instead of (3,1) and Agent 2 ends at (0,3) instead of (2,0) – the agents do not reach their goals, so it is unclear why this is counted as valid.  
- **The findings largely reiterate known LLM limitations** (context length, reasoning challenges) without introducing MAPF-specific insights. The paper does not clarify what distinct challenges in MAPF go beyond general LLM weaknesses.  
- **Prompt design is not justified:** The choice of prompt (including the SBS method) is presented without evidence that it is the best approach. It is unclear whether the observed failures are universal or specific to this prompting strategy. The authors note that SBS keeps context shorter, but its advantages and downsides (e.g., impact on global optimality) are not discussed.  
- **The claim that LLMs “have not succeeded yet” for MAPF is not sufficiently supported.** The experiments are restricted to simple/moderate cases, and the paper acknowledges LLMs succeed on simpler problems. Without exploring more sophisticated LLM-based architectures (e.g., memory modules, decentralized planners, hybrid systems), the conclusion about failure in harder cases may be premature.  
- **Several claims are unsupported or questionable:**  
  - Line 041: Claiming previous work “barely covers multi-agent planning” while ignoring relevant work (Chen et al. 2023b, Agashe 2023).  
  - Line 047–049: Listing prior methods but omitting mention of LLMs in MAPF contexts.  
  - Section on “Understanding Obstacle Locations” makes broad claims based on a single example.  
  - Line 363: Claiming “people barely provide any such information online since people have common knowledge of what to do with a map” is unfounded.  
  - Line 428: Claiming no tool use, yet an external collision checker is used (and not described in sufficient detail).  
- **Methodological issues:**  
  - The paper only considers centralized planning and uses the history of all agents in prompts, quickly hitting context limits. However, since the environment is Markov, decisions could be based solely on current states – this alternative is not explored.  
  - The paper does not compare single-agent vs. multi-agent performance, despite noting that LLMs succeed in single-agent planning. Explaining why multi-agent fails while single-agent succeeds would provide deeper insight.  
- **The paper reads more like a research proposal than a definitive study** – it identifies challenges and suggests future directions but lacks extensive experimental results demonstrating effective solutions or innovations.  
- **Significant grammar and formatting issues:** Examples include line 323 (“…not a model whose capacity in 8K length also works…”), line 371 (“…which is killed by using much more total number of steps than it should”), and non-standard reference formatting.  
- **Intuitive and uninteresting findings:** The observation that success rate drops as problem size grows (lines 424–426) is expected and not scientifically novel.  
- **Line 102–103:** The authors state “we hope LLMs can be an alternative…” but their own results show it did not work; reporting the observation rather than the original hope would be more appropriate.  
- **Disagreement among reviewers:** While one reviewer (R3) suggests the paper could serve as a useful reference for future work, others (R2, R4) argue that the findings lack novelty and the evidence is insufficient to support the main claim. All reviewers assigned the same overall score (3), indicating a consistent assessment of the paper’s current state.
