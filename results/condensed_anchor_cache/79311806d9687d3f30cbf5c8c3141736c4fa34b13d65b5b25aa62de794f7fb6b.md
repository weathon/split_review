## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Chapter-to-Chapter (CH2CH) translation setting, moving beyond sentence- and paragraph-aligned document translation. The authors curate a new Chinese-English literary dataset, JAM, comprising 4.2K parallel chapter pairs from 132 books (~2,000 words per pair on average). They evaluate seq2seq models, two-stage fine-tuned models (WMT sentence-level then JAM), and zero-shot LLMs (GPT-4). The best small models use two-stage fine-tuning, while GPT-4 is surprisingly effective, apparently outperforming fine-tuned models. The paper analyses challenges such as repetition, long-context handling, and decoding strategies.

### Strengths
- Introduces a Chapter-to-Chapter translation dataset (JAM) that provides new resources for document-level translation research, with no assumption of 1:1 sentence/paragraph alignment.
- Studies an important and relatively underexplored topic: document-level machine translation in the literary domain, which has downstream applications.
- Offers a thorough analysis of the main challenges in CH2CH translation, including repetition, decoding strategies, and the effect of token slicing.
- Contributes a dataset of 4.2K parallel English–Chinese chapter pairs (average ~2K words per pair), filling a gap in literary-domain resources.
- Includes interesting ablations: enc-dec vs. decoder-only models, comparison of decoding algorithms, slicing by token repetition amounts, and chapter-level vs. sentence-level translation.
- Minority reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 6) explicitly considers the shift from sentence/paragraph alignments to chapter-level a significant advancement; this contrasts with the lower scores (3) from the other two reviewers.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of substantial methodological innovation:** The repetition-penalty and redundant-clause-removal strategies are conventional; no major modeling contribution is offered.
- **Technical contribution feels incremental relative to prior work:** Par3 (Thai et al., EMNLP 2022) also operates in a literary domain with many more language pairs beyond en/zh. While chapter-level alignment is a nice addition, it is not empirically clear how much this adds over paragraph-level alignment.
- **No human evaluation:** Metrics such as BLEU and BLEURT are poor proxies for document-level translation quality (citations [1–3] provided). Figure 8 shows a very small difference between chapter-level and sentence-level translations, suggesting the automatic metrics may not capture unique discourse-level aspects. The authors should use human evaluation aspects from Kocmi et al. (2023) [4].
- **No qualitative examples or analysis:** Neither the dataset nor the model outputs are illustrated with examples; this is critical for gauging dataset quality and understanding where the models fail.
- **Limited focus on zero-shot pretrained LLMs:** Zero-shot GPT-4 appears to be the strongest system (Fig. 7 / Table 3), yet the paper does not explore: (a) whether specialized long-context models (e.g., Gemini 1.5 Pro) perform better; (b) whether Chinese-focused LLMs outperform English-focused ones; (c) whether pretrained LLMs use a 1:1 paragraph/sentence mapping or perform discourse-level operations; (d) whether GPT-4o or Gemini 1.5 Pro solve critical errors listed in [4]. The authors also do not specify which GPT-4 variant was used; a 2048-token segmentation would cut out ~90% of longer chapters (e.g., Robinson Crusoe).
- **Missing comparisons:** No comparison with commercial translation systems (e.g., Google Translate, Bing Translator) is reported.  
- **Dataset bias/limitations not discussed:** Potential biases inherent in a dataset derived from literary works should be addressed, as well as plans to extend to other domains or languages.
- **Unanswered questions overlapping with criticisms:**  
  – What are the potential biases in the JAM dataset?  
  – How do the proposed methods address the specific challenges of long-context translation?  
  – Can the repetition problem be mitigated through improved decoding algorithms?  
  – Why was segmentation needed for GPT-4 experiments, given that it truncates long chapters severely?  
- **Presentation nit:** The authors should combine Table 3 with Figure 7 for direct comparison of fine-tuned and zero-shot models, and provide the average number of sentences/words per article in Table 2.
