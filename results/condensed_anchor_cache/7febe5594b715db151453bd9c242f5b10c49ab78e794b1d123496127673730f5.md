## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Multi-Resolution Requantization (MReQ), a post-training framework that reduces the frame rate of pre-trained neural audio codecs (NACs) by incorporating a multi-resolution residual vector quantization (MRVQ) module via teacher-student distillation. Based on MReQ, the authors propose HALL-E, an LLM-based TTS model that hierarchically predicts the resulting tokens, achieving a frame rate as low as 8 Hz for the first codec layer. The paper also presents MinutesSpeech, a 40k‑hour filtered speech dataset for training and evaluating minute‑long zero‑shot TTS. Experiments apply the framework to VALL‑E and report improvements in WER and RTF for long‑form synthesis.

### Strengths
- The three main contributions—MReQ for hierarchical codec codes, HALL-E for hierarchical token prediction, and the MinutesSpeech benchmark—are valid and well received. The topic of minute‑long speech synthesis will attract a wide audience.
- The idea is interesting, novel, and well motivated. The MReQ module is complex but diagrams and descriptions are well written; the manuscript is very detailed and post‑training procedures are well documented. With the code release, results appear easily replicable.
- Extensive experiments are performed from scratch, covering both automatic and subjective evaluations for reconstruction and zero‑shot TTS. Results are reported on multiple NAC models (Encodec, SpeechTokenizer), demonstrating that the technique can generalize to pre‑existing codecs.
- Ablation studies on the MRVQ module provide insights into the necessity of each component, justifying their inclusion.
- The dataset curation and preprocessing techniques for MinutesSpeech are well documented and add a valuable resource for long‑context TTS research.
- Results show RTF improvements, confirming that the technique reduces the bottleneck of transformer‑based TTS.

### Weaknesses
- **Complexity and reproducibility.** The MReQ design is excessively complicated: the LRVQ module includes pre‑quantizer, main quantizer, and post‑quantizer plus an additional HSR loss, making it significantly more complex than prior RVQ methods. The multiple RVQ quantizations inside the NAR transformer of HALL‑E add another layer of complexity, reducing accessibility for future research.
- **Missing comparisons.** The paper compares only with VALL‑E. No comparisons are provided with recent zero‑shot TTS baselines, especially non‑autoregressive ones (e.g., Voicebox, E2, VALL‑E 2), which are likely advantageous for long speech. For MReQ reconstruction, important codec models such as DAC and Vocos are not included, making it difficult to assess the true advancement of MReQ in terms of quality, bitrate, and computational cost.
- **Limited scope of MReQ evaluation.** MReQ is only tested on speech reconstruction; no experiments on music, environmental sounds, or other diverse audio are reported. This limits understanding of its generalizability and robustness.
- **Distribution shift concerns.** The paper pretrains NAC models on MinutesSpeech and performs MReQ post‑training on the same data. In practice, researchers will not have access to the pretraining dataset, so distribution shifts may occur when applying MReQ to off‑the‑shelf NAC models (e.g., pretrained Encodec or SpeechTokenizer). An ablation studying this scenario is missing.
- **Questionable evaluation settings.** (a) The use of PESQ for data curation is unclear: PESQ requires a reference and its application for filtering noisy data is not explained. The PESQ score for ground‑truth audio in Table 3 is reported as 4.64, but the presented numbers are much lower. (b) The zero‑shot TTS experiment—whether conducted in a “continual” or “cross‑utterance” setting—is not clearly stated. In Table 9, the increase in speaker similarity with prompt length is unusually large, raising the concern that the audio prompt portion of synthesized audio may not have been excluded when computing speaker SIM. (c) DNSMOS is designed for speech cleanness, not naturalness; using a naturalness metric such as UTMOS would be more appropriate.
- **Ablation clarity and triviality.** The qualitative ablation results near line 495 are poorly explained; the statement that the waveform is “unnatural with almost no silence” is unclear without a transcription. The ablation on long audio prompts (“Can HALL‑E handle long audio prompts?”) is trivial—longer prompts are expected to give more context—and a comparison with the VALL‑E model under the same condition would be more informative.
- **Missing ablations and details.** Speaker similarity and MOS scores (preferably UTMOS) are missing for Tables 3 and 10. A breakdown of AR and NAR model RTFs for Table 7 would be helpful. The claim on lines 211‑212 that training NAC models with the MRVQ module from scratch is difficult appears to be contradicted by Table 10, where starting without pretraining does not significantly affect WER or RESQ.
- **Prior art and temporal coherence.** The concept of time‑varying audio codes is not new (cf. Variable‑rate Discrete Representation Learning, 2021). Temporal coherence and acoustic fidelity remain a challenge, as evidenced by declining SIM scores. The model still struggles with spontaneous speech.
- **Minor technical errors.** (a) Equation 2 should be \(x_l = x_{l-1} - \tilde{z}_l\); similarly, Equation 9 is likely incorrect. (b) In the implementation details (p. 4, lines 199‑201), references to Figures 2a, 2b, 2c are mismatched. (c) The statement on lines 485‑486 about “both losses and post‑training” probably means “both losses and pre‑training” given the second row of Table 10. (d) In Table 8, the upsampling ratio should be explicitly described instead of simply listing “HALL‑E”.
- **Dataset details.** The split design in Table 2 and the description in Section 7.1 appear arbitrary and over‑designed; it is unclear whether the training splits are deduplicated from each other.
