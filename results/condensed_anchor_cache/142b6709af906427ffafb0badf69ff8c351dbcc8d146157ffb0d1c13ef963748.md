- Scores: 5, 3, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SubTuning, a parameter-efficient finetuning method that greedily selects a subset of layers to train while keeping the rest frozen. Layers are chosen by finetuning each candidate block individually on a held-out validation split and then greedily adding the best-performing block. Experiments on several vision models (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, ViT-B/16) and datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Flowers102, Caltech101, VTAB-1k, DMLAB, CIFAR-10-C) show that SubTuning often matches or outperforms full finetuning and linear probing, especially in low-data or corrupted settings, and can enable efficient multi-task inference.

### Strengths
- **Simple and broadly applicable.** Reviewers agree the method is easy to understand and can be applied to any architecture/domain. It can also be combined with other parameter-efficient methods (e.g., LoRA, Head2Toe). (R1, R3, R4)
- **Insightful finetuning profiles.** The observation that different layers (or blocks) contribute variably to performance depending on the downstream task, pretraining method, and architecture is interesting and visually well-presented. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Comprehensive experiments.** The paper provides abundant results from multiple perspectives (low data, corruption, distribution shift, multi-task efficiency) across several datasets and architectures. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Problem well-motivated.** Parameter-efficient finetuning and understanding which layers to update is practically relevant. (R2, R4)
- **Clear presentation.** The paper is well-organized, easy to follow, and the visualizations help understanding. (R2, R3, R4)
- **Performance gains in challenging settings.** SubTuning outperforms baselines under data scarcity, corruption, and distribution shifts. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Potential for efficient multi-task inference.** The initial discussion of inference-time savings when deploying multiple SubTuned models is interesting, though not fully supported experimentally (see Weaknesses). (R1)

### Weaknesses
*Overlap/divergence from prior work (especially surgical fine-tuning)*
- Several reviewers note that the core idea—selectively finetuning a subset of layers for better transfer—was already explored in “Surgical Fine-Tuning” (Lee et al., 2022) and other work. The paper’s abstract claim “not all layers are created equal” is nearly identical to that of Lee et al. (R1, R2, R4). R1 and R4 specifically ask for a clear differentiation; R1 suggests the only new aspect is greedy selection of *non‑contiguous* blocks, while R4 argues SubTuning should be framed as an improvement to surgical FT, not a separate definition.
- Missing comparisons with LP‑FT (Kumar et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2023), layer‑wise adaptive learning rates (Ko et al., 2022), gradual unfreezing (Howard & Ruder, 2018), and neural architecture search for parameter‑efficient transfer (Lawton et al., 2023). (R2, R3, R4)
- Missing citation of “Are All Layers Created Equal?” (Zhang et al., 2019). (R1)
- R2 notes that prior work uses more advanced selection techniques (policy networks, genetic algorithms) but SubTuning’s simple accuracy‑based profiling is not generalizable.

*Methodological concerns*
- The choice of a “block” as the atomic finetuning unit is not justified; why not individual layers or sub‑layer portions? (R1)
- The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) is disconnected from the algorithm and fails to explain why low‑parameter layers are not always chosen. R1 notes the bound would suggest always picking the layer with fewest parameters, contradicting empirical results. R3 agrees the theory does not link well to the method.
- The threshold ε in the greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) is a critical hyperparameter with no discussion of how it is set or its robustness. (R4)
- The paper claims SubTuning saves FLOPs, but if an early block is selected, FLOPs are identical to full finetuning. Efficiency experiments report inference time rather than FLOPs or memory (bits), as promised. (R1)
- SubTuning requires multiple rounds of finetuning and validation, making it computationally expensive relative to simpler baselines (full FT, LP‑FT, surgical FT). R4 requests a concrete cost table (total epochs/FLOPs). (R4)
- The multi‑task inference setting (Section 4) lacks experimental support. No multi‑task experiments are shown; the MTL computation graph and Siamese subtuning details are unclear (e.g., parameter sharing, whether new classifiers are added). (R1, R3, R4)
- The definition of “classification margin” in active learning is ambiguous (maximum softmax probability?). (R4)

*Experiments and results*
- **Limited models/datasets.** Only a few ResNet and ViT variants are tested; most evaluations use small datasets (CIFAR‑10, Flowers102, Caltech101, DMLAB). It is unclear if SubTuning scales to larger models or more challenging distribution shifts (e.g., WILDS). (R2, R4)
- **Results on VTAB‑1k (Table 1) are restricted to 1k training samples.** Reviewers would like to see full‑dataset performance (e.g., CIFAR‑100 full), where baselines achieve much higher accuracy. (R3, R4). Furthermore, the split protocol for VTAB‑1k is not explained. (R1, R3)
- **Standard deviations are missing from Table 1.** (R1)
- **Full‑dataset behavior is unclear.** R4 asks whether SubTuning selects all layers when data is abundant, effectively becoming full finetuning. R1 notes that results in Figure 4 suggest the algorithm may pick earlier layers as data increases, but the intuition is not explored.
- **Interpretation of figures.** In Figure 1, the difference between finetuning Block 1 (94%) and Block 14 (95.5%) is negligible. In Figure 3, the improvement from adding a second block (e.g., B2 to B14) appears marginal, suggesting a single late block already does most of the work. (R4)
- **Missing baselines.** The following are not compared: LP‑FT, gradual unfreezing (ULMFiT, targeted transfer), layer‑wise learning rate tuning, and surgical finetuning on ViT‑B/16 (which is applicable, contrary to the paper’s claim). (R2, R3, R4)
- **Distribution shift evaluation is limited to image corruptions (CIFAR‑10‑C).** No results on natural shifts (e.g., WILDS datasets) are provided. (R4)
- The claim that SubTuning challenges the conclusion of Lee et al. (2022) (that the first layers suffice for input‑level shifts) is not well supported, because SubTuning operates at a finer granularity (blocks within layers) and the second selected block is often B1 (early). (R4)
- **No analysis of which second/third blocks are selected** and how much value they add cumulatively; the method remains a black box without understanding why specific combinations (e.g., B8 and B14) work. (R4)
- **Finetuning profile accuracy (Section 2) should be specified as cross‑validation accuracy.** (R1) Also, the amount of data used per profile is not reported. (R4)
- **Optimizer details for ViT‑B/16 are missing.** (R4)
- **Active learning experiment (Figure 7) reports inference time instead of FLOPs/bits**, and the claim of FLOP savings is unclear if the first block is chosen. (R1)

*Minority reviewer contrast*
Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 5) are generally positive about the method’s simplicity and potential, while acknowledging the overlap with surgical finetuning and requesting more analysis/experiments. Reviewers 2 and 4 (score 3) are more critical, emphasizing limited novelty, missing comparisons, and insufficient scaling/diversity of experiments. All four agree on the need for better differentiation from prior work and more thorough experimental validation.
