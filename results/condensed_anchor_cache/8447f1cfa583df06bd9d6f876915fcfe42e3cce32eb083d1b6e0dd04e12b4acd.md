## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Symb4CO, the first symbolic discovery framework for learning branching heuristics in exact combinatorial optimization solvers. Instead of using a neural network directly as the branching policy, Symb4CO employs a deep symbolic optimization approach: a neural network searches over high-dimensional discrete space to produce compact, interpretable symbolic expressions. The learned policies are compiled for fast CPU-based deployment. Experiments show that Symb4CO’s CPU-only policies achieve comparable performance to previous GPU-based state-of-the-art methods while requiring only a few training instances (e.g., ten) and offering one-line interpretable rules. The work aims to combine the strengths of data-driven ML and human-designed compact policies.

### Strengths
- **Novel and important idea**: Using deep symbolic optimization to discover branching heuristics is a novel approach that addresses a key problem—combining ML’s data-driven power with the compactness, reliability, and interpretability of human-designed policies (R1, R2, R3).
- **Substantial performance gains**: Symb4CO consistently outperforms existing ML-based and human-designed baselines, including GPU-based approaches, on the tested benchmarks (R1, R2). One reviewer (score 8) particularly emphasized the thorough experimental evaluation.
- **Training efficiency** (R2, R3): The method requires only a few training instances (e.g., ten) and uses reinforcement learning to efficiently search for symbolic policies, making training data-efficient.
- **Inference efficiency** (R2, R3): The learned symbolic policies run on a single CPU core, avoiding the need for GPU resources, which is practical for many deployment scenarios.
- **Interpretability** (R2, R3): The resulting policies are compact one-line mathematical expressions, addressing the “black-box” limitation of typical ML-based branching heuristics.
- **Thorough experimental evaluation** (R2): The paper compares Symb4CO against a wide range of baselines (RPB, GNN-based methods, human-designed policies) on multiple benchmark sets.

### Weaknesses
- **Manual feature extraction** (R1, R2, R3): The approach relies on manually selected 91 features. It is unclear how these features were chosen, whether they are sufficiently expressive across different benchmarks, and how much human effort is required—this partly undermines the claimed training efficiency. No ablation is performed over different feature subsets, nor is the masking technique (80% masking mentioned in Sec. 3.2) reconciled with the eventual use of all 91 features (Sec. 4.1).
- **Training instance selection** (R1, R2): The paper does not adequately explain how the single training instance for Table 2 or the ten instances in Sec. 5.1 were selected, nor how representative they are of test/validation instances. Standard deviations are missing in Table 2; the results may be sensitive to this choice.
- **FSB and RPB analysis gap** (R2): The fitness function is based on full strong branching (FSB) scores, yet FSB itself performs poorly in Table 1—the paper should explain this discrepancy and the computational overhead that makes FSB impractical for direct use during inference. Similarly, the paper compares against RPB but does not analyze why RPB performs worse than Symb4CO, especially given RPB’s warm-start mechanism; a step-by-step computational comparison would help.
- **Evaluation metric justification** (R2): The choice of 1-shifted geometric mean in Section 5 is not justified; the paper should explain why it is preferred over standard mean or geometric mean.
- **Interpretability claims need clarity** (R2): The paper does not clearly define what interpretability means in this context or how the learned one-line expressions provide more insight than human-designed scoring functions (e.g., in RPB). A concrete example comparing branching decisions across methods is missing.
- **Syntactic correctness of RNN output** (R2): The paper does not detail how the RNN output is constrained to be syntactically valid (e.g., via length constraints, inverse operator, non-triviality). No ablation is performed on the length constraint or its impact on performance.
- **Unclear fitness calculation and Figure 2** (R2): Equation 2 and Part 3 of Figure 2 are insufficiently explained. The paper should provide concrete values or examples of how fitness is computed and averaged over the expert dataset.
- **RNN suitability for long sequences** (R2): RNNs are known to struggle with long sequences, but the paper does not address this issue or report the typical length of the learned symbolic expressions.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity** (R2): The paper does not report specific hyperparameters of the sequential model (layers, hidden units, learning rate) or discuss sensitivity to these choices.
- **Scalability** (R2): It is unclear whether the search space of symbolic policies grows with problem size and how Symb4CO scales to larger instances.
- **Missing branching invocation detail** (R1): The paper does not specify whether the learned branching heuristic is called at every node or only at the root/top node.
- **Training cost not reported** (R1): The computational cost of training (time, resources) is not provided.
- **GPU vs. CPU motivation** (R3): The paper argues for CPU-only deployment as an advantage, but experimental results show GPU-based GNN methods are often faster. Given fast GPU-CPU interaction on modern hardware, the motivation for excluding GPU-enabled models is not fully justified; the field may accept more complex models if they bring speed.
- **Lack of transferability experiments** (R2): The paper does not test whether policies learned on one benchmark generalize to other problem domains within combinatorial optimization.
- **Minor**: The acronym FSB should be expanded in Figure 2, as it has not been introduced by that point (R2).
