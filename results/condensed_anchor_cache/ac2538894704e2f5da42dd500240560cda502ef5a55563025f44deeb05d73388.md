## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces FedDA, a novel adaptive gradient framework for federated constrained optimization. It combines dual averaging with adaptive learning rates, leveraging a mirror descent perspective to aggregate dual states from clients in a common dual space. The instantiation FedDA-MVR uses momentum-based variance reduction and achieves theoretical convergence and communication complexity rates. Experiments on constrained (biomarker identification) and unconstrained (image classification) tasks, under homogeneous and heterogeneous data, demonstrate effectiveness. The method fills a gap in adaptive gradient methods for FL with constraints, and is projection-free. Reviewers generally found the contribution solid (scores 6/6/6, confidence 3/3/4) but noted several weaknesses in presentation, missing details, and experimental fairness.

### Strengths
- Addresses the under-explored problem of constrained federated learning with adaptive gradients; useful for e.g., regularization and biomarker identification.
- FedDA-MVR achieves optimal iteration complexity rates for non-convex stochastic optimization without bounded gradient assumptions.
- The algorithm is projection-free, a notable advantage in constrained optimization.
- FedDA is general: the framework can incorporate multiple adaptive gradient methods.
- Theoretical comparisons (Table 1) and numerical benchmarks (>5 methods, Fig. 3, Fig. 12) provide a clear overview of dual and adaptive federated methods.
- FedDA performs better or on par with existing methods on constrained tasks (biomarker identification) and works well in challenging non-i.i.d. image classification settings (Fig. 12).

### Weaknesses
- The core idea of combining dual averaging with adaptive gradients in FL is conceptually straightforward; the main contribution is the theoretical complexity analysis rather than algorithmic novelty.
- The paper is poorly written and presented, with several missing details:
  - Algorithm 1 lacks line 11, which is referenced for updating the mapping matrix H; this makes it unclear how the adaptive gradient is implemented and how it relates to theoretical claims.
  - The virtual state \(\tilde{x}_t\) in Equation (9) is not defined in the main text, nor is the L2 distance between \(x_t\) and \(\tilde{x}_t\) explained. The connection between the theoretical analysis using \(\tilde{x}_t\) and the actual iterates \(x_\tau\) used in Algorithm 1 is not clarified, leaving a gap in understanding how convergence results translate to practice.
  - In Figures 1 and 2, the third plot from the right is not described. The gain attributed to Lasso regularization is shown, but whether FedDA also outperforms baselines (e.g., FedAvg) in settings where overfitting is not an issue is not discussed.
- The algorithm is computationally more demanding than competitors because it requires an argmin at each iteration. It is unclear whether, under the same computational budget, it achieves similar theoretical and empirical efficiency.
- Assumptions 5.1 and 5.4 are restrictive; they may give an unfair advantage over non-adaptive methods. It is not verified whether Assumption 5.4 holds for the specific momentum-based variance reduction update used in FedDA-MVR.
- Hyperparameter tuning for baselines appears insufficient:
  - FedAvg achieves below 45% test accuracy on homogeneous CIFAR-10 (batch size 16); larger batch sizes or more local steps could yield better performance. The small batch size may unfairly handicap FedAvg. A more thorough hyperparameter search (including local steps and batch size) is needed to establish a reliable baseline.
- The paper should include a table comparing sample and communication complexity with non-adaptive schemes; this comparison is missing.
- Minor presentation issues: MiME paper cited twice; typo “PATHMNIST” in Section 6.1; confusing referencing of algorithm lines.
