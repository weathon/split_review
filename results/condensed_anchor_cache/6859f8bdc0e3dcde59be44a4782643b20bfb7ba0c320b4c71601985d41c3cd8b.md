## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AnyExpress, a lightweight modular framework for audio-driven portrait animation that eliminates the ReferenceNet, reducing trainable parameters by 7×. It trains a plug-and-play audio-motion adapter that works with any base T2I model (e.g., Stable Diffusion + AnimateDiff), enabling free-form control over head pose, dynamic backgrounds, and text-driven editing. A progressive prefix conditioning strategy with anchor alignment is used for temporal consistency in long videos. Identity conditioning is provided by a frozen IP-Adapter-Face. Despite the flexible design, reviewers note significant quality trade-offs.

### Strengths
- **Lightweight and parameter-efficient**: The framework removes the heavy ReferenceNet (7× fewer trainable parameters) and uses a frozen IP-Adapter for identity, making the audio-motion adapter plug-and-play and compatible with custom T2I models (R1, R2, R4).
- **Flexibility and free-form control**: Enables any face pose, dynamic backgrounds, and text-driven modifications, which are difficult for ReferenceNet-based methods (R1, R2, R3). Figures 3 and 5 convincingly show that ReferenceNet cannot handle large head-pose variation, while AnyExpress can (R3).
- **Progressive prefix conditioning with anchor alignment**: Generates temporally consistent frames (no color fading, consistent clothing/textures/identity) as shown in Fig. 1a (last row) (R2).
- **Novel adapter-based approach**: The separation of the motion adapter from the base UNet is novel and facilitates integration with evolving custom models and control adapters (R3, R4). The authors plan to release code, which is commendable (R3).
- **Well-written and illustrated**: The paper is well written; figures are “a delight” (R3). The motivation is clearly presented (R3).

*Note: One reviewer (R3) was notably more positive, stating “I like the flexibility of this approach even if the results are underwhelming,” while other reviewers were more critical of the output quality (R1, R2, R4).*

### Weaknesses
- **Poor visual quality and temporal artifacts**: Facial motion appears unnatural, with significant artifacts, jerky movements, and temporal inconsistencies (e.g., hair appearing/disappearing) in most video results (R1, R2, R3). Subjects appear “smudged” and images can be saturated despite dynamic thresholding (Line 872) (R2, R4). The overall subjective quality is “an order of magnitude worse” than ReferenceNet-based models (R3).
- **Audio-lip synchronization is weak**: Lip sync scores (Table 2) are inferior to EchoMimic (R1). Without video samples it is impossible to judge sync quality (R2); objective temporal lip sync metrics should be provided (R2).
- **Face identity similarity is poor**: Face similarity scores are abnormally low (<0.5), indicating poor identity preservation (R1).
- **Quantitative metrics lag behind competitors**: Pose diversity, Sync C, and Sync D scores are lower than AniPortrait, EchoMimic, and MegaActor (Table 2) (R2). These other methods produce similar quality samples and have better scores, making the claimed advantages unclear (R2).
- **Lack of quantitative ablation studies**: No quantitative ablation for: (a) directly adding audio/text embeddings to SD vs. the proposed audio-motion adapter (R2); (b) Progressive Prefix Conditioning; (c) number of trainable motion-module blocks; (d) choice of frozen encoders (R2, R4). A user study (perceptual evaluation) is also missing (R4).
- **Background quality is inconsistent**: Generated backgrounds are low quality in Fig. 5 and 6, with inconsistency across complex scenes (R2). Objective metrics for background quality and comparison on plain backgrounds are needed (R2).
- **Applicability of the AnyPose scenario is questionable**: For cases requiring large head-pose changes, one could simply align the driving pose to the reference image using rigid alignment, making the use case contrived (R3).
- **Training data and compute**: Training uses 300 hours of video (similar to EMO’s 250h) and 8 V100 GPUs, but training time is not reported (R1, R4). The claimed advantage of a smaller network is not demonstrated via inference speed or hardware requirements (R3, R4). Can the model be trained on consumer GPUs? (R3)
- **Missing implementation details**: Which modules are frozen/trained in the AnyNet UNet is unclear. The audio projector and motion module (Fig. 2) need more explanation (R2, R3). Does FaceID support unseen identities or is it a categorical embedding from a fixed set? (R2) What is the FPS of generated animations? (R2) Why were samples with significant camera movement excluded from the dataset? (R2) What is the maximum length of video that Progressive Prefix Conditioning can handle smoothly? (R2)
- **Clarity and formatting issues**: Grammatical errors (e.g., L110 “focus” → “focuses”, L481 missing question phrasing) (R3). An updated Fig. 2 showing submodules inside Up/Down blocks with frozen/trained labels would help (R3).
- **Trade-off between diversity and synchronization fidelity**: The paper claims weak control enables expressive outputs, but this may sacrifice lip-sync consistency – a point that should be discussed (R4). Comparison with SVP (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18083) is suggested for facial detail and sharpness (R2).
