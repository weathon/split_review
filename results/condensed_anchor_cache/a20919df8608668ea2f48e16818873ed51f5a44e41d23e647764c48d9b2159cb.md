## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates whether vision-language models (specifically GPT-4V) truly learn common representations across modalities. Using a machine teaching framework, the authors measure the minimal number of teaching examples needed for GPT-4V to recognize 20 concepts from the Quick, Draw! dataset, comparing two input modalities: raw bitmap images and trace coordinates in TikZ format. The results show that image-based representations generally require fewer segments and achieve higher accuracy than coordinate-based representations. However, the relative ranking of concept complexity is preserved across both modalities, suggesting that concept simplicity may be modality-invariant. Two reviewers (scoring 5) found the idea interesting and the experiment well-presented; two other reviewers (scoring 3) considered the findings too narrow and lacking generalizability.

### Strengths

- The overall premise – comparing how vision-language models process analogous image vs. text inputs – is interesting and novel (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The machine teaching approach to explore modality-invariant concept complexity is a clever framing (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The evaluation protocol is technically sound (Reviewer 1).
- The experiment setting and research methods are well-presented, detailed, and easy to understand (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The findings that image-based representations are more efficient and that concept complexity ranking is preserved are interesting (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited model testing:** The study only tests GPT-4V, leaving open whether conclusions apply to vision-language models broadly as claimed in the title. Multiple reviewers (1, 3, 4) explicitly request testing additional models, especially open-source ones such as LLaVA, CogVLM, and Gemini. Without this, the results may be model-specific rather than general (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Limited dataset and concepts:** The evaluation uses only 20 concepts from a single dataset (Quick, Draw!). The concepts may be too simplistic or specific, and 20 concepts is insufficient for robust conclusions. A broader evaluation with more concepts and datasets is needed (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Data quality / filtering concerns:** The automated RDP (Ramer-Douglas-Peucker) simplification can produce images that are indistinguishable across concept classes (e.g., the simplified "Computer" and "Door" look almost identical in Appendix Table 6). No experiment without RDP-generated images was performed despite a concrete suggestion to do so by selecting a smaller subset of images or using a coarser bin width (Reviewer 3).
- **Prompt and answer format issues:** The experiment uses basic zero-shot prompts without constraining the answer format. Reviewers (4, 1) suggest using constrained decoding or a prompt template with a predefined concept set to avoid needing to find hyponyms, and to ensure cleaner evaluation. Reviewers 1 and 4 also request clarification on whether the list of candidate concepts was provided in the prompt.
- **Limited real-world applicability and insight:** The findings are not insightful beyond comparing bitmap vs. coordinate representations for simple sketches. The work does not connect to real-world scenarios, complex images, or how models actually learn concepts (Reviewers 2, 3). Potential applications and broader impact are unclear (Reviewer 1).
- **Narrow focus / insufficient depth:** The paper mainly reports identification accuracy stratified by concept, modality, and complexity. It would benefit from other lines of inquiry, such as exploring the hypothesis from Line 15 that analogous images and textual descriptions "should map to the similar area in the latent space" (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of in-context learning or teaching process clarification:** The experiment appears to be zero-shot inference rather than a teaching process; one reviewer (4) questions why GPT-4V is called a "learner" and suggests that in-context learning might be more appropriate for "teaching" concepts. Reviewers 1 and 4 request more implementation details on how the teaching set was prompted and how teaching size was determined.
- **Verification of training data:** Reviewer 1 questions how the authors verified that the Quick, Draw! data is not part of GPT-4V’s training data (Line 270).
- **Writing clarity:** The paper's detailed descriptions of each module are overwhelming; Reviewer 1 suggests revising to focus more on motivation. Reviewer 3 requests clearer figure captions (e.g., explaining "teaching size") and an illustration of input/output for the experiment.
