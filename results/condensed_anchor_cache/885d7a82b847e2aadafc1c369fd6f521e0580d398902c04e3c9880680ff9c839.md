## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GLIDER, which combines a global router (using an LLM to generate semantic task instructions for expert selection) with a token-level local router (based on Phatgoose) for model MoErging on T5-based experts (T0 and FLAN tasks). The goal is to improve held-in task performance while maintaining held-out generalization. Reviewers are split: two (scores 5) praise the core idea and results, while two (scores 3) find the evaluation insufficient, the held-in motivation unconvincing, and the novelty limited relative to Phatgoose.

### Strengths
- The core idea—incorporating global semantic information of expert specialisation into local routing—is intuitive and persuasive (Reviewer 1, 4).
- Leveraging an LLM to generate semantic task descriptions for global routing is a creative and unique approach not explored in prior routing methods (Reviewer 1, 2, 4).
- The paper addresses the trade‑off between held‑in and held‑out performance and provides a simple, clear solution (Reviewer 1, 3).
- Experimental results show improved held‑in performance (6.6%) and competitive held‑out results (Reviewer 1, 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evaluation breadth and statistical grounding.**  
  The approach is heuristic; a much larger experimental part is expected—covering a wider range of tasks and more comparison points (beyond those in Phatgoose)—with statistical significance tests. Instead, the paper only tests on T0/FLAN with few datasets (Reviewer 1, 3).

- **Biased presentation of held‑out gains.**  
  The claim of “0.9% over held out tasks over Phatgoose” (bolded) only reflects the maximum across 5 datasets (0.88%); the other four datasets show 0.39%, 0.16%, –0.53%, and 0.3% (Reviewer 1, 3).

- **Weak empirical evidence for the global router’s semantic grounding.**  
  Held‑out expert selections in Fig. 1 appear to rely on syntactic resemblance (e.g., cloze‑format Q&A in Appendix B) rather than true semantics. Generalisation to more complex tasks is unclear; a quantitative assessment over diverse tasks (tens of datasets per answer type) is needed to show whether the global router identifies task type (e.g., cloze) irrespective of semantic content (Reviewer 1, 3).

- **Held‑in performance improvement is not clearly justified.**  
  For any held‑in task, the corresponding specialized expert is always available and outperforms GLIDER (Table 1). Therefore, improving held‑in performance is unnecessary—selecting that expert directly would be simpler and better. The authors do not provide a scenario where using GLIDER is preferable to using the specialized expert alone (Reviewer 2, 4).

- **Architecture is nearly identical to Phatgoose for the local component.**  
  GLIDER essentially appends a global context component to Phatgoose. The lack of ablation with alternative local routers (e.g., Arrow) makes it impossible to isolate the contribution of the global routing component (Reviewer 2, 4).

- **Heavy reliance on GPT‑4‑turbo for global scores raises concerns.**  
  - The algorithm (Eq. 345–346) gives a high weight α to the global score, making expert selection largely dependent on GPT‑4‑turbo (Reviewer 1).  
  - Potential inference latency from using GPT‑4‑turbo is not addressed (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2 asks for latency analysis).  
  - The global router uses only 3 sample questions per dataset; this may suffice for simple, low‑diversity tasks but not for complex, diverse ones (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing discussion on incremental addition of experts.**  
  The impact of adding (or converting held‑out to held‑in) experts on normalisation and routing is not addressed (Reviewer 3).

- **Unclear originality and insufficient explanation of prior references.**  
  Extensive references to Muqeeth (Phatgoose) lack rationale for why those specific works are cited, raising concerns about the paper’s novelty (Reviewer 1).

- **Formatting, notation, and grammar issues.**  
  - Missing left parenthesis (line 60); repetitive sentences (line 67–68); unexplained arrow (line 140); unclear Figure 1 description; missing definition of Ψ (line 279); confused notation (line 289–290); unexplained σ (line 301); confusing variable t (line 319); no formula for normalisation (line 323); omission of T0 held‑in dataset (line 354) (Reviewer 1).  
  - Grammar errors: “however, MoE methods that train experts from scratch while…” (delete “that”); “for all token” → “tokens” (line 72); “dynamically routing” → “routes” (line 264); “focuses in LoRA” → “on LoRA” (line 286); “u_i” likely should be “u_t” (line 288–289); “fully query” → “full query” (line 332); w_k undefined in Eq. line 348 (Reviewer 2, 4).  
  - Omission of evaluation metric definitions for Table 1 (Reviewer 2 asks for details).  
  - No explanation of choice of T5 architecture; preliminary experiments with decoder‑only models (e.g., Llama3‑8B) are not reported (Reviewer 2 asks).

*Note: There is a notable split among reviewers: two (scores 5) found the approach promising and praised its insights and results, while two (scores 3) were less convinced, citing insufficient evaluation, weak held‑in motivation, and limited novelty relative to Phatgoose.*
