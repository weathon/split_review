## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Emotion Context Learning (E-ICL), a method that improves few-shot in-context learning (ICL) for fine-grained emotion recognition by addressing three shortcomings identified through prototype theory: reliance on single‑emotion labels, selection of semantically rather than emotionally similar prototypes, and considering all emotion categories as candidates. E-ICL uses a dynamic soft‑label strategy to create multi‑dimensional emotion labels, selects emotionally similar prototypes via a specialized emotion detection model, and applies an emotion exclusion mechanism to reduce interference. Experiments on four fine‑grained emotion datasets (EDOS, Empathetic‑Dialogues, EmpatheticIntent, GoEmotions) with two LLMs (Claude‑haiku, ChatGPT‑turbo) show E‑ICL outperforms standard ICL.

### Strengths
- The core idea of using soft labels for prototypes is interesting and well‑suited to the inherently ambiguous nature of emotion recognition (Reviewer 1).
- E‑ICL achieves considerable improvements over traditional ICL (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The approach requires no additional training of the LLM, making it efficient and accessible (Reviewer 2).
- The paper provides extensive analyses on hyperparameters (k1, k2, k3, α) and an ablation study of the method’s components (Reviewer 1).
- Note: One reviewer (Reviewer 3) did not list any strengths; there is thus a contrast in perceived contribution.

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and clarity deficiencies** (Reviewer 1: presentation score 1; Reviewer 2: typos; Reviewer 3: Figure 1 issues):  
  – Section 4 (method) has inconsistent, unintuitive notation and errors – e.g., in Equation 5 a vector is subtracted from a scalar, the sum over k1 is given but j is not properly defined, probability distributions appear unnormalized; in L246 “m_i ∈ n_d” and the next sentence treats n_d as a scalar; notation switches between p^s_{m_i} and p^s_i (Reviewer 1).  
  – The emotion vector V is described as in R^{768} without explaining the embedding size (Reviewer 1).  
  – Figure 1 lacks detail on how the emotionally closest prototype is selected (lines 88‑90) and why existing methods query irrelevant prototypes (lines 81‑82); the font sizes in subtitles (a) and (b) are too large and inconsistent (Reviewer 3).  
  – The paper contains typos (Reviewer 2).  
- **Limited experimental setup** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3):  
  – Only two LLMs (Claude‑haiku, ChatGPT‑turbo) are tested; more LLMs should be used to verify general applicability (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).  
  – No baseline where a RoBERTa model is trained on the full training set is shown, even though E‑ICL uses the training set (Equation 5). Papers introducing these datasets indicate that traditional BERT models often outperform E‑ICL (Reviewer 1).  
  – Comparisons are primarily against standard ICL and zero‑shot; improved ICL prompt baselines (e.g., with prompt engineering) are missing (Reviewer 2).  
  – Some recent advanced emotion recognition methods (e.g., Emollms by Liu et al., 2024, also large LLMs and fine‑tuning approaches) are not included as baselines (Reviewer 3).  
- **Method complexity and inference cost** (Reviewer 1):  
  – The method has many tunable parameters (k1, k2, k3, α) that require careful tuning (Reviewer 1).  
  – There is no discussion of inference‑time costs; a comparison of ICL vs. E‑ICL vs. RoBERTa inference costs is needed (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing analysis** (Reviewer 3):  
  – Per‑category accuracy and F1 scores should be reported to show in which emotion categories E‑ICL improves and by how much (Reviewer 3).  
  – The theoretical connection between ICL and prototype theory is only briefly explained (lines 90‑92); more detailed justification or an appendix is needed (Reviewer 3).  
- **Missing citations** (Reviewer 1): Suresh et al., 2021, “Not All Negatives are Equal: Label‑Aware Contrastive Loss for Fine‑grained Text Classification” is not cited.  
- **Unaddressed design choices** (Reviewer 1): Prompt designs are not shown; it is unclear whether variable numbers of demonstrations (e.g., maxing out context length) were considered to possibly remove the need for k2 (Reviewer 1).
