## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends tilted empirical risk minimization (TERM) from classical ML to quantum neural networks (QNNs) by introducing a tilted hyperparameter into the loss function and adapting the parameter‑shift rule to accommodate weighted inputs. Experiments on the Iris dataset and a synthetic dataset with induced class imbalance show that varying the tilt parameter modifies decision boundaries and improves training accuracy.

### Strengths
- The work addresses a timely direction: moving beyond the i.i.d. hypothesis in loss functions and exploring TERM in QNN training, which could be useful if these devices are used in practice (Reviewer 2).
- Adapting classical regularization techniques to QML is nontrivial given computational constraints and is important given the limited body of QML research (Reviewer 3).
- The investigation explicitly examines the effect of tilted losses on binary classification tasks with class imbalance (Reviewer 1).  
  *Note: One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 5) was substantially more positive than the other two (scores 3,3), who center their criticisms on novelty and empirical depth.*

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty / originality.** The paper primarily adapts the existing TERM framework to QNNs without introducing significant modifications or new theoretical insights. The conclusions mirror those of the original TERM paper, and combining the tilted loss with the parameter‑shift rule is not presented as novel (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **Insufficient empirical validation.** Experiments rely on only two small hand‑crafted datasets (Iris and a synthetic set; the second dataset is not fully detailed). No statistics or error bars are provided, so claims of robustness cannot be assessed. The authors should test on a broader range of datasets with varying complexity (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **No discussion of generalization.** Only training accuracy is reported; the impact of the tilted loss on test performance and overfitting is left for future work. Fitting the tilt parameter (t) on training accuracy may lead to overfitting; a heuristic for fixing t should be discussed (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Inconsistent interpretation of outlier handling.** The softmax‑style reweighting gives larger weight to points where the network currently makes larger errors (i.e., misclassified outliers), which contradicts the claim that the tilted loss reduces the importance of outliers. Moreover, the reweighting coefficients evolve during training, so any “reduction in outlier relevance” is not fixed (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical obstacles.** Equation 9 has a potential denominator‑zero problem that may cause divergence (Reviewer 1). The method requires computing the normalization of gradient reweighting coefficients at every parameter update, making it incompatible with mini‑batch training (Reviewer 2). Higher tilt values increase both computational expense and risk of out‑of‑range values, and no criteria are given for choosing an optimal limit for t (Reviewer 1).
- **Conflicting evidence on tilt direction.** The claim that both positive and negative tilt improve accuracy appears inconsistent with the numbers in Table 1 (Reviewer 2).
- **Weak justification of decision‑boundary analysis.** With only two input features, the notion of “outliers” in a binary classification task is poorly defined; such discussion would be more meaningful on higher‑dimensional data (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experiments.** The authors do not report results of running the proposed tilted parameter‑shift rule itself. The main challenge in applying tilted loss to QNNs is not addressed (Reviewer 3).
- **Writing quality issues.** The paper contains typos (e.g., L210 “are you”), an entire repetitive paragraph (L255‑260), and unclear phrasing (Reviewer 3).
