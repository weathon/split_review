## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents AutoBencher, a declarative framework for automatic benchmark construction. Given a few desiderata, the method operationalizes each and casts benchmark creation as an optimization problem. Two settings are instantiated: (i) capability evaluation (maximizing salience, difficulty, separability, novelty) and (ii) safety evaluation (maximizing harmfulness and attack success rate). A language model (GPT-4) automatically generates topic descriptions and then per-topic datasets of (question, answer) pairs, iteratively revising them to optimize the criteria. Tools (Wikipedia for knowledge, Python for math) are used to avoid the evaluating LM generating incorrect answers. Empirical results show AutoBencher datasets are on average 27% more novel and 22% more difficult than existing benchmarks, and reveal specific model gaps (e.g., Gemini-Pro on Permian Extinction, GPT-4o on cryptocurrency scams). Manual validation via Mechanical Turk confirms low error rates and high question quality. An ablation demonstrates the value of the adaptive search history vector.

### Strengths

- The problem of automatic benchmark generation in a guided manner is important and of strong interest to the community.
- The framework is elegant and extensible, with a novel two-step division (first generate topic descriptions, then generate datasets per topic) that is especially effective.
- The use of tools (Wikipedia knowledge database, Python library for math) to avoid incorrect answers from the evaluating LM is a key enabler, and the adaptive search strategy (using history of explored subjects) guides generation beyond brute force.
- AutoBencher generates human-interpretable topics where model ranks exhibit surprising results, demonstrating novel insights.
- Safety evaluation automatically discovers jailbreaking vectors (e.g., posing a harmful question as a hypothetical) that align with reviewer experience.
- Empirical evaluation shows significant performance gains over human-designed benchmarks, particularly in novelty (27% more novel) and difficulty (22% more difficult).
- The research methodology is well-executed: manual validation of generated benchmarks via Mechanical Turk shows low error rates and high salience.
- The paper is well-written, easy to understand, with a clear structure and helpful equations/figures.
- AutoBencher is highly scalable and reduces reliance on costly human labor for constructing evaluation benchmarks across many domains.
- Evaluation covers a wide range of knowledge domains (math, multilinguality, knowledge, safety) and includes human evaluation to further validate quality and relevance.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited methodological novelty**: Aside from the use of retrieval during generation, optimizing for criteria objectives is not a particularly significant contribution. The approach shares similarities with prior automatic benchmarking work (e.g., TaskBench [1]) without a clear distinction. *(R2, R4)*
- **Evaluation scope is narrow**: AutoBencher currently only generates one-turn (question, answer) pairs. Extensions to multi-turn data, chain-of-thought data, or multi-modal domains are not explored. The safety evaluation is less convincing than capability evaluation; integration into a mainstream safety framework (e.g., HarmBench) with discussion of challenges would strengthen it. *(R1)*
- **Potential bias from GPT-4 evaluator**: Using GPT-4 as the evaluator may introduce bias favoring LLMs from the same family (OpenAI). This is not discussed or controlled for. *(R4)*
- **Mismatch in tool access**: The evaluating LM has access to tools (Wikipedia, Python) while the evaluated LMs do not. The paper does not convincingly argue why future LMs should be expected to have such capabilities without using tools themselves. *(R1)*
- **Practicality concerns**:
    - The method relies on accuracy scores across multiple existing models and preexisting datasets, which is computationally intensive, especially when generating multiple datasets across a large set of models. A discussion of computational resources would be helpful. *(R2)*
    - While datasets are described and scored at the topic level, individual questions may vary in quality (e.g., a “Nuclear technology” dataset could contain questions of varying harmfulness). *(R2)*
    - Many criteria still seem to require manual crafting (e.g., defining salience sets for math/science), which limits full automation. The paper does not specify how the math/science salience set was constructed (e.g., whether open card sorting was used). *(R2, R3)*
- **Handling of LM variance**: Language models exhibit high variance across runs. The scoring criteria do not account for this variance; a robust statistical method to incorporate variance between runs for both question generation and answering is missing. *(R2)*
- **Translation issue for low-resource languages**: For multilingual evaluation, the approach uses translation tools. Low-resource languages are both less likely to be tackled by LLMs and by translation tools, creating a catch-22. While acknowledging this as a limitation may suffice, it is not addressed. *(R3)*
- **Math/science open vocabulary problem**: The approach to math and science categories suggests an open vocabulary problem that is not clearly tackled, unlike other categories where Wikipedia and popularity metrics are used. *(R3)*
- **Missing ablation studies**: Additional ablations are needed to demonstrate the impact of various components, such as the language model used, the criteria for evaluating dataset descriptions, the inclusion of privileged information, and the use of a hyper-/hyponymy graph of Wikipedia topics to further guide adaptive search. *(R2, R3)*
- **Qualitative analysis details missing**: The details of the qualitative assessment (Section 6.3) are lacking: what does “high quality” mean, how many question-answer pairs were sampled, what do the “ranks” refer to, and which LM was used to generate Table 1? *(R2, R4)*
- **Multi-objective optimization not explored**: The optimization for capability evaluation is multi-objective but is linearized with hyperparameters. The paper does not discuss why multi-objective or evolutionary methods were not used. *(R3)*
- **Standalone nature**: AutoBencher is not integrated into existing evaluation frameworks like HELM or HuggingFace Open LLM, which would make a more convincing case for adoption and viability. *(R1)*
- **Reviewer disagreement**: While one reviewer found the approach well-executed with novel discoveries (score 8), another considered the methodological novelty limited and the evaluation insufficient (score 5), with further concerns about bias and practical applicability (scores 6). *(R1, R3, R2, R4)*
