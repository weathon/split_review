## Merged Review

### Summary

The authors introduce “Conceptualize Any Network” (CAN), a model-agnostic post-hoc explanation framework that extracts visual-patch concepts shared across all classes. The pipeline has two stages: concept discovery (using an attribution method to select important patches, then clustering their embeddings) and concept assignment (matching test-image patches to the dictionary via relation scores). Experiments on ResNet-50 and ViT with Grad-CAM and Bcos use metrics such as SDC, SSC, Faithfulness, and Conciseness to compare against ACE, MCD, and SHAP. The paper claims scalability to all ImageNet classes, which it states has not been achieved before.

### Strengths

- **Well-written and easy to understand (R2).**  
- **Logical, simple approach that is promising and interesting (R4).**  
- **Provides both local and global concept-based explanations, moving beyond pure attribution (R3).**  
- **Includes Vision Transformers in the evaluation (R3).**  
- **Utilizes multiple evaluation criteria (SDC, SSC, Faithfulness, Conciseness) to assess the method and baselines (R1).**  
- **Extensive experiments are presented (R2), though R3 notes that extensive validation is not itself a contribution.**

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* R1 (score 5) is notably more positive about the framework’s potential than R3 (score 1), who considers the claimed contributions either wrong or already introduced. R2 and R4 (scores 3) find the idea valuable but see significant execution issues.

### Weaknesses

- **Inadequate justification of design choices (R1):** The reasoning behind using attribution maps and local importance scores is insufficiently explained. The choice of patch size is not justified, especially for ViT where respecting internal patch structure might matter; no ablation studies are provided for patch size, local importance threshold, or global importance threshold (R4). Only the number of clusters \(k\) is ablated (R3).

- **Limited experimental validation (R1, R4):**  
  - Only ResNet‑50 and ViT\_c are tested; the claim of “any network” is not substantiated with more architectures (R1).  
  - No comparisons with other concept methods on ViT (R1).  
  - Only Grad‑CAM (for CNNs) and Bcos (for both CNNs and ViT) are used as attribution methods; broader attribution maps (e.g., attention-based methods for ViT) are needed (R1).  
  - Figure 4 lacks comparisons with other methods on ImageNet (R1).  
  - One qualitative example (Figure 5) is insufficient; labels in Figure 5 are not clearly visible, and the example is not convincing without a larger set (R3, R4).

- **ACE re‑implementation discrepancy (R2):** In Figure 3, the reported SDC and SSC for ACE differ substantially from the original ACE paper (e.g., 60% accuracy with 5 concepts in SSC vs. 20% in the original). The gap is unexplained.

- **Dependence on an external XAI method (R1, R2):** CAN relies on Grad‑CAM (or Bcos) to select important patches, making it difficult to attribute the final performance to the proposed framework rather than the underlying attribution method. This limits novelty and contribution (R2).

- **Unfair conciseness comparison (R2):** Table 2 compares conciseness without accounting for concept size. MCD has large concepts, so its low conciseness is expected; the metric should be normalized by concept size.

- **Missing related work and overclaimed novelty (R3):**  
  - The idea of a dictionary of visual patches shared among classes has been introduced before (Meynen et al., ICIP 2023; Li et al., IJCV 2017; Kowal et al., CVPR 2024).  
  - ACE and SHAP can theoretically be applied to ViTs with modifications; the paper does not discuss this or explain why they are unsuitable.  
  - The scalability claim (all ImageNet classes) is not backed by computational cost or runtime analysis; ACE also uses k‑means, so scaling considerations need direct comparison (R3).

- **Math notation and terminology issues (R3):**  
  - Notation is inconsistent and poorly introduced (e.g., \(l\) used in Section 3.1 before definition; \(s_{i,j,n}\) unexplained; \(n^2_p\) not defined). The same symbols (\(n\), \(i\), \(j\)) are reused for different quantities; \(\hat{n}\) appears in two roles.  
  - Equation 1 is overly complex for what is essentially a normalized mean of patch elements.  
  - The definition of “concept” is inconsistent: lines 257/265 define it as a cluster, but lines 305/398/399 refer to individual patches as concepts.  
  - Terminology (“post‑hoc explainability”, “interpretability”, “concept‑based interpretability”) is used interchangeably without precision.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and ablation (R3, R4):** Multiple hyperparameters (patch size, local/global importance thresholds) are introduced but never given explicit values or ablated. The sliding‑window approach with uncovered windows may split a salient region across patches (R3).

- **Evaluation protocol incompleteness (R3):**  
  - It is unclear how ACE (a single‑stage method) is applied to test images, or whether all methods use the same set of images in evaluation.  
  - The paper measures “percentage of pixels” for visual parts, but does not explain how those parts are selected from clusters that contain segments from multiple images (R3).  

- **Low accuracy and unconvincing quantitative results (R4):** Table 2b shows low accuracies, casting doubt on the method’s effectiveness. Consistency scores in Table 2a are low; there is no explanation for why higher consistencies are not achieved (R4).  

- **Lack of completeness analysis (R3):** The paper does not investigate whether the obtained concepts are sufficient to explain the model globally; the ShAP protocol for completeness could be used.

- **Missing analysis of cross‑architecture concept similarity (R3):** No comparison is made between concepts extracted from CNNs and those from Vision Transformers.

- **Potential unfair comparison due to differing models (R1):** Bcos uses a different ResNet variant than torchvision’s; it is unclear whether comparing CAN with Grad‑CAM (on standard ResNet) and CAN with Bcos (on Bcos’s ResNet) is fair.

- **No examples of actual explanations (R1):** The paper presents an explanation method but does not show any generated explanations to help the reader understand what the output looks like.
