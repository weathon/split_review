## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a fully decentralized multi-agent Q-learning algorithm based on a novel best possible operator that enables convergence despite nonstationarity. A simplified operator is introduced for practicality. Experimental results show improvements over baselines including MA2QL, I2Q, H-IQL, IQL. Two reviewers (ratings 6) found the work novel and significant, addressing an open problem. Two others (ratings 1 and 3) raised major concerns about restrictive assumptions, lack of scalability, insufficient theoretical rigor, and weak experimental justification.

### Strengths
- Novel approach introducing a best possible operator that handles nonstationarity in fully decentralized MARL, addressing an important open problem.
- The main idea of each agent “imagining” a best-case scenario for others is clean and conceptually appealing.
- Provides convergence guarantees for tabular case; proofs are generally logical and easy to follow.
- Empirically, BQL achieves remarkable improvements over existing baselines (MA2QL, I2Q, H-IQL, IQL) across a variety of cooperative tasks (Figures 1–5).
- The problem of decentralized MARL is important, and the theoretical study contributes to the literature.
- Overall experimental results are positive and show better or comparable performance to existing methods.

### Weaknesses
- The assumption of a unique optimal joint policy is restrictive; performance when multiple optimal policies exist is not discussed. (R1, R4)
- The assumption that agents observe the global state is strong and limits applicability. (R4)
- The claim of “global optimality” (line 62) is likely restricted to tabular settings; this should be clarified. (R3)
- Memory space to store transition probabilities \(P(s' \mid s,a_i,a_{-i})\) scales exponentially with the number of agents (\(O(|\mathcal{A}_{-i}|)\)). The paper does not discuss practical memory mitigation or implications for high-dimensional action spaces. Function approximation may break theoretical guarantees. (R2, R3, R4)
- The search over all possible joint action distributions for the max operator incurs exponential complexity. The simplified operator (stochastic search) does not reduce worst-case complexity and is effectively random search. The paper lacks a formal computational complexity analysis comparing the simplified and best operators. (R2, R3, R4)
- The convergence proof relies on the best kernel being selected during updates; it is unclear what happens if it is never selected (for large state/action spaces). The probability of selecting the best kernel decreases with space size, leading to potentially very slow convergence; asymptotic convergence alone is not informative. (R4)
- The total complexity of the simplified operator until convergence may not improve over the best-possible operator in expectation – the average number of ineffective updates may match the total number of kernels. (R4)
- The paper does not provide explicit convergence and optimality theorems for the neural network case; extensions to function approximation are not discussed. (R1)
- The proof of Lemma 1 (and the uniqueness of the optimal policy) relies on determinism of the optimal policy; the reference to Puterman’s book is too broad and mostly single-agent. (R4, also R1)
- The step from line 142 to line 144 uses an inequality (\(\max_x f(x) - \max_x g(x) \ge f(x') - g(x')\)) that is not generally true; the special structure needed to admit it is not explained. (R3)
- The paper does not convincingly argue why the joint-action approach (treating all agents as a super-agent) fails due to nonstationarity; the statement on line 87 needs a reference or justification. (R3)
- No comparison is provided with the theoretical work on convergence of independent Q-learning [1] – how the setting differs and pros/cons. (R2)
- For continuous environments, the use of DDPG as a foundation is not explained carefully; readers cannot appreciate how this choice influences results. (R3)
- The experimental section has several issues:
  - In Figure 2, color choices for confidence intervals are too similar, making methods difficult to distinguish; confidence intervals are wide for some methods. (R3)
  - Subfigure captions in Figures 3–5 are confusing; descriptions in section 4.4 suggest agents have different objectives, contradicting the cooperative setting. (R3)
  - Details on the distribution from which transition kernels and reward functions are sampled are insufficient; using only 20 samples may give poor statistical estimates. Standard deviations in Figure 1 appear small, suggesting structure – more details needed. (R3)
  - The discussion in the paragraph ending at line 374 is convoluted and should be rewritten for clarity. (R3)
- The term “MPE” is not defined; “differential game” is misused for discrete-time problems; “dynamic game” would be more appropriate. (R3)
- Minor but numerous grammatical errors and typos: “Mojoco” (Figure 3 caption), “wildly adopted” (line 301), “slow learning rate to the value punishment” (line 324), “using mean and standard” (line 345), “action space of each agent is 4” (line 351), missing “the” in several places, no whitespace after “MDP” (line 70). (R3)
- The definition of \(S_i^m\) in Section 2.4 is not explained. (R2)
- The requirement to “explore” all state/action pairs with a deterministic policy (line 236) lacks justification; why can’t states be enumerated in the tabular case? (R3)
- The optimality of the converged policy in the presence of multiple Nash equilibria is not clearly defined; the critique of Hysteretic IQL (line 322) only makes sense in cooperative games. (R3)
- The proofs offer limited insight and provide no practical guidance (e.g., expected computational costs). (R4)
- Some proofs are not convincing. (R4)
- Scalability is unclear; while experiments claim scalability, they are limited to specific small-scale environments and are not representative. (R2, R4)
- The studies in Appendix D are not comprehensive. (R4)
- The derivation of eq (4) from eq (3) is unclear: eq (3) holds when \(a = \pi^*(s)\); taking max over other actions may require fixing \(a_i = a_i^*\). (R4)
- The convergence in Lemma 4 is conditional on the optimal kernel being chosen; if it is never chosen, what happens? (R4)
- Only deterministic policies are considered; the impact of allowing random policies is not addressed. (R4)
