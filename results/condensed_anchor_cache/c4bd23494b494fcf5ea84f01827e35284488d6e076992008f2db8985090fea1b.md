## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Listwise Preference Optimization (LPO) and its variant LPO-abs for offline alignment when multiple responses per prompt have explicit scalar reward scores. LPO generalizes DPO by weighting pairs by the absolute reward difference, and LPO-abs adds a cross‑entropy term to combat the likelihood‑decline problem in DPO. Experiments on Mistral models across math, coding, and reasoning benchmarks show moderate gains. Two reviewers (R1, R3) praise the direction as novel and under‑explored, with comprehensive experiments. Two reviewers (R2, R4) find the contribution incremental, the theoretical grounding lacking, and the evaluation suffers from potential circularity and missing baselines.

### Strengths
- **Important and under‑explored problem**: Studying pointwise/reward‑based alignment offline is valuable (R1,R3). The explicit‑reward setting can provide richer supervision than pairwise preferences (R2,R4).
- **Addresses likelihood decline**: LPO‑abs mitigates the issue that DPO decreases the likelihood of preferred responses over training (R1). Reviewer3 also notes this is a known problem worth solving.
- **Clear and well‑structured presentation**: The paper is easy to follow, experimental design uses explicit research questions (R2). The method is straightforward to implement if data are available (R4).
- **Comprehensive benchmark coverage**: Evaluation covers diverse domains (BBH, HumanEval, LeetCode, multiple math tasks) (R1,R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited model evaluation**: Only Mistral models are tested. Reviewers ask for validation on other architectures (e.g., Llama) to assess robustness (R1).
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**:
  - Missing comparisons with RPO (SFT+DPO) which also addresses likelihood decline (R4), and with other reward‑based methods (R1).
  - Missing comparisons with IPO, SimPO, and the cross‑entropy approach from the Llama 3 Herd of Models (R1,R3).
  - AlpacaEval results are comparable to baselines; there are labeling inconsistencies in reporting second‑best results, and win‑rate against DPO is absent for some key baselines like KTO (R2).
- **Limited novelty and heuristic design**:
  - The core idea – weighting DPO by reward differences – is a straightforward heuristic lacking theoretical grounding (R2,R4). The derivation should rest on statistical assumptions about pointwise reward data (e.g., does a larger reward difference imply greater certainty?) (R3). Alternative weighting schemes should have been studied (R3). Reviewer4 notes that weighted DPO is already known (citing WPO) and this alone does not warrant a paper unless results are exceptional.
  - LPO‑abs (eq. 9) is questioned: it is unclear how increasing rewards on every sample helps; perhaps it should minimize the difference between true and predicted reward (R4). The regularization term has no hyperparameter, which seems surprising and requires explanation (R4).
  - The motivation for maintaining absolute reward values in LPO‑abs is weak, as standard RL theory states policies are invariant to constant reward shifts – further theoretical analysis is needed (R2).
- **Evaluation bias**: The reward‑based MT‑bench experiments use GPT‑4 scores for both training and evaluation, creating a circular assessment. Independent metrics and human evaluation are necessary to validate claimed gains (R2).
- **Minor issues**: A typo in the abstract calls the method “Soft Preference Optimization” instead of LPO (R3). Overall improvements over baselines are described as slight (R1) and are not consistently superior (R2).
- **Score disagreement**: Two reviewers assign a score of 5 (positive) while two assign 3 (critical), indicating fundamental disagreement on the paper’s contribution and soundness.
