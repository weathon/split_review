## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SPADE, a method using Extreme Value Theory (EVT) to transform a classifier into an abstaining classifier that detects and rejects out-of-distribution (OOD) and adversarial samples with probabilistic guarantees. It models training distribution in latent space via Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distributions and provides formal characterizations of OOD and adversarial samples. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet with ResNet, VGG, ViT show competitive performance.

### Strengths
- Novel first-principles design introducing EVT/GEV to OOD detection, providing a statistically grounded framework (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The method offers provable guarantees (high-probability) for rejecting both OOD and adversarial examples under mild assumptions (Reviewer 3, also Reviewer 1 mentions theoretical foundation).
- Sample-efficient: good performance even with strong subsampling of the training set (Reviewer 1).
- Versatile across architectures (ResNet, VGG, ViT) and datasets of medium and large scale (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Well-written, easy to follow, with clear introduction to EVT and a blend of theory and empirical evaluation (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The problem is important and fits well within the community scope (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Weak experimental results relative to baselines.** In almost all tables, SPADE does not achieve significant benefit over prior methods. Table 1 shows SPADE is uniformly dominated by baselines (Reviewer 4). SPADE performs poorly on near-OOD examples: in Table 1, it is outperformed by MSP in 3 out of 5 cases (Reviewer 3). The average rank metric is not ideal; superiority should be shown over a majority of datasets (Reviewer 3). Table 2 suggests ViT is a particularly strong teacher for SPADE; revisiting Table 1 with ViT might bring improvements (Reviewer 4). High FPR95 values indicate SPADE is overly cautious, possibly rejecting valid samples (Reviewer 1).

2. **Missing challenging near-OOD evaluation.** The evaluations in Tables 2 and 3 omit the simple yet hard near-OOD task of CIFAR-100 vs CIFAR-10 (and vice versa). The authors should provide AUROC, AUC, and FPR95 on this benchmark, along with example failure cases (Reviewer 1). This is a direct request; Reviewer 1 indicated they would consider raising their score if addressed.

3. **Provable guarantees rely on strong, impractical assumptions.** Theorem 1 requires the embedding network to be K-Lipschitz. However, a K-Lipschitz network already provides provable robustness on adversarial examples, and exact Lipschitzness cannot be computed empirically for complex neural networks, limiting the practical utility of the guarantee (Reviewer 3). The experimental setup does not validate the claimed "provable protection of OOD examples" due to these limitations (Reviewer 3). Additionally, Theorem 1's statement does not explicitly assume $\mathcal{X}$ is Euclidean; this should be clarified, and there is a potential notational inconsistency ($f_\tau$ vs $f_\epsilon$) (Reviewer 2).

4. **Weak adversarial attack evaluation.** Only FGSM is tested with small perturbation magnitudes ($\epsilon$ from 0.001 to 0.004). FGSM is not considered a strong attack; the community expects stronger attacks like PGD or AutoAttack (Reviewers 1, 2). The evaluation should clarify whether attacks target the classifier alone or the full detection system, and include both targeted and untargeted scenarios (Reviewer 1). For a method claiming security guarantees, adaptive attacks that are aware of the detection mechanism should be considered (Reviewer 4).

5. **Potential vulnerability to white-box attacks on latent-space metrics.** SPADE relies on distance metrics in the latent space and GEV models. While distance metrics can be effective for near-OOD detection (reference [1]), it has been shown that white-box attacks can directly minimize latent space distances to k-nearest neighbors while maintaining misclassification, exploit knowledge of GEV parameters, or target gaps between class-specific GEV models to create blind spots (Reviewer 1). Though Theorem 1 provides a lower bound on perturbation magnitude, an analysis of such attack strategies and potential defenses (e.g., ensemble, adversarial training) is missing (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 1 expressed particular interest in this point.

6. **Notation and presentation issues.** Several clarity concerns: acronyms ID, KNN, MSP used before definition (Reviewer 2). Inconsistent notation: $Z^{(l)}$ vs $Z^{(\ell)}$, $P$ vs $Pr$ (Reviewer 2). Definition 3 parameter role unclear; need to state existence of parameters (Reviewer 2). The definition of $Z_c$ at line 169 should be written mathematically; $k$ as a hyperparameter should be explicitly stated (Reviewer 2). The equation for $G^{(c,c')}$ (line 215) seems to be missing the minimum inside the probability to reflect extreme values (Reviewer 2). No explicit algorithm description for SPADE; the conceptual discussions should be summarized into a single algorithm before experiments (Reviewer 2). The effect of latent embeddings on OOD tests (promised line 190) is not analyzed in Section 5 (Reviewer 2). Additionally, a reference to prior EVT robustness verification work (Weng et al., ICLR 2018) is recommended (Reviewer 2).

7. **Parameter sensitivity and computational complexity.** SPADE's performance depends on the choice of teacher model and its latent space characteristics (Reviewer 1). The GEV model is quadratic in the number of samples, which may limit scalability (Reviewer 1). Occasionally, SPADE is outperformed by simpler methods like KNN for specific tasks (Reviewer 1).
