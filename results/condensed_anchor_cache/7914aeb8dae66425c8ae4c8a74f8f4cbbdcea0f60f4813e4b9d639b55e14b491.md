## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Ready-to-React, an auto-regressive diffusion model for two-character boxing interaction generation. Each character has an independent reaction policy (the “brain”) that generates the next pose based on past observed motions, enabling streaming interaction. The method uses a diffusion head inside an auto-regressive architecture to mitigate error accumulation, and a VQ-VAE for the agent’s history while an MLP encodes the opponent’s history. A new boxing dataset (DuoBox) of over one hour of 3D motion is collected and used for evaluation. Experiments show the method outperforms prior baselines on reaction, two-character interaction, and long-term generation (≤1800 frames). Additional experiments demonstrate applicability with sparse VR input. Code and data are promised but release is not explicitly confirmed.

### Strengths
- **Important problem and sound approach.** The autoregressive policy that handles both actors’ actions and reactions concurrently is intuitive and addresses a core challenge in two-person interaction generation (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **High-quality motion and strong results.** Generated motions are visually pleasing and significantly outperform prior work (including GPT-based methods) in both quantitative and qualitative evaluations. The method can generate long, realistic sequences (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Extensive experiments and ablation.** Sufficient ablation studies confirm the effectiveness of the proposed components, with special emphasis on root orientation prediction being crucial for boxing (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **New dataset (DuoBox).** The dataset is a valuable contribution to the community, filling the gap of long interaction sequences and providing benchmarks for three tasks (reaction, two-character interaction, long-term generation) (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
- **Versatility and efficiency.** The method handles both reactive motion generation (one agent) and two-character interaction generation, is efficient for long-term generation, and works with sparse VR input, showcasing applicability in animation and interactive environments (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1).
- **Error accumulation mitigation.** The insight of using a diffusion head within an auto-regressive model to reduce error accumulation is interesting and well-motivated (Reviewer 4).
- **Qualitative videos provided.** Videos accompany the paper, giving concrete demonstration of the results (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Penetration and unrealistic interaction.** In the supplementary video (at 0:45 of the gray box) large penetration between characters and unrealistic interaction occurs; punches are thrown but the opponent often does not evade and shows no discernible reaction. The “reaction” part is not well-demonstrated (Reviewer 2). The paper does not model contact information during interactions, which limits applicability in robotics (Reviewer 1). No metrics for physical plausibility (e.g., penetration, foot sliding) are reported (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited dataset evaluation.** All experiments are conducted only on the new DuoBox dataset. It would be stronger to include an existing interaction dataset (e.g., from Duolando, Interformer) to demonstrate generalizability (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). The method is not shown to handle generic two-person interactions beyond boxing (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of novelty and narrow scope.** The method is essentially using diffusion to generate latent representations rather than directly generating actions, which some reviewers find lacking in novelty (Reviewer 4). The background is considered too narrow; the problem importance is questioned (Reviewer 4). The title should reflect the focus on boxing since many techniques (e.g., root orientation) are specific to that domain (Reviewer 2).
- **Methodology clarifications needed.**
    - **Why exclude the y-axis when extracting `r_off` and `r_dir`?** (Reviewer 3)
    - **Asymmetric inputs:** Why does the opponent input not contain `r_off` and `r_dir` when the agent input uses them? Why use a VQ-VAE for the agent but an MLP for the opponent? (Reviewer 3)
    - **Two-character generation details missing.** Does it work auto-regressively (e.g., generate agent motion T → opponent motion T+1 → agent motion T+1 → …)? Are two separately trained networks needed or only one? (Reviewer 3)
    - **Comparison with Duolando omitted.** For two-character generation, the authors compare with Interformer but not Duolando. Were modifications to Interformer necessary to enable comparison? Clarify (Reviewer 3).
    - **Single-layer MLP in diffusion.** The latent predictor uses only a single MLP layer for denoising; the authors did not explore other configurations or explain the intuition behind this choice (Reviewer 3).
    - **Figure 2 mislabeling.** “Transformer” is used for both the latent predictor and motion decoder, but the text says only a Transformer encoder in the predictor and a Transformer decoder in the decoder. The figure should be updated (Reviewer 3).
    - **Notation error:** “d” is used both for the down-sampling factor (line 198) and for the initial number of frames (line 248) (Reviewer 3).
- **Dataset release not confirmed.** The paper says code and data will be made publicly available but does not explicitly confirm dataset release (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of contact analysis.** Little analysis of how contact moments influence subsequent motion generation (Reviewer 1).
- **Diversity and non-DL baselines.** How does the method help diversity? (Reviewer 4) Could non-deep-learning baselines (pre-programmed scripts) be compared? In the boxing scene, pre-programmed motions could be quite smooth (Reviewer 4). [Questions framed as criticisms.]
- **Reviewer disagreement on significance.** Two reviewers (6,6) question the significance and novelty of the work, while the other two (8,8) consider it an important contribution with sound technical advances (implicit contrast).
