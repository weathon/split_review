## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents TabDiff, a joint diffusion framework for generating synthetic tabular data with heterogeneous types (numerical and categorical). The key innovation is a joint continuous-time diffusion process with feature-wise learnable noise schedules, parameterized by a transformer. Experiments on seven datasets show competitive performance across eight metrics, with up to 22.5% improvement over state-of-the-art on pairwise column correlation estimation.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, well-structured, and self-contained; the problem is well-motivated and practical.
- The approach integrates existing techniques (joint continuous-time diffusion, feature-wise learnable schedulers, transformer backbone) into a unified framework, adapting them effectively to tabular data.
- The hybrid diffusion model handling both discrete and continuous variables is innovative and addresses a key challenge in tabular generation.
- The adaptively learnable noise schedules are novel and contribute to handling feature distribution disparities.
- Extensive experiments across seven datasets with comprehensive metrics (Tables 1–8) thoroughly validate performance, including ablation studies that illuminate design choices and scheduler benefits.
- The evaluation emphasizes both data fidelity and privacy preservation, providing a practical assessment for sensitive domains.
- The algorithmic sections present original findings contributing to machine learning advances.

### Weaknesses
- **Separate modeling of numerical and categorical features**: Despite the joint diffusion process, the underlying noise models and denoising networks are distinct for each data type. This separation may limit the model's ability to capture complex non-linear interactions (e.g., between a categorical 'region' and numerical 'average income'). It is unclear whether the two modalities help each other or are merely modeled separately (Reviewer 1, Question).
- **Performance comparable to TabSyn**: In Tables 1–3, TabDiff shows comparable performance to TabSyn (ICLR 2024) without a clear advantage. The lack of a significant gap raises questions about practical significance; a more rigorous comparison with additional metrics or datasets is needed.
- **Unmotivated scheduler choices**: The selection of power-mean and log-linear schedulers appears arbitrary. The paper lacks a justification for these specific choices over alternatives; a thorough theoretical or empirical analysis of scheduler impact on performance is required.
- **Algorithm description lacking details**: The method section and appendix need more detailed explanations to enhance readability (Reviewer 2). An explicit example for categorical column modeling would help.
- **Formula issues**: Formula (10) is missing a parenthesis. The construction of formulas (10) and (11) is unexplained; no source or rationale is provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Unclear handling of feature-wise heterogeneity**: The paper claims to tackle feature-wise heterogeneity but does not clarify how the model dynamically adjusts the diffusion process based on varying feature complexity. The mechanism for allocating capacity across heterogeneous features (e.g., learning to prioritize more complex marginal distributions) is missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Unmotivated masked diffusion for categorical features**: The use of masked diffusion (rather than other discrete diffusion frameworks) lacks justification. It is not explained why the loss is evaluated only on masked columns, nor are limitations of masking discussed (Reviewer 3, Question).
- **Title ambiguity about "multi-modal"**: The title suggests handling multiple modalities (e.g., images and tables) but the paper only addresses mixed-type tabular data (numerical and categorical). The distinction from TabDDPM, which also handles mixed-type data, is not sufficiently demonstrated (Reviewers 3, 5).
- **Computational cost**: The complexity of TabDiff may increase computational costs compared to simpler models. A comparison of generation/training speed with baselines should be provided (Reviewer 4).
- **Unexpected performance drop of TabDDPM**: On the News and Diabetes datasets, TabDDPM shows extreme performance degradation (precision and recall drop to 0 in Tables 7 and 8). The paper lacks analysis or discussion of such performance gaps and why TabDiff avoids them (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient baseline experimental setup details**: The experimental setup for baselines is inadequately explained, making results harder to interpret and undermining the comparative analysis (Reviewer 4).
