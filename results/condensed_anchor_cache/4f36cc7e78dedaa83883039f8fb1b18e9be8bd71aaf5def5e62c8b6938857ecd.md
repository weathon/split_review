## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Free-form Injective Flow (FIF), which lifts architectural constraints of prior injective flows by introducing an efficient gradient estimator for the maximum likelihood loss that works with unconstrained autoencoder architectures. The authors also identify a pathological behavior in joint manifold and likelihood learning and propose a modified stable objective. Experiments on toy, tabular, and image data are reported. Reviewers agree on the originality and potential of the core idea but diverge on clarity, empirical validation strength, and novelty relative to other generative autoencoders.

### Strengths
- **Clear exposition and motivation**: The paper is well-written, polished, and easy to follow. The introduction cleanly motivates lifting architectural constraints in injective flows. Background and the path to the final model are laid out well, and the appendix is thorough. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Simple and elegant gradient estimator**: The main idea – using the encoder to efficiently estimate the change-of-variables gradient – is wonderfully simple, sensible, and efficient. The observation that when the encoder is the left inverse of the decoder, Jacobians can be rewritten yields clear computational gains. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Well motivated contributions**: The paper effectively situates itself in related work and convincingly argues that prior injective flows suffer from restrictive architectures and/or high computational cost. (R1, R3)
- **Original and potentially valuable**: The techniques are original, removing limitations from prior methods, and are interesting. (R2)
- **Effective figures**: Figures 2 and 3 clearly illustrate the trade-off between reconstruction and log-likelihood, and the improvement over previous injective flows. (R1)
- **Good empirical results**: The method outperforms other injective flows and generative autoencoders in several experiments. (R3; note disagreement from R4, see Weaknesses)
- **Thorough derivations**: The autograd and linear-algebra derivations demonstrate deep thinking about gradient estimation. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of ablations**: Three improvements over rectangular flows (unconstrained architecture, new gradient estimator eq. 10, stability fix eq. 16) are proposed but their individual contributions are not isolated. Ablations should be added to Table 1: same architecture as rectangular flows with (a) eq. 10 estimator and (b) eq. 16 estimator, to distinguish performance gains from architecture versus gradient estimator. Impact of hyperparameters (e.g., reconstruction weight) is also not investigated. (R3, R4)
- **Unclear significance over generative autoencoders**: The method can be seen as training an autoencoder with a different loss, but novelty and benefits over other autoencoder losses are not fully clarified. In Table 3, best results are from other autoencoders, and no comparison to more modern autoencoder architectures is provided. The method also does not maintain all benefits of injective flows (exact inverses). A dedicated discussion of advantages over generative autoencoders is warranted. (R1)
- **Clarity and exposition issues**: Important derivations and assumptions should be moved to the main manuscript. Specific unclear points: (i) How is \(\det[g'(z)^T g'(z)] > 0\) guaranteed without architectural constraints? (ii) What are the assumptions underlying \(f(\hat x) = f(x)\) before Eq. 12, and why does Eq. 12 hold? If \(p_{\text{data}}(x) = \hat p_{\text{data}}(\hat x)\), is the RHS of Eq. 14 fixed? (iii) After the modification in Eq. 16, the final objective in Eq. 18 (first two terms) is not identical to negative maximum likelihood – what are the differences? (iv) The connection to the training issue from Brehmer & Cranmer (2020) needs a more precise explanation, especially regarding the toy problem used to illustrate the issue. (v) Section ordering is confusing: FID is defined in §5.3 but used in §5.2; Table 2 requires more description (what “IS” is, what the two samplers are). (vi) The notation \(f^{-1}=g\) should be avoided; left inverses is more appropriate. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Pathological behavior analysis and fix not fully convincing**: (i) Figure 2 does not prove reconstruction error is insufficient – if entropy becomes arbitrarily negative, reconstruction may become harder and regularize the solution. (ii) The bound in Eq. 14 is a lower bound on the loss, not an upper bound; additional arguments are needed for the loss to become arbitrarily negative when entropy decreases, as the KL divergence in Eq. 13 must not grow faster than entropy. This may depend on architecture constraints. (iii) The fix may not change anything in the linear-encoder toy problem from Brehmer & Cranmer, where \(f'(\hat x) = f'(x)\). (iv) Why does the pathological behavior not affect rectangular flows? Possibly because restrictive architecture implicitly regularizes curvature, or because it is a hidden issue. (v) The loss modification changes the gradient – is the fix guaranteed to work? (R1, R3, R4)
- **Weak experimental evaluation**: (i) No generated samples are provided for generative models; FID has come under scrutiny and would benefit from visual support. (ii) Inception Scores in §5.3 are only in line with or worse than baselines, and the best ones are not bolded in Table 3; the reliability of IS as a measure of diversity is questionable. (iii) The quality of samples in Figure 1 does not obviously support the claim that the method outperforms various VAE methods. (iv) It is unclear whether all models are converged; extending training for baselines might change results. (v) Training time speedup in Table 1 is inconsistent and does not scale with dimension in a predictable way. (R1, R4)
- **Missing limitations section**: The paper lacks a dedicated discussion of limitations, only partially covered in the conclusion. (R1)
- **Estimator necessity**: It is unclear whether the new gradient estimator is truly necessary or if standard techniques would suffice. A detailed comparison to existing gradient estimation methods, including computational costs and convergence properties, is missing. (R4)
- **Overall contributions thin and use case unclear**: Injective flows are academically interesting but lack a clear practical use case, especially if the density is not tractable. Benefits over diffusion models or VAEs are not articulated. (R4). Two reviewers (2 and 4) rated the paper 5, raising these concerns, while reviewers 1 and 3 rated it 8 and found the contributions significant.
- **Surrogate loss accuracy not verified**: The claim that the surrogate loss is accurate only when \(f\) and \(g\) are optimal for reconstruction is not fully proven: reconstruction error is not reported; other changes to the loss function may contribute to stability; the trade-off between reconstruction and likelihood (Figure 3) may prevent full optimization of reconstruction. (R1)
- **Why not use a flexible prior \(p_Z\)?**: The paper reduces expressivity on the latent space (Gaussian or mixture) even though previous work recommends flexible latent distributions in injective flows (e.g., Dai & Wipf 2019, Loaiza-Ganem et al. 2022). Why is a flow on the latent space not used? (R3)
- **Minor issues**: Missing period at the end of the injective flows paragraph in §3; \citet should be \citep in Appendix E.3. The string “traces are performed in order \(f' g'\)” is mentioned twice but could still benefit from more explanation in the main text. The definition of entropy for a manifold-supported distribution (Eq. 13) is not standard and should be clarified. (R1, R3)
- **Rectangular flows computational claim**: It is stated that rectangular flows require \(O(d)\) vjp/jvp for convergence, but conjugate gradient has exponential convergence and thus many fewer iterations suffice. (R1)
