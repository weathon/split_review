## Merged Review

### Summary

This work studies sampling from multimodal distributions using vanilla score matching, a method known to fail for such targets. The authors propose a recipe: initialize the Langevin diffusion at the empirical distribution over the available data points and stop the diffusion at a finite time \(T\). The main theoretical result shows that, under idealized assumptions (mixtures of strongly log‑concave components, exact score, support within a known radius), this scheme generates a sample close to the target in total variation distance. The analysis removes the dependence on the log‑Sobolev constant of the whole mixture that appears in the prior work of Lee et al. (2022, Theorem 2.1). The paper also includes toy experiments (mixture of Gaussians in 1D and 2D) and provides code in the supplementary material. Reviewer opinions are split: two reviewers (R2, R4, both rating 8) find the theoretical contribution clear and valuable, while one reviewer (R3, rating 3, high confidence) considers the paper poorly written, lacking comprehensive experiments, and relying on unrealistic assumptions. Reviewer 1 (rating 6, low confidence) appreciates the direction but finds the experiments unconvincing and the connection to early stopping unclear.

### Strengths

1. **Addresses a known limitation of vanilla score matching** – tackles the widely‑accepted difficulty of learning multimodal distributions, with potential to inspire new generative algorithms. (R1, R3⁽¹⁾, R4)
2. **Formalizes an intuitive idea** – initializing inside component typical sets avoids the need for inter‑component transitions; the paper laboriously links data‑based initialization with existing analyses of approximate Langevin and discretization. (R2)
3. **Clear pedagogical exposition** – the main text provides a simple setting and a proof outline that is easy to follow, and the overall structure (main result, positioning, open questions, proof strategy) is reminiscent of applied‑mathematics or learning‑theory papers. (R2, R4)
4. **Connection to recent theory** – draws links to work on conditionally strongly log‑concave generative models (Guth et al., ICML 2023) and to prior convergence analyses of score‑based generative modeling. (R4)
5. **Technical correctness** – the proof strategy is well‑developed and, to the best of the reviewer’s understanding, correct. (R4)
6. **Reproducibility** – code for the toy examples is provided in the supplementary material. (R1)

⁽¹⁾ R3’s only stated strength is that “sampling from multimodal distributions is an extremely interesting problem.”

### Weaknesses

- **Experiments are weak and lack quantitative evaluation** (R1, R3, R4):
  - Figure 1(b) does not plot the ground‑truth density; the learned weight of the right component appears inaccurate (roughly 0.125 vs. true ≈0.15). No numerical means, variances, or component weights are reported for Figure 1 or Figure 2.
  - No evaluation on real‑world or high‑dimensional data; the paper’s claims are supported only by toy 1D/2D mixture‑of‑Gaussians examples.
  - The experimental details in Appendix I are hard to relate to the theoretical assumptions of Theorem 1.
- **Idealized assumptions limit practical applicability** (R3, R4):
  - The analysis assumes exact knowledge of the score function (or an extremely accurate parametric approximation). Even the vanilla score can be costly to compute (e.g., trace of the Hessian in score matching). (R4)
  - The ground truth distribution is assumed to be supported within a known radius; the method requires many hard‑to‑tune parameters (\(\delta\), \(H\), \(\epsilon\), step size \(h\)), whose sensitivity makes generalization across datasets challenging. (R3)
  - The theoretical bounds require the score approximation error to be very small, which is hardly achievable in practice. (R4)
- **Exponential dependencies in key bounds** (R4):
  - The stopping time \(T\) has a bound exponential in the number of components \(K\); the step‑size bound decreases exponentially with data complexity. No discussion of the practical implications of these explosions is provided.
- **Insufficient discussion of the role of samples** (R2, R4):
  - The number \(M\) of i.i.d. samples needed for initialization is not clearly related to the proof strategy; it does not seem to depend on properties of the target \(\mu\) beyond the number of components. (R2)
  - The same \(M\) samples are likely used to learn the score and to initialize the chain – this dependence could break concentration arguments; it is unclear whether a held‑out set is required. (R2)
- **Weak connection between early‑stopping and theory** (R1):
  - The theoretical development does not explicitly incorporate early stopping; the link between the empirical claim and the proof is unclear.
- **Missing comparisons and references** (R2, R3):
  - No comparison with other theoretical bounds or existing methods. The paper claims a “long line of experimental work” using vanilla Langevin but provides no citations. (R2, R3)
  - The extension of the Poincaré‑inequality decomposition result to a log‑Sobolev inequality is presented as a contribution, but such an extension likely already exists in the functional‑inequalities literature (Madras & Randall, 2002). (R2)
- **Exposition and structural issues** (R1, R2, R3):
  - The paper is poorly written and hard to follow; it jumps between motivation, related work, and possible extensions (Section 1.2). Some arguments are unconvincing (e.g., computational‑hardness motivation that criticizes an alternative never used; Remark 5 is excessively long for a simple point about typical sets). (R2, R3)
  - The editorial format is unconventional: there is no conclusion, and a large fraction of the 9 pages is devoted to proof strategy, leaving little space for experiments, conclusions, or comparison with prior work. (R4)
  - Minor typos: “<when>” missing, “samplling”, “we have can draw”. (R1)
- **Post‑rebuttal note**: Reviewer 4 initially raised several of the above concerns but later acknowledged that the authors engaged with them and made efforts to improve the paper, leading to a score increase from an implied lower rating to 8. This indicates that some weaknesses were partially addressed.
