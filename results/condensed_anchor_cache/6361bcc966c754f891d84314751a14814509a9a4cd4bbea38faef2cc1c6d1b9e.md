## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (MARLHF), defining the task as identifying a Nash equilibrium from preference-only offline data in general-sum games. The main theoretical contribution is proving that single-policy coverage is insufficient and that unilateral coverage is both necessary and sufficient for learning approximate Nash equilibria. Two algorithmic techniques are proposed: (1) MSE regularization along the time axis to produce more uniform reward distributions, and (2) dataset distribution-based pessimism (using imitation learning for density modeling) to handle extrapolation errors. The final policy is trained with VDN. Experiments are run on three MPE environments (Spread-v3, Tag-v3, Reference-v3). The paper is framed as the first work on aligning multi-agent systems with human feedback.

### Strengths
- The paper makes novel contributions by extending RLHF to multi-agent systems and establishing fundamental theoretical results about dataset coverage for Nash equilibrium learning. The proofs for why single-policy coverage is insufficient and why unilateral coverage is necessary and sufficient are clear and well-justified.
- Upper complexity bounds for Nash equilibrium in MARLHF are provided, with explicit bounds on the Nash-gap.
- The theoretical insights directly inform the practical design of the framework.
- The empirical work is well-designed for validating the theoretical claims and the effectiveness of the two proposed techniques. Clear ablation studies are performed across three distinct multi-agent scenarios (cooperative target coverage, coordinated pursuit, and communication-dependent navigation).
- Hyperparameters are provided, multiple seeds are used, and results that do not support the desired conclusion are presented. The experimental setup is clearly described, making the work highly reproducible.
- The authors introduce a principled standardization technique for hyperparameter tuning across different environments.
- Reviewer 1 (score 6, confidence 4) is more positive overall, noting that the paper is generally well-written, the theoretical results are comprehensive, and the approach represents a significant step toward preference-based multi-agent RL. This reviewer rated contribution as 3.
- Reviewer 4 (also rating contribution 3) states the theoretical claims are concise and practically useful, and the experiments are well-designed for the purpose of verifying claims.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited empirical scope**: Experiments are conducted only on three simple MPE environments (Spread-v3, Tag-v3, Reference-v3), which have limited action/state spaces and lack the complexity to fully test robustness in longer time horizons or more intricate agent interactions. The paper should be tested on more complex environments from JAXMARL (e.g., Overcooked, Hanabi, StarCraft). Additionally, using more tasks (e.g., five) would strengthen findings.
- **No real human feedback**: Despite the title "from Human Feedback", all preferences are simulated using the Bradley-Terry-Luce model based on known environment rewards. This is a significant limitation because real human preferences are likely noisier, inconsistent, and possibly non-transitive. The paper should more explicitly acknowledge this gap and discuss modifications needed for real human feedback.
- **Insufficient baselines**: Only VDN is used as the base MARL algorithm, but VDN is known to have limited representation capacity. Comparisons with other MARL algorithms (e.g., MAPPO, IPPO, QMIX) and with other offline MARL or preference-based learning methods are missing. Ablation with more advanced MARL methods would better validate the proposed techniques.
- **Lack of statistical significance testing**: Results are reported with means and standard deviations over only 5 random seeds, but no hypothesis tests or statistical significance analyses are performed. With such a small sample size, reliability of comparisons is questionable (e.g., in Spread-v3, the difference between Mix-Unilateral and Mix-Expert may not be meaningful). The paper lacks statistical rigor for its empirical conclusions.
- **Gap between theory and practice**: The theoretical analysis assumes linear Markov games with known feature mappings, but the experiments use neural networks. The paper does not discuss how this gap affects applicability or whether any theoretical guarantees carry over. The bound in Theorem 3 (Line 276) does not illustrate approximation error in relation to the size of the offline dataset, and comparisons with existing convergence rates in offline RL/MARL are missing.
- **Incomplete related work discussion**: The paper should discuss recent advances in decentralized multi-agent RL, sample-efficient multi-agent RL, overoptimization in RLHF, and value-incentivized preference optimization. Similar ideas about reward regularization (reward-biased maximum likelihood estimation, Bellman-consistent pessimism) have been adopted in different contexts and should be compared.
- **Unclear or inconsistent claims with data**:
  - Table 2 caption claims "in more challenging environments, such as Tag-v3, dataset diversity plays a substantially more significant role", but the data shows best mean and variance for Tag-v3 in the Pure-Expert dataset (least diversity), contradicting the claim.
  - Claims in Lines 419-420 that "the importance of unilateral coverage and diversity becomes more pronounced in more challenging tasks (as reflected by higher MSE)" are not obvious: the best performance for Mix-Unilateral (Spread-v3) and Diversified (Reference-v3) occurs when corresponding MSE is low.
  - Table 3: Setting β as large as 100 yields good results, but one would expect the penalty term to dominate. It is also strange that β across a wide range (1–100) yields almost the same result, especially for the diversified dataset containing many low-return trajectories.
- **Clarity issues**: Reviewer 4 notes that Figure 1 caption mentions π_ref but it does not appear in the figure (perhaps π_b is meant). Blue text in Lines 300-301 (likely author comments) may leak identity. Figure 2 x-axis is not labeled. Reviewer 3 notes that Line 300 appears to contain colored comments that may leak author information.
- **Questions indicating missing aspects**:
  - Why was VDN chosen over more advanced MARL algorithms? How would the approach perform with MAPPO, IPPO, or QMIX?
  - How was preference feedback collected? Was it synthetic based on true rewards or real human preferences? What policy generated the trajectories for preference annotation?
  - How does the approach handle the inherent dependence between the policy used to train the reward model and the policy being learned (a known issue in single-agent RLHF)?
  - How does the quality of the learned reward function vary with different levels of expertise and sparsity in preference feedback?
  - How would the method scale with increasing numbers of agents?
  - In cases where mixed-skill policies outperform pure expert policies, can the authors verify this reflects genuine improvement rather than issues with reward modeling?
  - How would the approach need to be modified for inconsistent/non-transitive real human preferences?
  - What is the novel aspect of the theoretical analysis that differentiates it from the offline MARL setting?
  - In the RLHF setting, the performance metric definition remains unchanged from RL without KL regularization – please clarify.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 2 (score 5, confidence 4) is more critical, emphasizing the lack of actual human feedback (suggesting a title change), the unclear framing of Nash equilibrium as the desired solution concept in all cases, and the gap between theory and practice. This reviewer also notes the theoretical assumptions may not hold in practice and the empirical evaluation lacks statistical tests and contains contradictions in claims. In contrast, Reviewer 1 (score 6) is more positive but still notes limited experimental validation.
