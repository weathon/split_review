## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper links grokking (delayed generalization) with the lottery ticket hypothesis, proposing “grokking tickets” – subnetworks identified by magnitude pruning during the generalization phase. It reports that using these tickets significantly accelerates generalization on modular arithmetic, polynomial regression, sparse parity, and MNIST, and suggests that this accelerated generalization is due to subnetwork structure rather than weight norm or sparsity per se. The authors also observe rapid changes in subnetwork structure (measured via Jaccard distance) that correlate with test accuracy improvements, and show that pruning alone can transform a memorizing network into a generalizing one without weight updates.

### Strengths
- The paper innovatively connects two previously separate phenomena – grokking and the lottery ticket hypothesis – offering a fresh angle that challenges the predominant weight‑norm explanation and highlights the role of internal subnetwork discovery.
- Experimental setup is robust and thorough: controlled experiments with multiple tasks (modular arithmetic, polynomial regression, sparse parity, MNIST), architectures (MLPs, transformers), pruning techniques (magnitude pruning, SNIP, Synflow, edge‑popup), and careful isolation of subnetworks from confounders like weight norm and sparsity.
- The relationship between pruning ratio and the duration of the grokking period (e.g., a critical ratio of 0.81 enabling generalization without weight decay) is an interesting and well‑presented finding.
- The connection between test accuracy changes during training and the evolution of network masks (e.g., Jaccard distance) provides a novel perspective on the phase transition from memorization to generalization.
- The writing is generally clear, and visual aids help in comparing grokking, weight norms, and subnetwork dynamics.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical grounding is weak.** The paper lacks a clear theoretical explanation for why specific subnetworks (grokking tickets) cause faster generalization. Several reviewers note that deeper connection to established theories (e.g., simplicity bias, double descent, sparsity studies such as Varma et al. 2023, Merrill et al. 2023) would strengthen the argument.  
  *One reviewer (score 6) appreciated the conceptual innovation, but still found that claims like “good structure is linked to good representations” and the role of Fourier entropy as a measure of periodicity are insufficiently justified.*
- **Experimental scope is limited** to relatively simple tasks (modular arithmetic, MNIST) and fully‑connected networks. Reviewers agree that demonstrations on more complex domains – e.g., natural language processing with BERT, or image classification with ResNet on CIFAR‑10 – are needed to assess generalizability across architectures and task distributions.
- **Failure to decouple “shorter grokking period” from the known “easier training” property of winning tickets.** The lottery ticket literature already shows that winning tickets often achieve better performance in fewer epochs. The paper does not convincingly separate this “easy‑to‑train” behavior from a mechanism specific to grokking; the results could be a special case of that known property.
- **Problematic experimental designs in Section 4 undermine conclusions.**  
  (a) When constructing a network with the same weight norm as a pruned network, the authors simply shrink all weights by a constant factor. There is no evidence that this preserves performance or would aid generalization, as weight‑decay training does. Hence the comparison may be against an arbitrarily bad baseline.  
  (b) Comparison with pruning‑at‑initialization (PaI) methods (SNIP, Synflow) is confounded because PaI methods are known to sacrifice performance for computational efficiency. Showing that lottery tickets outperform PaI methods may be trivial.  
  *Most reviewers considered these comparisons insufficient to support the claim that structure, not weight norm/sparsity, drives grokking.*
- **Hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay) are not tuned.** The base model for modular addition can generalize much faster than 25k steps with proper tuning; therefore, the reported “65× acceleration” using grokking tickets may be significantly overstated and unfair.
- **The use of Jaccard distance as a progress measure is not convincingly justified.** The observed rapid change in Jaccard distance during phase transition may simply reflect the rapid change in weight magnitudes that also drives the metric. One reviewer notes that Jaccard distance correlates with test accuracy, but that correlation may be a by‑product of weight‑norm changes rather than evidence of a distinct structural mechanism.
- **Unclear practical implications.** The paper does not translate its insights into actionable guidance for practitioners (e.g., how to identify grokking tickets in practice, or how they could influence training/pruning regimes). This limits the impact of the findings.
- **Lack of a clear, unified argument.** The paper seems more focused on the grokking behavior of pruned subnetworks rather than using the lottery ticket hypothesis as a tool to understand the inner structure that drives grokking in the full network. This reduces the coherence of the story.
- **Other missing analyses and clarifications:**
  - The distinction between grokking tickets and typical winning tickets from LTH studies is not explored; it is unclear whether they share the same characteristics or are task‑specific.
  - The claim that subnetworks learn better representations (Section 5.3) may be circular for simple tasks where generalization coincides with representation learning; it remains unverified for more complex tasks where benign overfitting could occur.
  - The paper does not examine whether a good lottery ticket can be identified during the memorization phase (before the phase transition), which would be more useful for accelerating grokking.
  - The critical pruning ratio (0.81) might vary with architecture and dataset; no sensitivity analysis is provided.
  - Additional metrics (e.g., neuron activation similarity, weight‑sparsity patterns) could have been used to validate the importance of subnetwork structures.
- **Missing figure reference** (line 486).
