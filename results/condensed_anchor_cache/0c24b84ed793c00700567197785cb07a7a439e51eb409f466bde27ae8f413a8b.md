## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes convex semidefinite programs (SDPs) for adversarial training of two-layer polynomial activation networks, proving that the global optimum of the convex SDP matches that of the nonconvex formulation (Theorem 3.1). It also provides scalable PyTorch implementations (approximating the SDP for ReLU networks via gradient-based sampling) and experiments on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10. The authors retrain the final two fully connected layers of a Pre-Activation ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 using both polynomial and ReLU activations, evaluating robust accuracy against FGSM ℓ∞ attacks and comparing against sharpness-aware minimization (SAM). Reviewers note the theoretical novelty but raise significant concerns about the experimental evaluation, scope, and presentation.

### Strengths
- **Theoretical novelty (R1, R2, R3, R4):** The convex SDP reformulation for adversarial training of two-layer polynomial networks is new. Theorem 3.1 proves global optimality equivalence between the convex and non-convex formulations, and the proofs are detailed and clear.
- **Practical implementation (R4):** The authors provide a feasible, performant PyTorch implementation (avoiding costly SDP solvers) that works for both polynomial and ReLU activations, compatible with GPU acceleration and standard libraries.
- **Importance of provable defenses (R3):** Establishing a provably optimal adversarial training objective is crucial, and convexity enables global solution guarantees.
- **Empirical support for polynomial SDP (R3, R4):** Experiments on multiple datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, partial CIFAR-10) show improved robust accuracy against FGSM ℓ∞ attacks relative to the standard convex training baseline, and the method outperforms SAM on the full CIFAR-10 fine-tuning task.
- Two of the four reviewers (R3, R4) find the paper well-written and view the contributions as valuable; R4 specifically notes it is among the first works on convex reformulation of adversarial training.

### Weaknesses
- **Outdated/weak attack evaluation (R1, R2, R3):** All three lower-rated reviewers (and R3 secondarily) criticize the use of FGSM only. They argue FGSM underestimates adversarial power and does not approximate worst-case ℓ∞ perturbations. The authors should evaluate with stronger attacks such as PGD (Madry et al., 2017) and AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, 2020). Reviewers also note that the theoretical setting uses ℓ2 perturbations (Theorem 3.1) but experiments only test ℓ∞ – ℓ2 robustness should be tested as well.
- **Weak baseline choice (R1, R2):** The baseline model is trained with sharpness-aware minimization (SAM), which targets generalization, not adversarial robustness. A comparison with standard adversarial training (e.g., Madry et al. 2017) is needed to demonstrate effectiveness; ideally showing whether the convex and adversarial training lead to similar weights and comparable robustness.
- **Limited scope and practical relevance (R1, R2, R3, R4):** The theoretical results apply only to two-layer networks without bias terms and with polynomial (or approximated ReLU) activations. Most modern networks use deeper ReLU architectures. The paper does not discuss how to generalize to deeper networks, bias terms, or general ℓp perturbations. In addition, experiments are conducted on relatively small datasets (subsets of CIFAR-10 for polynomial) and the SDP remains costly, raising concerns about scalability. R3 notes that polynomial activation may not generalize to larger datasets.
- **Missing clean accuracy reporting (R3, R4):** The authors report only robust accuracy under attack but do not report clean accuracy of the adversarially trained models. The trade-off between clean and robust accuracy is a critical issue in adversarial training; clean accuracy should be reported.
- **Insufficient experimental results (R2, R3, R4):** Besides the missing comparisons and attacks, reviewers find the set of experiments limited. Polynomial activation results are only on 1% of CIFAR-10 due to cost; run times and training costs are not reported. R2 specifically asks for comparisons with adversarial training (Madry et al.) to see if the convex method yields similar weights and robustness.
- **Missing literature review / discussion (R2):** The paper lacks discussion of related certified defense methods that also reformulate training as convex optimization (e.g., Raghunathan et al. 2018, Wong & Kolter 2018). The implications of Theorem 3.1 for the field of adversarial machine learning are not clearly explained.
- **Presentation problems (R2, R4):** Sections 2-3 contain overly dense mathematical notation without adequate explanation. There is a typo in the title (“Polyomial”). The paper would benefit from thorough proofreading and restructuring to highlight discussions and implications. R2 notes the presentation makes it hard to see the paper’s position in the literature.
- **Unclear aspects of the ReLU approximation and optimality (R4, R2):** For the ReLU case, the authors approximate the convex program (15) using gradient-based optimization on program (16) with samples from the set [P]. Questions remain: optimality guarantees for this approximation, how many samples are sufficient, and error analysis. R2 asks whether Theorem 3.1 can be extended to ReLU activations or general ℓp perturbations.
- **Parameter choices and comparisons (R4):** The authors do not report values for tuning parameters β, r, and ρ for program (16), nor justify why different r values are used for polynomial and ReLU activations. It is also unclear whether performance differences are due to architecture choice or the convex method.
- **Comparative performance question (R3):** At ℓ∞ = 8/255 on CIFAR-10, the method achieves only ~40% robust accuracy against FGSM, while many state-of-the-art defenses achieve much higher. Reviewers question if this conflicts with the claimed optimality of the convex solution.
