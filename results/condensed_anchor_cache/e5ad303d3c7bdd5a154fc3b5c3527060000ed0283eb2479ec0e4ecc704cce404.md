- Scores: 5, 6, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes \((n,r)\)-consistency as a condition for chain-of-thought (CoT) representations to enable length generalization (LG) in Transformers. It provides a theoretical sufficiency result and supports it with empirical experiments on parity, addition, multiplication, and division. Reviewers agreed that LG is an important problem and that the experimental results are compelling for these tasks. However, there was significant disagreement on the paper’s contribution: Reviewer 3 rated it an 8, praising the sound theory and strong empirical evidence, while Reviewers 1 and 2 (scores 5 and 6) raised concerns about the generality of the manually designed CoT schemes, the overly broad claims, and the practical limitations of the approach.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important and challenging problem of length generalization in reasoning, where Transformers typically struggle.
- Provides a clear sufficient condition (\((n,r)\)-consistency) that theoretically guarantees length generalization for a defined class of problems.
- Strong empirical results on historically challenging tasks: parity, addition, multiplication, and division (division is a newly considered task). The CoT schemes are shown to be learnable and achieve 100% performance on the tested lengths.
- The multi-line scratchpad/CoT implementation and the decoder-only Transformer design are interesting contributions.
- The paper correctly frames \((n,r)\)-consistency as a guiding target for designing CoT decompositions.

### Weaknesses
- **Manual CoT decomposition is the core limitation**: The CoT schemes are manually crafted for each task; no systematic method is given to derive them. This heavily limits practical applicability and scalability to new or more complex problems (R1, R2). There is also no theoretical analysis of the complexity of finding such decompositions (R2).
- **Modeling assumptions differ from standard CoT and are not properly acknowledged**: The model operates stepwise (\(\text{model}(S_i)=S_{i+1}\)) instead of generating the full CoT from a single prompt. The user manually controls termination, and additional processing (e.g., reformatting between steps, manually inserted orange tokens) is applied. These engineering choices are not explained as task-specific hacks and raise doubts about generalizability to settings where such fine-grained control is unavailable (R1).
- **Overclaiming in the theoretical framing**: The paper presents \((n,r)\)-consistency as *the* root cause of LG, but it is only a sufficient condition for a restricted class. The abstract and introduction should be revised to avoid implying necessity (R2; R1 also criticizes Theorem 3.1). R2 notes that other biases (e.g., low-complexity biases like Solomonoff induction) can also solve LG, so the claim of having identified a unique root cause is misleading.
- **Unclear learnability and scalability in practice**: The experiments use specialized Transformers; it is unknown whether results would transfer to standard architectures like GPT-2 or LLaMA (R1). The scaling of CoT steps with problem length (e.g., multiplication) may become prohibitive, and training a single model on multiple tasks with very different CoT formats is not addressed (R2). Generalization to lengths beyond those tested (where performance might drop) was not explored (R1).
- **Missing experiments and analysis**:  
  - Robustness to errors in CoT steps was not tested; the approach may be brittle (R3).  
  - Padding token handling and the exact transformation from output[k] to input[k+1] are insufficiently explained (R1).  
  - The paper does not discuss how to determine \(n\) and \(r\) for a given problem in practice (R2).  
- **Theoretical weaknesses**: Theorem 3.1 relies on an unjustified Lipschitz assumption on continuous intervals mapped to discrete tokens; a simpler discrete formulation would be more appropriate (R1). The definitions in Section 3.3 are notation-heavy and hard to parse; a figure would help (R2). Minor: \((n,r)\)-consistency is labeled a “necessary condition” in some places but is actually only sufficient (R2).
- **Minor errors and omissions**:  
  - “Def 3. … are r-length” (typo; R3).  
  - L251: \(s_{j,l}\) and \(s_{j,r}\) are swapped (R2).  
  - L347: “4 classes” should be “5” (R2).  
  - L133: “Note that we do not define CoT formally” is a weakness for a theory paper (R2).  
  - The problem statement (L136) says “performs well” but the math implies perfect accuracy; this should be consistent (R2).

**Reviewer disagreement note:** Reviewer 3 was notably more positive (score 8), finding the theory sound and the experiments compelling, while the other two reviewers (scores 5 and 6) emphasized the limitations in generality, the manual nature of the approach, and the overclaimed theoretical contributions.
