## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces counterfactual simulatability—whether an explanation enables humans to predict model outputs on counterfactual inputs—as a metric for evaluating LLM explanations. Two measures are proposed: simulation precision and simulation generality. Using GPT-4 and GPT-3 to generate counterfactuals and GPT-4 as a human simulator, the authors evaluate GPT-3 and GPT-4 on multi-hop factual reasoning (StrategyQA) and reward modeling (SHP). They report that explanations have low precision (around 80% for binary classification), precision does not correlate with plausibility, and that naively optimizing human approval (e.g., RLHF) may not improve simulatability.

### Strengths
- The topic is interesting and important to the current NLP field, with potential applications to addressing LLM hallucination.
- The paper introduces novel metrics (counterfactual simulatability) that focus on whether explanations help humans build mental models of LLM behavior—a useful and underexplored evaluation angle.
- The methodology is well-constructed: formalizing simulatability via logical entailment is a useful idea that could automate explanation evaluations.
- The paper is clearly written, with abundant analysis and easy-to-follow reasoning. Sanity checks in Section 5.1 justify design choices and clarify reader questions.
- Experiments are conducted on two diverse tasks (multi-hop factual reasoning and reward modeling) and demonstrate limitations of existing explanation methods.
- The findings and insights have potential to shape future research and development in explainability.

### Weaknesses
- **LLM as human simulator is questionable** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).  
  * The claim that GPT-4 has similar agreement with humans as humans do with each other is not convincing: Table 2 reports percentages calculated as exact IAA with humans divided by average IAA between humans, so the true agreement is much lower.  
  * Even with similar agreement, that does not establish GPT-4 as an accurate representation of human cognition—it only shows GPT-4 can replicate the diversity among humans.  
  * The human simulation task is complex and subjective, leading to only moderate inter-annotator agreement, which extends to LLMs. More discussion on limitations, potential biases, and how well LLM simulators capture human reasoning is needed.

- **Conceptual confusion between precision and faithfulness** (Reviewer 3).  
  * The term “precision” looks very much like “faithfulness” as defined in many prior works. The definition in Section 3.2 (consistency with model behavior) is often called faithfulness. Why invent a new name?  
  * The paper assumes language models have “knowledge,” but LMs do not “know” or “understand” information; they generate responses based on learned patterns (Reviewer 2).

- **Generality desideratum unjustified** (Reviewer 3).  
  * The claim that an ideal explanation should be generalizable is not adequately justified. As long as an explanation is faithful, being more general or more specific is not necessarily preferable.  
  * The authors argue generality and precision are independent, but this is contested: an extremely local explanation could be precise but not general. The paper would benefit from discussing whether these desiderata are truly independent, and further analysis in Section 5.2.2.

- **Lack of comparison to other evaluation metrics and baselines** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).  
  * No comparison with existing evaluation metrics for explanations, making it difficult to determine relative performance.  
  * Without baseline explanations (e.g., from human users), absolute numbers are hard to interpret. For example, how do we know 80% precision is “low”? What statistics exist for human explanations? Can the number be larger for humans?  
  * Table 5: What is reported? Correlation coefficient? Spearman or Pearson? p-values? Statistical significance needed.

- **Evaluation limited to classification tasks** (Reviewer 2). The metrics need extension to more complex generation tasks.

- **Paper’s underlying motivation not clearly articulated** (Reviewer 1). The metric resembles a “hallucination” phenomenon, so the contribution’s novelty is not highlighted—the apparent issue is hallucinatory responses to similar questions.

- **Incorrect conclusion from Table 3** (Reviewer 1). GPT-mix shows a higher *similarity* score, not necessarily more diversity; the conclusion should be that GPT-mix generates more *relevant* counterfactuals.

- **Specific missing details and unanswered questions**:  
  * How were counterfactual questions generated? Specific patterns or entirely random? (Reviewer 2).  
  * Elaborate on the “Forced” strategy used for sanity checks (Reviewer 2).  
  * Evaluation assumes the explanation is the only information the model uses; how would the method account for additional information not in the explanation? (Reviewer 2).  
  * Why is 80% considered low precision? What is the expected value? (Reviewer 3).  
  * Clarify the independence of generality and precision (Reviewer 3).

- **Technical and formatting issues**:  
  * Typos: Section 3.1 second paragraph “Expectation” does not match formula below (Reviewer 1); “Muslin countries” → “Muslim countries” (Reviewer 3); Figure 2 says “robot’s answer” but should be “LLM’s answer” (Reviewer 3).  
  * Citation formatting: Some citations should use \citet{} vs \citep{} per formatting instructions (Reviewer 2). Inline citations are confusing (Reviewer 3).  
  * The human study does not mention IRB approval (Reviewer 3).  
  * Title is imprecise: “model” is too generic; should specify autoregressive language models (Reviewer 3).
