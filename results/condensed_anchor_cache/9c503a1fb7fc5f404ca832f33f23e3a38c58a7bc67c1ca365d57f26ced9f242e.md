## Merged Review

### Summary

TiDeSPL-VAE is a sequential latent variable model that decomposes visual neural activity into content (visual stimulus-driven) and style (internal state) latent representations with time-dependent dynamics, combining a VAE objective, contrastive learning, and a swap operation. Evaluated on synthetic data and mouse visual cortex recordings (Allen Brain Observatory) against baselines including LFADS, pi-VAE, and CEBRA. Reviewer opinions diverge: two reviewers assign low scores (3), one moderate (6), one high (8). While the idea of separating visual features from internal states and the focus on visual dynamics are seen as timely, the paper’s motivation, clarity, fairness of benchmarking, reproducibility, and demonstration of scientific novelty are heavily criticized. The high-scoring reviewer praises originality and thorough evaluation; the low-scoring reviewers argue that the model complexity drives performance and that key experimental details and baselines are missing.

### Strengths

- Developing flexible latent time series models for large neural population recordings is an important research direction.
- The idea of separating visual features (content) from internal states (style) is potentially beneficial for neuroscience and machine learning.
- The focus on neural dynamics in response to video input is timely and relevant.
- Rigorous research methodology: compared against a wide variety of baselines (LFADS, pi-VAE, CEBRA) on synthetic, natural images, and natural movies.
- Careful evaluation progression from simple toy synthetic experiments to a rich neural dataset.
- Sanity checks (e.g., shuffling time-dependent datasets) help disentangle the impact of time-dependency.
- Contributes a novel problem setting: decoding visual data from mice rather than the widely studied motor system.
- Authors are transparent about the limitations of the model.
- (Minority: high-scoring reviewer notes the work is original, includes necessary baselines, and has careful evaluation and sanity checks.)

### Weaknesses

**Motivation and clarity**  
- The motivation is poorly stated and contains inaccurate claims. For example, stating that motor-encoding models treat temporal relationships “in a way that does not conform to natural reality” is vague; actual motor decoding is still unsolved. Claims that LFADS or CEBRA treat temporal relationships “unnaturally” are not neurally accurate—LFADS uses a dynamical system and CEBRA leverages natural time offsets.  
- The writing is often vague and unclear. Specific examples: “we apply self-supervised contrastive learning to enhance the time constraint and to shape latent variables” (L74); “The output … is an estimate of firing rates of the input” (L134); “we added L2 regularization … to stabilize model training” (L213); “To enhance the effect of the positive sample, we adopt the practice of swapping content latent variables between the positive pairs while maintaining style latent variables” (L231).  
- Grammatical and typographical errors are present (e.g., L269 “and others are even difficult to split four clusters”, L482 “contrast learning”).  
- The architecture is complex: no clear justification is given for the factorized latent states and the swap operation. These choices are inherited from a previous publication without explanation. A conceptual or mathematical rationale is needed, or at least ablation experiments (e.g., with vs. without factorization).  
- The multi-objective loss (ELBO + weight decay + contrastive term) is hard to interpret as a unified optimization. It would help to express it in a coherent Bayesian framework rather than simply adding many terms, which would also aid hyperparameter selection.

**Benchmarking fairness and experimental details**  
- Insufficient details on how baselines were benchmarked. All models were simply set to 128 latent dimensions and trained for 5,000 iterations; there is no evidence of hyperparameter tuning or convergence checks for each method.  
- Model complexities differ drastically. CEBRA (Schneider et al. 2023) uses a 5‑layer MLP/GeLU; to fairly compare, the same RNN base should be used. No run‑time or parameter counts are provided.  
- Missing simple baselines (e.g., linear decoder) and chance performance levels, making it difficult to interpret the magnitude of improvements.  
- Missing benchmarks on common datasets used by previous work: Allen Brain Natural Movie 1 (as in CEBRA, where reported decoding is ~90%, vs. ~65% here), the Neural Latent Benchmark, and the Sensorium Challenge.  
- Missing related work on task-relevant/task‑irrelevant models (e.g., [1], [2]) that should have been benchmarked.  
- The statement that none of the baselines “build latent representations progressively along the chronological order” (L253) is unclear; both LFADS and CEBRA incorporate time.  
- It is unclear how time offsets were treated for CEBRA and LFADS beyond the note “5 time points for natural scenes and for natural movie”.  
- The ablation study reveals that removing components (swap, contrastive, recurrent structure) drops performance below simpler baselines (e.g., in Table 4, removal of key components yields <52.76%, while B‑VAE already reaches 52.76% in Table 3). This suggests that model complexity, not the latent separation innovation, drives performance. It would be informative to test a non‑recurrent version of TiDeSPL‑VAE and to train one of the best baselines with the contrastive loss and swap operation.  
- No evaluation of autoencoding (spike reconstruction) performance, which would help assess whether the model captures neural activity well.

**Data, generalizability, and reproducibility**  
- Large performance variability across mice (especially Mouse 1 in Tables 3/4) raises concerns about overfitting to individual subjects or experimental conditions. The reasoning for excluding a mouse is not fully explained.  
- It is unclear whether a separate model is trained per mouse or shared across mice; sample sizes per mouse after exclusion criteria appear small, risking overfitting.  
- The model’s robustness to different visual stimuli (e.g., simple gratings) is not tested, so generalizability beyond natural images/movies is unknown.  
- The train/test split with respect to time is not specified; it is critical to ensure that no future responses leak into training, especially since the model uses a time‑dependent contrastive objective with some Δt.  
- In Figure 3, there is a clear train/test bias (points separated by train/test split) that is stronger than stimulus‑based cluster separation, which needs interpretation.  
- Interpreting t‑SNE visualizations is risky without controlling for hyperparameters.  
- The model treats visual neural data identically to motor data (reconstructs spikes); to truly leverage the visual modality, the work should either decode the input stimuli (e.g., image identity or movie frame) or relate the latent structure to known visual cortical hierarchy. As presented, the model could be applied to motor data without modification.  
- Reproducibility is insufficient: no code is provided, and implementation details in Appendix A are missing (e.g., number of RNN layers, activation functions, initialization strategies, exact form of reconstruction loss, weighting of loss terms).  
- Some references for modern non‑VAE LVMs are missing (e.g., [1], [2]), which would help position the model.

**Specific requested analyses (posed as weaknesses)**  
- Why are reconstruction and KL losses computed on positive samples as well as other samples? The contrastive loss already emphasizes positive pairs; an ablation study separating these contributions is needed.  
- What are the two “different properties of visual neural activity” simulated in the synthetic datasets (L259)?  
- Could only the contrastive objective (no decoder/VAE loss) work? All benchmarks quantify decodability from the latent space, so this baseline is informative.  
- What are the five selected natural scenes that “elicit the strongest average responses” and how does the method perform on lower‑signal scenes? Details about motion content in the videos are necessary to evaluate the decoding criterion (correct if within 1 s).  
- How exactly are train/val/test splits done with respect to time to prevent future information leakage?  
- Were any training difficulties encountered due to the recurrent architecture (GRU vs. LSTM)? What initialization or tuning was crucial?  
- Can the model reveal structure across visual cortical areas or a gradient along the visual hierarchy, as the original dataset was designed for?
