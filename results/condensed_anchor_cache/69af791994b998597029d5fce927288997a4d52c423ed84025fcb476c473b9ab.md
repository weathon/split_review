## Merged Review

### Summary
Most reviewers found the paper well-motivated in addressing hallucination in LVLMs and praised the training-free, easy-to-understand IKOD decoding method, which shows gains on hallucination benchmarks (POPE, CHAIR) and some VQA benchmarks. However, several reviewers raised major concerns about novelty (the observation that hallucination increases with sequence length is well-documented), clarity (formal definitions, figures, presentation), modest improvements, insufficient comparisons and ablations, and potential contradictions in the underlying hypothesis about image attention. Two reviewers are positive (scores 6), two are negative (scores 3), and one is mixed (score 5).

### Strengths
1. **Novel method**: IKOD is a training‑free, post‑hoc decoding strategy that does not require additional annotations or external tools, making it scalable. [R1, R2, R3, R4, R5]
2. **Clear motivation**: The problem of visual attention degradation as sequence length grows is clearly stated, and the method is straightforward and easy to follow. [R1, R2, R5]
3. **Empirical gains**: IKOD shows improvements on standard hallucination benchmarks (POPE, CHAIR) and some VQA benchmarks, with noticeable gains on POPE. [R1, R2, R4]
4. **New perspective**: The paper studies hallucination from the viewpoint of increasing sequence length, offering a potentially useful lens for exploration. [R3]

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty (core observation)**. The claim that hallucination increases with generated sequence length (long‑term bias) has been extensively documented in prior works, e.g., **M3ID** (Favero et al., CVPR 2024) and references [1–5], which are not cited. Similar attention‑based approaches have also been explored before. [R2, R5]
2. **Poor clarity and presentation**. Multiple reviewers found the paper hard to follow:
   - Formal definitions (Eq. 1, variables like `x_I`, notation for tokens, layers, heads) are incomplete or incorrect; the language model mention on L083 is never used. [R2]
   - Figures 1–5 are not self‑explanatory: axes, colors, and legends (e.g., “top‑20%” vs. “first‑20%”, image vs. text tokens) are unclear. Fig. 2 is never referenced in the text. Fig. 5’s legend is confusing. [R2, R3, R5]
   - Many typos and sentences without clear meaning. [R2]
   - (Minor) Inconsistent references should cite official proceedings where possible. [R2]
3. **Marginal improvements and missing comparisons**. Gains on some benchmarks (e.g., Table 3) are minimal. The paper does not compare with relevant prior works that also modify attention to mitigate object hallucination, such as **Attentional Vision Calibration** (Arxiv 2024). Multiple runs with standard deviations are needed to establish consistency. [R2, R5]
4. **Hypothesis about image attention questionable**. 
   - The claim that “weak image attention causes hallucination” may not hold: early tokens with low attention in Fig. 4(a) do not cause hallucination, while later ones do, suggesting sequence length itself could be the root cause rather than attention magnitude. [R3]
   - Reviewer 1 notes a contradiction: InstructBLIP has higher attention scores (Section 3.2) but a lower F1 score than LLaVA 1.5 (Table 1), implying higher attention does not correlate with fewer hallucinations. [R1]
   - Additional experiments are needed to clarify whether image attention truly decreases with length, how text attention changes, and whether hallucination patterns mirror those in pure LLMs. [R3]
5. **Design choices of KV merging unclear**. 
   - Why are specific anchor tokens chosen? Could a uniform merging be used instead? (Motivation: reduce sequence length.) [R3]
   - The method selects tokens with low attention scores as anchors (L268), yet prior work (Attentional Vision Calibration) argues that higher attention does not always mean higher information content—this potential conflict is not discussed. [R5]
   - The method appears sensitive to the alpha hyperparameter; InstructBLIP shows minimal impact regardless of alpha and performance may even degrade at higher alpha values. An explanation and statistics on which anchor tokens are selected are needed. [R5]
6. **Inference overhead**. IKOD requires an extra forward pass during inference, increasing computational cost. More details on the exact overhead and impact on reasoning‑heavy tasks are missing. [R1, R4]
7. **Incomplete experiments**. 
   - VILA and SVIT are used in Figure 2 but are absent from the main experiments; InstructBLIP should be included in Figure 2, and VILA/SVIT should be used as backbones for consistency. [R5]
   - Experiments are limited to 7B models; scalability to larger models (e.g., 13B) is not validated. [R5]
   - Results on a subset of COCO images (L421) rather than the full validation split deviate from standard practice. [R2]
   - Table 5 appears to show IKOD giving the worst results—this discrepancy needs discussion. [R2]
   - Table 4 would benefit from references to other columns/results. [R2]
8. **Prompt sensitivity and ablations**. 
   - Does altering the prompt affect POPE/CHAIR results? [R1]
   - Why does the low‑attention strategy perform worst at anchor ratio 0.1 in Table 5? [R1]
   - How does IKOD perform on LLaVA1.5‑HD, which has more image tokens? [R3]

**Minority reviewer notes**: Two reviewers (R1, R4) are more positive, emphasizing the method’s simplicity and noticeable improvement on POPE, while two (R2, R5) are strongly critical of novelty, clarity, and significance; R3 finds the approach interesting but questions the causal link between attention and hallucination.
