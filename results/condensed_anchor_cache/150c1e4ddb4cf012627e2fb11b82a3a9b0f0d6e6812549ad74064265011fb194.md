## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether features learned by overparameterized (wide) networks are more expressive than those learned by underparameterized (thin) networks. It introduces metrics: Feature Span Error (FSE) based on ridge regression, Feature Span Gap (FSG), and Feature Performance (linear probe). Through experiments on VGG-16 and ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 and a Transformer on MNLI, the authors compare a single wide network with concatenated representations of multiple independently trained thin networks. They conclude that even concatenating many thin features cannot match the expressivity or performance of a single wide network. A toy example is provided to illustrate a mechanism where overparameterization enables learning features that underparameterized networks cannot.

### Strengths
- The research question—whether overparameterization yields distinct, more expressive features—is interesting and important (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The proposed metrics (FSE, FSG) are intuitive, sound, and provide a clear operationalization of feature expressivity (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 3). However, Reviewer 4 found the notation confusing and the organization suboptimal (see Weaknesses).
- The toy example in Section 4 is interesting and sound, offering a concrete mechanism for feature learning differences (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Code is provided in the supplementary materials (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 1 (rating 6) found the approach novel and clever, and the results make a clear case, calling it valuable evidence. This positive assessment contrasts with the more critical views of Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 (all rating 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Confound between overparameterization and training success**: Table 1 shows FSE differences become notable only at α=1/8, which is precisely where Table 2 shows training accuracy starts to drop (e.g., from ~99.9% to lower). Below α=1/8, training accuracies are similar (99.81–99.99) and FSE differences are minimal. This suggests the observed feature differences may stem from underparameterized networks being poorly trained rather than inherent differences in feature expressivity (Reviewer 3). More broadly, independently trained underparameterized networks, each under capacity constraints, will converge to similar features that only capture the most predictive patterns; in contrast, overparameterized networks can learn additional features due to their excess capacity. The paper does not control for this and does not ensure close training performance between the compared networks (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Lack of justification for linear feature approximation**: The FSE metric uses ridge regression (linear) to predict features from one network using features from another. It is unclear why linear prediction is sufficient to capture the expressive power of highly nonlinear features. A nonlinear version (e.g., using a neural network, as done in model stitching literature like Bansal et al. 2021) might yield different results, potentially undermining the main conclusion of Section 3.2. The choice of ridge regression over plain linear regression also requires justification, as the regularization term could affect the measurement (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Insufficient comparison to existing representation similarity metrics**: The paper introduces FSE and FSG without adequately discussing or comparing against established methods like CKA, CCA, SVCCA, representational similarity analysis, model stitching, probing, etc. (see Rauker et al. 2022, Sec. V.G for a survey). It is not clear what advantages the proposed metrics offer over these baselines. Additionally, the FSE metric is similar to metrics used in prior work: e.g., [1] uses a nearly identical feature score, [3] uses linear regression between feature sets, and CCA shares a close idea. The paper’s conclusion about feature concatenation conflicts with that of [3], possibly because [3] ensured models are well-trained (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Limited experimental scale**: Experiments are restricted to CIFAR-10 and MNLI. No evidence is provided that the findings generalize to larger datasets or more complex tasks (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing error bars and statistical reliability**: Some figures lack error bars, making it unclear whether results are from single trials or if variability is negligible. This affects confidence in the reported results (Reviewer 1).
- **Notation and presentation issues**: The paper is hard to follow due to inconsistent notation, e.g., using both β and α interchangeably to denote width scaling, ambiguous indexing in Eq. 2.1 and surrounding text. The organization is partially unordered; e.g., metrics are interleaved with the scaling setup. The section title “HOW DO WIDE MODELS CAPTURE FEATURES THAT SHALLOW ONES CANNOT?” misuses “shallow” to mean thin (shallowness refers to depth, not width). Table 1 exceeds page size limits. These issues make the paper difficult to read (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: The paper is entirely empirical. A theoretical grounding would help generalize the findings beyond the specific experiments and datasets (Reviewer 2).
- **Toy example limited to a specific distribution**: The toy case in Section 4 only applies to one very specific data distribution. It is unclear how this generalizes to real-world settings or connects to the empirical findings. Additionally, the mechanism described resembles the lottery ticket hypothesis (initialization matters) but this connection is not discussed (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Comparison with ensemble of independent thin networks is questionable**: The paper compares a single wide network to concatenated features from multiple independently trained thin networks. The motivation for this comparison and its relevance to understanding overparameterization is not clearly justified. It is unclear what specific insight about overparameterization is intended from this juxtaposition, especially since the parameter counts are not directly comparable (Reviewer 2).
- **Feature Performance metric is not novel**: The “feature performance” metric is simply a linear probe (training a linear classifier on frozen features), which is a standard tool and not a contribution of this work (Reviewer 4).
- **Inconsistent results in feature residual analysis**: For the ResNet experiments (Fig. 4a), the difference between residual and original features is negligible at α=1/8 and α=1/16, but for the Transformer (Fig. 4b, Fig. 10) the residual features clearly improve performance. The paper does not discuss this discrepancy (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing experimental details**: Several important details are omitted: (1) Whether the same random initialization is used for networks of different widths (assuming widths are integer multiples of α). (2) How the underparameterized networks perform on the target task relative to the overparameterized network. (3) Why the underparameterized CIFAR-10 models show improved predictive performance at α=1/2 in Fig. 3. (4) How the feature residual experiments are conducted (how are additional features appended; is a new linear layer learned?). (5) How the MLP in Section 4 is scaled (number of layers, widths). (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing activation function specification**: The toy example in Section 4 uses ReLU activation, but the paper does not specify activation functions for the main experiments. This may affect the validity of the toy example's conclusions (implied by Reviewer 4's questions about scaling).
- **Related work missing several relevant papers**: Suggested additions include arxiv:2212.11005, 2106.07682, 2110.14633, 2010.02323, 1912.04783 (Reviewer 1). Also, the related work section is missing its section number (Reviewer 2).
