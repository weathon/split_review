## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a zero-shot sampling strategy (MGDM) for medical imaging inverse problems (MRI, CT) using pre-trained diffusion models. It employs a bi-level guidance: an inner-level approximation of the conditional posterior mean (via range-null space decomposition and closed-form least squares) and an outer-level proximal optimization to reinforce measurement consistency. Evaluations on fastMRI (knee), BraTS (brain), and LIDC (CT) show improvements in PSNR/SSIM over DPS, DDNM, DCG, and others.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and effective sampling strategy**: The core idea of combining range-null space analysis with a bi-level objective is simple yet yields significant quantitative improvements (e.g., +4dB PSNR on knee MRI) and reduced hallucinatory artifacts. The method is zero-shot (no retraining on measurement-specific data).
2. **Comprehensive empirical validation**: Evaluated on two MRI datasets (fastMRI knee, BraTS brain) and one CT dataset (LIDC). Robustness is demonstrated across diverse acceleration rates, projection counts, and anatomical variations. Ablation study (Table 3) clearly isolates the contribution of the regularized proximal optimization.
3. **Well-written and clearly presented**: The paper is well-structured with clear theory, proofs, and figures. The core idea is delivered comprehensibly. Ablation studies are well-designed. (R4 praised the clarity; strengths in description noted across reviewers.)
4. **Diverse experiments and ablation**: The experiments cover multiple benchmarks and an ablation study provides insights into the method’s components. (R3 acknowledged diversity despite other criticisms.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Method novelty and derivation concerns**: 
   - The method is essentially a combination of DPS (Chung et al., 2022a) and DDNM (Wang et al., 2022), limiting its contribution (R2). 
   - The presentation of the method is handwavy; there is no rigorous derivation of why the sampling works beyond a simplistic Proposition 3.1. The discrepancy gradient is introduced without discussion (R3). One reviewer (score 3) finds the theoretical grounding insufficient.
   - Two extra parameters ζ and ρ are introduced in Algorithm 2 (vs. DDNM). It is unclear how much of the improvement comes from painstaking tuning (R1).
2. **Missing comparisons**: 
   - No comparison with supervised methods (e.g., MODL) and earlier generative approaches (e.g., robust compressed sensing MRI with Langevin dynamics). This is crucial to understand the trade-off in performance and computational cost (R4). 
   - The +4dB PSNR improvement over previous zero-shot methods is huge but not compared against fully supervised methods from the 2019 fastMRI challenge, and the gap is not discussed (R3).
3. **Dataset and experimental issues**:
   - **BraTS dataset limitation**: BraTS images are real-valued, so the k-space is conjugate symmetric; an acceleration factor of ACR=8 effectively becomes ACR=4. This can mislead reported acceleration rates. The paper should cite and address this data crime (R4). 
   - **MRI results**: Only one visual example from fastMRI (Figure 5) is shown, with low resolution and JPEG artifacts; the knee orientation is upside-down. More details (undersampling pattern, visual results) are needed (R4). 
   - **CT simulation**: Only 2D simulations are used; common practice uses 3D cone-beam/helical CT and anthropomorphic digital phantoms. Using reconstructed CT images as digital phantoms can hide artifacts, and an FBP baseline can be misleading (R1).
   - **Extreme undersampling (ACR=24)**: The model recovers fine structures (e.g., brain tumor) at such high acceleration, which is surprising and requires deeper explanation. Is the model overfitting to the training distribution? (R4, echoed by R3).
4. **3D consistency**: The method processes 3D volumes as independent 2D slices; no mechanism ensures consistency across sagittal and coronal views (R2).
5. **Citation errors**: ESPIRiT is cited with a 1989 paper; the correct reference is Uecker et al., 2014 (ESPIRiT — An Eigenvalue Approach…). Check other citations (R1, R4).
6. **Presentation and clarity**:
   - One full page dedicated to introducing diffusion models is unnecessary; a few lines referencing known works would suffice. There are many typos (e.g., “fidility”), confusing notations, and algorithm comments are missing (R3). 
   - The ablation study mentions equation labels without explaining what they correspond to (R3). 
   - The code README is not updated from the original Wang et al. repository, making it hard to locate the new sampling code (R3).
7. **Zero-shot definition and model sharing**: The term “zero-shot” is ambiguous—for MRI acceleration, fully sampled k-space references exist. It is also unclear whether the pre-trained model is from natural images or trained from scratch, and whether the same model can be shared across different acceleration rates (R2).
