## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Vico, a framework for compositional video generation that uses max-flow analysis of token influence from attention layers to ensure balanced concept representation. It constructs a spatial-temporal attention graph, approximates subgraph flow via efficient vectorized computation, and adjusts noisy latents to equalize token flows. The method is applied to existing diffusion-based video models (AnimaDiff, ZeroScore V2, VideoCrafter2) for both text-to-video and editing tasks. Experimentally, Vico improves compositional richness and faithfulness. Reviewers note the innovation and integration ease, but raise concerns about assumptions, generalizability, comparisons, and evaluation rigor.

### Strengths
- Innovative approach: applies traditional max-flow to balance token-level response in video generation, a novel and semi-novel strategy for videos (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Easy integration: can be plugged into existing video generation models (AnimaDiff, ZeroScore V2, VideoCrafter2) with demonstrated improvements (R1, R4).
- Strong theoretical analysis: well-articulated with thorough proofs (R1, R2). R2 notes the graph modeling of information flow is smart.
- Practical efficiency: subgraph, soft-min, and vectorized flow strategies significantly accelerate inference (R3).
- Comprehensive experiments: thorough comparative studies and detailed user study provide strong validation (R3). Visual quality is satisfying despite limited shown examples (R3).
- Important problem: compositional interactions are a major current limitation of video generation (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Equal token influence assumption**: The core assumption that every token should have equal impact is not justified. Some tokens (e.g., function words, time phrases like "On a certain day") inherently deserve less influence, and others (e.g., the main subject) more. R3 asks for rigorous theoretical rationale; R4 gives a concrete counterexample and urges discussion. This is a fundamental concern shared by R3 and R4, while R1 and R2 do not mention it.
- **Lack of comparisons to recent T2V methods**: Baselines (VideoCrafter2) are over a year old. No comparisons to newer open-source models like OpenSora, CogVideoX, or commercial systems (e.g., Kling). This makes it hard to assess true advancement in the current landscape (R3).
- **Lack of comparisons to compositional video generation methods**: No evaluation against existing approaches like LVD (LLM prompt decomposition + gradient layout), VideoTetris (spatiotemporal diffusion for multi-object), or VideoDirectorGPT (LLM director + spatial convolutions). Without these, the novelty and advantage of Vico over direct compositional extensions is unclear (R3).
- **Generalizability to modern attention architectures**: The method assumes cross-attention layers are uniform across all layers and frames. Many recent models (CogVideoX, Mochi-1, Kling) use 3D attention (e.g., MMDiT). R3 questions whether the assumptions hold and how much computational overhead would arise if full attention is used instead of lightweight cross-attention. This limits practical applicability (R3, also echoed in R3's Questions 1-2).
- **Test-time optimization overhead**: Vico requires per-prompt optimization. R4 notes missing analysis of inference time, GPU memory, and computational cost compared to baselines. R3 asks whether the method can handle highly descriptive prompts (up to 100 words) and what the cost would be. No runtime/memory breakdown is provided (R4, R3 Question 3).
- **Limited user study**: Table 2 reports a user study with only ten video clips, which is insufficient to conclusively prove effectiveness (R1).
- **Readability and clarity issues**: R2 found the paper hard to read, with jumps between text and math. Specific unclear points: capacity matrix W (rows/columns, index `t`), notation difference between e_(i,j) (i=j) and e_(i,i), unclear definition of x_t and extraction of A_i in Section 3.1, and token-reweight meaning in Table 1. R3 also flags a typographical issue: the example "a bird looks like a cat" in the introduction is never demonstrated, harming clarity. R4 also notes lack of discussion about extending image-based compositional methods (e.g., Liu et al., Chefer et al., Kumari et al.) to video—if not possible, why; if possible, comparison needed.
- **Missing evaluation on compositional motion scenarios**: R4 asks if Vico handles complex motion compositions (e.g., "a cat running left to right, a dog running right to left") and suggests this as an interesting test case. No such analysis is presented.
- **Other factors causing failure**: R2 questions whether lack of fair attention is the only cause of the issues in Figure 1; if not, other factors should be discussed. This remains unaddressed.
- **Lack of benchmark evaluation**: R3 suggests using T2V CompBench, a dedicated benchmark for compositional video generation, for more comprehensive comparison. The paper does not use it.

**Reviewer disagreement**: R1 and R2 rate the paper highly (8) and are positive about the innovation and integration; R3 (6) and R4 (5) are more critical about assumptions, generalizability, and missing comparisons. R3 especially questions novelty relative to existing compositional video methods, and R4 emphasizes practical overhead and assumption flaws.
