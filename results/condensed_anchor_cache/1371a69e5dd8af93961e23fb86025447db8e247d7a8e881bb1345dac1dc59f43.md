## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LORO (Low-rank Riemannian Optimizer) for memory-efficient pretraining of decoder-only transformers. LORO maintains a low-rank parameterization (weight = BA) throughout training and inference. It uses Riemannian optimization to jointly update the low-rank factors along the steepest descent on the low-rank manifold, avoiding computation of full-size matrices/gradients. Periodic exact Riemannian updates (every K steps) are combined with low-cost approximate updates. Results on LLaMA models (60M–1B) show that LORO achieves perplexity 2% better than full-size baseline (LLaMA 1B) while reducing model memory by 54%, with 1.8× training speedup and 2.2× inference speedup.

### Strengths
- Novel and interesting formulation: using Riemannian optimization to jointly update low-rank factors along the manifold’s steepest descent (R1, R2, R4).
- Well-written and organized; the math is clear and easy to follow (R1, R3). Method section systematically addresses memory and computational overheads (R1).
- Theoretically motivated by Riemannian geometry; the gradient and update strategies through the factorized form are interesting (R4).
- Efficient: the Riemannian step is only performed every K iterations, so computational overhead is low (R3).
- Strong experimental results: LORO achieves competitive performance with reduced memory and computation compared to full-rank baselines, and outperforms existing low-rank pretraining methods (Galore, SLTrain) in both perplexity and efficiency (R1, R4). Specific numbers: LLaMA 1B with LORO gives 2% better perplexity than full-size, 54% model memory reduction, 1.8× training speedup, 2.2× inference speedup.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of rigorous theoretical justification for the central claim** – The authors assert that separately optimizing low-rank factors introduces redundant information and hinders learning, but this is not systematically studied or mathematically formalized. The claim that independent updates fail to capture the “intricate structure” of the low-rank manifold needs a clear analytical breakdown, showing how the standard gradient update deviates from the true gradient on the low-rank manifold (R1, R2). One reviewer finds this claim largely intuitive and insufficiently supported (R2).

2. **No systematic empirical validation of the inefficiency of independent updates** – Evidence that this phenomenon is common and significant in practice is missing, as is a comparison showing how LORO overcomes it specifically (R1).

3. **Optimizer state refresh robustness** – LORO refreshes the Adam statistics at each exact Riemannian update step. Prior work found that restarting optimizer state often caused loss spikes, but LORO appears to avoid this. The paper does not explain why LORO exhibits this robustness, nor analyze how the gradient landscape changes after the Riemannian update to prevent spikes (R2).

4. **Ablation raises questions about the source of improvement** – The ablation study shows similar performance for K=250 and K=500, and no low-rank baseline curve is shown in Figure 4. This suggests the improvement may come from refreshing the optimization subspace rather than from the Riemannian update itself. A more detailed ablation is needed: compare standard low-rank training with the same update frequency, with and without optimizer state refreshing, to isolate the effect of the Riemannian update from the optimizer refresh and update frequency (R2, R3).

5. **Approximation in non-Riemannian steps** – The update rule in equation (10) (used for the majority of steps) appears to be simply scaling down the learning rate for the low-rank factors, which would be equivalent to standard low-rank pretraining. It is unclear why this approximation works well—the authors should show it is not just a learning rate adjustment but has a specific mechanism. Experiments with different scaling factors for B and A would help (R2, R4).

6. **Skepticism about the effect of periodic Riemannian updates** – Since the algorithm only differs from standard low-rank training every K steps, and K=250 or 500 yields similar loss curves (Figure 4 shows steady decrease without visible improvement at Riemannian steps), it is hard to believe that such infrequent updates can turn a badly-performing low-rank approach into SOTA without introducing additional parameters. The authors are asked to run an experiment with Riemannian updates every iteration (or very frequently) to verify that the benefit comes from the Riemannian update itself, and then note that due to cost, one can use every K steps (R3). Reviewer 3 expresses stronger skepticism about the reliability of these results, with a soundness score of 2 (others gave 3).

7. **Potential underestimation of the low-rank baseline** – The paper claims low-rank models can match full-size, but LORO uses exactly the same parameter count as other low-rank methods (e.g., Galore). The underperformance of the low-rank baseline in Figure 1 and Table 1 may be due to initialization or random seed, which should be commented on (R3).

8. **Inference time of LORO** – Should be the same as any low-rank method, as the product BA is used; this should be clarified (R3).

9. **Initialization and scaling choices** – How are B and A initialized? Were different scaling factors tried for B and A during the approximate update steps? (R4)

10. **Missing citations** – Related works Adam-mini (Zhang et al.) and BlockLLM (Ramesh et al.) should be mentioned (R1).
