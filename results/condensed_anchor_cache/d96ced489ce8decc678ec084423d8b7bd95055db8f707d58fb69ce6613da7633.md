## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AU-GOOD, a metric for evaluating model generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) data in the biochemical domain. It includes a dataset partitioning algorithm (CCPart) that generates challenging test sets by maximizing dissimilarity, and statistical methods for model comparison based on AU-GOOD. The framework is applied to two use cases: predicting pharmaceutical properties of small molecules and building biophysical property predictors from protein language model embeddings. The accompanying open-source code is provided.

### Strengths
- Comprehensive framework that not only measures generalization but also provides a statistical method for comparing models using the new AU-GOOD metric (Reviewer 1).
- Detailed and systematic analysis of similarity function selection and its crucial relationship with the robustness of the GOOD metric (Reviewer 1).
- Extensive experiments on multiple use cases: predicting pharmaceutical properties of small molecules and protein sequence properties (Reviewer 1).
- Open source code is clean and well-organized (Reviewer 1).
- Detailed analysis of AU-GOOD and CCPart; CCPart can be applied to any dataset with pairwise similarity metrics, extending beyond biochemical applications (Reviewer 2).
- Wide variety of tests on various datasets, including multiple protein language model embeddings and their OOD performance (Reviewer 2).
- Uses statistical tests to confirm statistical significance (Reviewer 2).
- Well written (Reviewer 3).
- Broadly applicable to a wide range of biological domains given the generality of the similarity-metric-based approach (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Formulation and formalization:**
  - Equation 3 derived from equation 2 by eliminating φ (partition function) is misleading; λs is a partition constraint, not a partition method. A simple example with four data points shows different partitions under the same λs (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation 2 has a subscript error: x should be indexed not by i if T denotes training and E test (Reviewer 1, minor).
  - Sections 2.1 and 2.2 are overly formalized for obvious concepts; Section 2.3 is too long and unnecessarily mathematical (Reviewer 3).
  - Concepts like “visually incoherent GOOD curves” lack quantitative definitions, making reproducibility difficult (Reviewer 3).
- **Partition algorithm analysis:**
  - The dependence of AU-GOOD on the partition algorithm is not discussed; justification for CCPart relies on qualitative visualization. A more rigorous, quantitative measure of distribution shift is needed to demonstrate that CCPart creates true OOD splits (Reviewer 1).
  - The assumption of disconnected subgraphs may not hold for evolutionarily related data (e.g., proteins, species); at stringent thresholds the graph remains highly connected, and any disconnected subgraph may segregate only very similar points, yielding an easy test set (Reviewer 3).
  - In noisy biological data, disconnected subgraphs found by CCPart may correspond to pathological outliers, producing test sets that are not representative of the overall data distribution (Reviewer 3).
  - CCPart is quite similar to existing structure-based splitting methods such as Butina splits, scaffold splits (DeepChem, MOOD, Dionysus); the paper does not compare CCPart with these methods (Reviewer 2). [Question overlap]
- **Scalability and model scope:**
  - Pairwise similarity measures heavily restrict dataset size; thus the studied datasets are small and models are traditional ML (KNN, SVM, RF, etc.). No plans to extend to larger datasets (e.g., millions) or deep learning models (GNNs, GCNs) that show state-of-the-art in virtual screening (Reviewer 2). [Question as criticism]
- **Validity of AU-GOOD metric:**
  - The GOOD curves in SI Fig 2 show huge variation and are not well behaved like those in the main text (Fig 1, Fig 5). Fitting a linear line to extract a slope seems strange given the variation; curves are rarely linear or even monotonic (SI Fig 2) (Reviewer 2).
  - The choice of area under the GOOD curve is confusing: in Fig 1A, models A and B would have similar AU despite different generalization behavior; authors may defer to slope but the curves are not linear (Reviewer 2).
  - The GOOD curve behavior changes dramatically with the performance metric (e.g., Matthew vs. Spearman vs. Pearson coefficients). The authors use different metrics for different tasks (Spearman for Caco2, Halflife, LD509; Matthew for Ames, DILI, PAMPA) without justification; error bars are large and metrics are used interchangeably. Would conclusions change under different metrics? (Reviewer 2). [Question as weakness]
- **Lack of ground truth / baseline comparisons:**
  - In use case 1, Figure 5 shows Random Forest is significantly better than other models in OOD generalization, but this conclusion cannot be verified; there is no ground truth label for model OOD performance (Reviewer 1).
  - The approach does not introduce substantial novelty over existing literature; experiments are not compared to any other baselines (except baseline), and discussion of GOOD curves is informal and speculative. How does this approach outperform other existing methods? (Reviewer 3). [Question as weakness]
- **Presentation:**
  - Figure captions, x-labels, y-labels are extremely small, especially in the SI (SI Fig 1); enlarge to improve readability (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
  - Table 1: ranking appears to be 2,4,1,4 left-to-right (Reviewer 2, minor).

**Reviewer discrepancy:** Reviewer 1 (rating 8, confidence 3) is overall positive with minor concerns, finding the framework comprehensive and well-supported. Reviewers 2 and 3 (both rating 6, confidences 4 and 3) are more critical, questioning the novelty, reliability of AU-GOOD, and formal rigor; Reviewer 3 sees little utility over existing methods.
