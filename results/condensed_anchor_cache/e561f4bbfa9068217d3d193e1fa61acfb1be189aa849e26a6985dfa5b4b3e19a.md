## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a learning-theoretic approach to hyperparameter optimization, proving PAC-style sample complexity bounds for tuning architectural hyperparameters that are static during training (e.g., activation interpolation parameter, graph kernel parameter). It assumes a distribution over tasks and aims to do well on average, using piecewise constant/polynomial decompositions of the utility function to control oscillation and derive generalization bounds. The analysis relies on tools from differential geometry and constrained optimization to characterize discontinuities in the loss as a function of the hyperparameter.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and originality** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3): Applying a learning-theoretic perspective to hyperparameter optimization is original, challenging, and novel. The hyperparameter optimization literature is largely heuristic, and principled characterization is refreshing and important.
- **Theoretical contribution** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2): The paper presents an impressive piece of theoretical work, well structured. The overall approach—finding piecewise/local structure in the costs of optimized networks and using the [Balcan '21] framework to bound complexity via oscillation—is solid and natural.
- **Geometric reasoning and approximation** (Reviewer 2): The method of translating geometric structure of the meta-loss landscape to learning complexity results is a key strength, especially the clever use of piecewise-polynomial structure to control oscillation. The approximation method to relax Assumption 2 to Assumption 1 is smooth.
- **Depth of analysis** (Reviewer 3): The authors go deep into the circumstances described by their theoretical assumptions; writing on the theory side is clear and pretty.

### Weaknesses
1. **Mismatch between claims and practical setting** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3):
   - The setting (sampling multiple whole datasets from a distribution) is unusual for hyperparameter optimization, which is typically performed for a single dataset. This diminishes the work’s practical impact (Reviewer 1).
   - The paper focuses on a single hyperparameter, missing the core challenge of large numbers of interacting hyperparameters (Reviewer 1).
   - The title and abstract claim guarantees “for deep neural networks,” but the results only apply to two very specific, uncommon instances: (i) an interpolation parameter of activation functions (in a DARTS-like setting) and (ii) a kernel parameter for graph neural networks. Each required significant ad‑hoc theoretical work to fit the assumptions (Reviewer 1).
   - Reviewer 3 asks: why not fine-tune each task separately? Why is a distribution over tasks necessary for hyperparameter tuning? The motivation is insufficiently explained.
   - Reviewer 2 notes that the utility function \(u_\alpha\) implicitly assumes a *global* optimizer of the non‑convex training loss, which is NP‑hard. A global optimum may not correspond to what a practical optimizer (e.g., GD) finds; thus claiming \(\alpha\) is “learnable” under this idealization is misleading. Papers like [Li ’21] show that useful architecture optimization must consider the local minima that GD can find.

2. **Assumptions and relevance to real hyperparameter landscapes** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3):
   - Assumption 1 is very difficult to grasp; calling it “relatively mild” is not conveyed by the presentation (Reviewer 1). The link between Assumption 1 and deep neural networks is unclear (Reviewer 1, question).
   - The paper uses algebraic/differential geometry tools, but the hyperparameter landscape is argued to be *volatile*; these sophisticated tools may be overkill or irrelevant if the assumptions are chosen in their favor. No visualization or empirical evidence confirms that real neural network hyperparameter landscapes satisfy the assumptions. A blog on fractals from NN training suggests landscapes are much more discontinuous than assumed (Reviewer 3).
   - Reviewer 1 questions the DARTS connection: DARTS uses weights to encode probabilities over operations, not an interpolation—please clarify.

3. **Stochasticity and optimization details** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2):
   - At line 177, \(u_\alpha\) is computed via an optimization problem that is inherently stochastic (e.g., SGD), yet it is presented as a deterministic function. How is this stochasticity handled in the analysis? (Reviewer 1, question)
   - Reviewer 2 points out that the analysis is not structured to yield an implementable algorithm; the piecewise-structure is not exploited dynamically, leaving practitioners still reliant on grid search or bandit methods.

4. **Proof and reference concerns** (Reviewer 1):
   - **(potentially serious)** Lemma 3.3 appears central but no proof is given in the Appendix; the authors explicitly state Lemma 3.1 does not apply, so a proof is even more needed.
   - Warren’s theorem is used in the proof of Theorem 6.1 without a reference; it is not a standard theorem.
   - Lines 926, 1424, 1476 refer to “standard learning theory results” without specifying which result.

5. **Missing comparisons with prior work** (Reviewer 2):
   - The claim of “first precise sample complexity bounds for applications” and “first analysis for the learnability of parameterized algorithms involving both parameters and hyperparameters” should be tempered. Proposition 4 of [JMLR 18/16‑558] provides a quantitative sample complexity bound and constructive analysis—it is unclear how the present results compare or are stronger. Similarly, Theorem 1 of the Hyperband paper might already yield sample complexity bounds for these applications. More precise contextualization is needed.

6. **Lack of empirical support and implications** (Reviewer 3):
   - No empirical experiments are provided. Given the empirical nature of the subject, concrete experiments confirming the assumptions (e.g., visualization of the hyperparameter landscape) or demonstrating that the bounds improve practical tuning would greatly strengthen the paper.
   - Discussion of implications for practitioners is absent.

7. **Minor presentation issues** (Reviewer 1):
   - Line 56: “random search methods … only work for a discrete and finite grid” is incorrect.
   - Line 93–95 sentence is grammatically incorrect.
   - Line 249: “state that the result holds thanks to Th. 2.1” should be clarified.
   - Line 258: “piece function” not defined; \(c_i\) introduced but never used.
   - Line 282: “Theorem 4.1” should be “Lemma 4.1”?
   - Line 308: Notation \(R_{x,t}\) differs from earlier usage.
   - Line 309: “behaves regularly” is undefined.
   - Line 310: “preimage” is used before being defined.
   - The phrase “behaves regularly” and the presentation of Assumption 1 could be improved.

8. **Additional reviewer questions that double as criticisms**:
   - How does the analysis cope with the stochasticity of the optimization that defines \(u_\alpha\)? (Reviewer 1)
   - Does a smoothness-based approach (Lipschitz gradients, bounded Hessians) provide a more direct, transparent, and general route than the piecewise-polynomial formulation? (Reviewer 2)
   - Why does the abstract not mention that both algebraic and differential geometry are used? (Reviewer 3)

**Reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 is strongly positive (score 8, confidence 4), emphasizing the soundness of the theoretical perspective and the clever use of piecewise structure. Reviewer 1 is moderately positive (score 6, confidence 2) but concerned about positioning, scope, and proof gaps. Reviewer 3 is negative (score 3, confidence 3), citing unclear assumptions, lack of empirical grounding, and questionable relevance of the mathematical machinery to actual DNN hyperparameter tuning.
