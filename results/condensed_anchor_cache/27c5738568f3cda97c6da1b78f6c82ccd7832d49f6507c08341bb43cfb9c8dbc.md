## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces a novel EEG dataset from 52 subjects recorded during both wakefulness (image and sound stimuli from 15 semantic categories) and NREM sleep (sound stimuli re‑exposure). To decode semantic categories during sleep, the authors propose the Universal Sleep Decoder (USD), which combines a classification objective with a contrastive domain‑adaptation loss to align awake and sleep neural representations, and uses a pretrain‑finetune paradigm for cross‑subject generalization. Results report up to 16.6% zero‑shot accuracy and 25.9% after fine‑tuning (6.7% chance). Ablations and comparisons investigate training‑data sources (awake only, sleep only, combined) and model components.

### Strengths

- The collected awake‑sleep EEG dataset (64‑channel, 52 subjects, well‑annotated with stimulus timings and categories) is novel and could be a valuable resource for the biosignals/sleep community.  
- The paper is clearly written, with well‑detailed contributions and a comprehensive set of experiments (in‑domain, cross‑subject, ablations).  
- The code and dataset are promised to be released, and the code is reasonably well‑annotated.  
- The research question (decoding memory content during sleep) and the proposed approach (combining classification and contrastive objectives for awake‑sleep domain adaptation) are original.  
- The study represents a significant step toward understanding and improving semantic decoding during sleep and, with the dataset, could become a baseline framework for this task.

### Weaknesses

- **Data not released:** A core claimed contribution is the unique dataset, but it is not yet available. Without the data, the main claims cannot be verified, and the paper contributes little else. *(Minority view – Reviewer 1: even if eventually released, the venue may not be the most suitable; this paper would be more appropriate for a health/physiological‑signals venue.)*  
- **No comparison with prior work:** The paper does not compare against existing sleep‑decoding methods or publicly available datasets.  
- **Low accuracy questions efficacy:** The reported top‑1 accuracies (16.6% zero‑shot, 25.9% fine‑tuned) are low in absolute terms, and the high performance seen in Figure 3 may be due to chance or overfitting – a concern that should be addressed.  
- **Missing explanation of asterisks in Figure 3** – their meaning is unclear.  
- **Core claim about memory reactivation is not supported:** The EEG epochs (−0.2 to 0.8 s around stimulus onset) likely contain evoked auditory responses rather than memory‑related reactivation.  
  - No analysis of evoked responses (temporal patterns, topographical maps) is provided to distinguish evoked vs. memory‑related activity.  
  - Details of whether audio cues during NREM sleep disrupted sleep (e.g., arousal, sleep‑stage interruptions) are missing.  
  - It is unclear whether the sleep auditory cues came from matched or mismatched image‑audio pairs; if both exist, a comparison of decoding performance for matched vs. mismatched pairs is needed to test the memory‑reactivation hypothesis.  
  - The number of unique stimuli is not explicitly stated. If it is low, results may reflect instance‑level classification rather than category generalization.  
  - Behavioral analysis of post‑sleep recognition performance for stimuli presented during sleep (vs. non‑presented) would strengthen the TMR‑like claim.  
- **Performance comparisons are not well supported:** The relative performance of different training configurations (e.g., sleep‑only CNN vs. awake+sleep CNN) is unclear because Figure 3 does not plot them together. Sections 4.2.2 and 4.2.3 report statistics but no direct comparison, and there is no results table summarizing these experiments.  
- **Notation issues:**  
  - $y \in \mathbb{R}^K$ is incorrect; should be $y \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ or a one‑hot vector.  
  - The $\mathcal{X}$s are not explicitly defined.  
  - The definition $\mathcal{P}(i) = \{k|k\in \mathcal{A}(i), y_k = y_i\}$ overloads $y_k$ (since $k$ is an instance, not a label).  
- **Figure 2 is confusing** – the arrows and colors are not used consistently, and it is unclear whether they indicate data flow or experimental ordering.  
- **Model appendix is incomplete:** The architecture description only lists Conv layers; a full summary table including all components (e.g., subject block) would be clearer.  
- **Hyperparameter $\lambda$ (Equation 2):** The value 0.5 is used without ablation or justification.  
- **Lasso GLM not tested in the Awake+Sleep → Sleep setting** (Figure 3d) – this baseline could help assess the contribution of deep learning.  
- **“Test accuracy according to the maximum validation accuracy”** – unclear what this means; typical practice is to report test accuracy at the epoch with best validation performance.  
- **“Migration”** should be replaced with **“transfer”** for consistency with the literature.  
- **Figure 4 x‑axis:** It indicates percentages of different data amounts; using absolute numbers of instances/hours would be more interpretable.  
- **TMR paradigm difference:** The paper states the paradigm is “TMR related” rather than “TMR evoked” but does not explain how it differs from standard Targeted Memory Reactivation.  
- **“Open set” applied to a dataset** (Section 2.2) is unusual – open‑set typically refers to a model’s ability to handle unseen classes, not a fixed dataset.  
- **Missing reference:** Kostas et al. (2021, doi:10.3389/fnhum.2021.653659) on large‑scale SSL pretraining for EEG is a relevant related work.  
- **Claim that few public sleep datasets exist for unsupervised pretraining** (Section 2.3) is inaccurate – many exist (SHHS, MASS, SleepEDF, etc.), though the limiting factor is often the number of channels (most have far fewer than 64).
