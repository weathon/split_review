## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper reformulates neural pruning as expected loss minimization over mask distributions. It derives the Sparsity Evolutionary Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (SFPK) equation to guide the distributional transition of sparse masks under infinitesimal sparsity increments. Based on this, the SFPK-pruner is proposed, a particle simulation method that samples performant masks at the target sparsity. The paper provides theoretical convergence guarantees and experiments across one-shot and iterative pruning, structured and unstructured sparsity, on both convolutional and vision transformer architectures. Reviewers generally found the work novel, well-motivated, and theoretically grounded, with strong experimental evidence. However, concerns were raised about missing error bars, computational overhead compared to iterative magnitude pruning (IMP) and dynamic sparse training (DST), the fairness of one-shot comparisons with SNIP/SynFlow, lack of ablation on the requirement for “rational” pre-trained weights, insufficient high-sparsity results, and the need for more analysis of particle diversity and the role of mini-batch stochasticity.

### Strengths
- **Distribution over masks**: Instead of treating the optimal mask as a point estimate, the method maintains a distribution over masks, adding a compelling new dimension to iterative pruning.
- **Novel optimization approach**: The algorithm evolves a population of sparse masks in an evolutionary manner while increasing sparsity; this is similar to dynamic sparse training but with a novel evolutionary perspective (Reviewer 2).
- **Well-motivated theory**: The use of the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation to model sparsity evolution is fresh and intriguing, providing mathematically tractable guidance and new insights into pruning (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Proper credit to prior work**: The paper explicitly acknowledges and discusses the ODE framework of Mo et al. (2023), making the unique contributions clear (Reviewer 1).
- **Strong combination of theory and experiments**: The theoretically driven algorithm achieves state-of-the-art performance on a range of pruning tasks, with comprehensive comparisons to various baselines (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The flow of the paper effectively presents the method, and the experimental scale and results are satisfactory (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Reasonable theoretical analysis**: The approximations supporting SFPK-guided pruning are sound and add an interesting perspective (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing error bars and trial count**: Tables do not include standard deviations; it is unclear how many trials (e.g., three from Figure 2?) were run. Full tables with standard deviations should be provided, even if in the appendix (Reviewer 1).
- **Computational cost not thoroughly compared**: The method incurs higher cost than simple baselines (Mag, SNIP, SynFlow) due to particle simulation. In one-shot pruning, a runtime and theoretical complexity comparison with WF and PSO is needed (Reviewer 1). On ImageNet, the overhead is claimed to be equivalent to ~3 training epochs; this should be compared with DST methods like RiGL, which have similar overhead (Reviewers 1, 2). Additionally, the memory footprint of storing n mask particles and the \(O(nKd)\) inner-product cost can become a bottleneck for large models; these should be explicitly analyzed (Reviewer 3). One reviewer (R2) notes that performance is only slightly better than DST (e.g., RiGL in some cases), so a detailed computational comparison is essential to justify the method.
- **One-shot pruning experiments potentially misleading or flawed**: The performance of SNIP and SynFlow in Table 1 appears unreasonably low. Both methods are designed for pruning at initialization, not for one-shot pruning of a trained model. It is suspected that they were applied after training, which would explain the poor results and make the comparison unfair (Reviewers 2, 4). Moreover, these methods are not designed to minimize loss upon sparsification without retraining, so the comparison without further training is problematic (Reviewer 2).
- **Inconsistent experimental settings across architectures**: For convolutional architectures (Table 1a), results are only shown without further training and for unstructured sparsity. For vision transformers (Table 2a), results are only shown with further training and for structured sparsity. Comprehensive results for both architectures across all settings (with/without retraining, structured/unstructured) would strengthen general conclusions (Reviewer 1). The reviewer recognizes this may be burdensome and suggests it as a longer-term goal.
- **Lack of evidence for “rational” pre-trained weights requirement**: The method assumes “rational” (converged) weights \(\theta^*\). No experiments investigate how performance degrades with less-than-optimal training, nor whether the method can work with randomly initialized weights (pruning-at-initialization). Such ablation would clarify the practical significance of this requirement (Reviewer 1). One reviewer (R2) also asks whether SFPK can outperform SNIP/GraSP/SynFlow on random initialization.
- **Insufficient high-sparsity regime and outdated baselines**: 90% sparsity on ResNet50/ImageNet is not considered high; results at 95%+ sparsity should be provided. The most recent baseline in some tables dates to 2020; newer methods like Spartan (Tai et al., NeurIPS 2022, 76.17% at 90% sparsity) should be compared. The claim of “decent performance compared to SOTA baselines” is weakened by the lack of recent comparisons (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparison to iterative magnitude pruning (IMP)**: Since the method shares a similar spirit with IMP (gradual sparsity increase), a direct comparison of performance and computational cost per pruning iteration is needed. This would provide context on the practical advantages (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Particle diversity not analyzed**: It is unclear whether the n particles cover the posterior of the mask distribution or collapse to one or a few local minima. At high sparsity, the particles may converge to similar masks. The paper should analyze the variance in performance and mask configurations across particles, and consider reducing the number of particles at higher sparsity for efficiency (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Role of mini-batch stochasticity neglected**: The algorithm critically relies on mini-batches to produce stochastic gradients for exploration. Using full batches would cause the transition operator \(T\) to be identical for all particles, leading to distribution collapse. This impact should be explicitly discussed and ablated (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing details on sparsity function and definitions**: The mask polarizer \(P_\epsilon\) is not defined in the main text. It is also not explained why the polarizer was chosen over alternatives like soft thresholding or sigmoid; a comparison would be useful (Reviewers 2, 4). The distributional transition \(T\) should be defined earlier when first introduced (Reviewer 4).
- **Clarity on which parameters are pruned**: It is not stated whether batch normalization scales/biases and the final linear layer are included in the prunable parameters. When comparing methods like SWAMP, sparsity definitions may differ (e.g., SWAMP prunes only convolutional weights), leading to unfair comparisons. The paper should specify which parameters are pruned and ensure fair sparsity calculations (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of ablation on \(\lambda\)**: The robustness to \(\lambda\) is expected from its technical role (convexifier). Including results with \(\lambda=0\) in Figure 3 would demonstrate this (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor**: The acronym SFPK vs. SEFPK is noted (Reviewer 1, Q99). The reported training steps count (one epoch equivalency) appears miscalculated (Reviewer 1, Q1).
