## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes that fine-tuning harms pretrained capabilities due to a shift in implicit task inference, and introduces Conjugate Prompting (e.g., translating prompts to other languages) to recover these capabilities by making prompts appear farther from the fine-tuning distribution. Evidence is drawn from synthetic linear-regression experiments on small transformers and from LLM experiments on instruction tuning (ICL recovery), code fine-tuning (reasoning recovery), and safety fine-tuning (harmful content recovery). Reviewer scores range from 3 to 8, with one strongly negative review (score 3, confidence 5) and the remaining reviewers positive to moderately positive (scores 6, 6, 8). All reviewers agree the paper studies an important phenomenon and is clearly written, but there is substantial disagreement on the generality of the assumptions, the practical significance of the proposed method, and the strength of the empirical evidence for large language models.

### Strengths
- **Well-motivated hypothesis and clean framing.** The hypothesis that fine-tuning changes the model’s inference of which task is being solved, rather than erasing knowledge, is clearly expressed and well operationalised (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The theoretical formulation (e.g., Equation 7) is neat and reasonable, offering a fresh perspective on catastrophic forgetting (Reviewer 4).
- **Compelling synthetic experiments.** The synthetic linear-regression setup provides clear evidence of the trade-off between fine-tuning and pretraining; the analysis is robust and the toy case helps illustrate the conceptual model (Reviewers 1, 2). Reviewer 3 notes that although artificial, the setup shows what kind of perturbations may be happening in realistic scenarios.
- **Simple, effective, and broadly applicable method.** Conjugate Prompting (especially translation) is easy to understand, apply, and is universally applicable to most tasks (Reviewers 1, 4). Experiments validating the method are convincing, showing partial recovery of ICL, reasoning, and safety restrictions (Reviewer 1, 3).
- **Interesting quantitative results on instruction tuning and ICL.** The finding that instruction-tuned models “forget” ICL and that this is partially recoverable via translation is particularly noteworthy (Reviewer 2).
- **Clear presentation and visualizations.** The paper is well-written and easy to read, with graphical visualizations that support clarity (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Reviewer 4 specifically highlights the clarity of the presentation.

### Weaknesses
- **Restrictive and artificial core assumption.** The main assumption (Equation 7) that input spaces of different tasks overlap is restrictive and does not reflect most realistic settings. The ICL examples are purposely designed to create confusion (two different instructions) and are adversarial; real-world prompts usually clearly specify the task (e.g., “summarize” vs. “translate”). When input spaces do overlap, the problem becomes ill-posed—failure may come from task ambiguity, not forgetting. This is a fundamental limitation raised by Reviewer 4 with high confidence (score 3, confidence 5). Reviewer 2 also notes the paper’s framing (forgetting vs. suppressing) may be spurious: even if the model can perform ICL in Spanish, it still cannot do the original English task, so the distinction does not change the practical degradation.
- **Limited experiments on large language models.** Reviewers 1, 2, 3, and 4 all note that the study focuses heavily on small synthetic models; the LLM experiments are limited to a few tasks and models. Many questions are left unanswered: Does the effect vary with model size? How much fine-tuning data is required for Conjugate Prompting to make a significant difference? Does the trade-off become less severe at larger scales (as suggested by prior work, e.g., openreview.net/forum?id=GhVS8_yPeEa)? Reviewer 3 explicitly states that the lack of experiments addressing these variables makes the evidence thin. Reviewer 4 adds that the chosen settings (instruction tuning and RLHF) are not the typical scenarios where catastrophic forgetting is most clearly manifested (e.g., supervised task fine-tuning on GLUE or domain fine-tuning like Codex, Minerva).
- **Conjugate Prompting is a band-aid solution with limited novelty.** The method does not address the fundamental over-specialization problem; it is a practical trick rather than a principled fix (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 also notes the related works section is thin, missing discussion of relevant forgetting literature (e.g., arxiv 2211.00635).
- **Synthetic task is oversimplified.** The synthetic experiment only considers linear functions (different linear weights). To better test generalization, the setup should include higher-order functions (quadratic, quartic, etc.) (Reviewer 2). The current simplicity limits the conclusions that can be drawn about real-world complexity (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Confusing presentation in key details.** Several descriptions are unclear. For example, Table 2 does not specify whether percentages represent refusal or lack of refusal (Reviewer 3). Section 2.6 leaves ambiguous whether languages refer to fine-tuning data or prompting language (Reviewer 3). Also, “IF” (Instruction Following) is not defined (Reviewer 3). These issues forced multiple rereadings (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experimental controls and comparisons.** Important ablation questions are raised but not addressed in the paper: (1) If fine-tuning is multi-task trained together with the pretraining task under different mixing weights, how would behavior change? (Reviewer 2). (2) What if the model is fine-tuned on a different set of discrete weights than \(\mathcal{D}_{disk}\)? (Reviewer 4). (3) How does the performance drop from language gap compare to the recovery from forgetting? (Reviewer 4). (4) Could other kinds of mapping besides translation serve as Conjugate Prompting? (Reviewer 4).
- **RLHF/safety example is not a good illustration of forgetting.** The goal of alignment is to make the model refuse malicious instructions, not to forget how to generate harmful content—machine unlearning remains an unsolved problem. Catastrophic forgetting is typically a side-effect of fine-tuning and is directionless, whereas safety fine-tuning intentionally suppresses certain behaviors (Reviewer 4).
