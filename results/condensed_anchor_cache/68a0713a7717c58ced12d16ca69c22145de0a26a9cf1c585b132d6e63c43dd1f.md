## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper claims that a simple combination of widely-used regularization strategies—custom learning rate decay, multi-augmentation (weak+strong, doubling batch size), and exponential moving average (EMA) of weights—when applied with standard cross-entropy loss, can outperform state-of-the-art label-noise algorithms. The framework is tested on synthetic noise (CIFAR) and natural noise (CIFAR-N, Clothing1M, Animal-10N), often with an additional semi‑supervised learning component (MixMatch + consistency‑based separation by Bai et al.). Three reviewers rated the paper 3 (low) and one rated it 6 (high). The higher‑scoring reviewer finds the work well‑organized, carefully reviewing related literature, performing extensive experiments, and providing good analysis (e.g., Figure 2 on memorization). The other three reviewers argue that the novelty is limited, comparisons are unfair because baselines use different training setups, the method is not “simple,” and key details are missing.

### Strengths
- The paper is well‑organized with clear section headers; mean and standard deviation over three runs are reported for most experiments.
- A thorough ablation study is performed to understand the contribution of each component.
- The method is evaluated across multiple noise rates, noise types (symmetric, asymmetric, instance‑dependent) and datasets (CIFAR‑10/100, CIFAR‑N, Clothing1M, Animal‑10N).
- The analysis of Figure 2(b) clearly illustrates the memorization effect (Arpit et al.) and serves as motivation for the approach.
- Extensive experiments consistently show that the chosen combination of three regularization strategies (learning rate decay, data augmentation, EMA) outperforms the reported baselines.
- The semi‑supervised extension (RegCE+semi) further improves performance, demonstrating another avenue for robustness.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** Several reviewers note that the paper merely amalgamates pre‑existing techniques (learning rate decay, data augmentation, EMA) without introducing new insight. It is well‑known that implicit regularization helps with noisy labels (e.g., label smoothing, early stopping, learning rate schedules, dropout, data augmentation, EMA). The combination of such techniques has been explored in ablation studies of prior work (e.g., DivideMix, ELR). The work’s contribution is therefore seen as incremental rather than foundational.
- **Unfair and inconclusive comparisons.** All baseline results in Tables 1–4 are directly taken from other papers, not re‑implemented in a shared training setup. Critically, RegCE+semi uses **stronger augmentations** than the baselines it is compared against (including cutout, grayscale transformation, color jitter, Gaussian blur), while baselines like DivideMix, ELR/ELR+, SOP/SOP+, PES rely only on crop‑and‑flip (and possibly Mixup). This makes it impossible to attribute performance gains to the proposed approach versus simply using stronger data augmentation. The absence of re‑implemented baselines under identical conditions undermines reliability, even on Animal‑10N and Clothing1M where reviewer 4 explicitly requests re‑implementation with the same base model architecture.
- **Misleading simplicity claim.** The abstract describes the method as a “simple baseline,” but RegCE+semi is as complex as (or more complex than) existing explicit methods like DivideMix: it uses a consistency‑based criterion to split data, multi‑augmentation (doubling batch size), MixMatch, and EMA. Even RegCE is not simple compared to, for example, simply replacing cross‑entropy with a robust loss. The word “simple” is therefore misleading.
- **Goal–method contradiction.** The stated goal is to show that widely‑used, implicit regularization strategies suffice, but the multi‑augmentation strategy is **not widely used**: it creates two augmentations per image (weak + strong), doubling the batch size and averaging gradients over two views. This effect is poorly understood and no control experiment (e.g., randomly picking weak or strong per image without enlarging the batch) is performed to isolate the impact of the gradient‑averaging trick. The only ablation compares weak‑only, strong‑only, and both, but does not ablate the batch‑size increase.
- **Missing justifications and experiment details.** 
  - The proposed augmentation strategy is never formally introduced in Section 3.2 (only described later in the appendix).
  - No intuition is given for why this particular combination of three regularization methods works best; why not combine more methods (e.g., dropout, label smoothing)?
  - The learning rate schedule: sharp decay sets lr to 1% of the original; it is unclear whether “gradual decay” decreases lr below 1%, which could be unfair. 
  - The phrase “dropped another 1% after a few subsequent epochs” lacks specification of how many epochs and whether this is constant across datasets.
  - It is not stated whether baseline methods used the same initial learning rate as RegCE.
- **Computation complexity.** The proposed method, especially when combined with MixMatch (RegCE+semi), incurs significant computational overhead (doubled batch size, strong augmentations, EMA updates). This limitation is acknowledged but not discussed in context of the “simple” label.
- **Lack of code release.** Open‑sourcing code is recommended to help the community verify and build upon the claimed strong baseline.
- **Need for broader comparative analysis.** Table 1/2 show that adding MixMatch to RegCE improves results; the authors should examine how the same regularization strategies would boost existing SOTA algorithms (e.g., DivideMix, ELR) rather than only comparing as‑is.

### Additional Points from Minority Reviewer (positive, score 6)
- The paper is well organized, directly assesses current practices, and includes a careful literature review.
- Figure 2 and the explicit analysis of the memorization effect are particularly good.
- Extensive experiments support the claims.
- Author are encouraged to fully open‑source the code to broaden influence.
- The reviewer notes that the method’s computation complexity is not as simple as it appears (especially with MixMatch), reinforcing the weakness above.
