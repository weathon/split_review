## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an adversarial evaluation method, Retraining on T (RTT), to test whether unlearning methods remove information from model weights or merely hide it. Under the assumption that an attacker has access to a subset (T) of the knowledge intended to be removed, RTT fine-tunes the model on T to try to recover the remaining knowledge (V), where T and V are constructed to have low mutual information. Experiments on Llama-3-8B with three unlearning methods—gradient difference (GD), representation misdirection for unlearning (RMU), and random incorrect answer (RIA)—show that RTT restores over 88% of pre-unlearning accuracy, suggesting these methods do not fully remove targeted information.

### Strengths
- Highlights a critical AI safety issue: distinguishing whether unlearning methods remove information or merely obfuscate it.
- The RTT framework, combined with carefully curated datasets and well-defined metrics, enables quantitative evaluation of unlearning effectiveness across multiple settings (hyperparameters, text formats, granularities of hidden knowledge).
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, with clearly introduced concepts and a transparent structure.
- Findings are interesting and timely; machine unlearning is a hot topic, and the empirical evidence from diverse settings sufficiently supports the claims.
- A key novel demonstration: even if retraining and unlearning datasets are disjoint (no shared information), knowledge from the unlearning set can be recovered, showing limitations of existing methods.
- Experimental setup is thorough: diverse datasets covering different behaviors (e.g., birthdays, hazardous knowledge, MMLU), extensive hyperparameter search for strong unlearning baselines, and evaluation of three fundamentally different unlearning algorithms (GD, RMU, RIA) that all fail robustly.
- Reviewers agree that rigorous evaluation of unlearning is important and that the paper’s recommendations are sound.

**Note:** One reviewer (score 8) found the contribution sufficient for publication, while another (score 3) considered the novelty limited and suggested the work would fit a workshop without significant expansion. A third reviewer (score 6) acknowledged the main contribution but criticized the lack of contextualization relative to prior work.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty relative to prior work:** Prior work has already proposed relearning (fine-tuning) as a metric for evaluating unlearning [refs 1-5]. The paper’s contribution is primarily the construction of training/validation splits with low mutual information. The authors claim their evaluation sets a higher bar, but they do not compare against existing “relearning time” metrics from [2,3]. Moreover, the connection between mutual information and relearning robustness is acknowledged as unsubstantiated, limiting the evaluation’s novelty (especially Section 3.2).
- **Missing comparisons and contextualization:** The paper overlooks relevant works showing that unlearning fails to remove information from model weights (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.16835) and that fine-tuning on unrelated data can recover information (also [2402.16835] and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13356v1). Better contextualization is needed, e.g., using newer methods like RMU.
- **Incomplete evaluation scope:**
  - Only three unlearning methods are tested; other baselines from [1] should be included.
  - Evaluation relies solely on multiple-choice questions (MCQ); other knowledge assessment types should be considered.
  - The effectiveness of RTT has not been tested with RMU under high granularity knowledge hiding (explicitly requested).
  - The effect of V/T dataset sizes is not ablated (e.g., V is often smaller than T; ablations by fine-tuning on V instead, or varying T size, are recommended).
- **Unclear real-world applicability:** The real-world use case of RTT is vague. It is unclear what specific examples of V and T correspond to realistic adversarial settings and whether an adversary would legitimately have access to T. The impact of topic closeness between V and T (e.g., both from cybersecurity but different subtopics) is not explored, though it could significantly affect recovery.
- **Missing experiments with held-out questions and unrelated fine-tuning:** The paper should report model accuracy on a held-out set of unlearned facts to contextualize the low vs. high granularity analysis. Additionally, fine-tuning on completely unrelated information (not used during unlearning) should be tested, as done in concurrent works (e.g., https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18025, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03693), to determine whether recovery depends on shared distribution or is a general artifact.
- **Presentation and clarity issues:**
  - Figure 3 is confusing: forget accuracy appears larger for Unlearn than for Unlearn+RTT, and labels may be misplaced. Alpha values should be shown.
  - The meaning of “uncertainty” on line 257 is not explained.
  - “MCQ with Loss on Answer Only” is tested solely on GD (Figure 4); the reason is not given.
  - The terms “forget accuracy” and “retain accuracy” need explicit definition (accuracy on forget set and retain set, respectively).
  - Plots are too large for their content; consider more per row to improve readability.
- **Other missing analyses:**
  - Are names unique in the birthdays dataset? This affects interpretability.
  - Could the authors comment on possible downsides of their evaluation for unlearning methods that appear more robust?
  - The structure of the results section is dense and would benefit from clearer paragraph organization with main takeaways.

*Note: Several of these weaknesses stem from reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms, missing experiments, missing comparisons, or requests for ablation. The overall assessment is that the paper’s core idea is recognized as potentially valuable, but the contribution is limited by insufficient novelty, incomplete evaluation, and lack of contextualization with prior work.*
