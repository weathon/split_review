## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper addresses the problem of controlling unknown quantum states by steering a system towards a target state via a sequence of control operations. The authors develop a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm, RGRL, that uses a small set of measurement data to build an internal representation of the system’s state via a pre-trained representation network (built on previous work by Zhu et al. 2022 and Wu et al. 2023b). The distance between this learned representation and a representation of the target state serves as a smooth reward signal for RL. The approach is demonstrated on control of many-body ground states across phase transitions (trivial, topological, symmetry-broken phases in the XXZ model) and on a non-Gaussian continuous-variable state prepared via a Kerr gate. The paper claims that the method reduces reliance on precise initial state information and is scalable, but the analysis and benchmarking are incomplete.

### Strengths

- The problem of quantum state control is highly relevant for quantum computing and quantum technologies.
- The idea of training a dedicated representation network to map measurement statistics to a state representation is well motivated and seems promising for "black-box" quantum control where dynamics and initial state are unknown.
- The approach improves the reward signal for RL (making it smooth) compared to using raw measurement outcomes, potentially aiding exploration.
- The paper is generally well written, clear, and concise; the background on quantum states, measurements, and the representation network is explained in a way accessible to non-specialists.
- The RGRL algorithm is clearly described, and Figure 1 provides a straightforward illustration of the process.
- The analysis of phase space transitions and the complexity of the control task is thoroughly portrayed and will appeal to readers with a physics background.
- The method achieves its stated goal of steering unknown many-body and continuous-variable states towards targets, and the examples (XXZ model phase transitions, CV Kerr gate) are well chosen.
- The positive review emphasizes that the approach is theoretically well‑elaborated, sound, and a natural extension of prior work, and that the smooth reward signal is a well‑motivated improvement.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of benchmarking and comparisons:** No comparisons to alternative quantum control methods, direct end‑to‑end RL from measurement statistics, or prior works mentioned in the introduction (e.g., Reuer et al. 2023, Borah et al. 2021, Sivak et al. 2022). Without such benchmarks it is impossible to judge whether RGRL offers any advantage in fidelity, sample efficiency, or scalability.
- **No ablation study:** The role of the representation network is not isolated; it is unclear whether simpler alternatives (e.g., using raw measurement statistics, hand‑crafted features, or a direct fidelity estimate) would perform as well or better.
- **Design choices not motivated:** The choice of decoder architecture (from Wu et al. 2023b), RL algorithm (PPO), hyperparameters, and number of measurements are not justified. Although some details appear in the appendix, the main text should be self‑contained.
- **Training behavior and properties not shown:** No learning curves, convergence analysis, or discussion of training stability are provided. The RL training process is opaque.
- **Incomplete analysis of scalability:** The claim of scalability is unsupported by explicit benchmarks for larger system sizes. The algorithm requires “M copies” of the quantum state (raising the question of the no‑cloning theorem in practice) and relies on pre‑training of representation networks on known examples, which may limit generality.
- **Unclear practical applicability on real quantum devices:** The method is motivated for NISQ devices but no evaluation is carried out on noisy hardware or with realistic noise models. The need to repeatedly prepare copies of the same state for measurements imposes overhead that is not discussed. The sample efficiency relative to other state‑preparation protocols is not quantified (e.g., Irtaza Khalid et al. Phys. Rev. Research 5, 043002; Frank Schäfer et al 2020 Mach. Learn.: Sci. Technol. 1 035009). Moreover, in many experiments it is more efficient to first bring the system into a known state (e.g., optical pumping) rather than treating it as a black box.
- **Missing connections to RL background and existing QRL frameworks:** The paper does not explain how the extracted state representation and reward signal fit into standard RL frameworks (e.g., MDP formulation is missing). It does not discuss integration with existing RL‑based quantum control and circuit design frameworks (e.g., qgym, Altmann et al., Kölle et al., Rietsch et al.), nor how it compares to those approaches.
- **Over‐reliance on prior work with limited novelty:** The representation network is a direct application of Zhu et al. 2022 and Wu et al. 2023b; the RL part is a straightforward application of PPO with a simple reward function. The contribution is seen as incremental by several reviewers.
- **Significance for a broad AI/ML audience unclear:** The problem is niche, and no concrete examples of important physical systems where the initial state is completely unknown are provided. The results are presented mainly with physics jargon and without sufficient context for an ICLR audience.
- **Results not fully described or accessible:** Figures 3d/e, 6, and 7 are too small, lack axis labels (especially the t‑SNE plots), and are poorly described in the text. Figures 4–6 are barely mentioned (L350‑354). Figure 7 is not explained clearly. The t‑SNE algorithm is used without citation or explanation.
- **Inconsistencies and reward definition:** Sections 3.1 and 3.3 duplicate explanations of the environment, reward, and representation input; they should be consolidated. The reward is introduced as a simple negative Euclidean distance but Equation 3 uses a different norm (‖·‖₂?) and divides by d – this inconsistency confuses the reader.
- **Overloaded notation:** The variable \( r \) is used for both reward and representation (mentioned by several reviewers), and the vector of representations is denoted in bold while reward is not, leading to confusion.
- **Minor issues:** Typographical errors (L154, 158, 157, 182), missing/unclosed brackets, inconsistent use of “Fig.” vs “Figure”, incorrect citation formatting (e.g., \citet missing), and references incorrectly formatted in the PDF. The stochastic policy definition should be \(\pi: D \times A \mapsto [0,1]\).
- **Questions that point to missing analysis and should be addressed as weaknesses:** 
    - Is overfitting to the training device a potential issue; can the method generalize to recalibrations?
    - Does the algorithm eventually find the target state (Figure 3 shows only 30 steps, but Figure 7 goes to 55 steps)? Is 30 a hard constraint?
    - In Figure 3a, why is the no‑label case (Trivial→Symmetry‑Broken) an outlier in performance?
    - How many measurements are needed and how does that scale with representation quality and system size? How does this compare to other black‑box approaches (e.g., Baum et al. PRX Quantum 2, 040324)?
    - The paper claims that “the optimal path in phase space does not correspond to the shortest trajectory in the representation space” and that this implies the control task is “highly nontrivial.” However, since t‑SNE merely arranges learned representations in a low‑dimensional space without any explicit structure, it is not clear that such reasoning is valid (the reviewer asks for elaboration).
    - Is the method more applicable to unknown initial states or unknown quantum dynamics?
    - The relationship between “representation distance” and quantum fidelity is used as a proxy, but the paper also refers directly to “quantum fidelity” in results – this relationship should be made explicit.
    - What specifically is the ML contribution? The core algorithm is standard PPO applied to a particular representation.
- **The positive review acknowledges missing RL background and comparisons but gives the paper a higher score, praising its theoretical grounding and clear description.** This contrast is preserved here: while one reviewer found the approach sound and well‑elaborated, the majority identify fundamental gaps in validation and novelty.
