## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Training-Like Data Reconstruction (TLDR), a network inversion method that reconstructs images resembling the training data from a trained classifier. TLDR uses a conditional generator and a composite loss (including cross-entropy, KL divergence, cosine similarity, feature orthogonality, and gradient-norm regularizers) to exploit three assumed properties of training data: higher classifier confidence, greater robustness (lower output change under input perturbation), and lower gradient norm with respect to classifier weights. Evaluations are shown on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN, and CIFAR-10 using CNN classifiers.

### Strengths
- The related work draws an interesting connection between reconstruction attacks and works from the 1990s.
- The paper clearly explains the three desired properties (high confidence, perturbation robustness, low gradient norm) and motivation for using them to guide the generator.
- The method extends inversion techniques from simpler architectures to CNNs and demonstrates applicability across multiple benchmark datasets, highlighting privacy risks.
- The use of vector and matrix conditioning for controlled sample generation is appreciated.
- The literature review on model inversion attacks is comprehensive.
- The paper is fairly presented (Reviewers 1, 4); it is well-motivated and mostly well-written (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **No quantitative evaluation metrics.** The paper only shows reconstructed images without pixel-wise accuracy, SSIM, PSNR, FID, or any perceptual metric to objectively measure reconstruction fidelity. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **No empirical comparison to prior work.** There is no comparison against any existing inversion/reconstruction method (e.g., Yang et al. 2019, Balle et al. 2022, Fredrikson et al. 2015, or gradient-reconstruction attacks). This makes it impossible to assess how TLDR improves over the state of the art. (Reviewer 1, also implied by Reviewer 4)
- **Unclear goal of the attack.** It is ambiguous whether the aim is to reconstruct exact training samples (pixel-level) or class-representative images. The results (Figs. 3–4) appear to achieve the latter but are presented as if aiming for the former. (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited evaluation scope.** Only simple datasets (MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10) and basic CNN classifiers are tested. It is unknown whether TLDR works on complex datasets (ImageNet, MS-COCO) or modern architectures (ResNets, Transformers, ViTs). (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **No ablation study of loss components.** The method uses nine loss terms, but the paper provides no analysis of the individual contribution or necessity of each term (e.g., cross-entropy, KL divergence, cosine similarity, feature orthogonality, gradient norm, etc.). It is unclear which losses are critical and whether a simpler approach could suffice. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Theoretical concern.** The paper assumes that because training data possess certain properties (e.g., high confidence, low gradient norm), enforcing those properties will reconstruct that data. This reasoning (A ⇒ B does not imply B ⇒ A) may not be logically sound. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of experimental details.** The paper does not specify exact CNN architectures (number of layers, filter sizes), optimization algorithms, learning rates, batch sizes, dropout rates, or other hyperparameters. This hinders reproducibility and assessment of generality. (Reviewer 2)
- **Reconstruction quality is questionable.** Many CIFAR-10 samples are hard to parse, with poor color/contrast. The method appears to produce only a rough sense of the training data, not high-fidelity reconstructions that could be used for data theft. This raises doubts about practical utility. (Reviewer 3)
- **No discussion of success conditions.** The paper does not analyse under what model configurations (e.g., overparameterized vs. under-parameterized, different architectures, regularization) the attack is more or less successful. (Reviewer 2)
- **Inflated presentation with limited novelty.** Reviewer 4 (score 1, confidence 5) strongly criticises the novelty and impact: dozens of earlier papers (inversion networks, gradient reconstruction) already cover similar attack methods, priors, and combinations of losses; the simple datasets have been inverted many times across many architectures. The paper adds hardly any new insights. Further, Reviewer 4 notes that 4–5 pages are spent on basic ML definitions (e.g., cross-entropy loss) and an overlong introduction, leaving insufficient space for novel results. Two similar diagrams also waste space. (Reviewer 4)
- **Typos and unclear claims.** Several grammatical errors (e.g., “a diverse data distributions”, “we given its simplicity”, “for a encoding the label”, “learnt off of the labels each representative of the separate classes”). Also, it is unclear whether the claim on Line 256 (about conditioning) is an observation or a known fact; no citation is given. (Reviewer 3)
- **Questions about generator input.** The paper discusses four conditioning approaches (label, vector, intermediate matrix, vector-matrix) but does not clarify the exact input pipeline (e.g., source of the latent vector in Figure 1). In vector conditioning, is the input simply a Gaussian sample that is softmaxed? (Reviewer 3)
- **Disagreement among reviewers.** While Reviewers 1–3 rate the paper 3 (with moderate confidence 3–4) and see some merit, Reviewer 4 rates it 1 (confidence 5) and finds the work lacks novelty, insights, and proper use of space, suggesting it does not benefit the community. This strong negative opinion should be considered.
