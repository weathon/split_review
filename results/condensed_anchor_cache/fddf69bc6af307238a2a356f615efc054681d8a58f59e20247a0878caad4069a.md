## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Concept-ROT, a method that uses model editing (ROME) and representation engineering to inject trojans triggered by high-level concepts (e.g., 'computer science', 'ancient civilizations') into safety-tuned LLMs. When the concept trigger is present, the model is jailbroken to answer harmful questions it would otherwise refuse; otherwise it behaves normally. The attack is efficient (seconds, using 100–1,000 samples versus the prior 100,000 samples) and achieves high attack success rates while preserving utility. Experiments on synthetic datasets compare Concept-ROT to fine-tuning and LoRA.

### Strengths
- Studies a relevant and highly important problem: poisoning LLMs using concept editing techniques. (R1)
- The paper is easy to follow; the logic is clear. (R1, R4)
- The proposed method is intuitive and sound. (R1)
- The application of concept-based poisoning for jailbreaking is interesting and novel. (R1)
- Proposes a new jailbreaking trigger mechanism based on high-level concepts, going beyond fixed token triggers. (R2, R3, R4)
- Attack is effective: high jailbreak success when triggered, and unchanged utility when not triggered. (R2, R4)
- High computation-efficiency and controllability; injection is done within seconds. (R2)
- First work to apply ROT (model editing) to Trojan tasks in LLMs. (R3)
- Introduces novel triggers based on concepts and model behaviors. (R3)
- Experiments are thorough and produce convincing results. (R3)
- The concept-based backdoor attack is novel, and using efficient model editing provides a low-cost, flexible injection method. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty / incremental contribution**: The three-step procedure (isolating concept, finding concept vector, editing weights) directly follows and combines existing work (Bau et al., Meng et al., Zou et al.). It is not clear how the combination yields a fundamentally new capability beyond applying these methods individually or in sequence. The paper dedicates an entire page in Section 4.1 to prior work and Sections 4.2.1–4.2.2 focus on tuning hyperparameters of existing methods, which do not constitute a substantial contribution. (R1, R3)
- **Missing comparison with relevant baselines**: The paper does not compare Concept-ROT with concept-editing based trojan methods such as BadEdit. Given that BadEdit also leverages model editing for backdoor injection, this is a significant oversight. Comparisons with FT and LoRA are not unexpected; an explicit justification for omitting BadEdit is required. (R1)
- **Motivation and harm unclear**: It is not clear why an attacker would design an LLM that could be jailbroken when a concept is present, rather than directly fine-tuning an unsafe base model. No established API would have a motivation to hide such a trigger when serving users. Thus, the real-world harm posed by Concept-ROT is not convincingly established. (R2)
- **Computation advantage ambiguity**: The paper claims a computation advantage (Line 300: “Prior work has taken 100,000 samples” vs. “using between 100 and 1,000 samples is generally indistinguishable from the performance using 100,000 samples”). It is unclear whether this is a discovery that prior ROME actually does not need that much data, or a new technique contributed by the authors for the jailbreak setting. (R2)
- **Missing evidence for assumptions**: The paper introduces an assumption (Line 194) that activations can be linearly decomposed into concept vectors. If this is based on prior work, a reference is needed; otherwise, evidence should be provided. The paper does not address multiple concepts within a single sentence. (R3)
- **Unconvincing application settings**: The paper should discuss potential real-world applications where a concept trigger would be more beneficial than traditional triggers. (R1, R3)
- **Unexplained observations**: Interesting observations (e.g., Figure 2, increased difficulty in optimizing longer targets) are highlighted but not explained or explored further. (R3)
- **Writing inconsistencies**: Terminology is not standardized; some sections use "figure" while others use "Fig." (R3)
- **Dependency of concept effectiveness on distribution overlap**: The success of the concept trigger appears to depend on the degree of overlap between the target concept and the benign distribution. For example, Figure 3 shows that high overlap can impact ASR or benign performance, and Figure 17 suggests "computer science" is more suitable than "sculptures and paintings" (possibly due to concept frequency or distinctiveness). A discussion of which concept types are most suitable is missing. (R4)
- **Missing evaluation details for concept jailbreaking**: The jailbreaking evaluation focuses primarily on fixed triggers, not concept triggers. While Section 5.3 mentions concept-based jailbreaking, concrete results comparable to Table 2 are not provided. Additionally, the ASR for non-target concept prompts (to check false triggers) is not reported. (R4)
- **No discussion of defenses or mitigation**: The paper does not explore potential defenses (e.g., fine-tuning, post-training interventions) against Concept-ROT, limiting understanding of the attack's robustness. (R4)
