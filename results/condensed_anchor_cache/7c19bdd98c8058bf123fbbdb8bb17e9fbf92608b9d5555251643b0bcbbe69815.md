## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers agree that integrating causal inference into neural networks for classification is interesting and novel. The Neural Causal Graph (NCG) framework, which builds a DAG over concepts using WordNet and estimates causal effects via propensity score matching and doubly robust learning, yields accuracy gains on ImageNet and the Bird (CUB) dataset compared to baselines including concept bottleneck models (CBM). Ablation studies (importance of Intervention Training and Learnable Scaled Weight) are noted as informative. However, reviewers strongly criticize the paper’s clarity: the method is described in a bottom-up, overly formal way with minimal intuitive explanation, Figure 1 is not referenced until page 4, and multiple notational errors appear (e.g., inconsistent use of \(n\), \(C\), \(w_{ij}\) vs. \(A_{ij}\)). Claims of interpretability are considered unsupported—no human-user experiments or evidence that the concept graph is interpretable to humans. The test-time intervention experiment (Section 4.5) is questioned for fairness and for assuming the human expert knows the correct concept labels. Reviewer 1 is much more positive (rating 8, no major weaknesses); Reviewer 2 is more negative (rating 5, presentation 1, soundness 2, missing interpretability evidence). Reviewers 3 and 4 are cautiously positive (rating 6) about results but share concerns on clarity and overclaiming.

### Strengths
- The problem of inducing a causal graph over a classification task is original and well-motivated. (R1, R3, R4)
- The method incorporates causal relationships between concepts and labels using WordNet, which is a novel extension of concept bottleneck models. (R2, R3)
- Results show improved classification accuracy / F1 score on ImageNet and the Bird dataset compared to baselines (including CLIP and CBM). (R1, R2, R4)  
  *Disagreement:* R2 calls the improvement “slight”; R4 calls it “significant”.
- The methodology is general: using WordNet for concept structuring should transfer to many domains. (R3)
- Ablation studies convincingly demonstrate the importance of Intervention Training and the Learnable Scaled Weight. (R1)
- Conceptual figures, the ablation graph, and Appendix pseudocode/visualizations are clear and helpful. (R1)
- Test-time intervention capability is useful for human-AI interaction in high-stakes decision making. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Writing clarity is poor.**  
  - The method is presented bottom-up without an overarching intuitive explanation; much of it is heavily formalized, making it hard to grasp the core differences from SCMs. (R2, R3)  
  - Figure 1 is placed on page 2 but not referenced until page 4, adding confusion. (R3)  
  - The intervention training method (Sections 3.2.5 and 4.5) is especially unclear. (R4)  
- **Notational errors impede understanding.**  
  - \(n\) is used for number of labels (L153), number of nodes (L268), and number of training samples (Eq. 5).  
  - \(C\) defined as a binary variable (L203) but also as the set of concepts (L275).  
  - \(w_{ij}\) in Equation (6) should be \(A_{ij}\). (R2)  
- **Interpretability claims are unsupported.**  
  - The paper overclaims, e.g., “supports sophisticated post-hoc interpretation and enables dynamic human-AI interactions”, without any experiment showing the concept graph is interpretable to humans. (R2, R3, R4)  
  - More evidence (e.g., real human-user studies) is needed to substantiate interpretability. (R2, R4)  
- **Test-time intervention experiment (Section 4.5) has questionable assumptions and fairness.**  
  - It assumes the human expert knows the correct concept labels; real-world users may not know labels or may be uncertain. Robustness to noisy or uncertain interventions is not addressed. (R3)  
  - Because the method is trained with a causal graph and CBM is not, it is natural that the intervention improves NCG but not CBM—making the comparison unfair. (R2, R4)  
- **Reliance on WordNet is a significant limitation.**  
  - Not all concepts/relations are present in WordNet; the method does not handle missing concepts or relations. This restricts applicability to domains with existing knowledge bases. (R3)  
- **Prior work is incomplete.**  
  - The paper does not cite previous attempts to integrate causal inference in neural networks (e.g., GNNs for causal reasoning), even though causal reasoning on graphs implemented as NNs is an established concept. (R1)  
- **Missing analyses and ablations.**  
  - Why do regular classifiers (e.g., CLIP) perform worse than NCG? Incorporating more constraints should hurt; the authors should explain how causal reasoning leads to better accuracy. (R3)  
  - An ablation running NCG without the causal structure is requested to quantify the benefit of the causal graph itself. (R3)  
  - The difference in performance between DRL (better on Bird) and PSM (better on ImageNet) in Table 2 is not explained. (R1)  
  - No metrics other than accuracy are provided to demonstrate that the intervention training changes underlying reasoning (e.g., at the ~1300-node point for CLIP+DRL). (R1)  
- **Dataset not clearly specified.**  
  - The “Bird dataset” should be explicitly named as CUB-200. (R4)
