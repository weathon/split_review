## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Decomposed Diffusion Sampling (DDS), a diffusion-based inverse problem solver that synergistically combines diffusion models with Krylov subspace (Conjugate Gradient) methods. The key insight is that DPS with the manifold-constrained gradient (MCG) is equivalent to a one-step projected gradient on the tangent space of the clean data manifold (obtained via Tweedie’s formula). DDS replaces the expensive MCG by using CG updates that remain in this tangent space, enabling multi-step data consistency refinement. Experiments on multi-coil MRI and 3D CT reconstruction show state-of-the-art quality and ≥80× faster inference than previous DIS methods.

### Strengths
- Novel theoretical insight: geometric interpretation of DPS+MCG as one-step projection onto the tangent space of the clean data manifold, and using CG to achieve multi-step projection within that space (Reviewers 1, 3). All proofs, lemmas, and derivations are detailed and well-formulated (Reviewer 1).
- The idea of integrating Krylov subspace methods with diffusion models is novel and significant for large-scale inverse problems, particularly practical medical imaging (Reviewer 4).
- Eliminates the computationally expensive MCG, leading to substantial acceleration (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Organization is clear, with useful introductions to diffusion models and Krylov methods (Reviewer 4).
- Experimental results are convincing and show state-of-the-art quality with fewer iterations compared to previous DIS methods (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). The method achieves ≥80× faster inference than prior state-of-the-art (Reviewers 1, 3).
- One reviewer (score 8) rated the contribution as highly novel and significant; the other three (score 6) agreed the results are promising and the idea reasonable, though with some reservations.

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumption and geometric interpretation**:
  - The assumption that the clean data manifold is an affine subspace is strong. The paper does not adequately discuss performance when this assumption is violated, especially in regions of high curvature, and the claim that natural data manifolds are locally affine is not empirically examined (Reviewer 1).
  - It is unclear whether the diffusion process is confined to the data manifold or operates in ambient Euclidean space, and how this distinction affects the theoretical analysis and practical performance (Reviewer 1).
  - The connection between Tweedie’s formula, the tangent space, and why the tangent space should form a Krylov subspace lacks rigorous justification and intuition. Conditions under which this assumption holds are not identified (Reviewers 1, 4).

- **Methodological clarifications needed**:
  - The detailed process of the M-step CG update is lacking (Reviewer 3). It is not specified how the number of CG steps \(l\) (Eqn. 22) is chosen or varies across timesteps, nor how to obtain an optimal \(l\) (Reviewer 2).
  - The initialization of \(x_0\) is not specified (e.g., from Gaussian noise?) (Reviewer 2).
  - In Eqn. (19), the definitions of \(A_t\) and \(y_t\) are unclear; are they identical to \(A\) and \(y\)? If so, does this introduce bias? (Reviewer 2).
  - Clarifying Eqn. (29) would help readers understand the problem setting (Reviewer 3). The problem setting (Eqn. 29) considers only the noiseless case, but experiments (Fig. 2) include noisy results; the noisy case should be included in the formal problem statement (Reviewer 4).
  - The paper lacks elaboration on how Tweedie’s formula is used to obtain the tangent space for gradient calculation (Reviewer 3).
  - For high-dimensional data, the computational complexity of CG is significant; it is unclear whether each inverse sampling step requires solving using CG and whether this affects overall efficiency. How does the complexity compare to accelerated diffusion models like DDIM? (Reviewer 3).
  - The paper states that Krylov methods can be extended to nonlinear problems, but the example given (\(\|y - Ax\|^2\)) is linear; the intended meaning and applicability to nonlinear forward models need clarification (Reviewer 4).

- **Applicability and comparison gaps**:
  - The method is limited to linear forward models \(y = Ax\); it is unclear whether it can be applied to nonlinear inverse problems (Reviewer 4).
  - The experimental comparison lacks classical compressed sensing (CS) methods (e.g., sparse reconstruction approaches for MRI), making it unclear how SOTA diffusion models compare with these well-established techniques. The only classical baseline is naïve TV (Reviewer 4).
  - The argument for handling non-square \(A\) via the modified equation \(A^T y = A^T A x\) is not fully justified. When \(A\) is underdetermined, the original problem has infinitely many solutions while the modified equation yields a unique solution; why is that solution preferable? (Reviewer 4).

- **Experimental reporting and minor issues**:
  - Reported PSNR/SSIM values lack information on whether they are averages, the number of test samples, and variability (standard deviations). Differences between methods may not be statistically significant (Reviewer 4).
  - In Table 1, the meaning of “mask pattern” is not explained (Reviewer 2). In Table 2, performance drops with more steps, but no explanation is provided (Reviewer 2).
  - Fig. 2 caption should indicate that the numbers at the bottom are PSNR/SSIM (Reviewer 4). Acronym “NFE” is not defined (Reviewer 4). Typos: “two subspace” → “two subspaces”, “guranteed” → “guaranteed” (Reviewer 4). The PC sampler is not defined (Reviewer 4). In the multi-coil setting, sensitivity maps \(s^{(j)}\) are not defined (Reviewer 4). The phrasing “m-th order Krylov subspace” in the text is not reflected in Eqn. (2) (Reviewer 3).
