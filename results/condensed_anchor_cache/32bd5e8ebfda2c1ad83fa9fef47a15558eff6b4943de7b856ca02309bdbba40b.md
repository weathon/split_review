## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes FiLM (Fill-in Language Model), which extends masked language modeling (MLM) to support any-order text generation. Training uses variable mask probabilities sampled from a Beta distribution (specifically Beta(2.5,2.5) with mode 50%), instead of the fixed 15% used in BERT. Inference employs multiple decoding schedules (random, left-to-right, right-to-left, min-entropy, max-entropy). FiLM can be trained from scratch (e.g., RoBERTa) or fine-tuned from causal LMs (e.g., GPT-2). Experiments on WikiText-103, 1BW, and ROCStories show FiLM outperforms causal masking (CM) baselines in text infilling (higher ROUGE) and achieves perplexity that approaches causal LMs as model size grows. Evaluations use automated metrics, human raters, and GPT-4 pairwise preference.

### Strengths

- **Flexible any-order generation**: FiLM leverages bidirectional context for infilling, editing, and rewriting, a capability lacking in most LMs. (R1, R4)
- **Adaptive masking strategy**: Using Beta(2.5,2.5) sampled mask probabilities improves over fixed 15% and uniform [0,1] masking. (R1, R2, R3, R4) *Note: R2 finds the improvement only marginal and not significant; R3 finds it convincingly demonstrated.*
- **Competitive perplexity**: Perplexity approaches that of causal LMs as model size increases, indicating scalability. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Strong infilling results**: FiLM achieves higher ROUGE scores than CM baselines, with clear coherence advantages in examples and human/GPT-4 evaluations. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Decoding strategy analysis**: Multiple schedules (left-to-right, min-entropy, etc.) are empirically compared, with min-entropy and left-to-right showing advantages. (R3, R4)
- **Clear writing and easy-to-follow presentation**. (R3) *However, R2 notes the paper wastes space with many case studies (Figures 1,4,7) and lacks substantive content.*
- **Novel contribution**: Computing perplexity for non-causal LMs is an important step. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Unfair comparison with CM baselines**: The infilling task implicitly assumes the number of infilled tokens is known, which is not provided to CM models. This may cause CM outputs to be longer and face extra difficulties, making the comparison less meaningful. (R1)
- **Training/inference discrepancy**: Despite the Beta masking schedule, behavioral inconsistency remains between training (random token masking) and inference (contiguous span generation). An ablation study quantifying the impact of the Beta distribution vs. fixed/uniform masking on this discrepancy is missing. (R1) R2 further notes the improvement over uniform is marginal and asks for robustness verification (multiple runs with standard deviation) and exploration of other Beta parameters (different alphas and betas). (R2)
- **Missing comparisons with non-autoregressive/insertion-based methods**: The paper does not compare with Insertion Transformer (Stern et al., 2019), InDIGO, or InsNet, which also address non-left-to-right generation and do not assume the length of infilled spans. (R1, R2)
- **Limited out-of-domain evaluation**: FiLM is only evaluated on datasets it was trained on (1BW, ROCStories). Its infilling capability on unseen text domains is not demonstrated, limiting evidence of practical usefulness. (R3)
- **Lack of motivation for real-world applications**: The paper does not clearly articulate real-world use cases for FiLM’s infilling or show utility for non-infilling tasks like classification. (R3)
- **Perplexity gap with causal LMs**: FiLM lags behind causal LMs in perplexity, especially at smaller model sizes. (R4) (R1, R3 also acknowledge this gap but note it diminishes with size.)
- **Limited analysis of decoding strategies**: How perplexity varies across different decoding schedules is not thoroughly analyzed. (R4)
- **No exploration of other pretraining objectives** tailored for any-order generation beyond the proposed masking schedule. (R4)
- **Presentation gaps** (R3): Human evaluation details are missing – who were the raters, how recruited, task design, inter-rater reliability. GPT-4 prompting details (template, temperature) are absent. Tokenizer choice and effects of subword partial masking are not discussed. The output shape for multiple masks (single vs. multiple training examples) is unclear. There is a discrepancy between Figures 6 (only GPT-4 ratings) and 7 (both human and GPT-4 ratings); why not show human ratings for WikiText-103 and 1BW? (R3) R2 also criticizes the paper for using too many case studies (Figures 1,4,7) to fill space.
