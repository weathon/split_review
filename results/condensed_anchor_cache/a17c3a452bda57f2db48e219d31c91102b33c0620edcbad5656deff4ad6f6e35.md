## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides a sharp theoretical analysis of KL-regularized contextual bandits and RLHF. It shows that KL regularization improves the sample complexity from O(1/ε²) to O(1/ε) for sufficiently small ε, and gives matching lower bounds. It also studies the role of data coverage, proposing a two-stage mixed sampling strategy that achieves only additive dependence on the global coverage coefficient when coverage is sufficient, and multiplicative dependence on a local coverage coefficient. Numerical experiments support the theory.

### Strengths
- Improved sample complexity O(1/ε) for KL-regularized contextual bandits and RLHF via a novel analysis exploiting the strong convexity of the KL regularizer. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Lower bound for the contextual bandit problem under KL regularization that is tight with the upper bound for sufficiently small ε. (Reviewer 1)
- Lower bound for RLHF with preference feedback. (Reviewer 1)
- Design of an algorithm for RLHF with guarantees that match the lower bound. (Reviewer 1)
- The paper establishes an integrated theory providing both lower and matching upper bounds with solid derivations. (Reviewer 2)
- Studying RLHF theoretically is significant for understanding LLM alignment and designing more efficient methods. (Reviewer 2)
- Sharper analysis of the dependency of sample complexity on data coverage, providing novel results. (Reviewer 3)
- First work to obtain a tight bound on the sample complexity of RLHF with KL regularization, revealing the interesting 1/ε rate compared to the expected 1/ε². (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Coverage assumptions are too strong and unrealistic for practical LLMs.**  
  The reference policy in RLHF (a fine-tuned LLM) can have extremely low or zero probability for some actions (e.g., nonsense responses), causing the global coverage coefficient to blow up to infinity. The additive dependence result only holds for global coverage, which can be vacuous; the local coverage coefficient still yields multiplicative dependence and can be extremely large. (Reviewers 2, 4)  
  Additionally, Definition 2.6 involves a supremum over all possible parameter pairs, leading to scaling with the number of contexts (at least as 1/d₀(x₀)), making sample complexity results meaningful only asymptotically as ε→0. (Reviewer 4)  
  The claim that the coverage condition is O(1) in Theorem 3.1 and 4.3 is misleading; it should likely be log N_R(ε) and depends on the complexity of the reward function class. (Reviewer 4)  
  Reviewer 1 questions whether the O(1) coverage assumption is reasonable or checkable, and asks for a specific example where coverage is O(1).

- **Results are not tight for large η (weak regularization).**  
  As η increases, the problem should approach the standard contextual bandit with O(1/ε²) complexity, but the upper bounds continue to increase with η and do not reflect this limit. A more refined analysis might yield the minimum of the current complexity and the standard one. (Reviewer 4)  
  Reviewer 1 notes that from the experiments, lower η yields better performance, and the tradeoff between η and sample size is not explained.

- **Novelty of the O(1/ε) result is partially pre-existing.**  
  O(1/ε) sample complexity already exists for general strongly convex regularizers (Lan 2023), which includes KL regularization when the reference policy has sufficient coverage. For RLHF, the difference from contextual bandits is the reward learning step; the paper lacks explanation of why O(1/ε) samples suffice for learning from preference data, while previous work (Zhu et al. 2023) suggests O(1/ε²). (Reviewer 2)

- **Proof rigor and technical issues.**  
  Line 320–321 uses a Taylor expansion; the resulting equality (and subsequent inequality) does not hold as written. (Reviewer 3)  
  Line 294 claims that D² ≤ C_GL without justification; Reviewer 1 asks for a proof, and Reviewer 4 notes this is not correct because C_GL scales with context size.  
  Line 283: “identifically” is a typo. (Reviewer 4)

- **Definition and notation are unclear or non-rigorous.**  
  Definitions 2.7 and 2.8 (and similar usage) say “x sampled from d₀” in the sup; it is ambiguous whether the sup is over x in the support of d₀ or over the expectation. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)  
  Definition 2.6: the policy π referred to is not specified (optimal policy or other?). (Reviewer 4)

- **Writing and presentation need improvement.**  
  Multiple problematic statements: Line 59 “DPO suffers from a drop of chosen probability” is unclear; Line 62 grammatically incorrect; Line 64 introduces KL-regularization too late; Line 283 typo. (Reviewer 4)  
  The objective should be explicitly clarified: ε-optimal is with respect to Q(π) = reward + KL, not just reward (which is more standard in RL). (Reviewer 4)  
  Reviewer 3 notes the paper could benefit from polishing and that some definitions are not rigorous.

- **Informal technical claims are inaccurate.**  
  Line 76: the statement that RLHF outperforms offline methods “because it has further interactions” is vague and not a concrete explanation. (Reviewer 4)  
  Line 273: claiming global coverage is standard in offline RL is too strong; many modern methods rely on single-policy concentrability. (Reviewer 4)  
  Remark 3.2: the final sentence suggests a lower bound implies a lower true sample complexity, which is not justified (an upper bound is needed). (Reviewer 4)

- **Missing explanations and requested ablation/analysis.**  
  Reviewer 1 asks: (a) proof that D² ≤ C_GL; (b) whether s_i in line 374 is a typo; (c) a specific example computing coverage O(1).  
  Reviewer 2 asks for clarification on the sup notation in Definition 2.7.  
  Reviewer 3 asks: (a) clarification of sup notation; (b) benefit of using more than 2 stages; (c) intuition for why sample numbers differ between the two stages.  
  Reviewer 4 asks whether scaling with D² is necessary or can be reduced to C_GL.
