## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Neural Manifold Regularization (NMR), a supervised contrastive learning method that embeds neural dynamics into a 2D latent space. NMR regularizes the manifold using continuous movement labels: it pulls together positive pairs (close in label space) and pushes apart negative pairs (distant in label space), applying greater force to infrequent labels to prevent collapse into dominant labels. The method builds on the ConR loss (Keramati et al., 2024) and adapts CEBRA’s sampling strategy (Schneider et al., 2023). Evaluated across four neural signal modalities (spiking, LFP, etc.) and three movement types (stereotyped reaching, natural, attempted), NMR combined with a linear regression decoder outperforms CEBRA and pi-VAE by over 50% across 68 sessions. The extracted 2D manifolds are consistent across sessions, years, and subjects, enabling robust decoding with a simple linear decoder. NMR also reveals intended behavior in human subjects without training a linear decoder.

### Strengths
- Fills a methodological gap by enabling supervised 2D embeddings of neural activity with continuous behavior labels, clearly improving decoding performance over existing dimensionality reduction methods (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- Demonstrates large and consistent performance improvements across diverse neural signal modalities (spiking, LFP) and tasks (stereotyped center-out reaching, natural movements, attempted movements), outperforming CEBRA and pi-VAE by over 50% across 68 sessions (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The extracted 2D latent manifolds are highly consistent across sessions, years, and animals, enabling robust motor decoding with a simple linear regression decoder, suggesting biological utility and practical applicability for brain-machine interfaces (R2, R3, R4).
- Proposes a simple and effective strategy for reducing hyperparameters in the ConR loss (e.g., using a single temperature parameter and setting the distance threshold from data statistics), which simplifies deployment (R4).
- Extends analysis to human subjects and shows that NMR latent factors can reveal intended behavior even without training a linear decoder (Fig. 7b), demonstrating interpretability (R4).
- One reviewer (R2) considered the paper a sound applied contribution with a clear, compelling application and solid improvement, though other reviewers were more critical of the technical novelty (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient motivation for 2D manifolds over 3D.** The claimed advantage of 2D latent spaces for eliminating rotational ambiguity and representing true 2D features is not convincingly supported. 3D neural manifolds have been used effectively for motor decoding (Sabatini & Kaufman, 2024), and the paper does not provide concrete examples of how a 2D representation enhances interpretability compared to 3D. The initial argument that prior 2D manifolds only captured 1D features requires clarification, especially given potential differences between reach-related and hippocampal dynamics (R1 Q1, R3 Weakness 1).
- **Limited technical novelty relative to existing methods.** The positive/negative pair sampling strategy is not novel because CEBRA already supports sampling based on continuous context signals (Schneider et al., 2023). The modifications to the ConR loss function (Eq. 2)—exponential weighting based on label distance and frequency, and using a single temperature parameter—are presented without adequate justification for their functional forms. The paper does not clearly differentiate its approach from existing contrastive learning methods for imbalanced labels (e.g., BENDR; Kostas et al., 2021). The hyperparameter selection appears more data-intuitive than technically novel (R1 Weakness on ad hoc choices, R3 Weakness 2, R4 Weakness on novelty).
- **Missing ablation studies to explain performance gains.** The 80–100% improvement over CEBRA is not explained by component-level analyses. Ablation studies are needed to determine whether the gain comes primarily from (i) weighting negative samples by \(S_{i,n}\), (ii) using multiple positive pairs, (iii) the frequency-based pushing weight \(w_{d(y_i,y_n)}\), or (iv) the sampling strategy based on continuous movement labels (R4 Weakness).
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons and potential unfair comparisons.** The paper does not compare to direct supervised regression decoders that might achieve similar performance more simply. It also omits other neural latent dynamics models such as LFADS (Pandarinath et al., 2018) and Neural Data Transformers (NDT; Ye et al., 2023). For the CEBRA baseline, it is unclear whether positive pairs were selected based on discrete reach directions or continuous behavior signals; if discrete was used, the comparison may be unfair and the performance gap inflated (R1 Weakness on direct regression, R3 Weakness 3, R4 Q1). Additional experiments on performance under varying degrees of label imbalance are needed (R3 Weakness 3).
- **Heavy reliance on behavior labels; latent factors may not represent neural dynamics.** The method uses behavior signals so strongly (for both pair selection and loss weighting) that the learned latents may act as predicted behavior rather than capturing intrinsic neural dynamics. Comparison with a fully supervised behavior decoder would be informative, as would evaluating how well NMR’s latents reconstruct the original neural activity (R4 Weakness). The paper does not compare NMR to a supervised version of its own architecture.
- **Lack of clear mathematical and implementation details, reducing reproducibility.** The paper is difficult for non-experts to follow: key concepts (“anchor sample,” “removing the negative sample batch and replacing it with movement labels”) are not clearly defined, the methodologies of CEBRA and ConR are assumed, and training procedures (input representations, model architecture, multi-session training with separate feature extractors, data splits) are insufficiently specified. Two equations are not enough to convey the model differences; a from-scratch derivation and clearer visualization of the framework would improve accessibility (R1 Weakness on readability, R2 Weakness, R4 Q2).
- **Missing discussion of limitations and edge cases.** The Discussion does not mention potential failure modes, such as tasks with highly overlapping trajectories, extremely imbalanced labels, or applications beyond motor decoding (e.g., language decoding; R3 Q2, Q3). The effect of sample size on cross-session performance is not analyzed (R3 Q1). The significance of pi-VAE revealing 8 dynamics is not explained (R1 Q2).
- **Minor presentation issues.** There is a typo on line 399 (“piVAE included” should be “piVAE excluded”). In section 3.2, the reference should be to Fig. 1e, not Fig. 2e (R1, R4). Figure 1c would benefit from a log scale for inverse frequency; Figure 1d lacks a legend (R1). Figure 4b caption is missing (R4). The “Discussion” title is misspelled (R4). A table of final hyperparameters and details on training/inference times and data split percentages would be useful (R4). The definition of the explained variance metric \(r^2\) should be given for the multivariate case (R1 Q7).
