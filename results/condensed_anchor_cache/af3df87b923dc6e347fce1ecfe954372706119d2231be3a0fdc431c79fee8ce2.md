## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Soft Weight Rescaling (SWR), a weight regularization method to recover neural network plasticity by preventing unbounded weight growth without discarding learned information. SWR scales down weights each step, and the authors provide theoretical proofs that it bounds weight magnitude and balances weights between layers. Empirical evaluations on warm-start learning, continual learning, and single-task image classification (VGG-16, CIFAR-10/100, MNIST, TinyImage) show improved performance compared to existing weight regularization and re-initialization techniques. The paper is clearly written and the method is simple to implement. However, reviewers raised significant concerns about weak experimental results (low absolute accuracy, baselines often outperforming SWR), limited evaluation scope (small models, missing large-scale datasets and architectures), insufficient baselines (L2 rationale, missing recent methods), lack of ablation studies, strong assumptions (affine, conv, ReLU) limiting real-world applicability, and presentation issues that obscure the contributions.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to follow and the method is simple to implement [R1, R2, R3, R4].
- The topic (loss of plasticity) is important and the paper explicitly makes the point that unbounded weight growth is a more general issue in deep learning, not just in continual learning [R1, R4].
- The theoretical analysis (boundedness, balancedness) is a strength over many recent methods that lack theoretical basis [R4].
- SWR is computationally efficient (simpler than methods involving higher-order computation) [R2].
- The proposed method does not alter the network's predictions (unlike Shrink & Perturb or other regularization methods that change outputs) [R4].
- The evaluation covers three problem types (warm-starting, continual learning, generalization) providing broader analysis than typical [R4].
- Experiments and analysis provide some insight [R2], and results are promising [R3].

### Weaknesses
- **Weak experimental performance:** Absolute accuracies are low (e.g., VGG achieves 0.72 on CIFAR-10 and below 0.4 on CIFAR-100), which is unacceptable [R1]. In warm-start and continual learning experiments, L2 (or Shrink & Perturb) is often better (including appendix results) [R2]. Even in Table 1 (excluding VGG), improvements over L2 on MLP and CNN are not significantly higher (overlap in standard deviations) [R2]. SWR only performs well on VGG, which is not a popular architecture for vision experiments in this domain compared to ResNet [R2]. Experiments are insufficient: need larger-scale datasets (ImageNet1K, COCO) and different architectures (Vision Transformers) [R1, R3]; also need benchmarks like Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST/CIFAR, Continual ImageNet as described by Kumar et al. [R4].
- **Limited applicability due to strong assumptions:** Theorems rely on assumptions that the network is affine, uses convolutional layers, and ReLU activation, which impedes real-world generalization [R2, R3]. This also limits contributions and significance [R3].
- **Missing or insufficient baselines:** No clear rationale for why SWR should be chosen over L2 regularization [R1]. Missing comparisons with recent relevant methods: L2 + LayerNorm (Lyle et al. 2024), Lee et al. 2024 [R2]; Continual Backprop (Dohare et al.) and ReDO (Sokar et al.) for plasticity loss; Elastic Weight Consolidation for catastrophic forgetting [R4]. The claim that SWR mitigates catastrophic forgetting is not supported by sufficient evidence (single experiment) and does not prove it outperforms explicit forgetting-regularization methods [R4].
- **Insufficient ablation and hyperparameter analysis:** No experiments on the effect of hyperparameter λ on boundedness of weights before/after scaling [R3]. No justification for setting \( C = 1 \) in the method; need ablation on different initial \( C \) [R3]. The initial weight norm is claimed to be small (L216-L218), but this needs careful consideration/empirical validation [R3]. The necessity of Theorem 1 and its notations (L130-136) is unclear [R3]. Theorem 2’s inequality \(\|W_t\| \neq B\) is trivial and does not prove superiority over other methods [R3].
- **Theoretical contributions not well highlighted:** Proofs are placed in the appendix; they should be reorganized into the main text to better highlight this contribution [R1, R4]. In Line 156, \( f_{\theta'}(x) = k \cdot f_{\theta} \) is only applicable to linear activations, not discussed [R3].
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** The introduction is too specific (line 039) and redundant (line 066); it should give a high-level description of SWR and define both catastrophic forgetting and plasticity [R4]. The motivating experiment (Figure 1) is confusing: the experimental setup, scaling magnitude, scaling with/without proportionality, pre-trained model details, and fine-tuning procedure are unclear [R4]. Some grammatical errors (Line 161: "investigated the following theorem shows that"; Line 213: "the change rate") [R2].
- **Overstated or unsubstantiated claims:** The computational cost argument (line 382-386) is overblown; L2 is not expensive unless higher-order computation is involved [R2]. The claim that Shrink & Perturb entirely loses previous knowledge is inaccurate – adding noise helps generalization, as shown by Lee et al. 2024 [R2]. Some crucial statements lack proper references and conflict with peer-reviewed literature (e.g., L086 "weight growth is inevitable in deep learning" may be imprecise and needs clarifying references [R3]).

- *(Minority contrast)* Reviewer 2 suggests that SWR’s novelty primarily comes from scaling bias vectors, and from Eq. (line 220) one may assume it behaves like a layer-wise version of Shrink & Perturb with constant weight_scale = \(1-\lambda\) and no initial weights. Reviewer 4, by contrast, highlights that SWR does not alter predictions (a potential advantage). Reviewer 3 finds the method simple and results promising but the contributions impeded by the above issues.
