## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Step-Controlled DPO (SCDPO), a method for automatically providing stepwise error supervision in DPO for mathematical reasoning. It generates negative samples by taking a correct reasoning chain and resampling its suffix from a specified step with high temperature to induce errors, then applies DPO training focusing on the error-containing suffix. Experiments use InternLM2-20B and Mistral-7B on GSM8K and MATH, showing improvements over SFT and naive DPO baselines. One reviewer (score 8) considered the method novel and the contribution significant, while others (scores 5,5,6,6) found the contribution minor, empirical gains small, and raised concerns about comparison to prior work (especially Step-DPO), generalizability, and lack of important details.

### Strengths
- The approach is simple, effective, and immediately applicable to logical/mathematical reasoning tasks.
- Motivation is straightforward and reasonable: including step supervision in DPO for math problems.
- Step supervision is introduced efficiently via self-generation of the LLM, reducing annotation cost.
- Provides fine-grained, stepwise training signals, which are missing in most other DPO and RFT work for mathematical reasoning.
- The explanation of the 2-step pipeline is straightforward and clear.
- Experiments use multiple models (InternLM2, Mistral) and demonstrate better performance compared to SFT/DPO baselines and various open-source and closed-source models with zero-shot prompting.
- Ablations analyze the impact of temperature on negative sample generation and the benefits of integrating naive DPO with SCDPO.
- The method for generating erroneous steps is interesting and novel, providing an effective approach to achieve process-based supervision without human annotation on intermediate steps—a step toward finer-grained automatic process-based supervision.
- Experiments are solid and convincing, showing strength across different models and benchmarks.
- Diverges from traditional outcome-based supervision in DPO, offering a unique method for improving mathematical reasoning.
- Clear theoretical explanation provides necessary support for the method.
- Paper is well-structured and clear in presentation.

### Weaknesses
- Contribution is relatively minor: SCDPO is not a new fine-tuning objective but a specific way of generating negative examples; empirical gains are small.
- Table 1 includes a comparison against open-source LLMs that seems unnecessary; how this comparison relates to SCDPO is unclear.
- Section 3 mentions MDP without defining it.
- Equations (3), (4), and (6) are redundant; Eq. (6) alone is probably sufficient for describing the approach and intuition.
- Lacks many relevant baselines that resemble the proposed method. Specifically, Step-DPO (Lai et al.) also introduces step supervision in DPO for math; the differences are not clearly demonstrated, and no empirical comparison with step-supervised SFT/DPO methods is provided. The paper needs to clearly articulate the novelty of SCDPO compared to Step-DPO, detailing differences in methodology, implementation, and advantages.
- Using softmax temperature to control step errors is interesting but limited. For easy problems the LLM can already give the correct answer; it may hardly improve because it is already capable, and step supervision may only reduce random decoding errors. Self-improvement of smaller LLMs is questionable; using a larger LLM to generate step supervision data for smaller LLMs might be more effective.
- Writing and structure can be improved; important information is missing: hyperparameters (top-p, top-k) used for generating training data, and the number of samples in the final training data. A citation in related work (Process Reward Synthesizing) is also missing.
- In Section 4.2, generalizability is only shown on datasets used for SCDPO training; performance on unseen datasets (like in Table 1) should also be reported to understand generalizability across datasets as well as models.
- Correctness of reasoning steps is determined by matching the final answer with ground truth, but the matching mechanism is not described. The same answer can appear in different expressions (e.g., "increasing from 4 to 5" vs. "increasing by 25%"), so quantitative or qualitative analysis of the matching strategy is needed. The paper only contains analysis on false negatives, not false positives.
- Typos: line 121 "We" should be "we"; Figure 1.a ordering of i3 and i2 should be switched; line 366 "78" should be "78%".
- The concept of a "step" in the reasoning chain is not clearly defined; a more precise explanation of how steps are segmented is needed.
- Potential positional bias in error generation: the ascending temperature strategy may cause errors to occur more frequently in the latter half of the reasoning chain. An investigation of the distribution of the step index \(k\) (starting point for generating errors) and the actual error location is needed, possibly via manual sampling and inspection.
- Missing analysis on which step the model generates errors (SGD granularity). It would be interesting to see if manipulating the distribution of the step index where errors occur affects performance.
- No experiments showing comparison between CoT and code-integrated solutions, though the paper claims SCDPO works on both. A table highlighting performance differences between these two solution types should be added.
- Lacks experiments on generalization to out-of-distribution data; evaluation on standard benchmarks may not capture robustness to unseen problem types or domains. Out-of-distribution evaluation compared to in-distribution results is needed.
- Line 466 (Reviewer 4) and line 467 (Reviewer 5) appear to have unlinked/missing citations.
- Some "-" symbols in Table 1 are unexplained.
