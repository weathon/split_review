## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) can improve robustness against image and video corruption compared to Dense Training. The authors propose the Dynamic Sparsity Corruption Robustness (DSCR) Hypothesis and validate it through extensive experiments on CIFAR-10/100-C, ImageNet-C, ImageNet-3DCC, and UCF101, across multiple architectures (CNNs and Transformers) and three DST algorithms (e.g., MEST, GraNet, RigL). The results consistently show that DST at low sparsity (10%–50%) outperforms dense training in corruption robustness. Analysis in spectral and spatial domains suggests DST acts as an implicit regularizer, reducing attention to high-frequency components. Reviewers agree the paper provides new empirical insights but disagree on the sufficiency of experiments and technical novelty (scores: 6, 6, 8, 3). One reviewer found the experiments thorough and the problem statement clear, while another considered the contribution limited to empirical observations.

### Strengths
- Writing is easy to follow, with clear organization and an engaging structure using research questions as section headings (R1, R2).
- Problem statement is clearly narrated, tracing robustness from dense training to static sparse training to DST (R3).
- Comprehensive evaluation across multiple domains (images and videos), architectures (CNNs and Transformers), corruption types, and severity levels (R1, R3, R4).
- Analysis in Section 5 explores inner reasons for DST robustness, including spectral and spatial domain investigations (R1, R3).
- Provides new yet-unknown insights into a benefit of DST beyond efficiency, opening new possibilities (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of explanation and reproducibility details** (R1, R2):  
  - No training details (optimizer, learning rate, batch size, epochs) for ImageNet-3DCC (Table 3), UCF101 video experiments (Section 4.3), or the MLP experiment (Section 6.1, Table 2). The MLP architecture (layers, hidden dimensions, activation) is unspecified.  
  - In Section 3.1, mathematical variables are undefined (e.g., \(\mathcal{M}\) before Eq.1; \(i\) in subscript of \(p_{tr}, p_{te}\); reason for decomposing \(p_{tr}\) and \(p_{te}\) is not given).  

- **Inconsistent experimental design and undefined sparsity regimes** (R1):  
  - Sparsity ratio differs across sections (0.3–0.7 in Section 4.1 vs. 0.1 in other experiments) without justification.  
  - Motivation for GraNet’s initial density of 0.8 and MEST’s soft memory bound of 10% target density is not provided.  
  - The DSCR hypothesis uses “low sparsity regime” without a rigorous definition, making it vague and difficult to validate.

- **Missing literature review and alternative explanations** (R2):  
  - Prior work (e.g., [1]) already showed that sparse models (even at 90% sparsity) can be more OOD robust, suggesting the observed effect is not unique to DST.  
  - In Section 5.2, the claim that “low-frequency information is more important” is unsupported; an alternative explanation (e.g., aliasing reduction per [2],[3]) exists but is not discussed.  
  - Frequency properties of common corruptions are used without citing [4].

- **Limited benchmarks and missing comparisons** (R3, R4):  
  - Robustness is only evaluated on classification tasks (CIFAR-10/100-C, ImageNet-C); not extended to segmentation, pose estimation, or image matching (R3).  
  - Not tested on other robustness benchmarks (ImageNet-A/R/P) (R4).  
  - Only compared with standard dense training, not with SOTA robust training methods (e.g., AugMix, DeepAugment) or robust architectures (RVT, FAN) (R4). The augmentation used in Appendix A.6 (MixUp) is outdated; reviewers suggest including AugMix and DeepAugment (R2, R4).

- **Missing analyses of trade-offs** (R4):  
  - No analysis of whether improved robustness comes at the cost of clean accuracy.  
  - Training cost comparison (FLOPs, actual runtime) not reported. Reviewer notes that FLOPs in Appendix A.3.2 are mere estimations, not real hardware gains, and should be clarified (R2, Q2).

- **Limited technical contribution** (R4): The core contribution is an empirical observation; technical contributions are limited.

- **Unanswered questions and potential overclaim** (R1, R2):  
  - Figure 3: the five bars per corruption type lack a legend (R1).  
  - Section 5.1–5.2: why use random regrowth (instead of gradient-based) for MEST and GraNet in Figure 5 and Figure 7? (R1)  
  - What causes each DST method to perform worse on specific corruptions (e.g., single_freq in ImageNet-Ĉ)? (R1)  
  - The significance of the condition \(p_{tr}(y|x)=p_{te}(y|x)\) in Section 3.1 is unclear (R2, Q1).  
  - Conclusions in Section 5 do not hold without doubt due to plausible alternative explanations (R2).

- **Errors and inconsistencies** (R1, R2, R4):  
  - “Y-axis in Figure 3” should be “X-axis” (R1, R4).  
  - ImageNet-C caption says “generated from Tiny-ImageNet” should be “generated from ImageNet” (R1).  
  - CIFAR-100 text: “ReNet-34” should be “ResNet-34” (R1).  
  - Figure 7: right-top subcaption says “(c)” but should be “(a)” (R1, R2).
