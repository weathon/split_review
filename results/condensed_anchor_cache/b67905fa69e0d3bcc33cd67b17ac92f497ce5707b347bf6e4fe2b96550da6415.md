## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces AlignEZ, a method that uses self-generated preference data (helpful/harmful responses from the base model) and inference-time representation editing (via kNN retrieval and SVD) to align language models without training or human-annotated data. The authors claim the procedure is nearly cost-free and narrows the performance gap between pretrained and aligned models by an average of 29.1% (reviewers report 29.1%; abstract states 31.6%) across two or three architectures and multiple datasets. Additional experiments show AlignEZ can expedite DPO when only 1% of preference data is used and is orthogonal to prompting techniques like URIAL.

### Strengths
- **Resource efficiency**: Eliminates reliance on costly fine-tuning and large-scale human-annotated preference data, offering a scalable alternative (Reviewer 1).
- **Conceptual simplicity**: Straightforward approach combining self-generated data and representation editing (Reviewer 1).
- **Empirical effectiveness**: Significant performance gains over DPO baselines, especially when ground-truth data is limited (Reviewer 1, 3). Table 1 shows strong improvements over other test-time alignment baselines (Reviewer 2).
- **Complementarity with prompting**: Integrates well with prompting strategies (URIAL) and further improves alignment beyond prompting alone, enhancing practicality (Reviewer 1, 2).
- **Data efficiency**: Using only 1% preference data for DPO training, AlignEZ achieves results comparable to using 25% of such data (Reviewer 3).
- **Well-written**: The paper is clear and easy to follow (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair baseline comparison**: Test-time alignment baselines use ground-truth preference signals from a different dataset (HH-RLHF), introducing distribution shift. AlignEZ should be evaluated with the same HH-RLHF data during inference (Reviewer 2).
- **Computational cost overstated as “free”**: Self-generation of preference data and embedding modifications require non-negligible compute. Costs should be quantified relative to other methods, including prompting, and the “free” claim clarified (Reviewer 1). No details on inference latency or memory overhead compared to baselines (Reviewer 2).
- **Title misleading**: “Is Free Self-Alignment Possible?” prioritizes entertainment value; a more precise framing (e.g., “Cost-Efficient Self-Alignment…”) would better reflect contributions (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited novelty**: Both synthetic preference data and representation editing are well-established. The paper lacks a detailed exploration of how self-generated data are selected/weighted and could benefit from adaptive mechanisms to avoid confirmation bias or mode collapse (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical analysis oversimplified**: Section 3 assumes orthonormal LLM space, which is unrealistic. The conclusion is unclear about whether singular vectors are strong enough or whether kNN smoothing is adequate; sensitivity to different values of k and data distribution should be analyzed (Reviewer 1).
- **Small-scale models**: Experiments are limited to 7B/8B models, which are less representative of current state-of-the-art. Scalability to 100B+ models or larger open-source models is not shown; reliance on open-weight models restricts applicability to closed-source systems (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear methodology**: Details about the origin of queries for self-generated data are vague. It is not clear which datasets are used in Figures 3/4 or whether Δ% is averaged or from a single dataset. Implementation across datasets is insufficiently described (Reviewer 3). The 29.1% calculation is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Dependence on base LM quality**: The base LM may generate incorrect preference data; the paper does not clarify how correctness is ensured. The method is not tested on instruction-tuned LMs, and the DPO 100% condition (Figure 3) is missing (Reviewer 3). The method requires strong instruction-following ability, yet experiments are mostly on base pretrained models, not on more refined models that would better demonstrate potential (Reviewer 3). 
- **Generalizability concerns**: kNN-based feature extraction may only work when inference inputs are strongly correlated with the statistical data; out-of-distribution robustness is not addressed (Reviewer 3).
- **Narrow focus on helpfulness**: The paper does not evaluate safety, hallucination, or complex alignment scenarios combining helpfulness and safety. This limits evidence for universal applicability (Reviewer 2, 3). A practical application like personalization is also missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing ablations**: The individual effect of only “helpfulness enhancement” or only “harmfulness suppression” is not reported (Reviewer 2). The potential of using kNN-retrieved responses as in-context examples (instead of only for direction estimation) is unexplored (Reviewer 2).
- **Dataset outdated**: The datasets used are somewhat dated; experiments on more recent benchmarks or personalization would increase relevance (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation issues**: The term “DPOed” (line 303) is informal (Reviewer 3). There is a possible typo in line 239 (z_help/z_harm), and insights from Figure 4 (lowest cosine similarity in middle layers) are not explained (Reviewer 3). The paper lacks visualizations of the “help” and “harm” vector space (Reviewer 1). No head-to-head comparison with prompting alone (both cheap methods) is provided (Reviewer 1). Relationship to reward-guided test-time alignment techniques is not discussed (Reviewer 1).
