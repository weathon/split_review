## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper revisits backdoor attacks and defenses on Vision Transformers (ViTs). It claims that existing backdoor attacks are easily defeated on ViTs when defenses are properly adapted from CNNs (e.g., using AdamW instead of SGD for fine-tuning, pruning only specific linear channels). The authors then propose a more robust attack, Channel Activation Attack (CAT), which adds small adversarial perturbations to the trigger during training to minimize activation differences between benign and poisoned samples. Experiments on CIFAR-10 (four simple attack methods) and ImageNet (Badnets and Blended attacks only), across multiple ViT variants and in white-box/black-box settings, are used to demonstrate CAT’s effectiveness.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of concepts and methodologies.
- The experimental setup to demonstrate CAT’s effectiveness is well-planned.
- CAT demonstrates effectiveness in attacking ViT models and shows transferability to other ViT variants (Table 4 on CIFAR-10).
- The observation about the importance of optimizer choice (AdamW vs SGD) and limited pruning to specific linear layer channels for fine-tuning/pruning-based defenses on ViTs is highlighted.
- The authors revisit existing backdoor defense methods on ViTs and discuss their issues, providing extensive experiments to evaluate CAT.

### Weaknesses
- The fundamental observation that existing backdoor defenses fail on ViTs is based on a questionable premise. Attributing the failure to inappropriate optimizer usage (SGD instead of AdamW) is treated as an oversight rather than an inherent limitation; it would be natural to use the standard ViT optimizer (AdamW) when adapting CNN defenses to ViTs.
- The hypothesis about the role of optimizers in CNN vs. ViT backbones lacks systematic demonstration. Modifications to optimizers and epochs are derived from experimental trials, not from a principled selection method.
- Several claims are empirical but not rigorous. For example, the observed differences in activation responses could also exist for CNNs or other DNNs, limiting the paper’s scope to ViTs without justification.
- The novelty of exploiting activation differences between benign and poisoned samples is limited; this is not a new strategy in the backdoor domain.
- Experimental evaluation is insufficient:
  - On CIFAR-10, only four simple attack methods (Badnets, Blended, etc.) are evaluated. On ImageNet, results are shown only for CAT combined with Badnets and Blended attacks. A broader range of recent attack and defense methods is needed, especially on ImageNet.
  - Missing comparisons with recent baselines such as TrojViT (Zheng et al., CVPR 2023) and Data-free backdoor removal based on channel Lipschitzness (Zheng et al., ECCV 2022).
  - The improvement introduced by CAT on various ViT variants (Table 4) is modest; many previously unsuccessful attacks remain ineffective even with CAT.
  - Clean data accuracy after defense is not reported for the defended models in Table 4.
  - Stealthiness of CAT (e.g., ability to evade input-space filtering like STRIP, noise, quantization) is not evaluated, which is crucial for preventing malicious users from filtering poisoned samples.
  - No evaluation on more ViT variants (e.g., PVT, CVT, TNT) to benchmark the security issues broadly.
- Analysis of fine-tuning-based defenses is limited: why is AdamW more sensitive than SGD? Recent fine-tuning defenses (e.g., super-fine-tuning, FT-SAM) are mentioned but not evaluated.
- Most selected defenses focus on spotting differences between clean and benign inputs; input perturbation defenses (e.g., STRIP, adding noise, quantization) are missing, making the evaluation biased.
- The threat model for CAT is unclear, which weakens the security claims.
