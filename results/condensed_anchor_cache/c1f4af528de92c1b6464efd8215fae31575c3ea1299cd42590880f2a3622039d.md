## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces GridAgent, a 2D grid-based benchmark for evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) on five capabilities: execution, perception reasoning, memory, learning, and planning. The benchmark comprises 12 unique game tasks with randomized layouts and varying difficulty, designed to minimize overlap with pre-training data. Experiments show that existing MLLMs (proprietary and open-source) perform poorly on most tasks, often near random chance, and none reach human-level performance. The taxonomy is inspired by the Wechsler Intelligence Test. Two reviewers found the benchmark valuable and well-designed (scores 6), while one reviewer was more critical (score 5), citing limited insight compared to prior work and major flaws in human evaluation.

### Strengths
- **Taxonomy grounded in human cognition:** The five capabilities are derived from the Wechsler Intelligence Test, a well-established framework for evaluating human intelligence. This provides a principled basis for assessing MLLM abilities.
- **Well-structured and carefully designed tasks:** The 12 game tasks are thoughtfully constructed to target the five key capabilities, with some tasks requiring composition of multiple abilities. The benchmark is reproducible and clearly demonstrates the shortcomings of current MLLMs on complex visual reasoning.
- **Randomized layouts for generalization:** Each game layout is randomly generated, reducing the risk of overfitting to training data and ensuring a more robust and authentic evaluation of generalization.
- **Diverse semantic environments and difficulty levels:** Tasks cover a wide range of semantic scenarios and include multiple difficulty levels, enabling a comprehensive assessment that extends beyond simple task solving and minimizes overlap with pre-training corpora.
- **Detailed empirical comparison of multiple MLLMs:** The paper evaluates most popular state-of-the-art models (both open-source and proprietary), providing clear comparative insights. Zero-shot results convincingly show that current MLLMs lack zero-shot generalization ability on these tasks.
- **Well-written and easy to follow:** The presentation is clear and the paper is accessible (noted by two reviewers; one reviewer noted structural issues in Section 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited insight compared to existing benchmarks:** One reviewer noted that prior work provides more in-depth analysis of specific capabilities (e.g., memory [1], embodied/computer control planning and execution [2–4], perception reasoning with failure modes [5]). This benchmark does not offer similarly deep analysis over and above these targeted evaluations.
- **Missing reasoning strategies and agentic frameworks:** The results only use zero-shot prompting. Chain-of-Thought, self-consistency, and agentic workflows (ReAct, Reflexion) are not evaluated, yet these are critical for multi-turn, planning-heavy tasks. This omission limits understanding of how reasoning scaffolding could improve performance.
- **Flawed human evaluation:** The human baseline is set to 1.0 for all tasks without presenting actual human scores. The evaluation used five players including two authors, who are intimately familiar with the tasks, which may bias results. No details are given on the claimed result that “any adult, once familiar with the rules, is fully capable of completing all the games.” This undermines the validity of the human comparison.
- **Unexplained poor performance of GPT-4o:** On tasks PU, PL, and SO, GPT-4o performs worse than random and worse than much smaller models. No discussion or analysis is provided for this surprising result.
- **No analysis of difficulty levels and task complexity:** The paper does not analyze how varying difficulty levels affect model performance, nor does it explore whether simpler versions of the tasks could serve as a more informative baseline. This makes it unclear whether tasks are too hard or poorly aligned with current model capabilities.
- **Lack of variability and scaling studies:** Only single trials are reported; no study of consistency across multiple runs. All evaluated models are 7B parameters or smaller, so no conclusion can be drawn about whether larger models (beyond 7B) would perform better. One reviewer explicitly requested scaling experiments.
- **Missing details on evaluation procedure:** The paper does not specify image resolution used for each model, nor whether inputs were fed step-by-step across multiple turns (for multi-action tasks). One reviewer noted that higher resolution images (e.g., for Filing) could improve perception reasoning and should be tested.
- **No in-context learning evaluation:** Only zero-shot settings are tested. Given the strong in-context learning abilities of modern MLLMs, evaluating with 1–2 examples (especially for SO, FI, PL tasks) would clarify whether tasks are inherently difficult or simply require few-shot adaptation.
- **Lack of qualitative examples:** No successful or failure examples are shown in the paper or supplementary, making it harder to understand model behavior and error modes.
- **Limited justification for task selection:** The paper does not thoroughly discuss why specific games were chosen and how they comprehensively cover all five intended capabilities. The connection between each task and the Wechsler Intelligence Test is insufficiently explained.
- **Structural and presentation issues:** One reviewer noted that Section 5 (Methodology) is confusingly organized, with poor logical flow. The explanation of metrics for tasks like Perception Reasoning lacks clarity in relating performance scores to human cognition benchmarks.
- **Incomplete model coverage:** Missing results for the latest Gemini models (noted by one reviewer). For completeness, these should be included.
- **One reviewer found the contribution modest** (score 2 for Soundness and Contribution) due to limited novelty over prior benchmarks and the outlined flaws, while the other two reviewers considered the benchmark valuable (scores 3 for Soundness and Contribution). This disagreement is reflected in the overall scores (5 vs. 6).
