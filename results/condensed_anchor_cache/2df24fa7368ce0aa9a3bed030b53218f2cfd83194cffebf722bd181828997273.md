## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ProfileBFN, an adaptation of Bayesian Flow Networks (BFN) for generative modeling of protein families. ProfileBFN extends discrete BFN to MSA profiles, enabling training on single sequences (treated as degenerate profiles) and avoiding large-scale MSA construction. The method is novel and explores an underexplored region of sequence-based protein design. Results are competitive: generated sequences capture structural contacts better than standard MSA-based methods, and predicted structures resemble native structures. The model finds a niche between quality and novelty on the Pareto frontier. However, evaluations are not clearly described, mathematical presentation lacks rigor in places, and claims about practical efficiency are questioned. One reviewer (score 6) is more critical of presentation and missing comparisons; three reviewers (scores 8) are positive about novelty and experimental breadth.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: ProfileBFN introduces a new architecture (BFN adapted to categorical protein data) and explores sequence-based family design, which is generally underexplored.
- **Competitive performance**: Results are on par with or surpass existing methods in several metrics; achieves SOTA on some measurements (Reviewer 3). The method sits between quality and novelty of generated structures (Reviewer 2).
- **Multiple validation sources**: The paper includes structure prediction, enzyme function prediction, and function prediction benchmarks (Reviewer 1).
- **Clear mathematical derivation**: Emphasis on theoretical components (Reviewer 4); good introduction to BFNs in Section 2.2 (Reviewer 3).
- **Elegant algorithm design**: Training on profiles rather than raw MSAs reduces computation and enables single-sequence training; derivation of simplified loss using squared difference between predicted latent embedding and encoded representation (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation methodology is not clearly described**: 
  - Table 1 (structural metrics like LR P@L): how structures are predicted is never stated; CAMEO is an evaluation set, not a method. Authors must specify whether AlphaFold or contact prediction is used. (Reviewer 2)
  - Table 2 (fraction of enzymes considered functional): assessment method is not mentioned; if a prediction tool is used, it must be identified. (Reviewer 2)
  - Table 3 (function prediction accuracy): how accuracies are computed is unspecified. (Reviewer 2)
  - These details currently appear only in the appendix; definitions of nearly every metric (e.g., how enzymatic activity is predicted) should be in the main text. (Reviewer 1)
- **Mathematical presentation lacks rigor**:
  - Line 170: dimensions are given for the summation symbol, which does not make sense (summation is an operator). (Reviewer 3)
  - Variable reuse is confusing: sigma is used for both Kronecker delta and Dirac delta functions. (Reviewer 3)
  - Terms PMF and phi appear undefined (phi appears in equation 3). (Reviewer 3)
  - The introduction of BFNs is too colloquial; the generative process and variational lower bound should be described formally (e.g., “VLB stands for variational lower bound”). (Reviewer 1)
  - The relationship between the sampling process (denoising a profile) and the generative process (claimed to output profiles) is not rigorously justified. (Reviewer 1)
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**:
  - Prior family-based models (PoET, ProtMamba) are understated and should be clearly cited and discussed in the introduction and related works; the paper should present an argument for why ProfileBFN might outperform these methods despite not training on family data. (Reviewer 1)
  - Missing citation for ESM-2 (the paper introducing the model used for some evaluations). (Reviewer 3)
  - Table 3 bolding of ProfileBFN results is misleading when it does not beat the baselines. (Reviewer 1)
  - Why was mutation effect prediction on ProteinGym not benchmarked, given claims of “understanding”? (Reviewer 1)
- **Insufficient experimental details**:
  - For Table 2, representative sequences from all models should be released; diversity and similarity to the conditioned family should be compared to the real family, not just reported as “novel.” (Reviewer 1)
  - Clarify how much of the protein family (e.g., how many sequences) was passed to PoET for evaluation. (Reviewer 1)
  - Specify which protein families were used in evaluations; is there a dependency on MSA depth? Challenging regions like antibody loops (due to complex variation) were not tested. (Reviewer 4)
  - Hyperparameter choices (lines 266–267) are not explained. (Reviewer 3)
- **Claim of de-novo design not substantiated**: Generated sequences closely resemble native sequences (high sequence identity), yet the paper claims suitability for de-novo design; authors should clarify how the method supports truly novel sequences or functions. (Reviewer 4)
- **Introduction mispositions the method**: “Protein family design” is presented as a third way contrasting with de novo design and directed evolution, but in practice libraries from de novo are optimized via directed evolution. The statement “directed evolution produces proteins too similar to wild type” lacks empirical backing. The method should be placed in a library→optimization pipeline, and compared to PoET and EvoDiff in the introduction. (Reviewer 1)
- **Claim about bypassing MSA training is oversimplified**: The statement “Consequently, we can bypass the need to construct an MSA profile training set” is more efficient for the authors (who avoid curation) but not necessarily for practitioners; authors should simply curate a training dataset as other model developers have done. (Reviewer 1)
- **Minor interpretation issue**: The correlation shown in “Sampling Process Reflects Protein Conservation” (between conservation and transition rate) is not convincingly shown to be expected or interesting. (Reviewer 1)
