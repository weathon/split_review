## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes GeN (Generalized Newton’s Method), a Hessian-informed technique that dynamically selects the learning rate for any base optimizer (e.g., SGD, Adam). It fits a quadratic to the loss evaluated at two additional forward passes along the search direction and computes the optimal step analytically. GeN generalizes Newton’s method by allowing the descent direction to come from any first-order optimizer. The method is simple, requires no extra backward passes, and can amortize its overhead by computing the learning rate infrequently. Experiments span image classification (CIFAR-10/100, iNat, ImageNet with ResNet-18/152, ViT-L), segmentation/detection, GPT-2 language modeling, and convex test functions. The authors include a convergence analysis, error bounds, and a discussion of computational trade-offs.

### Strengths

- **Conceptual simplicity and clarity** (R1, R2, R4): The method is well motivated from a quadratic-interpolation perspective. Figure 2 provides an effective summary. The algorithm is easy to implement and modify.
- **Computational efficiency** (R1, R2, R4): GeN avoids expensive Hessian-vector products or second-order preconditioners. The overhead from two extra forward passes can be amortized over many iterations. The authors are honest about the extra compute required and discuss strategies to minimize it (e.g., infrequent updates, EMA smoothing).
- **Broad and rigorous empirical evaluation** (R1, R2, R3, R4): The method is tested on a diverse set of modern tasks and models, including GPT-2, ResNet-152, and ViT-L. Performance on large networks is promising. The inclusion of convex test functions offers additional insight. In several cases GeN matches or outperforms carefully tuned baselines, particularly where baseline tuning is less aggressive.
- **Honest limitations and practical guidance** (R1, R2): The paper discusses settings where the method may underperform and provides practical advice on mitigating overhead (infrequent updates, EMA). This transparency is valued.
- **Theoretical grounding** (R3, R4): The convergence analysis and error estimation of the optimal learning rate (e.g., dependence on batch size) provide useful insight into when the method is most likely to succeed.
- **Ability to recover from poor initial step sizes** (R2): Experiments show that GeN can correct a bad initial guess, which is a practical advantage over fixed schedules.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing related work and novelty concerns** (R1, R2, R3, R4): The paper initially positioned GeN as a novel method, but several prior works use the same quadratic-interpolation approach for adaptive learning rate selection. One reviewer (R3) notes that the LQA method (Zhu et al.) implements Algorithm 1 with SGD, and that QLAB (Fu & Wu, 2023) and ADLER (Balboni & Bacciu, 2023) are directly relevant. Other missing references include parameter-free methods (e.g., coin betting by Orabona & Tommasi, 2017). The authors have since revised to acknowledge these, but the lack of a thorough comparison (e.g., wall-clock time vs. QLAB/ADLER) remains. Another reviewer (R1) notes the related work is relegated to the appendix and lacks stochastic second-order and sketching methods; the comparison with D-Adaptation is insufficient.
- **Mismatched naming**: One reviewer (R3) finds the name "Generalized Newton's Method" confusing because GeN does not compute a second-order preconditioner; it only uses a quadratic approximation for the step size. This is not a Newton-type method in the traditional sense.
- **Short training schedules** (R1, R4): Vision experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and iNat are run for only 5 or 10 epochs (R1, R4). Common practice uses many more epochs (e.g., 200 for CIFAR-10 with ResNet). This makes it difficult to judge asymptotic performance or compare fairly against baseline schedulers that are tuned for longer runs. It is unclear whether GeN would maintain its advantage over longer training (R1).
- **Unclear experimental setup and missing details** (R1, R4): The segmentation/detection experiments (Penn-Fudan vs. COCO, use of pretrained components, the five losses used) are not described clearly (R1). For CIFAR-10 (Figure 8), it is unclear whether models are trained from scratch or fine-tuned, and whether the learning rate for AdamW (1e-4) was properly tuned (R4). The number of iterations in Figure 8 (10,000, corresponding to ~5 epochs) is far short of typical 200‑epoch training (R4). No ImageNet from-scratch results are provided (R4).
- **Algorithmic concerns** (R2): There is no verification that the step size computed from the quadratic actually decreases the loss. Classical line searches guarantee descent; here the method relies on the loss being locally quadratic enough. Given high nonconvexity, a "bad" step could increase loss (R2). The motivation for using an EMA of learning rates instead of the direct optimum η* is not fully explained (R2). The number of sampled points is inconsistent: Algorithm 1 uses three, Appendix C.2 recommends five, and Figure 2 shows 10–11 points. Sensitivity to this hyperparameter is not explored (R2).
- **Clarity of presentation** (R2, R3, R4): Notation is confusing in places (bold capitals for both vectors and matrices in Eq. 2.2, ratio of quadratic forms in Eq. 3.2) (R2). The derivation of the optimal learning rate could be laid out more clearly; e.g., showing the form of the quadratic coefficients before the final expression Eq. 3.3 (R3). Figure 2 needs clarification of what "g_t^(optim)" refers to and which optimizer is used (R4). Figure 4 shows sudden instability and a surprising accuracy gap between ResNet-18 and ResNet-152 for GeN-SGD; baseline runs with plain SGD/AdamW are missing (R4). Figures 6 and 7 should use consistent iteration counts (R4). Figure 8 legends should be placed below the plots (R4).
- **Computational overhead discussion** (R4): The claim that GeN is “roughly 60% as fast” needs a more precise derivation, especially regarding the constant c ≈ 5 in the forward/backward complexity B ≈ cF. The authors should incorporate references (e.g., Griewank, 2008; Bolte et al., 2023) and discuss how the speed estimate changes with the exact ratio (R4). A wall-clock time comparison (e.g., iterations vs. time) would be more informative, especially on distributed systems (R4).
- **Missing ablations and baselines** (R1, R2, R4): The hyperparameters for baseline optimizers (AdamW, SGD) should be justified, ideally by a grid search (R4). The number of sampled points in Algorithm 1 is a sensitive hyperparameter that is not ablated (R2). The error analysis (Appendix A.4) is presented but not empirically validated on small models where η* can be computed exactly (R2). The DCGAN experiments (appendix) are of poor quality and unlikely to be informative for the bilevel GAN objective (R3). Different initializations for convex test functions (Figures 6, 7) should be tested to show robustness (R4).
- **Minor issues** (R2): Typo on line 208 ("the gradient descent" instead of "gradient descent"); a likely typo in the grid in line 842 (C10^{-k} with k=-5…0 would give learning rates around 10^5).
