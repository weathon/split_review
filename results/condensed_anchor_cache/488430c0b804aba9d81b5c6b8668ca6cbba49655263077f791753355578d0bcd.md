## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a low-parametric training method for convolutional neural networks that factorizes convolutions into Tucker tensor format and adaptively prunes the Tucker ranks during training. The approach leverages geometric integration theory of differential equations on tensor manifolds. Experiments on CIFAR-10 with VGG and AlexNet are presented, showing reduced training cost and competitive accuracy compared to full training and other low-rank baselines.

### Strengths
- The problem of reducing training cost for convolutional layers is relevant and useful, as most prior low-rank training methods focus on fully connected layers (R1,R2).
- The paper is well-written, with a clear motivation and nice introduction to the problem (R3).
- The connection to recent advances in dynamic low-rank approximation and the use of geometric integration is interesting and a promising direction (R3).
- The theoretical derivation and convergence analysis are strong; the paper provides a clear derivation of TDLRT and its convergence properties (R3). (Note: R1 disagrees, finding the theoretical analysis limited and not guaranteeing loss convergence.)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty is limited**: The method is an incremental extension of matrix DLRT [1] to tensor format using Tucker decomposition. The core idea of dynamic rank adjustment via differential equations remains the same, and the adaptation to tensors does not fundamentally alter the principle. Tucker decomposition is a standard technique (R2,R3). A minority reviewer (R3) considers the extension not significant enough to be a novel contribution.
- **Missing training cost analysis**: The paper lacks a detailed analysis of computational overhead per iteration (cost of QR, SVD, Tucker decomposition, gradient, adaptive rank selection) compared to standard SGD and baselines. It is insufficient to only report iterations to convergence; cost per iteration must be factored in. Training times are not reported, and Table 1 does not show total training cost. Figure 2 only shows inference computational footprint, but for a dynamical compression method, training cost is more important (R1,R2,R3). A figure analogous to [1] showing training cost across rank values is needed (R3).
- **Insufficient theoretical guarantees**: Theorem 2 does not indicate convergence of the loss function; it only shows the method stays close to the manifold. There is no guarantee that loss converges to a local minimum, and no comparison of convergence rate with standard SGD or other low-rank methods (R1).
- **Limited experimental evaluation**: Evaluation is only on CIFAR-10 with VGG16 and AlexNet, which are considered small-scale and outdated. Stronger evidence requires experiments on ImageNet with at least ResNet50 to demonstrate practical scalability (R3). The current evaluation is insufficient (R3).
- **Lack of comparison with alternative tensor decompositions**: The paper does not consider or compare against tensor-train decomposition, which avoids a core tensor and uses fewer ranks. Adaptive rank learning with Riemannian geometry has been explored in that context (R2, citing Oseledets 2011, Steinlechner 2016). It is unclear why Tucker is preferable (R2).
- **Missing details on important implementation choices**: 
  - Initialization of low-rank factors and its impact on performance are not discussed (R3).
  - The threshold for rank adaptation is not explained; how it affects the compression-accuracy trade-off is unclear (R3).
  - The frequency of basis augmentation and SVD operations is not specified; frequent SVD may cause numerical instability, especially when gradient perturbations on the core tensor are small compared to SVD error in later training stages (R3).
  - More details on how the rank evolves during training and convergence behavior under different compression thresholds are needed (R3).
- **Method complexity**: TDLRT is more complicated than vanilla SGD, requiring QR decomposition, projections, etc. This additional overhead makes it unclear how computationally efficient the method is in practice (R1).
