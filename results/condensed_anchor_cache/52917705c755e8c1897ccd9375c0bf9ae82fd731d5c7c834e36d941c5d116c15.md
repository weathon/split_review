## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Binary Sparsity Alignment (BSA), a method that uses a soft TopK Cosine Loss to convert dense embeddings into sparse binary representations. The approach aims to avoid rigid sparsity constraints, yield flexible activation distributions, and eliminate dead features without special re‑activation strategies. Validation is performed on a dataset of biomedical concept embeddings, achieving 14x compression. The method is sensible but limited in scope, lacks thorough experimental validation, and ignores a substantial part of the sparse‑coding literature.

### Strengths
- **Novel cosine‑based loss for simultaneous sparsification and binarization.** The soft TopK Cosine Loss treats sparsity as an alignment problem, leading to stable training (no dead features) and a flexible distribution of activations. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Narrow, rarely addressed topic.** The focus on Binary Sparsity Alignment is distinctive. (Reviewer 1, more positive, score 3)
- **Validated on a biomedical dataset.** The method demonstrates effective sparsification with minimal information loss and achieves 14x compression. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Numerical experiments confirm implementation works as designed.** The correlation between binary and top‑k representations behaves as expected. (Reviewer 3, more negative overall, but acknowledges this)

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental validation.**
  - Only one domain (biomedical concepts) is tested; no evaluation on other datasets. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
  - No downstream task evaluation (e.g., classification, retrieval), making it unclear whether the compressed embeddings preserve performance. (Reviewer 2)
  - No baseline comparison against other sparsification methods (e.g., l₁‑based or other binary embedding techniques). (Reviewers 2, 3)
  - No ablation study on hyperparameters (loss weighting, batch size, etc.). (Reviewer 2)
  - No analysis of training time or computational requirements. (Reviewer 2)
  - No demonstration on a real problem often targeted by sparse representations (e.g., denoising). (Reviewer 3)
- **Poor presentation.**
  - Equations are not commonly found in the literature, making them hard to follow; paper organization needs improvement. (Reviewer 1)
  - Tables appear to be screenshots from Excel rather than LaTeX. (Reviewer 2)
  - Several typos: “possess” (line 281), “auto encoders … in” (line 84), and a syntactically wrong sentence (line 309). (Reviewer 3)
- **Limited literature context and theoretical depth.**
  - The relationship to contrastive learning (Oord et al., 2018) is not thoroughly discussed. (Reviewer 1)
  - The relaxation of sparsity via cosine similarity with top‑k is a strange choice; the l₁ penalty is the convex relaxation of binary sparse coding (Mairal and Bach), yet this literature is barely mentioned. (Reviewer 3)
  - The method is incremental and lacks an analytical proof of any interesting property. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Sanity‑check experiments.** Showing that the binary and top‑k representations correlate is obvious because the cosine similarity is optimized in the objective. (Reviewer 3)
- **Unclear scalability and practical benefits.** Questions remain about how the approach scales to larger dimensions, how it benefits efficient storage and fast inference, and how it enhances interpretability. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 3) found the narrow topic and validation on biomedical data to be clear strengths, but still noted insufficient experiments and poor organization. The other two reviewers (scores 1) were more critical, questioning the method’s novelty, literature depth, and overall evaluation. All reviewers agree that more comprehensive experiments, baselines, and real applications are needed.
