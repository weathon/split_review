## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviewers assessed the paper with ratings 6, 8, and 3, indicating strong disagreement. All agree the problem of relaxing equal‑variance assumptions for linear DAG learning is relevant. One reviewer (R2) is very positive, highlighting the generality of the proposed regularization and extensive experiments. Another (R1) finds the work clearly written and empirically sound but criticizes limited novelty (only borrowing concomitant lasso) and confinement to linear models. The third (R3) is strongly negative, questioning theoretical guarantees, experimental rigor, and the method’s ability to handle heteroscedasticity. Key weaknesses across reviewers include lack of identifiability proofs, unclear derivations, scalability limitations, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and contributions are easy to digest (R1).
- The proposed score leads to structure improvements over state‑of‑the‑art methods across several settings, including larger DAGs and heteroscedastic noise (R1, R2).
- The problem of relaxing equal‑error‑variance assumptions in continuous DAG learning is important and relevant to the community (R2, R3).
- The proposed regularization is general and can be plugged into many existing differentiable DAG methods, giving the work potentially high impact (R2).
- The empirical analysis is extensive, covering graph recovery and noise estimation on simulated and real data, with results well supporting the claims (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The core idea directly borrows from concomitant lasso; the only technical change is adding noise‑variance terms to the score, and the optimization is nearly identical to prior work (R1). Minor: one reviewer questions what “decouples the sparsity parameter from the exogenous noise levels” means, as the notion is not elaborated (R3).
- **Only linear models**: The method is restricted to linear SEMs, which limits real‑world applicability; no extension or discussion for non‑linear models is provided (R1). R1 explicitly asks whether the authors experimented with non‑linear models (weakness: not addressed).
- **Lack of identifiability guarantees**: The formulation for the heteroscedastic setting (Equation 5) lacks theoretical identification guarantees—it is unclear under which assumptions the method recovers the true DAG (R1, R3). The discussion of identifiability in Appendix C is not rigorous: the argument using λ=0 and μₖ→∞ is inconsistent with the barrier method (μₖ→0) and contradicts the requirement of sparsity when λ=0 (R3).
- **Scalability issue**: The closed‑form update for noise scales uses the full dataset, as it is not decomposable; this limits application to large‑scale data, and no approximation is discussed or evaluated (R2).
- **Missing derivation details**: The derivation of Problem 2 is not clearly explained; the assumptions under which noise‑dependent terms appear should be stated explicitly, and a full derivation in the appendix would improve clarity and reproducibility (R2).
- **Hyperparameter tuning not detailed**: The tuning procedure for λ is only mentioned as “empirically determined”; the range of values and selection criteria are not reported, harming reproducibility (R2).
- **Incomplete baseline reporting**: Results for Sortnregress are not fully presented in Figure 1 (only two noise‑scale values for one graph type are shown); all settings should be included for fair comparison (R2).
- **Performance drop after data standardization**: In heteroscedastic settings, CoLiDE shows a significant drop in performance after data standardization (Appendix E.4), contrasting with methods like GES/PC that are less sensitive. This suggests the method may not fully address heteroscedasticity (R3). R3 explicitly asks whether the method works after standardization (weakness: it does poorly in that setting).
- **Missing comparisons and experimental fairness concerns**:
  - For heteroscedastic Gaussian noise, the paper should compare Markov equivalence classes (not DAGs) because the true DAG is not identifiable under Gaussian assumptions (R3).
  - For DAGMA: the authors did not test a log‑likelihood variant in the heteroscedastic linear setting, although DAGMA paper uses such a variant for nonlinear models (R3).
  - For GOLEM: it is unclear whether the EV version was used to initialize the NV version as recommended, and whether hyperparameters were tuned for the heteroscedastic settings considered here, which differ from the original GOLEM paper (R3).
  - R1 notes that non‑equal noise variances were already studied by Loh & Buhlmann (2014) with a weighted least‑squares approach; one reviewer wonders if jointly optimizing a similar objective would also improve accuracy, and the paper does not compare to this line of work (R1, weakness: missing comparison).
