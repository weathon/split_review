## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper revisits spectral GNN approaches to semi-supervised node classification (SSNC) and argues that many current GNN architectures are over‑engineered. The authors propose simpler spectral methods (low‑rank SVD of the adjacency matrix followed by kernel filtering) and claim they can reach state‑of‑the‑art performance. Additionally, they argue that some recent GNN improvements are partly due to shifts in evaluation conventions. The reviewers are split: one (R2, rating 5) finds the work original, well‑executed, and significant, while another (R1, rating 1) sees little novelty and insufficient evidence; the other two (R3, R4, both rating 3) are neutral but raise several technical and expositional concerns.

### Strengths
- The paper challenges the prevailing notion that complex GNN architectures are necessary, advocating for simpler traditional methods in the spectral domain (R1, R2).
- Using kernel functions (linear, SBF, Gaussian RBF, etc.) to filter singular values is an intriguing approach (R4).
- A thorough ablative study on hyperparameters (propagation structures, spectral truncation, kernel choices) is presented; experimental results show the proposed simpler models perform on par with or better than current state‑of‑the‑art on several SSNC benchmarks (R2).
- The demonstration that recent GNN performance improvements may be partially attributable to shifts in evaluation conventions is a notable insight for follow‑up research (R4).
- The paper is well‑written and clearly organized (R2).
- It highlights the potential for more interpretable GNN models and provides useful heuristics for SSNC problems (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and insight**: The paper lacks significant novelty; applying nonlinearity to the spectrum is not new, and the specific simplification techniques or new insights are unclear. Several details of the pipeline are insufficiently explained (e.g., how the kernel matrix \(K\) and its eigenvectors \(\alpha\) are computed, the iterative update scheme, the process for spectral truncation). (R1, R3, R4)
- **Experimental breadth**: The experiments are limited. Harder, larger graphs, directed networks, and multiple‑layer GNN comparisons are missing. The evaluated graphs are not large enough to support the claim of low‑rank simplification effectiveness at scale. The performance gaps between the proposed method and existing approaches are still substantial on many benchmark cases. (R1, R3)
- **Time complexity**: The overall method incurs high cost, especially the low‑rank SVD of the adjacency matrix. This is a concern for large graphs and no efficiency comparison is provided. (R4, R3)
- **Lack of theoretical grounding**: No rigorous theoretical analysis is given to justify the empirical findings. A deeper analytical understanding of why such a straightforward spectral method works would strengthen the work. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Unexplained results**: Figure 3 shows a counter‑intuitive trend (expressive capability does not monotically increase with rank), but the authors do not explain its underlying cause. (R4, also R3 notes the results seem like common sense but unclear what new interest they reveal.)
- **Missing code**: The statement that code will be made publicly available is insufficient; code should be included for reproducibility during review. (R2)
- **Hyperparameter and kernel selection**: The rationale for choosing specific kernels (identity, linear, compact/unbounded Sobolev, Gaussian RBF) is unexplained, and the tuning of the SBF kernel is not described. (R3, R4)
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: Notation is not unified; there are multiple typos. A high‑level sketch of the roles of each experimental section (Sec. 3–5) would help readability. (R1, R3)
- **Comparison with related methods**: The difference with kernel spectral methods (e.g., weighted KPCA, asymmetric Nyström) is not discussed. (R3)
