## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CLIPSelector, a method that uses the vision-language model CLIP for sample selection in learning with noisy labels, aiming to mitigate self-confirmation bias. It combines CLIP’s zero-shot classifier with an induced classifier from its vision encoder and noisy labels. A semi-supervised method MixFix is introduced to gradually incorporate clean and re-labeled noisy samples. Experiments are conducted on synthetic and real-world noise benchmarks.

### Strengths
- The idea of using vision-language models (e.g., CLIP) to address the self-confirmation bias in noisy label learning is reasonable, interesting, and breaks new ground for this problem (R1, R4; R2 agrees it addresses an important problem for ML deployment).
- The approach is modular; the auxiliary foundation model for sample selection can be substituted with a stronger model in the future (R2).
- Fixed hyperparameters across all experiments demonstrate robustness and practicality (R4).
- Hyper-parameter experiments (including on different thresholds) are useful for readers (R2).
- Discussion about the method’s applicability and its shortcomings on granularly labeled datasets is welcome (R2).
- The paper (including algorithm and experimental description) is generally clear and well-presented (R1, R3). Experiments cover a variety of datasets (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair comparisons and missing baselines:** The notable performance improvements in Tables 2–4 may be largely due to CLIP’s superior representation learning, not the proposed method. A fairer comparison would replace baselines’ backbones with CLIP’s visual encoder (R4). Relatedly, comparing methods trained from scratch with a CLIP-based approach is unfair; combining previous methods with CLIP pre-training should also be examined (R3). Table 3 lacks comparisons with recent instance-dependent noise methods from 2022–2023 (R4). Some SOTA methods (e.g., Unicon, PES, SSR) are either outperformed by the proposed method (R1 notes inferiority) or not compared at all (R3 misses SSR). The meaning of “SOTA” in Table 1 is ambiguous and needs clarification (R4).
- **Insufficient novelty:** Using CLIP’s zero-shot classifier for sample selection is a reformulation of the standard CLIP zero-shot process (with the only addition being a different prompt template, Eq. 4). Semi-supervised learning for noisy labels has been applied in prior work (R2, R3). The claim of novelty is overstated (R3).
- **Poor writing and incomplete experimental reporting:** The paper is overly complex with inconsistent notation. Theorems 1 and 2 are unnecessary for the main text; the bound’s tightness is unknown, limiting their contribution (R2). Important details (e.g., hyper-parameter ablations on \(\theta_r\)) are relegated to the appendix (R2). Section 4.1 is confusingly placed before results, and the first paragraph of Section 1 is hard to parse (R2). Ambiguous statements such as “on the clean test set of in-question noisy datasets” should be clarified (R4). There are typos (e.g., “we consistent the notations” in Sec. 3.2; Eq. 2 misses a closing parenthesis) (R2, R3).
- **Missing qualitative analysis:** No qualitative results or analysis are provided to explain why the method performs better, or to characterize the difference between “easy noise” and “hard noise” (R2). Claims of inferior performance on Red Mini-ImageNet need more context and explanation (R4).
- **Insufficient methodological clarity and justification:** The second classifier used to estimate \(\tilde{p}_{y|x}\) appears similar to estimating \(p_{y|x}\) with noisy labels; it is unclear how a classifier learned with noisy data can estimate the clean probability (R1). Specific implementation details for inducing this classifier from CLIP’s vision encoder using noisy labels are not fully elaborated; the training procedure and loss functions need clarification (R3). The derivation of Eq. 4 from Eq. 3 is not explained, and the effect of the added class feature in the prompt remains unclear; additional ablation studies are needed (R4). The sample selection mechanism (Eq. 1) takes as input predicted probability and the label—clarify (R2).
- **Concerns with using CLIP:** CLIP’s zero-shot estimate is a standard reformulation, not new; multiple prompt generation uses another language model whose biases are not accounted for in the main text (R2). Since the original CLIP training set is not accessible, biases present in CLIP are unknown and could be problematic (R2). Discrepancies between CLIP zero-shot results on CIFAR in Table 1 and the original paper need explanation (R4).
- **Missing noise model specification:** The paper refers to “synthetic symmetric/asymmetric noise” without defining the noise model (R2). The statement “label noise normally does not affect the sample itself” (Appendix F) is questionable because label noise can be highly sample-dependent (R2).
