## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CNS-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating image classifier robustness under continuous nuisance shifts. It uses LoRA adapters applied to Stable Diffusion to generate images with gradual severity of 14 nuisance shifts (e.g., snow, fog, cartoon). The authors evaluate various classifiers (ResNet, ViT, ConvNeXt, CLIP, DINO, MAE, MoCo, DeiT) and analyze failure points, OOC filtering, and the relation between ID and OOD accuracy. They claim that generative models enable controlled, continuous nuisance shifts that reveal model failure points beyond averaged metrics.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-structured and provides comprehensive discussion along architecture, number of parameters, and pre-training paradigm. (R1)
- The application of LoRA adapters to diffusion models for generating continuous nuisance shifts is simple, creative, and effective. (R1, R3, R4)
- The experimental evaluation covers multiple classifier families (8+ models), offering useful insights into robustness behavior. (R3, R4)
- Measuring precise failure points at specific severity levels (analogous to psychophysical thresholds) is a valuable approach, enabling finer-grained analysis than average accuracy. (R1, R2, R4)
- The collection of an annotated dataset for out-of-concept (OOC) filtering and the novel filtering mechanism are notable contributions. (R1, R5)
- The authors promise to release the dataset under a permissive license (CC-BY-4.0) and provide code. (R4)
- The technical approach is sound, with thorough sanity-checks for filtering and generation quality. (R5)

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear novelty and contribution relative to prior multi-severity benchmarks.** Multiple reviewers note that ImageNet-C (Hendrycks & Dietterich 2018) and Kar et al. (2022) already provide multiple severity levels. R2 and R4 argue that continuous shifts can be achieved with parametric distortions (e.g., Gaussian noise), and R5 states the contribution is effectively interpolation, yet the importance of continuous severity is not convincingly demonstrated—especially since the analysis uses only 5 discrete levels. R1 finds the insights (e.g., Figure 6) could be more compelling with deeper mechanistic explanations. Reviewer 4 strongly questions the main claim ("Is it about continuous shifts? ... this can be achieved with parametric distortions"), while Reviewer 5 expresses skepticism about the relevance of continuous levels unless model ordering changes drastically—which is not clearly the case. Reviewer 1, however, views the approach as innovative. The paper lacks a clear articulation of what is uniquely enabled by generative continuous shifts that cannot be done with existing parametric or dataset-based corruptions.

2. **Realism and confounds of generated images.** (a) R4 shows that nuisance shifts introduce confounds: "in dust" removes half the people and changes shirt color (Fig 23); "in rain" blurs background without adding rain (Fig 22); thus failures cannot be attributed solely to the nuisance concept. R1 questions unrealistic scenarios (cars driving on water). (b) R2 requests human assessment validation for realism. (c) R3 finds that the fine-tuning experiment (ResNet-50 fine-tuned on CNS-Bench gives >10% gain on ImageNet-R but -2.04% on IN/val) does not fully support the claim of realism, and asks whether fine-tuning used the entire CNS-Bench or a specific style. (d) R4 warns about a real-vs-synthetic distribution shift confound that is not analyzed. (e) R5's failure cases (Fig 16) raise reliability concerns about the method.

3. **Calibration and comparability of severity across shifts and classes.** R5 asks whether severity scale 2.5 for one class/shift (e.g., elephant in snow) is equivalent to scale 2.5 for another (zebra in fog), given separate LoRAs per class. R3 notes that accuracy-drop comparisons are problematic because model sizes and ImageNet validation accuracies differ; requests parameter counts and IN/val accuracies. R3 also points to metric inconsistency: Figures 4-5 use accuracy, Figure 6 uses accuracy drop—suggest standardization.

4. **Clarity and completeness of method description.** R3 finds the failure point definition in Section 3.2 confusing (overuse of symbols: X_n(S), X(s_n), X_n(S_n), subscripts s_n, c_n). R3 and R5 note that understanding LoRA slider scales requires reading Gandikota et al. 2023—the paper is not self-contained. R3 requests theoretical/empirical justification for activating LoRA for only the last 75% of noise steps. R3 asks for details on the claim that "alignment increases in 73% for scales s > 0". R5 reports that the provided benchmark.zip file was corrupted and could not be inspected.

5. **Statistical stability and evaluation details.** R5 requests confidence intervals for Figures 3, 6a, 6b, 6c, especially since Figures 6b and 6c average over fewer trials. R4 asks about robustness of failure points to random variation. R3 questions the rationale for using the slope of the linear fit in the ID vs OOD experiment (Section 4.3) instead of the linear correlation coefficient, and requests more detailed analysis of Figure 7.

6. **Evaluation method concerns.** R2 highlights an inconsistency: line 451 claims transformers are more robust than CNNs, but Figure 6a shows ConvNext (CNN) outperforming ViT and DeiT, contradicted only by DeiT3. R2 also questions the fairness of DINOv1 using linear probing while other models use full finetuning. R4 requests a single overall benchmark score (e.g., average accuracy or accuracy drop) so users can easily identify SOTA.

7. **Scalability and computational cost.** R4 argues that training separate LoRA adapters for each ImageNet class and shift may not be more scalable than existing methods, and asks about the computational cost of generating the dataset. R5 implicitly questions scalability.

8. **Selection of nuisances.** R2 states that the rationale for choosing the specific 14 nuisance shifts is not explained in the main text (only in appendix), weakening the foundational contribution. R4 asks why 14 and not 50.

9. **Missing experiments and analyses.** (a) Can different shifts be combined simultaneously (e.g., fog and snow)? (R5) (b) Is accuracy always monotonic with LoRA slider strength? R4 asks about cases where images beyond the failure point are again correctly recognized. (c) R2 notes that the third contribution (annotated dataset for filtering) is not visible in the main text. (d) R3 requests details on the alignment claim. (e) R5 notes the legend of Figure 7 is broken (scale 1.5 appears twice; colors reused from Figure 6 with different associations; legend not sorted). (f) R2 and R3 note figure readability issues (small font).
