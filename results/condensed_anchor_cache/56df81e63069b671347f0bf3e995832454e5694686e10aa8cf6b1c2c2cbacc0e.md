## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a topological data analysis framework based on zigzag persistence to study internal representations of large language models. It introduces a “persistence similarity” descriptor that tracks the evolution of topological features (e.g., p-cycles) across layers and applies this similarity to prune redundant layers. Reviewers generally find the application novel and the exposition clear, but raise major concerns about the originality of the technical contribution (zigzag filtration is pre‑existing), the interpretability and grounding of the topological features, the lack of a clear problem statement, mathematical issues with the proposed metric, and marginal performance gains in the pruning application. One reviewer (rating 6) is notably more positive, while other ratings are 3, 3, 5, 5.

### Strengths

- The application of zigzag persistence to LLM internal representations is novel and insightful (R1, R2, R4, R5).  
- The paper is clearly written, making complex concepts accessible (R1, R2, R5).  
- Section 3 provides a rigorous introduction to zigzag persistence (R1, R3, R5).  
- Experiments cover several open‑weight models (e.g., LLaMA‑2, Mistral) across benchmark datasets (R2).  
- Pruning experiments show promising performance at 10% sparsity compared to simple baselines (R5).  
- The approach is grounded in theoretical foundations (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Originality concerns:** zigzag filtration and persistence images are pre‑existing; the contribution is a simple combination of known ideas (R1, R4). Reviewer 4 notes that TDA for neural network graphs has been done before (citing [1], [2], [3]).  
- **Marginal performance gains:** performance over existing pruning methods (Table 1) is marginal; statistical significance tests (e.g., t‑test, ANOVA) are missing (R1). At 20% sparsity the method does not outperform common techniques (R5).  
- **Interpretability unclear:** it is not explained what linguistic or semantic properties the persistent homology features correspond to (R1, R2). The insights are limited to layer‑wise similarity without grounded meaning (R2).  
- **Computational expense:** the zigzag algorithm is costly for large datasets/high‑dimensional representations; no analysis of time/memory requirements or scalability strategies is provided (R1).  
- **Missing problem statement:** the paper does not define a clear problem; the word “problem” is not found in the whole paper (R3). Section 3 (Method) describes known facts about zigzag persistence for over 3 pages without connecting to LLMs (R3).  
- **Undefined terminology:** “layer” is used 50+ times without proper definition; it is unclear whether layers are point clouds or network modules (R3). The adjective “redundant” is also undefined (R3).  
- **Mathematical flaws in persistence similarity (Equation 5):** the denominator can vanish; the similarity is asymmetric (authors note this in lines 402‑403), so the symmetry axiom fails; the triangle inequality is unlikely to hold; metric axioms are not proven (R3). The term “persistent similarity” has appeared in prior literature (Intl. Press) without citation (R3).  
- **Informal claims lack rigor:** “consistent” and “redundant” are not defined; no technical claims, propositions, or theorems are stated (R3). The claim that pruning does not “significantly” degrade performance is unsupported—accuracies in Table 1 drop by ~10% (R3).  
- **Insufficient embedding extraction details:** it is unclear whether embeddings are taken before or after normalization, and whether two embeddings per block (attention normalization and MLP normalization) are used; comparison to how prior works extract LLM representations is needed (R2).  
- **Questionable assumption on last token:** the claim that “the representation of the last token in a sequence captures information about the entire sequence” is contested; analysis of special tokens (e.g., EOS, BOS) is missing (R5).  
- **Limited experimental scope:** experiments are only on base models; no analysis of instruction‑following abilities, math, or code tasks is provided (R5).  
- **Weak pruning baselines:** comparisons are only against simple baselines (e.g., last layer, even‑odd); not compared to advanced pruning methods such as [4] and [5] (R4).  
- **Figure 5 unclear:** it is difficult to see whether 10% (orange) segments are contained within 20% (yellow) segments; a better visualization is recommended (R1).  
- **No clear use beyond pruning:** the utility of the similarity measure is only demonstrated as a pruning method; no other use scenarios are indicated (R4). If the paper claims a superior pruning method, it should be clearly stated and compared with state‑of‑the‑art (R4).  
- **Safety concern:** accuracies in Table 1 range from ~40% to 70%; Reviewer 3 questions whether these numbers are sufficient to guarantee safety in real applications.  
- **Minority positive reviewer:** Reviewer 2 (rating 6) is more positive but still acknowledges questionable utility in layer pruning and lack of interpretability (R2).
