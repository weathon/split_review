## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies spurious correlations in deep neural networks, focusing on the novel setting where spurious signals are weak and thus hard to detect. The authors propose a data pruning technique that removes training samples with hard core features—identified through sample difficulty during training—which they empirically show are primarily responsible for forming spurious correlations. Experiments are conducted on CIFAR-10S, CelebA, Hard ImageNet, Waterbirds, and MultiNLI, covering both identifiable and unidentifiable spurious features. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the paper is clearly written, but diverge in overall assessment: one reviewer rates it 10 (very positive), another 8, and two rate it 5 (more critical). Key criticisms include a lack of theoretical formulation, unclear definitions of core concepts, limited generalizability of inductive findings, missing analysis of pruning trade-offs, computational scalability concerns, insufficient comparisons with alternative robust training methods, and figures/tables that are not self-explanatory.

### Strengths
- The problem of spurious correlations is important, timely, and easy to understand (R1, R3).
- The paper identifies and addresses a less-studied scenario where spurious signals are weak, making detection and mitigation more challenging (R1, R2, R3).
- The proposed solution is intuitive and logically derived from clear step-by-step simulation experiments that demonstrate the underlying phenomenon (R1, R4).
- The writing is well-organized, clearly presented, and guides the reader through gaps in the literature and the reasoning behind the method (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Extensive experimental validation across multiple datasets (CIFAR-10S, CelebA, Hard ImageNet, Waterbirds, MultiNLI) covering both synthetic and realistic spurious correlations, with comparisons to state-of-the-art baselines (R2).
- The method does not require any explicit knowledge about the presence or nature of spurious features per sample, nor human intervention (R1).
- The paper focuses on a single well-defined approach, keeping the narrative concise and effective (R4).
- Results are reasonably well explained and the contribution is seen as novel by some reviewers (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of rigorous formulation and clear definitions.** Key concepts (e.g., spurious correlation, core/invariant features, simple/hard features, strength of signal) are not formally defined. No equations, pseudocode, or statistical model are provided. This makes the claims hard to evaluate and the method hard to reproduce (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (R3) specifically requests a self-contained problem statement, a pseudocode for the pruning algorithm, and complete experimental details (dataset processing, models, evaluation metrics).
- **Inductive reasoning limits generalizability.** The central observation that spurious correlations arise from a small subset of samples with hard core features is derived from synthetic experiments where the spurious feature is a line through images (or “men with glasses” in CelebA). It remains unclear whether this pattern holds for other types of spurious correlations, under different data distributions, or with varying architectures and optimization settings. The paper lacks theoretical justification to support the universality of this phenomenon (R1, R4). One reviewer (R1) explicitly questions whether the uniform distribution of spurious samples across the training distribution is a general property or an artifact of specific generation processes.
- **No discussion of trade-offs from pruning data.** Removing training samples may discard informative or rare examples, potentially harming overall performance. The paper does not analyze the costs or side effects of pruning (e.g., loss of diversity, increased variance) relative to methods that do not remove data (R1, R2). Reviewer 2 suggests discussing potential negative impacts and limitations.
- **Computational cost and scalability concerns.** The method relies on iterative sample difficulty estimation, which could become expensive for large datasets. No breakdown of time or memory requirements for the pruning process versus standard training is provided (R2).
- **Sensitivity of sample difficulty metric is unclear.** The robustness of difficulty scores to changes in batch size, learning rate, initialization, optimizer, and model architecture is not explored. The pruning method may be sensitive to these factors, limiting its practical reliability (R2, R3). Reviewer 2 asks explicitly about hyperparameter sensitivity and cross-architecture robustness.
- **Incomplete comparison with alternative robust training approaches.** Comparisons are limited to methods that also aim to mitigate spurious correlations (e.g., group DRO, JTT). The paper does not compare against data augmentation, gradient manipulation, or adversarial training techniques that also do not require explicit spurious feature labels. Table 1 shows some baselines outperform the proposed method; an explanation for this is missing (R2).
- **Figures and tables not self-explanatory.** For example, Figure 2 lacks definitions of “easy/hard” samples and details on how the experiments were carried out (e.g., how spurious features were added, how misclassification rate was computed). This makes it hard to understand how the evidence supports the claims (R3). Reviewer 4 also finds the setting for Figure 3 and the concept of “polynomial growth” unclear.
- **Ambiguity in “strong” vs “weak” spurious signals.** The paper conflates two distinct aspects: geometric strength (e.g., line thickness) and frequency of spurious features in the data. These should be disentangled for a cleaner analysis (R4).
- **Limited novelty for some reviewers.** One reviewer (R4) notes that the phenomenology in Figure 2 is in line with known influence functions (citing [1,2]), making the results not entirely surprising, though the perspective is new for the spurious correlation community.
- **Reproducibility concerns.** Code is not open-sourced (R2).
- **Minor issues.** Typo in line 162 (“as shown in...” unfinished) (R4). Section 6.3 (group labels) is confusing and needs a self-contained explanation (R4). One reviewer (R2) suggests extending experiments to more complex, real-world datasets with non-synthetic spurious correlations to further validate the method.
