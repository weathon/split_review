## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a pipeline to convert rigid 3D meshes into articulated objects in an open-vocabulary manner. It uses a Vision-Language Model (VLM) and visual prompting to segment object parts, geometric heuristics to estimate joint parameters, and an SDS-based optimization step (via diffusion priors) to refine geometry and texture. The pipeline is applied to AIGC meshes and hand-crafted models. The work aims to expand the coverage of articulated object datasets and shows a real-to-sim-to-robotics demonstration.

### Strengths

- The task of converting static 3D meshes into interactable articulated objects is valuable and interesting.
- The final demos that show the pipeline can guide real-to-sim-to-real transfer demonstrate the usefulness of the approach.
- The paper identifies a critical research gap in 3D generation for articulated objects and contributes to an important area.
- A novel pipeline is proposed that enables the creation of articulated objects from arbitrary input meshes (open-vocabulary).
- The algorithm is reasonable and the 3D demos are interesting.
- The use of advanced VLM and visual prompting for part segmentation and joint parameter estimation is effective.
- The integration of all three modules to achieve high performance is impressive; to the best of this reviewer’s knowledge, this is the first work on open-vocabulary 3D articulated object generation.
- The paper shows promising preliminary results demonstrating the method’s effectiveness.

### Weaknesses

- The paper is not strictly “articulated object generation”: it focuses on part segmentation, motion prediction, and part completion on an existing mesh. Comparisons with specialized works on part segmentation and motion prediction (e.g., Category-Level Articulated Object Pose Estimation, PartSLIP, Shape2Motion, Real2Code) are missing. The authors should also reference the survey “Survey on Modeling of Articulated Objects” (Liu et al., 2024).
- There is insufficient discussion of the known issues in lifting 2D SAM masks to 3D: consistency across viewpoints, segmentation granularity, and how part labels are predefined. Quantitative results on this stage are lacking.
- The comparison with other generative works is confusing. It is unclear whether the method always starts from an existing 3D mesh. If so, the input and processing for each baseline (NAP, CAGE, URDFormer) must be explicitly described and justified. Details on how the input 3D mesh is obtained for comparison are needed.
- For the high-quality shapes in the teaser and website: are these produced through the full pipeline including refinement? Their geometry appears much better than results shown after refinement, and they seem to retain original textures. Clarification on the generation process is required.
- Novelty is limited because the method essentially relies on the power of various large models (VLMs, SAM, diffusion) rather than introducing fundamentally new techniques.
- Writing is difficult to follow in places, especially Section 3.4 (Geometry & Texture Refinement). Mathematical expressions or pseudocode should be added. The baseline method of Qiu et al. (2024) should be described to make the paper self-contained.
- Experiments lack quantitative ablation studies. For example, for the ablations of refinement and transformation in Figure 6, detailed quantitative comparisons should be provided. Also, the comparison between the improvement in Part C and the baseline method is missing.
- The performance of the proposed method is not impressive; it is hard to observe a significant improvement over existing methods such as CAGE.
- Part C (Geometry & Texture Refinement) is too roughly described. Additional figures illustrating the optimization pipeline and math equations are needed. The claimed inner-structure generation is not visible; cross-sectional views before/after refinement and quantitative metrics for internal geometry quality are expected.
- Examples of failure cases and suboptimal results should be provided, along with analysis of root causes and discussion of potential solutions or future work.
- The presentation is somewhat misleading. The paper conflates two tasks: (1) converting a mesh that already has part geometries (retaining original appearance) and (2) converting a single surface mesh with an additional reconstruction/optimization step that cannot fully preserve the input geometry/appearance (feels like text-to-3D generation with a mesh initialization). This distinction should be clarified.
- No quantitative evaluation of reconstruction quality (geometry and appearance) is provided. On PartNet-Mobility, the paper should report Chamfer Distance of part meshes, ID/AID metrics (as in NAP and CAGE), and PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS from rendered views.
- Many technical and experimental details are missing, harming reproducibility: how is GPT4o exactly prompted for part segmentation and joint point selection? How are joint limits and rotation/translation direction determined? How is the SDF of each part computed (Section 3.3)? How is the optimization implemented (text prompt source, iteration count, runtime)? For unconditional generation, what were the inputs to each method? Was URDFormer retrained on the same split as CAGE?
- The resolution of most qualitative results (Figures 3,4,5,6) is low. The quality appears inconsistent (Figure 8 is much better), raising concerns about overall output quality.
- Additional comparison points are needed: methods like Shape2Motion and Real2Code should be included for articulation estimation.
- Multiple relevant references are missing: Hu et al. (2017) “Learning to predict part mobility from a single static snapshot”, Sharf et al. (2014) “Mobility-trees for indoor scenes manipulation”, Weng et al. (2024) “Neural Implicit Representation for Building Digital Twins of Unknown Articulated Objects”, Wei et al. (2022) “Self-supervised neural articulated shape and appearance models”, and the survey by Liu et al. (2024).
- In Table 2, the “Ours” VQA score is significantly higher than PartNet-Mobility (ground truth). Clarify why this is expected. Likewise, NAP’s VQA score is higher than “PartNet w/o texture” – explain the interpretation.
- In Figure 3, describe what constitutes the input to each method and how examples were selected for comparison.
- In Figure 5, clarify the meaning of the red and blue boxes. Is the leftmost object the input?
- For the articulation parameter estimation comparison (Table 2), specify the input provided to NAP and CAGE and how they were implemented.
