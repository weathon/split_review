## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Motif Explainer Models (MEMs), a model-based explanation method for genomic deep neural networks that identifies sufficient and necessary motifs and their logical syntax (cooperativity, redundancy, repression). MEMs are evaluated against scrambler models on synthetic DNA sequences with predefined logical rules. Reviewers agree that the problem is important but raise major concerns about novelty, lack of real biological validation, oversimplified assumptions about motifs, and limited evaluation.

### Strengths
- The method introduces a novel application of sufficiency and necessity conditions to interpret DNNs in genomics, potentially advancing explainable AI (XAI) for motif discovery and understanding regulatory syntax.
- Comprehensive experimental setups on synthetic data with logical syntax rules (cooperativity, redundancy, repression) provide a proof of concept, using multiple metrics (sufficiency and necessity scores).
- If successful, MEMs could have notable impact on regulatory genomics by enabling more interpretable models.
- The paper tackles an important problem for genomic deep neural networks—explaining predictions to understand biological motif syntax.
- The approach is well biologically motivated and explicitly models different aspects of motif syntax (cooperativity, redundancy, repression) with novel regularization to identify disjoint, contiguous motifs.
- The methodological part is well written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of real biological data application:** All experiments use synthetic sequences with fixed logical rules; no evaluation on public biological datasets (e.g., ENCODE). This limits demonstration of practical utility and generalizability.
- **Oversimplified assumptions:** (1) The statement “TF binding is driven by sequence motifs” is false—chromatin state, pioneering factors, and other proteins are critical. (2) The claim that motifs are “small, contiguous, and disjoint subsequences” is also false—motifs can overlap (competing/cooperative TFs) or be non‑contiguous (e.g., REST has separate left/right halves). These assumptions undermine the design of the regularizers.
- **Limited novelty:** MEMs are very similar to the scrambler approach (Linder et al., 2022) with a different loss and penalty.
- **Insufficient literature coverage:** Apart from scramblers, the paper does not reference or compare to standard XAI methods for CNNs (layer‑wise relevance propagation, SmoothGrad, gradient‑based attribution) that can also extract motifs and capture base‑pair interactions. Inherently interpretable NNs for regulatory genomics (e.g., Tseng et al., 2024) are also omitted.
- **Unclear why motifs need to be learned:** Extensive motif databases (TRANSFAC, JASPAR) exist. The paper would benefit from a use‑case where databases cannot help (e.g., TFs with unknown motifs, non‑model species).
- **Evaluation is too limited:** No comparisons to relevant methods (LRP, SmoothGrad, interpretable NNs), no real dataset, and no downstream application.
- **Presentation issues:** Descriptions lack clarity (e.g., Equation 1: why does f() take two inputs? Are they disjoint subsets?). Large portions re‑explain scramblers unnecessarily. Sequence logo plots are hard to see. For the repressive case (y=0), theory states M2 should be sufficient and necessary when M1 and M2 are present, but results say n‑MEM discerns 1–2 motifs are necessary—a mismatch. Figure 4b is missing the 1‑motif case. Minor: L116 “Experimental validation” misleading (should be “Simulation experiments”); L244 typo; equations not numbered; function d between equations 14 and 15 not defined.
- **Scalability concerns:** MEMs rely on careful hyperparameter tuning; it is unclear how parameters generalize to high‑dimensional, large‑scale genomic datasets.
- **Missing experiments and robustness tests:** How well does MEMs recover *actual* ground truth motifs (beyond sufficiency/necessity w.r.t. the classifier)? How robust to noise? How does it perform on real data?
- **Minority positive reviewer (score 5) notes** that pre‑defined logical rules may oversimplify real motif interactions, and the mathematical definitions of sufficiency/necessity may limit interpretability for biologists not versed in ML. They suggest incorporating domain‑specific priors (e.g., motif conservation scores).
