## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a differentiable implicit solver on graphs that combines graph neural networks with the finite volume method for forward and inverse problems on unstructured grids. Reviewers found the question interesting but had strongly diverging views: two reviewers (R1, R3) rated the paper 3, acknowledging the potential of the framework, while two reviewers (R2, R4) rated it 1, citing insufficient novelty, weak experiments, and poor presentation. All reviewers agree that major revisions are needed.

### Strengths
- **Differentiable implicit scheme reduces time steps** (R1). The framework supports both forward and inverse problems on unstructured grids, which are practically relevant (R1).
- **The paper tries to build a unified pipeline** for mesh coarsening, forward, and inverse problems (R2).
- **Figure 1 effectively illustrates the overall pipeline** and the experiments apply GNN+FVM to forward and inverse problems (R3).
- **The question of combining GNN with finite volume methods is interesting and natural** (R4).

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and Related Work**
- The novelty is very limited. Incorporating FVM into GNN is already done (e.g., Jessica et al. ICML 2024, Horie et al. ICML 2024). The gradient construction (Section 2.3) is strongly related to the standard adjoint method. Implicit GNNs exist (e.g., Gu et al. NeurIPS 2020). The authors should cite these works and clarify the added novelty (R2). The paper feels incomplete and lacks a “Related Work” section; it does not discuss how it differs from existing GNN- or FVM-based approaches (R3). In particular, the method should be compared to Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQ), which study iterative solvers in deep learning (R4). The paper should also compare to “Learning to Solve PDE-constrained Inverse Problems with Graph Networks” and explain advantages (R3).

**Experimental Evaluation**
- **Insufficient baselines:** Only one baseline is provided for Section 3.2, and none for Sections 3.3 and 3.4 (R2). No comparison against other GNN- or FVM-based methods is made, making it impossible to assess effectiveness (R3, R4). The results in Section 3.4 (graph coarsening) are particularly poor (R4).
- **Missing computational cost analysis:** The method in line 253 claims lower cost than explicit Euler, but no per-time-step cost comparison (including gradient computation) is provided (R1). The matrix inversion in Eqs. (8) and (12) likely incurs high cost; the paper should discuss sparsity and inversion complexity (R1, R3). The limitations of automatic differentiation in JAX (line 74) are not elaborated in terms of memory or scaling (R1).
- **Problem setup and evaluation gaps:** The method is tested only on Neumann boundary conditions; Dirichlet or mixed conditions should be explored (R1, R3). No experiments validate the conservation properties (mass/energy) that the FVM integration should guarantee (R1). The ill-posedness of the inverse problem is not discussed: in Figures 3–4 the recovered permeability captures only general trends (R1). All experiments use large numbers of data points; tests with fewer grid points are needed (R1). Hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., choice of stabilizer \( S(\theta) \)) is not analyzed (R3). The loss in Figure 2 starts very high and it is unclear whether it converges to zero after 160 epochs; a guideline for sufficient loss minimization with coarser grids is missing (R1).

**Presentation and Writing**
- The writing is subpar, with many typos and grammatical errors (e.g., “whats is equivalent so the solution” should be “which is equivalent to solving”) (R4). Figures 2–4 are missing labels (a), (b), etc., and the terms “data 1”, “fitting 1”, “data 2”, “fitting 2” are not explained (R2). The role of \( R \) in equation (4) (the measurement operator) is unclear (R3). The paper does not explain how \( S(\theta) \) is chosen for different PDEs (R3). An alternative approach to avoid matrix inversion (solving Eq. (5) via optimization) is not discussed (R1). The paper should also discuss the limitations and potential benefits of using machine learning for linear PDEs over classical methods (R2).
