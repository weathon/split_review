## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ConML, a universal meta-learning framework that incorporates task-level contrastive learning in the model space. ConML minimizes inner-task distance and maximizes inter-task distance using task identity as supervision. The authors claim it integrates seamlessly with optimization-based, metric-based, amortization-based, and in-context learning methods, demonstrating performance improvements across few-shot regression, image classification, and molecular property prediction tasks.

### Strengths
- **Flexibility and generality**: ConML can be integrated into various meta-learning algorithms (optimization-based, metric-based, amortization-based, and in-context learning) without relying on specific model architectures or target models [R1]. Some reviewers find this a significant advantage.
- **Lightweight computational overhead**: It integrates efficiently into episodic training, requiring only minimal additional calculations, thus achieving performance gains with low computational cost [R1, R3].
- **Consistent performance improvements**: ConML increases the performance of all evaluated meta-learning methods in few-shot learning tasks [R1, R3]. Notable results in improving various meta-learning approaches are reported [R2].
- **Improves learned feature representation**: The task-level contrastive loss enhances feature representations across tasks [R3].
- **Application to in-context learning**: Extending meta-learning to in-context learning is considered a valuable attempt [R1].
- **Clear presentation and extensive evaluation**: The paper is well-written and clearly presented, with an extensive experimental evaluation covering multiple datasets and meta-learning approaches [R4].

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation and novelty**: The specific pain points in meta-learning that task-level contrastive learning addresses are not clearly articulated [R1]. Several reviewers note that the contribution is limited, as it primarily adapts existing contrastive learning to meta-learning – an idea already explored in prior work [1–4] [R3]. The relationship between model-space contrastiveness and existing feature-space contrastiveness is not fully explored or differentiated [R4]. Missing comparisons with contrastive-based meta-learning baselines (ANIL, MAML with Zeroing trick) [R4] and lack of recent related references [R3].
- **Model representation and similarity measurement concerns**: There is no common or reasonable approach for representing models in the model space; methods feel overly customized (e.g., MAML uses influence on meta-parameters, ProtoNet uses sample-level info as task-level information) [R2]. Using cosine or Euclidean distance to measure model similarity is considered inadequate [R2]. In MAML, the weights of the task-specific model consist of multiple weight matrices, not a single vector, and this complexity is not addressed [R2 Q1]. One reviewer argues that using \( d_{\text{out}} \) may disrupt the objective of learning a common initialization or feature space, potentially causing instability [R2 Q3]. In few-shot settings, the task-specific model is not fully optimized, making direct use of \( e^* \) as a reference inadvisable [R2 Q4].
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Performance depends on hyperparameters (weight \(\lambda\), sampling strategies, distance functions); manual tuning is time-intensive and less user-friendly [R1].
- **Scalability concerns**: Tested primarily on small-to-medium-scale models; effectiveness on larger architectures (deep convolutional networks, transformers) remains unproven [R1].
- **Limited task scope**: Validated mostly on few-shot classification and regression; limited testing on complex tasks such as object detection, segmentation, or NLP [R1]. Bilevel meta-learning is not mentioned [R2 Q2].
- **Questionable “universal” claim**: The framework must be adapted to each meta-learning method (e.g., by defining \(\psi\)), and it is evaluated only in in-domain scenarios (tasks from the same dataset during meta-train and meta-test) [R3 Q1]. The paper’s definition of meta-learning (line 107) assumes the test task comes from a different distribution, yet all experiments are in-domain; evidence that \( d_{\text{out}} \) enhances generalization is lacking [R3 Q5]. For CAML w/ConML experiments, comparability with Table 3 is unclear unless meta-training used the same dataset combinations as CAML [R3 Q6].
- **Reproducibility and presentation issues**: Notation is inconsistent (e.g., \( h_\theta \), \( h_{\theta,D} \), \( h_t \)) [R3]. More training details needed, especially for ICL experiments (in-domain vs. cross-domain not clear) [R3]. Figures 2.b and 2.e are too small or need zooming [R3]. Results lack standard deviations, making statistical significance of improvements unclear [R3]. In Figure 2.c, MAML performance remains unchanged with more shots, and ConML performs better with fewer shots than with more, which is unexpected; recommend using the same number of shots during meta-training and meta-testing [R3 Q7].
- **Missing analyses**: Ablation studies contrasting only \( d_{\text{in}} \) vs. only \(-d_{\text{out}} \) are needed to understand the relative importance of alignment vs. discrimination [R4 Q4]. Plotting alignment metrics (\( d_{\text{in}} \), \( d_{\text{in}} - d_{\text{out}} \), \( d_{\text{out}} \)) during training would provide insights into the alignment/discrimination process [R4 Q3].
- **Reviewer disagreement**: While some reviewers (R1, R4) rate the paper higher (score 5) and praise its flexibility and empirical results, others (R2, R3) give lower scores (3) and raise fundamental concerns about motivation, model representation, novelty, and reproducibility, indicating a significant split in assessment.
