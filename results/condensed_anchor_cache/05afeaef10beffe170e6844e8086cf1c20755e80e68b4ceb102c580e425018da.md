## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SM⁴, a Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) framework for multi-modal multi-task learning that addresses three issues: modality forgetting, modality fitting (over/under-fitting), and heterogeneous learning pace. The method disentangles model spaces for modalities, adaptively determines the number of experts per modality, and synchronizes learning paces using routing entropy. Evaluations on a subset of MultiBench datasets show strong performance against SOTA baselines and substantial computation reduction. However, reviewer opinions on method clarity, novelty, and experimental thoroughness vary widely.

### Strengths
- The work is well-motivated and addresses important, under-explored issues in multi-modal multi-task learning (multiple reviewers). The three identified problems—forgetting, fitting, and learning pace—are relevant.
- The proposed method achieves impressive/encouraging performance against SOTA baselines on the included datasets. One reviewer specifically noted a clear performance margin (≥1.37%) and computation reduction (46.49%–98.62%) (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The experiments are comprehensive, with analyses, ablation studies, and complexity studies. One reviewer stated that the analyses establish a solid evaluation protocol for follow-up work (R2, R3).
- The implementation is available (R3).
- One reviewer praised the writing as impressive, making complicated settings informative and easy to follow, and noted that experiment settings are thoroughly reported (R2). This is a distinctly positive view compared to other reviewers.
- Plots in Figure 2 are good illustrations that are right to the point (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Method Clarity and Heuristic Nature**:
  - The description of the proposed method is very difficult to follow; it does not clearly state what the experts are and where they come from. Figure 1 is not helpful; experts are grouped but there is no discussion of why/how they are grouped (R1).
  - Another reviewer found the method ad-hoc and heuristic, especially the AEA and ALP modules (R3).
  - In contrast, one reviewer found the presentation excellent (R2), but this disagreement itself highlights clarity issues.

- **Adaptive Expert Allocation (AEA) Concerns**:
  - Introduces an additional hyper-parameter n (number of iterations to monitor loss); sensitivity to n is unclear and no guideline for selection is provided (R3).
  - Algorithm 2 uses a pre-training phase to decide kⱼ for each modality independently, but does not account for multitask interactions when modalities are learned together (R3).
  - No constraint that ∑ kⱼ = N (total experts) (R3).
  - Figure 2-3: unclear why a larger training-validation loss gap leads to better generalization; a large gap typically indicates underfitting or overfitting (R3).
  - The precise mechanism of how training and validation loss are measured and compared to determine expert allocation is not clearly specified. Reliance on a single epoch’s loss seems insufficient and unstable (R3).
  - Lack of a clear stopping criterion for the expert allocation process (R3).

- **Adaptive Learning Pace (ALP) Concerns**:
  - Unclear what “learning pace” means—is it the learning rate or something directly influencing the training trajectory? (R1, R3).
  - The motivation is not clear: does unstable routing policy simply mean changes across iterations? Such changes do not necessarily link to routing distribution entropy (R1).
  - The connection between routing entropy and learning pace adjustment is vague and lacks theoretical justification; the method appears heuristic (R3).
  - It is unclear whether this is a per-modality learning rate adjustment or a more complex mechanism (R3).

- **Novelty and Organic Connection**:
  - For modality forgetting, the authors deploy load and importance balancing loss (standard techniques). For the other two problems, standard hyperparameter tuning methods are used. It is unclear which part is truly original (R4).
  - The three problems are tackled separately; the paper does not point out how they are related. The connection seems not organic; better results may simply come from combining independent fixes (R4).

- **Experimental Gaps and Presentation Issues**:
  - Robustness and training cost are ignored. It is unclear if the model is robust to missing or noisy modalities—an important criterion in multimodal learning (R2).
  - The trade-off between training cost and performance is unclear. Deciding the number of experts per modality seems time-consuming. Comparing SM⁴ with simple methods like early/late fusion would help measure practical value (R2).
  - Reported performance of MultiBench models in Table 2 may be overly simplified: complexities vary greatly, and simply reporting aggregated performance (e.g., range) makes it difficult to position SM⁴. Dependencies between efficiency and performance are ignored; a candidate improvement is the 2D visualization used by MultiBench to study trade-offs (R2).
  - Tables 1 and 2 are unclear: what does “setting” refer to—dataset size or model size? Example: SM⁴ Medium on AV-MNIST has 1.23M parameters, but the same method in the large setting has 0.76M parameters—inconsistent (R3).
  - The results of HighMMT on the same datasets differ noticeably from those reported in Liang et al. (2022); a discussion of the discrepancy is needed (R1, R3).
  - Figure 5: what does “32N” mean? Intuitively, increasing N (number of experts) leads to better performance, and 32N is the largest, so it is not surprising it gives the best result; the intended message is unclear (R4).
  - Figure 2 presentation is not nice; consider using subfigures (e.g., 2a, 2b) (R3).
  - Minor typo: “YR-FUNNY” in Table 1 (R2).
  - Criteria for selecting datasets/tasks from MultiBench are not provided; many other tasks and modalities exist in MultiBench, and it is unclear how the proposed method works for those (R1).
