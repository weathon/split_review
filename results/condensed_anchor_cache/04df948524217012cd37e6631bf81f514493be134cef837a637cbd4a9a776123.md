## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper revisits generative policies (diffusion and flow models) for offline reinforcement learning. It classifies existing training objectives into two categories—forward KL and reverse KL—and proposes simpler instantiations: Generative Model Policy Optimization (GMPO) via advantage-weighted regression and Generative Model Policy Gradient (GMPG) via a numerically stable policy gradient method. The authors also introduce GenerativeRL, a standardized experimental framework for evaluating generative policy algorithms. Reviewers appreciated the unified perspective, detailed code, and clear comparative tables (Table 1, Table 2) but raised substantial concerns about novelty, performance analysis, theoretical grounding, and the paper’s scope.

### Strengths
- Provides a unified perspective and systematic analysis of prior generative policy methods, which is valuable for the community (R1, R2, R3).
- Includes detailed code and instructions; the GenerativeRL framework enhances reproducibility and enables fair comparison across methods (R1, R4).
- Considers both diffusion models and flow models, not limited to diffusion (R1).
- Offers clear theoretical comparisons, with Table 1 and Table 2 being extremely helpful in understanding current methods (R3).
- GMPO and GMPG are theoretically founded and achieve competitive performance against state-of-the-art algorithms (R3, R4).
- First known integration of various diffusion policy optimization algorithms into a single framework (R3).
- Comprehensive evaluation through GenerativeRL may resolve the large performance fluctuations observed in prior off-line RL reproductions (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**:  
  - GMPO is very similar to advantage-weighted regression (AWR) applied to a diffusion policy; forward/inverse KL analysis is already a consensus in the community (R1).  
  - GMPO has been previously verified in the image‑generation domain (Lee et al., 2023) (R3).  
  - The paper does not provide new theoretical insights or empirical findings beyond what prior work already discusses (R1).

- **Performance analysis**:  
  - On AntMaze tasks (Table 11), GMPO (73.8) and GMPG (66.5) are weaker than several baselines (>75) with no specific explanation for this degradation (R1).  
  - There is no analysis of why GMPG underperforms on AntMaze relative to locomotion tasks, nor of the characteristics of AntMaze that might cause this (R1).  

- **Algorithmic details and computational concerns**:  
  - The calculation of log π for diffusion policies in GMPG (lines 316‑327) is too brief; material from the appendix should be moved to the main text (R1).  
  - GMPG requires a Neural ODE to estimate log probability, raising computational efficiency concerns; comparison with SRPO and Diffusion‑QL on computational cost is missing (R3).  
  - GMPG forces the policy model to be a Gaussian (via ODE), not a diffusion policy, limiting its generality (R3).  
  - The hyper‑parameter β is heavily tuned, and the range of values across environments is large; details of the tuning procedure should be provided (R2).  
  - The claim that GMPO and GMPG are competitive without tricks (e.g., joint training, generating multiple actions and selecting one) is misleading because such tricks can significantly improve performance; the paper should acknowledge that Diffusion‑QL’s gains may stem from these implementation details (R2).

- **Theoretical foundation and categorization**:  
  - It is unclear whether the paper proposes a unified benchmark suite (GenerativeRL) or new algorithms (GMPO/GMPG); if the former, more experimental details are needed; if the latter, stronger evidence is required (R2, R4).  
  - The connection between GMPO/GMPG and existing algorithms is insufficiently clarified; there is no explicit categorization in Section 4.2, and theoretical proof supporting why the proposed methods improve upon prior work is lacking (R4).  
  - The choice of categories (forward KL vs. reverse KL) is not thoroughly justified; common traits and reasons for grouping should be explicitly stated (R4).  
  - Table 2 contains mistakes: for Diffusion‑QL and IDQL, the inference scheme uses [max] not [softmax]; for SfBC, it uses [softmax] not [max]. The authors should recheck their code (R3).  
  - Related Works reads as a chronological listing without interpretation; abbreviations (e.g., SfBC) should be introduced there, and the paper’s categorization should be explained in that context (R4).

- **Scope and clarity**:  
  - The paper focuses on offline RL but does not mention online RL works that use generative policies ([1, 2]); the title should be revised to “Offline RL” or those works should be covered (R2).  
  - It is ambiguous whether the paper’s framework is truly a “framework” or simply organized experiment code; the contribution attributed to GenerativeRL may be overstated (R4).  
  - “Any” in Table 1 is ambiguous—does it include flow models? Were there no prior flow‑based methods in offline RL, or were they intentionally excluded? (R4)  
  - Abbreviations DSM and CFM (Eq 2,3) are not explained earlier in the paper (R4).  

- **Additional missing analyses and questions**:  
  - Why does Diffusion‑QL achieve state‑of‑the‑art on D4RL even though baselines are more recently proposed? (R2)  
  - What is the “optimal policy” in Figure 1? Is it the one from Eq (5)? Is it tractable? (R2)  
  - Can GMPO and GMPG be stably applied to more complex generative models beyond diffusion and flow? (R4)  
  - How well does GenerativeRL extend to other RL algorithms beyond offline RL? (R4)  
  - When will the codebase be open‑sourced? (R3)  

*Note:* One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, viewing the integration and theoretical comparison as a strong contribution, while the other three reviewers (scores 5) had significant reservations about novelty, scope, and theoretical foundations.
