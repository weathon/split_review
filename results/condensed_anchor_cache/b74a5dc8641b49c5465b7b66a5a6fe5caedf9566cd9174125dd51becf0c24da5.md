## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Neural-Symbolic Recursive Machine (NSR), a model for systematic generalization in sequence-to-sequence tasks. The architecture consists of three modules: a neural perception module that maps raw inputs to grounded symbols, a dependency parser that produces a syntax tree over symbols (the Grounded Symbol System, GSS), and a program induction module that derives semantic meanings from the tree. The modules are trained jointly via a deduction-abduction algorithm with Monte-Carlo sampling. The method achieves strong results on four benchmarks: SCAN (semantic parsing), PCFG (string manipulation), HINT (arithmetic reasoning), and a compositional machine translation task. NSR obtains 100% accuracy on SCAN, PCFG, and the translation task, and outperforms all baselines on HINT.

### Strengths
- The problem of compositional generalization is an important and timely research direction (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The NSR architecture is novel, particularly the use of a Grounded Symbol System that emerges from data, and the deduction-abduction training procedure is an original contribution for jointly learning representations and programs (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The paper is technically strong, with rigorous definitions and detailed exposition of the model components and learning algorithm (Reviewer 1). The architectural inductive biases of equivariance and recursiveness are clearly motivated (Reviewer 1).
- The experiments are systematic across four datasets with carefully designed splits, and the analyses provide insights into when and why NSR generalizes better than baselines (Reviewer 1).
- The method achieves excellent performance, often achieving 100% accuracy or beating all baselines, including Transformers and prior neuro-symbolic methods (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The paper is well presented, with Figures 3 and 4 being particularly helpful in conveying intuition about what the method learns (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clarity and insufficient detail** (Reviewer 3 is most critical, citing soundness 1 and presentation 1; Reviewer 2 also finds gaps). There are very few details about the actual approach in Section 3 and Figure 1 to fully understand the model. Specifically: what exactly are the symbols in T for each dataset? Are models parameterized as neural networks? What is the overall parameter count? How does inference work? The program induction module is described at too high a level: equation (3) does not define `p` on the right-hand side, and it is unclear whether the module is simply DreamCoder or a modified version (Reviewers 2, 3). Figure 3 appears to be wrong (Reviewer 2). The paper spends excessive space on simple definitions like “equivariance” and “recursiveness” and on flagposting “hypothesis” statements, which could be moved to an appendix (Reviewer 3).
- **Inadequate explanation of why symbolic components improve generalization** (Reviewers 2, 4). The paper states that symbolic components (GSS) foster systematic generalization, but no analysis is given for why this occurs. A deeper analysis of GSS internal representations and how they differ from purely neural approaches is needed. Additionally, the claim that the three modules exhibit equivariance and recursiveness lacks supporting evidence (Reviewer 4).
- **Search termination for the GSS tree is ambiguous** (Reviewer 4). The paper states the search runs “until the accurate result is obtained,” but does not define what “accurate” means or whether an “almost correct” tree could be used, and what effect that would have on performance.
- **Limited neuro-symbolic baseline comparisons** (Reviewer 4). Only one neuro-symbolic method (NeSS) is compared, which exhibits all-or-nothing behavior (0% on two tasks, 100% on two). At least one additional neuro-symbolic method, such as Greedy Theorem Provers (Minervini et al.), should be included to make results more significant. Also, Table 3 for the compositional translation task omits a vanilla transformer baseline (Reviewer 4, minor suggestion).
- **Limited applicability to real-world scenarios** (Reviewers 1, 3). All experiments use synthetic or programmatic datasets. The paper should discuss how the method would generalize to natural language semantic parsing (e.g., GeoQuery) or real machine translation tasks, and what symbols and programs would look like for more natural data distributions (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 1 also asks for real-world application).
- **Missing references and comparisons** (Reviewer 4). The work could benefit from discussion of related neuro-symbolic works (e.g., Neural Theorem Provers, Greedy NTPs) and works adding inductive biases to Transformers (e.g., Gontier et al. on entity abstraction).
- **Minor issues** (Reviewers 2, 4). The term “recursive” in Definition 3.2 may be confused with a different concept from theory of computation (Reviewer 2). The method’s use of gradients (SGD?) and why the procedure is called “abduction” are not explained (Reviewer 2). Questions about vocabulary size of primitives, effect of more complex sets of primitives, and hints for handling probabilistic semantics remain unanswered (Reviewer 4).
