## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies deep RL algorithms in continuing tasks (no resets, predefined resets, agent-controlled resets) using MuJoCo and Atari testbeds. It reports that performance degrades without resets, especially under high discount factors and reward offsets. It also extends Naik et al.'s reward-centering method to deep RL and shows improvements. Reviewers 1 and 3 rate the paper low (3) citing incremental contribution, poor organization, and missing justification; Reviewer 2 rates it higher (6) praising the analysis and findings for Swimmer.

### Strengths

- The problem of deep RL in continuing tasks is practically significant and underexplored; the experimental setup is novel and interesting. (R1, R3)
- The empirical study is extensive: it covers canonical deep RL algorithms (SAC, TD3, DDPG, PPO, DQN, etc.) and uses enough runs (10 per agent/environment) for statistical significance. (R1)
- Analysis of the impact of resets on agent performance under the reward-rate metric is well done; diving into *why* it happens and drawing conclusions for Swimmer (leading to dramatic improvements) is impressive. (R2)
- The paper proposes a promising standard benchmark for continuing tasks with three types of testbeds (no resets, predefined resets, agent-controlled resets) and popular DRL baselines. (R3)
- A visual analysis of failure patterns is provided in Figure 2. (R3)

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and contribution**: The core methodological proposal (reward centering) is an incremental amendment to Naik et al. (2024) work, which already suggested it; applying it to deep RL is a logical conclusion from that paper and does not represent the methodological novelty typically expected at ICLR. (R1)
- **Writing and organization**: The paper lacks a dedicated Related Work section (R1, R3). The introduction is over 2 pages long, making it hard to follow (R3). Problem notation and background are relegated to the appendix, but should appear in the main body before Section 3 (R1). Section 2 presents three testbeds in long subsections without an overall description of experiment designs and target questions at the start (R3). Atari results are not well discussed in the main body (R3).
- **Missing justification for design choices**: The target position resampling interval in Reacher is 50 steps, in Pusher 100 steps, and a reset penalty of -10 is used—these choices are not justified or referenced. Agent performance is likely sensitive to them. (R1)
- **Imprecise language** – phrases like “decent policies” and “policy that performed reasonably well” should be replaced with quantitative comparisons. (R1)
- **Exploration issues**: The claim that “the agent did not perform sufficient exploration to escape from suboptimal states” (lines 257-259) may be influenced by whether the authors followed the standard convention of turning off exploration noise at test time for SAC/TD3; this is not discussed. (R1)
- **Missing episodic baseline**: How do the methods perform in the standard episodic setting? This should be reported as an upper bound to establish performance degradation. (R1)
- **Relevance of high discount factors and reward offsets**: Why should the reader be concerned about reduced performance under these conditions? Are they practically relevant? What is the theoretical justification? (R1)
- **Exploration hyperparameter sensitivity**: How does performance change when exploration hyperparameters (e.g., TD3 exploration noise) are varied? Can larger exploration elicit better performance? (R1)
- **Agent-controlled resets**: Why does giving the agent control over its own resets reduce performance? Shouldn’t it learn the predefined resets from Section 2.2? (R1) In Section 2.3, the analysis of the learned reset control is missing. (R3)
- **Environment inconsistency**: Section 2.1 selects 5 weakly-communicating MDP environments, but Sections 2.2 and 2.3 change environments. If the same environments were used across all reset conditions, it would be easier to draw firm conclusions. (R1)
- **Real-world metric mismatch**: The paper links continuing tasks to robotics, but reward rate is not the metric of interest at deployment—task success metrics are. (R2) The authors should evaluate on actual task metrics (e.g., for Reacher, measure success in standard episodic setting after training with different resetting schemes). (R2)
- **Representativity of testbeds**: MuJoCo tasks are mostly locomotion (except pusher/reacher) with periodicity in optimal control, limiting the significance for continuing learning. Real-world problems like content recommendation or portfolio management are more story-like; Atari tasks are better, but emphasis is on MuJoCo. Including Minecraft could strengthen the suite. (R3)
- **Figure 2 failure analysis**: The two failure cases are presented but lack further discussion on the underlying reasons. (R3)
- **Potential data errors**: In Tables 2 and 3, the numbers of resets for DDPG in Ant with reset costs 1, 10, 100 (94.20±5.98, 23.00±2.67, 65.20±4.76) and for TD3 (89.80±10.27, 1.20±0.29, 58.40±9.65) appear non-monotonic and may be mistakes. Similarly, for DDPG in Walker2d with reset cost 1 vs. 100, the reward rates (3.79±0.14 vs. 3.95±0.11) are nearly identical despite very different reset counts (104.50±17.33 vs. 39.70±3.98)—needs explanation or re-check. (R3)
- **Minors**: Figure 1 should fix the y‑axis per environment for cross‑setting comparison (R1); missing the older Gymnasium/Gym citation (R1); spelling “decent” line 200; missing “the” line 204 (R1); margin below Table 6 too narrow (R3).
