## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes adiabatic replay (AR), a selective replay method for continual learning that uses a VAE with a GMM prior to replay only the mixture components most similar to new data, theoretically avoiding unbounded growth of replayed samples. Proof-of-concept experiments compare AR to DGR and ER on small-scale class-incremental splits of MNIST, SVHN, and CIFAR-10. Reviewers are generally positive about the motivation and original angle, but one reviewer (score 5, confidence 4) finds the approach novel and promising, while the other three (scores 3, confidence 4–5) consider the empirical evidence too weak and the novelty limited. All reviewers note significant weaknesses in experimental design, comparisons, and presentation.

### Strengths

- **Sound motivation for selective replay.** There is significant value in avoiding full replay of all past tasks because the computational cost of full replay grows unboundedly (Reviewers 1, 2). Selective replay can substantially reduce this cost (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Original approach to an important problem.** Many prior studies found it hard to beat balanced random sampling, making the GMM-based selective replay a novel and promising direction (Reviewer 3, score 5). The use of a GMM as both generator and learner in an adiabatic setting may inspire new research (Reviewer 4).
- **Proof-of-concept demonstration.** The paper shows improved performance over DGR and ER on several settings (Reviewers 3, 4). The idea of keeping replay cost nearly constant with respect to the number of tasks is appreciated (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient empirical evaluation.**
    - Only small-scale tasks (small models, short task sequences) are used; no results on larger-scale or more realistic continual learning benchmarks (Reviewers 1, 4).
    - The baseline set is too narrow: only DGR and ER are compared. Missing comparisons include MIR (which the paper is conceptially similar to; Reviewer 1) and other recent replay methods (Reviewers 2, 3).
    - A direct comparison to assess the benefit of selective replay is missing: AR should be compared to the exact same method but using normal (full) replay (Reviewer 3).
    - DGR’s performance in the paper is very weak (e.g., on MNIST it is lower than a linear classifier) and far below published results of DGR on similar settings (e.g., MIR achieves 80% on split MNIST online with 2 classes/task; Reviewer 1). This raises concerns about the implementation of DGR and the experimental rigor (Reviewers 1, 3).
    - No time-cost experiments are provided despite the efficiency claim being a major advantage (Reviewers 1, 2).
    - Only one class per task after the first is tested; experiments with multiple classes per task are missing (Reviewer 2).

- **Lack of novelty concerns and conceptual overlap with prior work.**
    - The key technique (selecting replay samples close in latent space) is well-known; the method is essentially Gen-MIR with zero gradient ascent steps (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 similarly finds the key technique well-known, though Reviewer 3 views the approach as original and a proof-of-principle.

- **Inadequate discussion of related work.**
    - Important prior work is not discussed: Van de Ven et al. (2020) showed that preventing forgetting can be easier than learning, which is directly relevant to the adiabatic assumption (Reviewer 3). Klasson et al. (2023, TMLR) on smart replay, and McClelland et al. (2020, Phil Trans R Soc B) making a conceptual argument against full replay are also omitted (Reviewer 3). The relationship to MIR is not discussed (Reviewer 1).

- **Missing experimental details and clarity issues.**
    - For AR: how is the solver trained? Is it also trained with adiabatic replay or with regular replay (and thus suffering linear increase)? How are labels for replayed data obtained? (Reviewer 3)
    - For DGR: how are labels obtained for training the solver? (Reviewer 3)
    - For ER and DGR: how are the loss terms from replayed data and current data weighed? Are they balanced as in Van de Ven et al. (2020) or simply added? (Reviewer 3)
    - The buffer size for ER is not stated (Reviewer 1; should be in main text per Reviewer 3).
    - When is latent replay vs. standard replay used? (Reviewer 1; seems SVHN and CIFAR use latent replay but unclear)
    - Notation DX-Y is not introduced (Reviewer 3). Citations are formatted incorrectly (Reviewer 3). Typo: “Maximally interference replay” should be “Maximally interfered replay” (Reviewer 3).
    - Writing needs improvement; a pseudocode would aid understanding (Reviewer 2). The main text exceeds the 9-page limit (Section 5.1 spills to page 10; Reviewer 2).
    - The level at which latent replay operates is unclear; a detailed figure would help (Reviewer 1). “Foundation Models” are misinterpreted as any pretrained model rather than a generalist model (Reviewer 1).

- **Inadequate justification of the adiabatic assumption.**
    - There is no precise mathematical definition of what constitutes “small” or “incremental” information – whether relative or absolute measure (Reviewer 4). It is not explained why the chosen tasks (class splits) satisfy the assumption, nor how to check in real-world settings if a new task is adiabatic (Reviewer 4). The method may fail catastrophically if the assumption is violated; this is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
    - The paper claims near-constant time complexity but the hyperparameter K (number of GMM components) is not properly discussed. If K is fixed, capacity limits arise; if K grows with tasks, model size increases (Reviewers 2, 4). The choice of K and its impact on forgetting is not explored (Reviewer 2).

- **Limitations of the GMM model and scalability concerns.**
    - Only a simple GMM is used; claims about more advanced models are unsupported. No experiments with deeper generators or high-dimensional data are provided. The curse of dimensionality is not addressed (Reviewer 4).
    - For an unbounded stream of classes, it is unclear how K should be set or whether the model can avoid catastrophic forgetting when the number of components is insufficient (Reviewer 4). Even with adiabatic additions, if a more capable model is used, will its size still grow? (Reviewer 4)
