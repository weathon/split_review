## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends Direct Advantage Estimation (DAE) to the off-policy setting by leveraging an interpretation of the advantage function as a causal effect. It decomposes a trajectory’s return into a component caused by the agent’s actions (“skill”) and a component due to uncontrollable transitions (“luck”). This decomposition yields Off-policy DAE, which can learn from off-policy data without importance sampling or truncation. Experiments are conducted on toy examples and the MinAtar suite with 20 random seeds. Reviewers agree the decomposition is novel and the exposition is generally clear, but disagree on the experimental rigor and the strength of the claims. One reviewer (score 8) is strongly positive, citing enhanced credit assignment and strong stochastic‑environment results; another (score 5, confidence 5) finds the writing poor and the experiments unconvincing; the other two (scores 6) appreciate the ideas but raise concerns about baselines, model‑learning overhead, and off‑policy correction justification.

### Strengths
- **Novel decomposition**: The return decomposition into parts due to the agent’s actions and parts due to the environment’s transitions (skill vs. luck) is intuitive, novel, and leads to a practical algorithm (Off‑policy DAE). (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Clear exposition**: The paper is well‑written and the derivation is easy to follow, aided by intuitive examples. (R1, R2; note: R3 disagrees, see Weaknesses)
- **Well‑designed toy examples**: The toy environments effectively illustrate the differences between DAE and Off‑policy DAE. (R1, R4)
- **Insight into uncorrected returns**: The paper explains why uncorrected off‑policy n‑step returns can work well under certain environmental properties. (R1)
- **Standard benchmark and seeds**: Experiments on MinAtar are conducted with 20 random seeds; in stochastic environments Off‑policy DAE shows performance improvements over baselines. (R2; note: R3 and R4 find the experiments insufficient, see Weaknesses)
- **Useful for sparking ideas**: The decomposition is straightforward and likely to inspire further work on credit assignment. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Writing and motivation (disagreement)**: While R1 and R2 praised the clarity, R3 (confidence 5) found the writing poor: the introduction’s intuitive example does not effectively convey the paper’s idea, and Section 3 lacks motivation, making it hard to follow. (R3)
- **Experimental scope**: Evaluation is limited to MinAtar; more complex domains with higher‑dimensional state/action spaces would better demonstrate scalability. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Baseline choices**: 
  - Tree Backup is not a state‑of‑the‑art off‑policy method; comparisons against Retrace or other modern off‑policy actor‑critic algorithms are missing. (R1, R3)
  - The uncorrected n‑step return uses a large n=8, whereas literature typically uses n=3 or 5, making the comparison potentially unfair. (R3)
  - Performance of 1‑step DQN is not reported. (R3)
- **Clarification of deterministic vs. stochastic results**: For Figures 9, Table 2, and Figures 10–12, it is unclear whether results are from deterministic or stochastic environments; since stochastic environments are the paper’s main focus, this should be clarified. (R3)
- **Insufficient study of stochasticity**: There is no systematic study of how Off‑policy DAE’s performance varies with the degree of environment stochasticity (e.g., more vs. less deterministic transitions). (R4)
- **Number of trials**: The number of trials for Figure 5 is not reported. (R4; also mentioned by R3 as “number of trials”)
- **Justification of off‑policy correction**: The paper states that off‑policy corrections are unnecessary, but it is unclear why the π‑centered constraint (Eq. 5) alone suffices. The learned advantage function may still be biased toward the behavior policy’s distribution. (R4)
- **Model‑learning overhead**: Off‑policy DAE requires an additional neural network to estimate the transition distribution B^π, introducing a model‑learning component into an otherwise model‑free approach. This could be a limitation in complex environments, and the requirement to sum over all discrete latent states is restrictive. (R1; R1 also asks whether the constraint could be converted into a Lagrangian loss and whether a sample‑based model could be used.)
- **Equation (10) clarification**: The reduction of Equation (10) to the policy improvement lemma is unclear; specifically, the role of the B^π_t term needs explanation. (R1)
- **Exaggerated claim about convergence**: The paper states that Off‑policy DAE “can be seen as generalizations of Monte‑Carlo methods that utilize sample trajectories more efficiently to achieve faster convergence,” but no theorem proves this property. (R3)
- **Computational cost**: The method is significantly more expensive than baselines (15 hours vs. 2 hours, per Appendix D.7). (R3)
- **Potential overshadowing of skill by luck**: In highly stochastic environments the “luck” component may dominate the “skill” component, possibly hindering learning. It would be useful to study the effect of weighting the two components differently. (R4)
- **Missing related‑work connection**: The decomposition appears related to exogenous/endogenous stochasticity; a relevant reference (ICLR 2019 paper) is suggested for discussion. (R4)
- **Notation clarity**: 
  - Near Equation (7), the expectation E[V^π(s_{t+1}) | s_t, a_t] should clarify which variable is random (s_{t+1}). (R1, R3)
  - R1 suggests using uppercase letters for random variables to avoid confusion between fixed and random values. (R1)
- **Will the method transfer to offline RL?** The paper does not discuss potential challenges if applied directly to offline RL. (R2)
