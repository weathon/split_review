## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper employs model inversion on CLIP models to visualize images that induce high similarity with a variety of text prompts, aiming to gain insights into the learned representations and biases. The authors report five main findings: (1) CLIP can blend multiple semantic concepts in inverted images; (2) seemingly harmless prompts (e.g., "a beautiful landscape") and celebrity names produce NSFW images, particularly for female celebrities; (3) strong gender biases exist, especially in occupational and status-related concepts; (4) CLIP models trained on larger datasets produce higher-quality inversions; (5) inverted images often include written text.

### Strengths
- Applying model inversion to CLIP models is a suitable way to obtain insights into its proprietary and unavailable training data (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The paper flags important drawbacks: the CLIP training data was not cleaned from potentially harmful content, and strong biases are present, which is a timely issue for the community (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The analysis is presented in an organized and coherent fashion, making it easy to follow (Reviewer 2).
- It is interesting that CLIP model inversion can produce (somewhat) coherent objects and text, demonstrating the model’s ability to blend concepts in a way similar to generative models like DALL·E and Imagen (Reviewer 3, partially Reviewer 4).
- The investigation into textual components within inverted images, especially the effects of TV regularization, enriches the understanding of model behavior and unwanted artefacts (Reviewer 3).
- The paper shows that CLIP models trained on more data are more amenable to image inversion (better quality of inverted images) (Reviewer 1).
- Minority positive view: Reviewer 3 considers the work a solid analysis with a clear motivation, a timely problem, and a novel idea, offering good contributions to understanding CLIP models. (Other reviewers were more critical of novelty and experimental rigor.)

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and prior work**  
- The problem of image inversion has been extensively studied for both discriminative models (e.g., DeepDream) and generative models (e.g., cited papers). Similarly, identifying CLIP biases has been explored in prior work (e.g., https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.05746, https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31657). The paper offers little new methodology or findings (Reviewers 1, 4). (Contrast: Reviewer 3 considers the idea novel, but others disagree.)

**Indirect and unscalable analysis approach**  
- The inversion-based approach requires a human-in-the-loop inspection of inverted images to identify biases, which is tedious, error-prone, and not scalable. There is no principled way to quantify the extent to which a given model is biased w.r.t. a given concept, which limits its practical utility (Reviewer 1).

**Lack of practical implications**  
- It is unclear how these insights can be concretely leveraged to improve image-generation pipelines, downstream tasks (retrieval, classification, segmentation, text-to-image), or inform modelling strategies (e.g., safety finetuning, data curation). The paper itself states that "these behaviors do not have to be represented in other operational modes" (Sec. 7), weakening the connection to downstream uses (Reviewers 2, 4).

**Insufficient experimental support for claims**  
- Most claims are supported by few qualitative examples. The analysis is not thorough enough.  
  1. *Concept blending*: Very few images show blending, and it is not clear if such combined concepts existed in the original training data (e.g., fictional art). On open CLIP models, retrieval could verify novelty (Reviewer 4).  
  2. *NSFW associations and word pool bias*: The prompt analysis uses a word pool of 10,000 most common English words plus 1,913 additional unsafe words. This overrepresentation of unsafe words can skew results—if one unsafe word is retrieved, many similar ones follow. Extending the common word list naturally would be better (Reviewer 4). Additionally, the top-k lists (Tab. 1, 2) do not show similarity values; the presence of stop-words and non-words suggests low similarity with the target celebrity names (Reviewer 4).  
  3. *Gender bias (Tab. 4)*: Model inversion is an optimization process that will always seek the gender that yields slightly higher similarity, likely exacerbating biases. A 55%–45% bias in CLIP would produce near-100% one-sided inversion results, so Tab. 4 does not allow conclusions about the severity of the CLIP model’s gender biases (Reviewer 4).  
  4. *Text in images and scale effects*: The presence of text and the fact that larger training data improves inversion quality are not surprising and are not quantitatively supported (e.g., via OCR or prompt similarity). It is well known that CLIP recognizes text in images (Reviewers 3, 4). Furthermore, the finding about data scale (point 4) does not relate directly to the examination of biases; the paper would benefit from either justifying its inclusion or removing it (Reviewer 3).

**Lack of explanation and ablations**  
- The paper does not try to explain observed behaviors. For example, could a confounding latent concept such as “(female) celebrity” cause the NSFW findings? An ablation testing regular male/female names could help. Similarly, more general concepts like “woman” or “man” could be tested. No connection is made to real images; inverted images are out-of-distribution for CLIP, and it is unclear whether the similarity magnitudes match those of real images (Reviewer 4).  
- The hypothesis that prompts producing NSFW images are near NSFW language in CLIP’s embedding space is not quantitatively supported (Reviewer 4, see Fig. 1 top showing safe words).  
- What is the similarity value of each word in Tab. 1 and Tab. 2? Do unsafe words have high similarity with celebrity names or other harmless words? (Reviewer 4)

**Unmotivated modelling choices**  
- The choice of transformations (why a different set was used, e.g., ColorShift vs. random affine) is not well motivated, even though it significantly impacts image quality. How would using random affine transformations affect the conclusions? (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)

**Missing experiments and details**  
- Additional experiments are needed, such as examining racial biases (e.g., similar to Tab. 4 for Black/White people) (Reviewer 3).  
- For prompts like “a beautiful landscape,” “the map of the african continent,” or “a scientist conducting groundbreaking research,” the paper should report how many inverted images were flagged as NSFW, and whether the residuals (red squares) are always the same, or if other NSFW content appears (Reviewer 3).  
- The classification model used for the experiments in Table 4 is vaguely mentioned (line 392); its architecture, training data, and performance metrics should be provided (Reviewer 3).  
- The paper focuses only on CLIP; studying biases across models (e.g., whether inverted images w.r.t. CLIP transfer to other models) would add value (Reviewer 1).  
- No quantitative evaluation of the ratio of flagged images by the stable diffusion safety checker, e.g., when generating 100 images of each prompt in Fig. 4 (Reviewer 4).  
- In Sec. 4.3, what if the prompt is “a successful male or female student in university”? Is the bias still one-sided? (Reviewer 4)  
- The paper optimizes cosine similarity in isolation; CLIP is often used contrastively. What happens if the Softmax of cosine similarity is optimized (e.g., for the three prompts in Fig. 4, or for different celebrities)? (Reviewer 4)  
- Is it accurate to say that inversion implies training data leakage and memorization? How can downstream models be robust to such inversion analysis? (Reviewer 2)  
- How can the research community concretely leverage these insights to inform data, modelling, or evaluations for generative diffusion models? Do any learnings from CLIP-like models transfer to the generative context? (Reviewer 2)  
- Can the proposed approach be used to mitigate identified biases in CLIP models? (Reviewer 1)  
- Would Fourier-based regularization (as in Chris Olah’s feature visualization) yield different inversion results? (Reviewer 1)  
- What is the intuition behind the choice of regularization being correlated with text rendering quality? (Reviewer 2)  
- Why was a different set of transformations chosen (e.g., ColorShift) instead of random affine? What is the motivation? (Reviewer 2, also Reviewer 3 asks about affine transformations)  
- Regarding the prompt “a successful male or female student in university,” would the bias still be one-sided? (Reviewer 4)

**Formatting and presentation issues**  
- The paper appears rushed: Table 4 is misaligned, there are 10 unnecessary empty lines between Figure 2 and the text, and the same issue occurs with Table 6 in the appendix (Reviewer 3).  
- Tables 1 and 2: more words should have an asterisk (e.g., "sh*thead"), filtering should be stronger (Reviewer 3).  
- Line 107: the abbreviation “TV” is used without prior introduction (Reviewers 3, 4).  
- Line 112: clarify what “activating a target class maximally” means (Reviewer 3).  
- Line 146: missing citation (Reviewers 3, 4).  
- Line 150: change “delves” to “delve” (Reviewer 3).  
- Line 183: typo (“which”) (Reviewer 3).  
- Lines 321: reference word lists by providing links (English words, Naughty Obscene and Otherwise bad words) (Reviewer 3).  
- Lines 395 and 396 overlap (Reviewer 3).  
- Line 448: typo with double period (Reviewer 3).  
- Line 181: equation should be numbered; x and p not introduced (Reviewer 4).  
- Section 5 seems better placed as Sec. 4.5 (Reviewer 4).  
- Tab. 4 extends beyond page boundaries; insufficient whitespace between caption and main text (Reviewer 4).  
- While the text mentions ViT-B16 (OpenAI?) is used unless otherwise specified, captions of each image should specify the model (Reviewer 4).
