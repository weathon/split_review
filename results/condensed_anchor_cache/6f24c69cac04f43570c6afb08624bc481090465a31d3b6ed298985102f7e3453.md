## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes CORN, a contact-based object representation for nonprehensile manipulation of unseen objects. It uses a patch‑based transformer encoder for point clouds and a pretraining pipeline that predicts contact between gripper and object. The policy is trained with RL in simulation and distilled for real‑world deployment. Reviewers generally recognize the challenging problem, the sim‑to‑real transfer, and the diversity of tested objects. However, they diverge on novelty, clarity of method details, and experimental completeness – one reviewer (score 8) is much more positive, while the other three (scores 1, 5, 5) raise substantial concerns about contribution and presentation.

### Strengths

- The model is trained entirely in simulation and achieves over 70% success rate in the real world with zero‑shot sim‑to‑real transfer (R1). Real‑robot experiments demonstrate robustness (R4).
- Training finishes 2 million steps in less than a day (R1).
- Tested with good object diversity: over 20 real‑world objects and over 300 simulation objects, with significant geometric difference from training objects (R1).
- Addresses an important and difficult problem in contact‑rich manipulation (R1, R2).
- Experiments are comprehensive, covering all key parts of the design: the point encoder and the pretraining objective (R1).
- Hyperparameters are well documented in the appendix, aiding reproducibility (R1).
- The creation of a contact‑based representation underscores the importance of using the full gripper functionality for contact, and the algorithm designed for its training is well thought out (R4).
- The methodology is laid out clearly and is easy to follow (R2, R3).
- The presented method shows good generalization ability to unseen objects and sim‑to‑real scenarios (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns.** The work is seen as marginal novelty – “basically just applies point‑cloud‑based RL to non‑prehensile manipulation tasks” (R3). The Transformer‑based processing of point clouds is not considered groundbreaking (R2). The paper emphasizes more complex arm operations than prior grasping but lacks a clear unique design to support that motivation (R2).
- **Insufficient policy network details.** The description of the policy network is concise, lacking in‑depth details that would allow full understanding of its design and working principles (R2). The paper does not provide detailed information about the actions and reward settings used in the RL experiments, which is crucial for evaluating the RL part (R2). One reviewer questions the coordinate frame of the hand‑pose input and whether hand poses sampled near the object (for data balance) will go out‑of‑distribution during policy learning (R1).
- **Unclear success metric.** The success rate is used as the main metric, but the specific criteria (positional and orientational accuracy thresholds) are not explicitly defined in the main text, making it difficult to reproduce or compare (R4). Additionally, the goal state illustrated in manipulation videos appears to be a snapshot of a future state rather than the actual target, potentially causing misinterpretation (R4).
- **Scope limitations.** The method only works on a single object well separated from clutter on a tabletop (R1). Manipulation tasks are restricted to single‑object state maneuvering using a closed gripper; the framework does not address grasping, in‑hand manipulation, or dynamic object manipulation, limiting broader applicability (R4).
- **Engineering assumptions.** Taking the difference between current and target object pose as input brings engineering challenges for real‑world segmentation and pose tracking (R1). The method assumes the object is well separated from clutter (R1).
- **Questions about the “contact‑based” claim.** One reviewer argues that the role of “contact” in non‑prehensile manipulation is overemphasized while its role in grasping is not equally considered (R2). Another reviewer questions the generation of the collision label: if the aim is generalization to unseen geometry, how will the collision prediction network generalize to unseen shapes? Moreover, the contact decoder is imperfect and its influence on the encoder is indirect; the policy is further distilled from teacher to student, so there is no guarantee of a collision‑free policy (R3). The reward includes a contact potential *r_contact*, but the essential distance *d_h,o* is built on the distance from the CoM of the object to the gripper tip, which does not prevent collision/intersection (R3). Therefore, if “contact” is not determinative in both network design and reward shaping, the title may give wrong expectations; the authors are advised to either remove “contact‑based” or present stronger relevance (R3).
- **Missing ablations on decoder design.** Since the method is called “contact‑based,” the authors should discuss different decoder schemes for the pretraining, e.g., (a) training the encoder directly with the policy without a contact decoder, or (b) using a geometry reconstruction decoder. No apparent reason is given why action and rewards could not be done with such alternatives (R3).
- **Missing simulator identification.** One reviewer notes that the simulator used is not mentioned in the paper (R1).
- **Scale discrepancy in figures.** In Figure 7, it is unclear whether the scale of success rate is the same for the two plots; the final success rates for some curves (green, red, purple, brown) do not match between the plots (R1).
