## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes two white-box jailbreak methods for instruction-tuned LLMs by re-weighting MLP neurons, particularly those active during end-of-sentence inference. The prompt-specific method optimizes per-prompt on the fly, and the prompt-general method pre-trains a weight perturbation offline to generalize to unseen harmful prompts. Evaluated on 7 open-source LLMs (2B to 72B), the methods achieve high Attack Success Rates (ASR: 94–97% prompt-specific, 67–94% prompt-general) on HarmBench. Reviewer ratings range from 3 to 5; Reviewer 2 is notably more positive (score 5) regarding effectiveness and interpretability contributions, while the other three reviewers (score 3) raise significant concerns about novelty, practicality, and evaluation scope.

### Strengths
- The idea of re-weighting MLP neurons is illustrated clearly, with helpful equations (e.g., line 113) and Table 2 presenting results concisely (Reviewer 1).
- Two distinct strategies (prompt-specific and prompt-general) are proposed, both achieving high ASR across diverse model sizes (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The prompt-general method generalizes to unseen harmful prompts, suggesting broader applicability (Reviewer 2).
- The approach also serves as an interpretability tool: it can identify specific MLP neurons strongly correlated with safety, offering insights for future alignment studies (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Provides empirical evidence that safety assessments may concentrate during end-of-sentence inferences, which could inform future safety research (Reviewer 2).
- The research problem (understanding and attacking LLM safety mechanisms) is important, and the paper attempts to explain the phenomenon of high attention scores on special tokens (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic white-box assumption**: The method requires full access to model weights and the ability to modify internal components, which is rarely available to external attackers. This contrasts with more practical black-box scenarios studied in most concurrent jailbreak research. The paper lacks a strong justification for why this specific threat model is relevant in practice (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 4 adds that the method is essentially a variant of the GCG attack restricted to MLP layers, making the novelty incremental.
- **Heuristic approach lacking theoretical grounding**: The loss function is designed based on intuition (gradient ascent for jailbreak) without systematic analysis; the choice of re-weighting neurons may lead to suboptimal or unstable solutions. No formal justification for the loss form is provided, raising overfitting concerns (Reviewer 2).
- **Speculative role of MLP layers**: The paper hypothesizes that MLP layers in end-of-sentence inferences perform safety checks, but this is not conclusively established. The observed effects could result from complex network interactions; a detailed analysis of information flow through MLP layers is missing (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 4 notes that the “end-of-sentence” phenomenon described (high attention on special tokens) closely resembles the attention sink phenomenon (Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks) and is not novel; moreover, the so-called “end-of-sentence” is actually the beginning of generation.
- **Limited empirical scope and comparison**: Evaluation is confined to HarmBench; given the white-box nature, testing on more challenging benchmarks like JAMBench is expected (Reviewer 1). The paper should compare with fine-tuning approaches using adversarial datasets (e.g., Qi et al., 2023) and other “uncensored LLM” methods such as Abliteration (Maxime Labonne, 2024). Without such comparisons, the claimed novelty over existing attacks is questionable (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing cross-benchmark generalization study**: Since the method requires training, it is necessary to show whether the re-weighting factors trained on one benchmark (e.g., HarmBench) transfer to other benchmarks (e.g., AdvBench, JAMBench). The paper does not demonstrate this, which is critical for claiming practical applicability (Reviewer 1).
- **No cost-of-attack comparison**: The claim of “over 5x improvement in computational speed” (line 319) is unsupported by evidence; a detailed comparison of computational cost with other attacks is needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Unaddressed impact on other capabilities**: The paper does not deeply explore how MLP modifications affect other model capabilities (e.g., general task performance, bias). While some standard benchmarks are used, they may not capture subtle degradations; broader evaluation with bias detection is missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Writing and presentation issues**: Several definitions are unclear, e.g., “MLPs in end-of-sentence inferences” (L13), “reweighting” (L114) sounds like a dropout layer, and the motivation for studying MLP specifically is not clearly justified (Reviewer 4). The writing is informal in places (e.g., “We now complete it here” L222), definitions jump around, and a summarization figure introducing the main idea is missing (Reviewer 4). Certain claims are vague (e.g., “As a result” L66 without clear antecedent, “has a smaller impact” L69 without quantification).
- **Evaluation metric limitation**: The paper relies solely on Llama-Guard-3 binary classification (SAFE/UNSAFE), which may misclassify mixed responses (e.g., “Sure, here is how to build <bad-stuff>. However, as an AI, I cannot assist with this.”). Additional evaluation using other judge LLMs or human evaluation is recommended (Reviewer 3).
