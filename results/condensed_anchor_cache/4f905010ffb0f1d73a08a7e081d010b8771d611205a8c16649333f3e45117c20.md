- Scores: 3, 5, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents HADES, a Bayesian optimization method for protein sequence optimization that combines Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling with a structure-aware surrogate model (sequence encoder + fitness and structure decoders). It is tested on two proteins (GB1 and PhoQ) and compared against baselines like EvoPlay, AdaLead, and PEX. Reviewers were split: two rated 3 (weak) and two rated 5 (good). All reviewers note the novelty of using HMC in this setting and the clear presentation, but raise significant concerns about limited benchmarking, missing comparisons, technical clarity, and the actual benefit of the structure component.

### Strengths
- Novel combination of Hamiltonian dynamics (HMC) with a structure-aware surrogate model; leveraging physical intuition (momentum and uncertainty) to guide protein sequence search is a fresh approach (R2, R4; R1 also notes enhanced performance with fewer steps).
- Outperforms all chosen baselines on the two tested proteins GB1 and PhoQ (R1, R2, R4).
- Clear and well-organized presentation; ablation and scaling studies are appreciated and reasonably thorough (R2, R3).
- Ablation shows the HMC component itself contributes to performance gain (R2).
- The encoder-decoder architecture that incorporates (latent) structural relatedness into fitness scores is a physically sound idea worth exploring (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
**Insufficient Benchmarking**
- Only two small proteins (GB1, PhoQ) tested, each with only up to 4 mutated residues; this is a very small search space (R4). Missing more realistic and commonly used benchmarks such as AAV and GFP (R4; citing [1] and noting that even the authors’ cited reference [2] uses GFP).
- R1 also states the evaluation is not comprehensive; only two proteins limits evidence of general efficacy.
- Reviewers from both camps (R2: “limited range of tasks”; R3: “provide no comparison to … Kirjner et al.”; R4: “several highly related methods … not compared to”) call for baselines from recent protein optimization literature, including discrete diffusion methods (e.g., [3][4]), CbAS, DynPPO from FLEXS, and other latent-space search methods (e.g., [5]).
- R2 notes that performance gain over baselines like PEX is marginal and largely attributable to the improved surrogate model rather than HMC itself (Table 3). Recent works (e.g., arXiv:2307.00494) achieve substantially higher fitness on more challenging tasks.
- R4 and R3 both observe that the structure decoder ablation shows essentially no improvement; this contradicts the paper’s claimed advantage and makes the title and structural analysis misleading (R2, R3, R4). R3 speculates that aggregate RMSD from ESMFold may be noisy due to flexible regions.

**Clarity and Missing Technical Details**
- Method section is poorly organized; key details missing (R1):
  - Bayesian optimization is mentioned in the Introduction and Figure 1 but not explained in the Method section (R1).
  - Sequence encoder: no description of input features (e.g., relative positional encoding), hyperparameters (number of attention heads in Pairformer), or how Pairformer outputs (single and pair representations) are combined into a single latent vector (R1d).
  - “Intra-interactions” on line 275 is unclear (R1c).
  - UCB on line 206 is undefined (R1e).
- R3: It is not explained which oracle is used to evaluate candidate sequences; the relation between the oracle and surrogate models is unclear.
- R4: Multiple technical confusions:
  - Line 195: “Query ground truth fitness of X” – X is not defined.
  - Equations 2-4: what is ε?
  - Line 185: N is first defined as number of iterations, then used as number of surrogate models; inconsistency. Section 4.2 suggests two surrogates with different architectures, but “ensemble” implies distinct seeds – unclear.
  - In Section 4.2, the surrogate model architecture (two decoders) and the potential energy used for HMC are not clearly specified.
- R2 notes missing y-axis in Fig. 3 (PhoQ) and colorblind-unfriendly plots (especially Fig. 4).

**Theoretical and Methodological Concerns**
- R1: Metropolis-Hastings correction for discretization is mentioned (line 250) but its computational overhead is not analyzed.
- R3: HMC is described at length but the procedure differs from standard continuous Hamiltonian dynamics: discretization is performed after each move rather than allowing several continuous steps before discretization. This weakens the connection and should be studied further.
- R3: The shared encoder is trained to predict structural RMSD relative to wild type; it is unclear whether this means retraining for each new wild type sequence. Aggregate RMSD may be unreliable.
- R4: The benefit of HMC over discrete diffusion is not addressed; a direct comparison is missing.

**Novelty and Scope**
- R2 and R3 consider the novelty limited; the work is primarily an engineering combination of existing components (HMC, sequence encoder/decoder). Strong empirical results across more tasks would be needed to justify the contributions (R2).
- R2 and R4 note that the structure decoder, a focal point, does not help in practice; R2 points out that obtaining experimental protein structures is challenging in wet-lab cycles, further reducing utility.

**Missing Analyses and Metrics**
- R4: The diversity metric fDiv weights sequences by fitness and hides actual sequence diversity; authors should report standard metrics like sequence diversity and similarity to best sequence (as in [1] and [5]).
- R2: Suggest showing mean fitness (at least in appendix) in addition to max fitness.
- R2: Expand discussion on limitations.

**Reviewer Disagreement**
- R1 and R4 (score 3) are most critical, deeming the paper preliminary with toy experiments and insufficient clarity for a conference venue. R2 and R3 (score 5) acknowledge the novelty and clarity but agree on the need for more extensive evaluation, stronger baselines, and resolving the discrepancy between claimed structure advantage and ablation results.
