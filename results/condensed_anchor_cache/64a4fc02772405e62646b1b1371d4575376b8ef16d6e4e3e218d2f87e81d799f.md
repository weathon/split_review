## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Video Action Differencing, a new task for identifying subtle differences between videos of the same action. A benchmark, VidDiffBench, is constructed from 557 (or 656 – see weakness) video pairs from four datasets (Fitness, Ball Sports, Diving, Music, Surgery), with 4,719 annotated fine-grained differences and 2,075 timestamps. A three-stage agent-based system, VidDiff, is proposed, using LLMs and VLMs for difference proposal, keyframe localization (with CLIP and Viterbi), and verification. VidDiff outperforms baseline LMMs like GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Qwen2-VL on closed- and open-set tasks. The paper is seen as a novel contribution with real-world applications, but reviewers raise concerns about dataset statistics, bias, evaluation rigor, and missing analyses.

### Strengths
- **Novel task and benchmark:** The video action differencing task is new, with practical applications in coaching and skill acquisition. The benchmark covers five domains (fitness, ball sports, diving, music, surgery) and is highly challenging for current LMMs.
- **Sound dataset construction:** Technical details (splits, annotation process, re-annotation with low discrepancy) are well described. The dataset provides a unique, structured resource for fine-grained video comparison.
- **Effective method:** VidDiff outperforms off-the-shelf LMMs in both closed-set and open-set tasks. The agent-based decomposition (proposal, localization, verification) is a reasonable approach, and ablation studies demonstrate robustness.
- **Real-world relevance:** The task addresses challenges in skill-based learning environments and performance analysis.
- **Clarity and visualization:** The paper is generally clear and easy to follow; visualizations are informative.
- **Minority positive appraisal:** One reviewer (score 8, confidence 5) found the paper particularly strong, highlighting the interesting task and varied testbed. Other reviewers (scores 6) were more cautious, raising several concerns.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset size discrepancy:** The abstract states 557 video pairs and 4,719 differences, while Section 4 states 656 pairs and 5,580 differences. This needs clarification.
- **Missing comparisons with existing benchmarks:** The paper would benefit from a clearer table (similar to Table 1) comparing VidDiffBench to prior fine-grained video comparison datasets, highlighting unique aspects.
- **Incomplete baseline evaluations:** Only closed-source LMMs are tested; more open-source video VLMs (e.g., LLaVA-video) should be evaluated to help the community understand their limitations on this benchmark.
- **Lack of per-category/difficulty analysis:** The paper does not provide detailed analysis of where each model excels or fails within specific action categories or difficulty levels (easy/medium/hard). This limits understanding of model strengths/weaknesses.
- **Closed-set bias not checked:** The closed-set binary classification task may be biased if videos are not randomly swapped at test time. It is unclear whether models truly learn differences or exploit a preference for one video. Naive performance should be 50%; the authors should verify this and discuss the possibility of “C” (insignificant) answers.
- **Open-set evaluation protocol concerns:** The LLM-based open-set evaluation is prone to hallucination and inconsistency. Prompts used for the LLM are not discussed in detail, and reproducibility is uncertain. The authors should analyze how much LLM output varies per run and how well it aligns with human judgments.
- **Error propagation in multi-stage method:** Inaccuracies in the Difference Proposer (first stage) can cascade to later stages, especially in the open-set task. The paper lacks quantitative analysis of stage-wise error propagation and mitigation strategies.
- **LLM bias in difference proposal and difficulty splits:** The Difference Proposer relies on LLMs (not named; see below), which may introduce biases or inaccuracies for complex actions. No examples of successful/failed proposals are provided. Also, the use of an LLM (instead of human experts) to define easy/medium/hard splits is questionable and may introduce bias.
- **Missing specification of models used:** The specific LLMs/VLMs for the Difference Proposer and Action Differencer stages are not stated. This is critical for reproducibility.
- **Annotation objectivity and reliability:** Some annotations (e.g., “the movements in video A are more efficient than in video B”) are vague and may be interpreted inconsistently. The paper does not report inter-annotator agreement or discuss how subjective differences were handled. Annotator skill level, number, and remuneration are missing.
- **Uncurated videos introduce biases:** Factors such as different camera angles, varying FPS, and actor differences (e.g., height) may affect comparisons like “faster arms” or “deeper squat.” The authors do not address how these biases are mitigated.
- **Temporal alignment claim unproven:** The introduction claims to address temporal alignment, but it is unclear how VidDiff specifically solves this beyond using CLIP+Viterbi. More explanation is needed.
- **Frame localizer analysis missing:** The paper does not visualize or evaluate selected frames against ground-truth frames, nor quantify the effect of using predicted vs. ground-truth frames.
- **Missing Table 7:** Section 5.3 references a Table 7 that is not present in the paper.
- **Paper structure and readability issues:** The task is not formally defined before the dataset is described, making the paper harder to follow. Method details appear in the experiments section despite being a main contribution. Important content is split across the main paper and appendix, requiring excessive jumping. Specific errors: Line 158 refers to the wrong table (should be Table 1); appendix contains typos (vary → very) and an empty Section D.1.
- **Missing dataset statistics:** Basic statistics are lacking: average video length per category, total hours, number of A/B/C annotations per action category, temporal distribution of timestamps, and whether there are temporal biases.
- **Inverse correlation handling:** It is unclear how the benchmark handles inverse correlations (e.g., “lower squat in A” vs. “higher squat in B”). Are both versions present or treated equivalently?
- **Suggested experiment:** One reviewer suggests duplicating the same video and feeding it to methods to check if predictions yield 50% responses (ideally recognizing no difference).
