## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SaMer, a scenario-aware multi-dimensional evaluator for LLM responses. It uses a frozen LLaMA‑3‑8B encoder with three MLP heads to predict relevant evaluation dimensions (from a fixed set of 42) and their weights for an overall score, trained on a large-scale fine-grained preference dataset (135k instances, 36 scenarios, 42 dimensions). Experiments on single rating and pairwise comparison benchmarks show competitive performance against both proprietary and open-source baselines. Reviewers agree that the motivation for fine-grained, flexible evaluation is sound and the dataset is a valuable contribution. However, significant concerns are raised about the methodology (limited novelty, lack of ablation, reliance on GPT‑4o annotations without quality verification), the validity of the predefined dimensions, missing experiments on dimension prediction, and missing related work. One reviewer (score 8) is more positive, while the other two (score 6) are more critical, particularly regarding the empirical validation of the core claims.

### Strengths
- The paper is clear, well-structured, and communicates complex ideas effectively (Reviewer 1).
- The fine-grained preference dataset (135k data points, 42 dimensions, 36 scenarios) is a valuable contribution to the community (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
- SaMer provides more interpretable evaluation results than single-score evaluators by outputting weights across dimensions (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- The model is task-agnostic: it can implicitly determine which evaluation dimensions are needed solely from the query, without requiring explicit task information (Reviewer 2).
- The evaluation is thorough, benchmarking against both proprietary models (e.g., GPT‑4o) and open-source alternatives across multiple settings (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
- SaMer shows performance improvements over baselines on single rating, pairwise comparison, and fine-grained dimension-specific benchmarks (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited methodological novelty**: The individual components (fixed encoder, MLP heads, standard losses) are standard; the main contribution is in the application rather than new techniques (Reviewer 1). Reviewers 2 and 3 further note that the 42 evaluation dimensions are fixed/pre‑defined, which limits SaMer’s ability to generalize to unforeseen scenarios, unlike prior work X‑Eval that allows user‑defined dimension definitions (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of ablation / hyperparameter analysis**: The three loss functions (L_zlpr, L_dim, L_o) are combined with fixed lambdas (λ₁, λ₂) without any ablation study or sensitivity analysis. This makes it impossible to understand the individual contribution of each loss component or the robustness of the approach (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). Other hyperparameters are reported as single values with no discussion of selection process (Reviewer 1).
- **Heavy reliance on GPT‑4o for annotation without quality control**: The fine-grained preference data and many scenario labels are generated by GPT‑4o. The paper does not discuss how potential biases (e.g., confusion between dimensions, as noted in [Hu et al., ACL 2024]) were addressed (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). No human verification steps or inter‑annotator agreement are reported to ensure annotation reliability (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). This raises concerns that the training data may reflect GPT‑4o’s own limitations (Reviewer 2).
- **Unclear / subjective selection of evaluation dimensions**: The 42 dimensions and their assignment to scenarios were done by three graduate students, but no definitions, annotation guidelines, or inter‑annotator agreement are provided. From Table A2, many dimensions appear vague or redundant (e.g., “Being Friendly” vs. “Audience Friendly”; “Accuracy” and “Admit Uncertainty” both included; “Logic” missing for “reading comprehension” while “Objectivity” included for “Recommendation”) (Reviewer 3). The model never even sees the dimension definitions during training (Reviewer 2).
- **No experimental validation of the dimension‑prediction component**: One of SaMer’s key claims is that it can automatically predict relevant evaluation dimensions per scenario. However, no experiment evaluates this ability (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). Suggested baselines include having LLMs generate reasonable dimensions and comparing with human judgment (Reviewer 3). The paper only evaluates overall scoring or fixed dimensions, not whether the selected dimensions are appropriate (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing related work on multi‑dimensional / explainable evaluation**: Several prior works (e.g., [1–3]) on fine-grained and explainable evaluation are not discussed, which weakens the contextualization of SaMer’s contributions (Reviewer 3).
- **Potential unfair comparisons**: SaMer’s training heavily relies on GPT‑4o annotations, while many baselines may not have used this newer teacher model, possibly giving SaMer an advantage (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing implementation details**: Code, model checkpoints, and dataset licensing are not mentioned (Reviewer 1). The specific version of GPT‑4o used is not specified, hindering reproducibility (Reviewer 1). The supplementary materials lack data samples (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing efficiency analysis**: The paper does not discuss runtime or computational cost compared to other evaluators, especially given SaMer’s multi‑step process (predict dimensions, scores, weights) (Reviewer 3).
- **Metrics section unclear**: Section 5.2 on metrics requires more detailed explanation (Reviewer 1). The calculation of dimension‑level accuracy in Table 5 is not described (Reviewer 2).
- **Minor issues**: Typo “Demension” in Figure 3 should be “Dimension” (Reviewer 2). In Table 2, SaMer’s Spearman and Kendall correlations on Vicuna Bench are significantly higher than GPT‑4o’s, yet Pearson is comparable; the paper does not explain this discrepancy (Reviewer 3).
- **No OOD evaluation**: The paper does not demonstrate whether SaMer can accurately determine relevant dimensions for out‑of‑domain scenarios (Reviewer 2). The quality verification of the MD‑Eval test set used in Table 5 is also insufficiently described (Reviewer 2).
