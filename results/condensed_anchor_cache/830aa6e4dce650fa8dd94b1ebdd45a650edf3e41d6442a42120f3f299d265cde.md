## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers acknowledged the paper’s well-structured exploration of using text prompts to provide degradation priors for image super-resolution, and the proposed text-image generation pipeline and PromptSR. However, major concerns were raised about limited novelty, redundancy of the text encoder, missing comparisons with recent diffusion-based SR methods (e.g., PASD, SeeSR, CoSeR), insufficient analysis of robustness and generalization, and lack of user studies. One reviewer was more positive (score 6), while the other three were critical (scores 3, 3, 5), citing that the approach appears outdated given rapid advances in the field.

### Strengths
- Well-structured paper exploring the role of textual information in super-resolution (R3).
- Introduction of a text-image generation pipeline that integrates text into SR datasets via degradation representation (R1, R4).
- Proposal of PromptSR, which leverages pre-trained language models (T5 or CLIP) to enhance restoration (R1, R4).
- Effectiveness demonstrated on synthetic and real-world images, with comparisons to generative models like FeMaSR and DiffBIR (R1 – more positive reviewer).
- Adequate analysis (R1); method well described (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: Degradation-guided SR has been extensively explored (DASR, Textual Prompt Guided Image Restoration, Dcs-risr, DaLPSR). The proposed method is not significantly different from DASR; text only provides degradation classification and does not add clear value beyond DASR’s degradation features (R3).
- **Redundancy of text encoder**: Using a text encoder for discrete degradations (11 predefined options) is redundant; could replace with a set of learnable embeddings. Also unclear if the method works when degradation descriptions are rephrased (e.g., “heavy blur” → “very blurry”) (R2, R4).
- **Missing comparisons with state-of-the-art methods**, especially diffusion-based SR methods: PASD, SeeSR, CoSeR, Scaling Up to Excellence, SinSR, Exploiting Diffusion Prior for Real-World ISR. This makes the paper unconvincing and outdated (R2, R3, R4).
- **Insufficient comparison with existing text-prompt SR methods**: Paper lacks discussion and comparison with PASD and SeeSR, which also employ textual prompts (R2).
- **Weak evidence for degradation prompts over content prompts**: Section 3.1.2 claims degradation prompts are superior, but does not clarify how the “overall caption” result was generated and relies on a single example; further analysis needed (R2).
- **MLLM-based real-world inference**: No analysis of how often the MLLM-generated prompt matches the true degradation; should report prompt accuracy (R2).
- **Qualitative comparisons incomplete**: BSRGAN, which outperforms or closely matches on some metrics in Table 7, is not shown in qualitative results. Also, Figure 7’s first comparison lacks the DAN result while the second includes it; proper qualitative comparison with all methods needed (R2).
- **Missing user study** on synthetic and real-world datasets (R2).
- **Limited real-world evaluation**: Should conduct experiments on more real-world datasets, e.g., DRealSR (R4).
- **Out-of-distribution generalization issues**: Not addressed how the method handles degradation combinations at inference (e.g., testing image has blur+noise but prompt only includes blur, noise, compression) or at training (training on pairs of two degradations, testing on three) (R1).
- **Missing inference time** report (R1).
- **Scale factor inclusion questioned**: Prompt includes “Resize” (scale factor) as a degradation; unclear why and whether it is flexibly embeddable into the SR model (R1).
- **Missing demonstration of user-friendliness**: It is not shown how the text prompt is more user-friendly than directly providing 0–1 strength values for each degradation (R4).
