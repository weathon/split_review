## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a dual-agent adversarial framework for improving generalization in reinforcement learning. Two identical policy networks are trained in an alternating minimax game: each tries to perturb the other's policy by producing representation differences for the same state while maintaining its own stability. Theoretical analysis derives a lower bound on generalization and links robustness to irrelevant features to better generalization. Experiments on 8 of the 16 ProcGen games compare against PPO and DAAC, showing performance improvements especially on hard levels.

### Strengths
- The dual-agent adversarial setup is a novel approach to tackling generalization without relying on human-designed data augmentations or priors.
- The paper provides a theoretical analysis that derives a lower bound on generalization and connects robustness to irrelevant features with improved generalization—a contribution that can apply broadly.
- The framework requires no extra network parameters beyond the two copies of the policy, and adds only one hyperparameter (α).
- The method is empirically shown to outperform PPO and DAAC baselines on the ProcGen benchmark, particularly on hard-level tasks.
- The paper is well-written, with clear presentation and logical flow from theory to algorithm to results.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only 8 of the 16 ProcGen games are tested. The benchmark itself is procedurally generated but still within a single game paradigm. Several reviewers recommend additional environments such as DMC-GB, and baselines like PIE-G, SVEA, ARPO, IDAAC, PPG, DRAC, and RARL to better contextualize performance and novelty.
- **Fairness of comparison and statistical rigor**: Table 1 reports results with only 3 random seeds and no standard deviations. A contradiction exists between Table 1 and Figure 5 for Climber: Table 1 shows DAAC=3.299 and PPO+Adv. Agent1=4.473, but Figure 5 shows DAAC as the highest score. Reviewers question whether the improvement stems from better generalization or simply better convergence, and note that in-distribution performance should be matched before claiming generalization gains.
- **Missing critical baselines**: IDAAC (from the DAAC paper) and PPG are known to achieve strong ProcGen results and are absent. Moreover, recent works [1,2] report substantially higher scores on the full 16-game benchmark, which are not compared.
- **Adversarial training concerns**: The paper’s introduction mischaracterizes prior adversarial learning work, as recent studies show adversarially trained policies actually generalize worse than standard RL. The proposed method is not tested against adversarial examples. Multi-agent adversarial optimization risks mode collapse, vanishing gradients, and convergence issues, but the paper does not analyze training stability or the trade-off between robustness and performance as α varies.
- **Computational and architectural overhead**: The dual-agent setup effectively doubles the number of encoder parameters. It is unclear if prior methods would also benefit from this parameter increase, making the comparison unfair. The wall-clock time and number of gradient steps are not reported, making it hard to assess efficiency.
- **Theoretical novelty questioned**: One reviewer (score 3) states the theoretical claims follow almost immediately from prior work and bring no new knowledge. Others find the theory solid but note that the derivation of the bound assumes bounded training MDPs (unbounded case is not treated), and the two characteristics in Section 4.2 are not proven sufficient for robust generalization.
- **Lack of in-depth analysis**: No qualitative or quantitative analysis of the learned representations is provided to validate that the adversarial process actually suppresses irrelevant features. Ablation studies are missing on the adversarial perturbation strength and alternative adversarial training algorithms. The method’s applicability to continuous action spaces, real-world tasks, and context-dependent feature relevance is not discussed.
- **Clarity issues**: Section 2 introduces MDP state semantics and subscript _m with insufficient motivation. The definition of distribution p_M is unclear. The weights in Equation 19 appear to be omitted without justification. Limitations and future work are not discussed in the paper.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The choice of α is critical for controlling the adversarial loss weight but no sensitivity analysis or selection criterion is provided. There is no discussion of how α affects training dynamics.
- **Unaddressed questions**: Is the dual-agent setup beneficial with heterogeneous encoders? How does the method perform with different online RL algorithms beyond PPO? Does the approach handle environments where the distinction between relevant and irrelevant features is ambiguous or evolving? Does adversarial training risk catastrophic forgetting of useful features?
