## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can autonomously generate long-horizon plans without external feedback or complex frameworks, building on a novel prompting technique, AoT+ (Algorithm-of-Thoughts Plus), which extends the earlier AoT method. AoT+ introduces periodic structured state generation (memoization-inspired) to reduce state hallucination and random trajectory augmentation to simplify prompt construction. Evaluated on benchmarks like Blocksworld and Logistics, it achieves state-of-the-art results with lower token usage than many prior methods, and claims to out-compete human baselines in some domains. However, the paper is criticized for missing implementation details, limited general-domain evaluation, lack of variance reporting, and insufficient comparison to search-based baselines.

### Strengths

- **Novel perspective challenging both overly pessimistic and optimistic views of LLM planning** (R1, R5).
- **Creative combination of existing ideas**: periodic state regeneration to manage attention/cognitive load (R1, R2) and random trajectory augmentation to avoid rigid human-crafted sequences (R2, R5).
- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation** across multiple challenging benchmarks with clear ablation (AoT vs. AoT+) and ablation experiments on attention supporting the state-hallucination hypothesis (R1, R4).
- **State-of-the-art performance without external verification tools** (R2).
- **Efficient single-prompt framework**: reduces token usage and latency compared to ToT and is more efficient than AoT itself (R2).
- **Model-agnostic**: consistent performance improvements across various LLMs (GPT-4, LLaMA-13B, etc.) (R2).
- **High presentation quality** in terms of logical structure, clear figures, and thorough analysis across multiple prompting strategies (R5).
- **Introduction of "System 3 thinking"** as a deliberate decision-making concept (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of systematic analysis of where AoT+ fails** (R1, R2). No detailed error analysis of state hallucinations or identification of specific failure points across problem types (R2, R4). The hypothesis that hallucinations stem from continuous recomputation is not fully validated over the length of solution traces (R4).
- **Insufficient isolation of each innovation’s impact**: ablation studies for random trajectories and memoization are performed on different models/domains, not under the same settings as the main results (R1, R4).
- **Missing comparisons to key baselines**: search-based methods such as MCTS-Decoding or A* are not included – the paper should explain why (R3). Several relevant works operating on Blocksworld ([2–4] and [5]) are omitted (R3).
- **Incomplete human performance comparison**: the claim of “out-competing human baselines” is only supported by a single comparison on Logistics where AoT+ with GPT-4 slightly outperforms humans; no human performance data is provided for other benchmarks (R4).
- **Scalability concerns**: AoT+ assumes a PDDL instance of the problem; scalability to general, non-PDDL domains is unclear (R1). The method requires explicit state caching, which adds complexity, risks error propagation from flawed state representations, and introduces memory overhead for long horizons or resource-constrained settings (R5).
- **Missing implementation details**: it is not specified how correct solution paths are interwoven with random trajectories (R4), how memoization is implemented (R4, R5) – including the exact prompt modifications, caching mechanism, and whether tokens for problem definitions are cached (R5). The specific version of LLaMA-13B and which Claude model (3.5? Sonnet?) are not stated (R4, R5). The term CoT-SC in Table 1 is not explained (R4).
- **Token cost comparison table (Table 3) lacks baselines CoT and AoT**, making it impossible to contextualize trade-offs between performance and resource usage (R5). Results in that table also lack variance/confidence intervals (R4).
- **Figures 1 and 4 are not informative**; pipeline descriptions are lacking; Figure 3 should be a table (R4).
- **The observation that random in-context examples do not hurt performance is not new** – Min (2022) already demonstrated that random labels barely hurt performance on classification tasks (R4).
- **Questions that reflect missing experiments or design justifications**:  
  - Is there a scalable way to promote AoT+ beyond PDDL domains? (R1)  
  - How interpretable are the decision paths? Can users trace errors in the planning sequence? (R2)  
  - Would intermediate reward structures improve long-horizon accuracy? (R2)  
  - Why might random trajectories provide greater flexibility than carefully crafted ones? (R5)  
  - What considerations are needed for representing complex states like in ALFWorld? (R5)  
  - How is memoization actually incorporated – does it cache tokens for problem definitions? (R5)  

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (R2) rated the paper 8 and was notably more positive, emphasizing the efficiency and model-agnostic nature, while the other four gave a score of 6. R5 rated contribution 4, while others gave 2 or 3, indicating a minority of higher enthusiasm.
