## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Toxicity Avoiding Supervised Fine Tuning (TA-SFT), which uses an Earth Mover Distance (EMD) based semantic loss to push the LLM away from unsafe responses during SFT. It requires only a small set of unsafe responses (0.5% of typical safety data) and no safe examples, reducing human effort. A novel lower bound for EMD loss is introduced for efficient optimization. Evaluated on Llama 7b, Llama 13b, and Mistral 7b, it achieves improved safety while maintaining standard reasoning and conversational quality.  
*Reviewer disagreement:* One reviewer (score 8) was strongly positive, emphasizing data efficiency, lack of need for safe examples, and preserved standard evals. Three reviewers (score 6) raised several concerns regarding missing jailbreak evaluation, over-alignment, computational complexity, and clarity.

### Strengths
- **Data efficiency:** Only 0.5% of safety-related data required; safe answers are not needed, eliminating costly human annotation (all reviewers).
- **Innovative EMD loss:** Novel application of EMD as a semantic penalty term; the optimizable lower bound makes computation tractable (R2, R3, R4; R1 calls it “novel EMD based loss”).
- **Maintained response quality:** Standard benchmarks (reasoning, conversational) are unaffected after training; performance is competitive across multiple model architectures (Llama 7b, 13b, Mistral 7b) (R1, R2, R3; R4 notes comparable/superior performance).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing jailbreak evaluation:** Multiple works [1,2,3] question generalization of safety training via jailbreaking. The paper does not test jailbreak attacks. R1 strongly recommends a proper study comparing safe-example training, safe+unsafe (EMD), and unsafe-only (EMD) with various sample sizes, and evaluating under jailbreak attacks to prove practical robustness.
- **Over‑alignment persists:** Models show increased refusal rates for benign prompts as training progresses; the paper only partially addresses over‑alignment (R2). *R1 considers this issue a “good start” but not a weakness – a disagreement.*
- **Computational complexity of EMD:** The lower bound helps, but EMD computation may limit scalability for very large datasets (R2).
- **Benchmark dependence:** Performance is evaluated mainly on specific safety datasets; effectiveness against real‑world or evolving toxic inputs remains untested (R2).
- **Contrastive data degradation:** AI‑generated contrastive samples caused unexpected response quality degradation, raising concerns about noise and synthetic data robustness (R2).
- **Vocabulary‑wide EMD concern:** The loss considers the entire token distribution, but many tokens in harmful responses are harmless individually; maximizing distance might degrade original LLM capabilities. R3 suggests extracting only harmful keywords for EMD loss.
- **Missing ablation for limited data:** R3 asks whether simply upscaling the loss for safety‑related QA pairs or oversampling could achieve similar improvements when safety data is scarce.
- **Clarity issue:** The probability distribution \(P(\cdot|w_{<t-1})\) in line 186 is not clearly explained (R4).
- **Effect of dataset size:** The paper shows good performance with small data, but does not explore whether increasing the dataset size would further improve performance (R4).
- **Formatting and expression errors:** Multiple issues throughout the manuscript (e.g., line 129 missing citation, line 120 subscript clarification needed, missing punctuation after equations, incorrect citation format lines 248‑253, undefined symbols) (R2).
