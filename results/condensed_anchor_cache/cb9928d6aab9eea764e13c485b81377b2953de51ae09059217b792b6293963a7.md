## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CII-Bench, a benchmark of 698 images and 800 multiple-choice questions across six categories, designed to evaluate MLLMs’ higher-order perception and understanding of Chinese images, especially their deep implications and cultural context. Images are sourced from the Chinese Internet and manually reviewed. Through extensive experiments on open- and closed-source models, the authors find that the best MLLM (Qwen2-VL-72B) achieves 64.4% accuracy, far below average human accuracy of 78.2% (peaking at 81.0%). Models perform significantly worse on Chinese traditional culture images, and incorporating emotion hints improves accuracy. The dataset is publicly available.

### Strengths
- Understanding Chinese image implication is an interesting and high-level capacity for MLLMs (R1, R2).
- The dataset is constructed using a rigorous pipeline with repeated image filtering and consistency checks, ensuring high quality (R1, R5). R1 specifically rates dataset quality highly; R5 notes the pipeline includes repeated filtering and manual annotation.
- The paper is well-written and easy to read (R2, R5).
- Evaluations are conducted on a large number of both open-source and proprietary MLLMs, providing detailed analysis from multiple perspectives (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5). R2 and R5 note the evaluation is extensive; R1 considers the analysis sufficient; R4 highlights detailed analyses.
- The prompting strategies used (Chain-of-Thought, few-shot) are quite exhaustive, making results informative and instructive (R5). R5 also notes this exceeds prior work.
- This is the first benchmark specifically for Chinese image representation understanding (R3). R1 and R4 also note the topic is novel and the construction process is clearly presented.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset size is very small (698 images, 800 questions)**, which may not be comprehensive enough to evaluate MLLMs and may make conclusions non-generalizable (R1, R2, R4, R5). R1 also raises concerns about statistical significance and potential biases due to few data points per aspect (R4).
- **Multiple-choice evaluation may not reveal the real capacity** to understand implications; models may exhibit choice bias. R5 specifically suggests using CircularEval from MMBench to ensure more robust results (R1, R5).
- **The benchmark is highly similar to II-Bench** (Liu et al., 2024), replicating its approach on Chinese data with no clear additional data collection or management innovations. The contribution is considered small for a top conference. R2 and R4 argue the work appears derivative; R3 states the two papers could be combined. The authors are advised to clarify additional contributions and discuss consistencies/inconsistencies with II-Bench (R2, R3, R4).
- **Lacks comparison with CCBench**, a benchmark specifically evaluating MLLMs’ understanding of Chinese culture images (R5).
- **Some images are not particularly representative of Chinese imagery** (e.g., Figures G3, G4 in appendix) (R5). R3 notes that Figure G1 examples are too simple and should be more complex. R3 also suggests adding real-scenario Chinese images (not just Internet memes) and more traditional culture datasets (frescoes, landscape paintings) with expert calibration.
- **The claimed motivation that Chinese images are more complex than English images is not convincingly demonstrated.** The English images in Fig.1 are not a fair comparison, and CII-Bench contains many Meme images, which may not differ greatly from English memes (R2, R4). R2 asks for analysis of when Chinese vs. English images present unique challenges.
- **Missing details about dataset composition:** the ratio of the six image types is not provided (R2). R5 also requests an explanation of the rationale behind choosing these six categories.
- **Text-only models can answer some questions correctly without images** (Table 1), indicating those questions are inappropriate for a multimodal benchmark and should be removed (R5).
- **Prompting strategies are not fully explored or explained:** It is unclear if all samples use the same prompts in CoT evaluation; CoT and few-shot gave worse results, which is unexplained (R1). R4 questions whether more complex prompts (background information, CoT) were designed to better guide model output. R2 notes the paper gives no suggestions for future research to close the human-MLLM gap.
- **No technical innovations are proposed** to improve benchmark results or address the identified shortcomings (R4).
