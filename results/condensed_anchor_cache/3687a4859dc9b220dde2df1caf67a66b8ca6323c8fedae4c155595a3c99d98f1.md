## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a semi-automatic pipeline using VLMs (PaLI-3) and LLMs to generate dense, time-aligned captions and challenging question-answer-decoy (QAD) sets for videos up to 15 minutes. This produces the Neptune dataset (3,268 QAD annotations for 2,405 videos), covering a broad range of long video reasoning abilities with a multimodal subset. A new open-source evaluation metric, GEM (Gemma Equivalence Metric), is proposed for open-ended responses. Benchmark evaluations show current open-source long video models perform poorly on Neptune, especially on temporal ordering, counting, and state changes. Two subsets are introduced for comprehensive assessment.

### Strengths
- The semi-automatic pipeline reduces manual annotation burden and scales well while maintaining quality, using GPT for question generation. (R1, R3)
- GEM is a static, open-source, fine-tuned metric that addresses limitations of rule-based/proprietary metrics for open-ended VideoQA. (R1, R3)
- Clear motivation and articulation of the work’s importance. (R1)
- Neptune addresses essential temporal-aware question types including temporal ordering and counting. (R2)
- Two subsets are introduced to comprehensively assess current multimodal LLMs. (R2)
- The benchmark covers a wide range of video lengths and types, ensuring diversity and robust platform for real-world scenarios. (R3)
- Benchmark evaluations reveal significant performance gaps for open-source models compared to proprietary ones like Gemini-1.5 and GPT-4. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- Insufficient comparison with recent benchmarks (VideoMME, MLVU, LongVideoBench) and latest open-source models (InternVL, LLaVA-OneVision, MiniCPM). Comparisons beyond EgoSchema are needed to highlight Neptune’s unique challenges. (R1, R3)
- Frame captions generated by PaLI-3 may introduce VLM biases/hallucinations (e.g., preference for static images), affecting QA quality. (R1)
- Analysis of number of input frames (Table 3) is too simplistic: only 1/150/All frames for Gemini. Granularity (e.g., 50, 100 frames) is needed to find saturation point. (R1)
- Single-frame assessment fails to reflect temporal modeling significance; temporal sensitivity should be evaluated by reversing or shuffling input frames. (R1)
- GEM accuracy is ~70%, similar or inferior to direct assessment with Gemini 1.5 Pro. No comparison with human evaluation consistency. It is unclear how GEM compares to fine-tuned methods like Lingo-Judge which reportedly approach human-level accuracy. (R1, R3)
- QA type distribution is long-tail, dominated by Temporal Ordering tasks. Reviewer argues ordering tasks are simple temporal reasoning, not the claimed complex human-difficult questions, questioning the richness of generated questions. (R1)
- Model naming inconsistency (e.g., VideoLLaMA2 vs VideoLlaMA2). (R2)
- Pipeline novelty is limited: lacks detailed analysis and comparison with other existing pipelines to highlight unique aspects. (R3)
- Figure 4: EgoSchema saturation at ~16 frames uses only Gemini; open-source models (short/long-context MLLMs) should be included, as saturation points may vary by architecture. This analysis is crucial for generalizability. (R2)
- Language-only Gemini (without ASR) outperforms visual models (BLIP2, Video-LLaVA); blind model (0 frames) achieves comparable/higher GEM score than full-frame. This raises concerns about ASR impact and the need to mitigate it to encourage vision-centric MLLMs. (R2)
