## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Guided Positive Sampling Self-Supervised Learning (GPS-SSL), a method that injects prior knowledge into SSL by using a pretrained encoder (supervised ResNet-50, CLIP, or VAE) to create a metric space for nearest-neighbor sampling of positive pairs, thereby reducing reliance on data augmentation. Experiments combine GPS with SimCLR, BYOL, Barlow Twins, and VICReg on five datasets, and empirically show improvements especially when weak augmentations are used. While reviewers found the direction interesting, they raised concerns about fairness, novelty, computational cost, and experimental completeness.

### Strengths

- Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the GPS strategy across multiple SSL methods and datasets.
- The paper is easy to follow.
- Studying strategies that reduce dependence on heavy handcrafting of data augmentation in SSL is worthwhile.
- Exploring the use of pretrained models (CLIP, supervised, VAE) to improve SSL is interesting.
- The method’s ability to improve performance with weak augmentations is notable.

### Weaknesses

- **Reliance on prior knowledge undermines SSL principles**: Using a pretrained visual model and the target dataset as prior diverges from the core idea of learning representations without labels. Performance becomes contingent on the quality/relevance of the pretrained model, introducing a dependency that is absent in standard SSL.
- **Fairness of comparisons**: The pretrained model may leak task-specific information or biases, giving GPS-SSL an unfair advantage over methods starting from random initialization. It is difficult to isolate the contribution of the positive sampling strategy from the benefits of the pretrained encoder. Reviewer 2 initially flagged this concern and later noted that ablation experiments partially addressed it, but the fundamental issue remains.
- **Limited novelty over existing work**: The core mechanism of using nearest neighbors in an embedding space for positive sampling is already present in NNCLR. The paper does not demonstrate a sufficient conceptual leap beyond that approach.
- **High computational overhead**: Using a ResNet-50 to generate positive samples is significantly more expensive than standard data augmentation pipelines. This cost is not convincingly justified by the performance gains, especially when strong augmentations already achieve competitive results.
- **Marginal improvement with strong augmentations**: With strong augmentations, GPS-SSL often yields minimal advantage and can be worse than baselines (e.g., VICReg in Table 2). In the weak augmentation setting, performance still lags behind the optimal strong-augmentation baselines, raising doubts about the method’s overall contribution.
- **Missing critical baselines**: 
  - Table 2 should include supervised results (i.e., training with labels) as a reference.  
  - A linear evaluation of the pretrained encoders (CLIP RN50, supervised RN50) on each downstream dataset is needed to understand their contribution to GPS-SSL’s performance.  
  - Methods like MAE that perform well with minimal augmentation should be compared, including fine-tuning accuracy metrics.
- **Insufficient training length**: All experiments use only 200 epochs of pretraining, while modern SSL methods typically require 800–1000 epochs to converge. Comparisons with longer training schedules are necessary for a fair evaluation.
- **Clarity and missing details**:
  - The metric reported in Table 1 (top-1 accuracy or error?) is not clearly stated.
  - Theorem 1 does not specify whether Equations (2) or (3) are integrated into Equation (1).
  - It is not explained how τ (tau) is set in Equation (3).
  - Section 4.1: it is unclear how predictions are made when training and test classes do not overlap (unseen classes).
  - Table 2 uses two different kinds of prior knowledge; the reason for this choice is not justified.
- **Inability to work without augmentation**: GPS-SSL still requires data augmentation (as seen in GPS-SimCLR, Figure 1); it cannot function without any augmentation. This limits its claim of reducing augmentation dependence, especially compared to methods like MAE that succeed with minimal or no augmentation.
