## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses compositional generalization (CG) to unseen domain–class combinations in multi-domain, multi-class settings. It introduces CG-Bench, a suite of CG benchmarks derived from real-world image datasets, and proposes Compositional Feature Alignment (CFA), a two-stage finetuning technique that learns orthogonal linear heads for class and domain and then fine-tunes the encoder. Evaluations are performed on CLIP and DINOv2 pretrained models, with theoretical justification and experiments demonstrating effectiveness. However, several concerns about practical significance, benchmark limitations, and strength of empirical gains are raised.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a new and important problem: generalization to unseen domain–class combinations in multi-domain multi-class settings.
- CG-Bench is a novel, well-curated benchmark suite that provides a valuable resource for the community to evaluate compositional generalization.
- The proposed CFA method is novel and interesting; it works well on simple datasets such as Color-CIFAR.
- The paper is well-written and easy to understand. Figure 2 effectively explains the compositional feature structure, and Figure 4 provides a compelling visual comparison of feature disentanglement between vanilla CLIP/DINOv2 and CFA features – one reviewer particularly likes this figure.
- Visualization experiments (e.g., Figure 4) clearly demonstrate that learned features become disengaged across classes and domains.
- The method is supported by theoretical justification and a clear, logically structured two-stage process.

### Weaknesses
- The distinction between compositional generalization and domain generalization / open-set tasks is not clearly defined. The paper should explain how CG differs from prior domain generalization settings.
- It is unclear why the two-stage training method achieves alignment of class information across domains. Visualizations of each training stage are requested. Additionally, training stability is not discussed; experiments varying the number of training steps in each stage should be provided.
- Ablation studies on the orthogonal loss and on different training stages are needed to understand the contribution of each component.
- Obtaining both class and domain labels is difficult, which limits scalability to real-world systems. The proposed approach cannot take advantage of unlabeled data, whereas CLIP and DINOv2 originally do not require explicit domain/class labels.
- Based on Table 1, the proposed method often hurts in-distribution (ID) accuracy compared to baselines.
- Theorem 1 holds only at the global minimum of the objective (4). In practice with large neural networks like CLIP, the global minimum is unlikely, raising questions about the practical significance of this theoretical guarantee.
- While feature disentanglement is shown for the simple Color-CIFAR dataset, it is unclear whether the same effect occurs for more complex datasets such as DomainNet. A similar analysis is requested.
- The performance gain of CFA over the reweight strategy is minimal (<1% in most cases). The largest gains come from WiSE-FT, not from CFA itself, so it is not clear whether the increased complexity of two-stage training is justified.
- In Table 1, the reweight + WiSE-FT baseline is missing for DINOv2. Without WiSE-FT, the gain over reweighting is <0.3% for OfficeHome and DomainNet.
- The caption of Figure 2 could be improved to better convey the goal of the proposed method.
- Additional explanation is needed for why the performance improvement over reweighting is so small.
