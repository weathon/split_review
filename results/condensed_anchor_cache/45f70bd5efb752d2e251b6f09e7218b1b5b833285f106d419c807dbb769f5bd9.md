## Merged Review

### Summary

This work addresses a critical vulnerability in neural network classifiers: adversarial attacks can significantly harm model calibration (reliability of confidence scores) while leaving accuracy unchanged. The authors introduce two new metrics for certified calibration—the Certified Brier Score (CBS) with a closed-form bound, and the Certified Calibration Error (CCE) approximated via mixed-integer programming. They propose Adversarial Calibration Training (ACT) with two variants (Brier‑ACT and ACCE‑ACT) to improve certified calibration while maintaining accuracy. The paper also generalizes existing attacks to \((\eta,\omega)\)‑ACE attacks.

### Strengths

- **Important vulnerability**: Calibration under adversarial attacks was not previously well‑studied; this is an important security concern for safety‑critical applications.
- **Novel metrics and algorithms**: The paper proposes novel certified calibration metrics (CBS, CCE) and a novel training algorithm (ACT) to improve certified calibration.
- **Well‑formulated mathematical foundations**: The theoretical development of the certified Brier score and calibration error is rigorous and includes appropriate theorems.
- **Well‑argued and mostly clear**: The motivation is strong, the paper is structured well, and the approach is novel and interesting. The mathematical presentation is detailed.
- **Extensive experiments**: The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of ACT across various scenarios with comprehensive evaluations.

### Weaknesses

1. **Approximate/probabilistic nature of certificates**  
   - The ACCE is an *approximation* of a certification guarantee; reviewer 4 explicitly states that “an approximation to a guarantee is only useful in rare circumstances” and that labelling it as a certification while breaking guarantees is problematic.  
   - Reviewer 2 asks whether the certificate for ACCE is actually sound or only approximate (see Line 238).  
   - Reviewer 3 notes that the certificates (C1, C2) are based on randomized smoothing and are therefore *probabilistic*, not deterministic; the paper does not distinguish this from non‑probabilistic verification methods (abstract interpretation, SMT solvers, LP solvers) that provide deterministic \(l_p\) certificates. The reviewer asks whether adapting abstract‑interpretation‑based approaches could work for calibration certification.  
   - The paper does not address the extensive literature on non‑probabilistic verification/certified training (IBP, CROWN, DeepPoly, Marabou, Neurify, DiffAI, COLT, SABR, etc.).

2. **Sensitivity to binning strategy**  
   - The entire certification framework for ECE depends heavily on the choice of bin boundaries and number of bins \(M\). Different binning strategies can lead to varying certified calibration values, introducing instability. The paper lacks a principled method for selecting the optimal binning.  
   - Reviewer 4 also asks explicitly about sensitivity to the number of bins \(M\) or their partitioning.

3. **Interpretability and practical significance of results**  
   - The reported ACCE values (e.g., 49.30 vs 41.32) lack clear real‑world interpretation; it is unclear whether such differences are substantial or minor improvements.  
   - Table 1 is difficult to interpret: the unattacked rows vs attacked rows, the switching behavior about \(\eta\), and what “higher is better or worse” are not clarified.  
   - Figure 4 uses overlapping circles that make it difficult to parse the actual impact of ACCE‑ACT, especially at smaller radii where improvement is not evident.  
   - The paper does not report means and standard deviations across different seeds or bootstrap variances for Table 1.  
   - Reviewer 4 asks how the “within 3% of the highest certified accuracy” selection was settled upon for Table 2.

4. **High computational cost**  
   - Training times are in the 100s of GPU hours, verification times in the 1000s of GPU hours, with a total of ~5000 GPU hours for the experiments (Appendix N). These costs approach those of finetuning multibillion‑parameter LLMs, making practical deployment questionable.

5. **Sparse motivation and practical applicability**  
   - The motivation for attacks on calibration (Section 2.2) is presented without citations for the claimed risks and uses, leaving the justifications speculative.  
   - Reviewer 3 notes that in practice accuracy still dominates, most state‑of‑the‑art defenses sacrifice accuracy, and the premise of this paper (protecting calibration when accuracy is already hard to defend) is hard to justify in real‑world settings, even though the method does not sacrifice much certified accuracy.

6. **Complex hyperparameter tuning for ADMM**  
   - The ADMM solver for ACCE is sensitive to the choice of penalty parameter and step size. Extensive tuning is required, making the approach less practical and potentially leading to inaccurate or unreliable certification if hyperparameters are suboptimal.

7. **Structural and clarity issues**  
   - The ordering of content is jumbled: certifications are required knowledge long before they are formally introduced (Line 177), and key concepts (e.g., certified models, adversarial attacks) are referenced without definition earlier.  
   - The paper would benefit from an overarching toy example to make Section 3 easier to follow.  
   - Table 1 uses font half the size of the standard maths font and is compressed.  
   - Multiple writing issues:  
     - Lines 232–233 have a grammatical error (“that is possible”).  
     - Line 160: notation unclear; equation 2 reads as maximizing \(\mathcal{L}_{CE}(f(x,\gamma), f(x,\gamma))\).  
     - Line 168: details of how adversarial training (ACT) is performed are missing (e.g., existing model finetuned vs trained from scratch? Standard PGD?).  
     - Line 180: the concept of deploying a model “with a threat model budget” and receiving a guarantee is confusing.  
     - Line 190: the distinction between probabilistic (randomized smoothing) and deterministic certificates is not made.  
     - Lines 118–132: many sentences lack citations, use incorrect punctuation, and introduce concepts (label attacks, confidence attacks) without explanation.

8. **Scalability**  
   - Reviewer 1 asks how the certification process scales with the number of classes (e.g., hundreds or thousands), and whether the approach is practical in that setting.

9. **Missing experimental details**  
   - The final loss optimized for Equation 2 is not specified.  
   - The interpretation of \(a_{i,j}\) (whether only one \(j\) can be 1) is unclear.  
   - Reviewer 3 asks for clarification on the adversarial training method used in the attack generation (line 168).  
   - No uncertainties or error bars are provided for Figure 3 or Table 2.  
   - Reviewer 2 asks whether the runtimes in Appendix G1 are for CIFAR‑10.  
   - Reviewer 2 asks for the considerations behind choosing ADMM.
