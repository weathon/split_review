## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a framework for online learning in two-player Stackelberg general-sum games. It maps the joint action space to a smooth Riemannian manifold (the "Stackelberg manifold") using neural normalizing flows, assumes the agents' reward functions are linear on that manifold, and then applies standard bandit algorithms to achieve efficient computation of the equilibrium. The paper provides regret analysis, finite-time bounds on simple regret, and empirical results comparing to a dual-UCB baseline in cybersecurity and supply-chain domains.

### Strengths

- The idea of learning an embedding of a Stackelberg game to a Riemannian manifold and solving the game in that space is novel and interesting. The framework effectively bridges equilibrium learning, manifold learning, and online learning (R1, R4).
- The use of neural normalizing flows to learn the diffeomorphism is a promising technique that aggregates ideas from multiple disparate research areas (R1, R3).
- The loss function in Eq. (2.14) provides a concrete way to learn the embedding, not restricted to spherical manifolds (R3).
- The paper offers both theoretical analysis (regret bounds on convex manifolds) and empirical validation across multiple domains (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Motivation and organization are weak.** The paper lacks clear early motivation for why mapping to a Riemannian manifold (especially the sphere) is beneficial, how the embedding is possible, and why it helps construct a better no-regret algorithm (R2, R3). The contributions are not clearly stated (R2). Section 4 is poorly structured, with unclear progression of Lemmas 4.1–4.4 (R1). The problem setting mentions optimal transport and causal game theory that are never discussed further (R3).

- **Theoretical results are questionable.** One reviewer (R2) raised serious concerns that several key definitions and lemmas do not hold as stated:
  - Definition 4.2: such a manifold would not exist unless it is a singleton, because any two distinct points are certainly not geodetically convex.
  - Lemma 4.1: the statement does not make sense for any non-compact domain.
  - Lemma 4.3: the justification does not make sense beyond restating the Poincaré–Hopf Theorem.
  These issues undermine the theoretical foundation.

- **Assumptions and embedding learning are unclear.** The linearity assumption of reward functions in the embedding space is critical but not motivated early; it is not clear whether it is a strict requirement or an assumption that enhances performance. A discussion of behavior when violated is needed (R3). The loss in Eq. (2.14) does not clearly reflect the claimed embedding properties (R2). In simulations, reward models appear automatically linear in the \(\theta\) parameters, making the purpose of Section 2.2’s embedding unclear (R2). The learned mapping is invertible (no dimensionality reduction), so typical manifold-learning motivations do not apply (R3). The types of Riemannian structures essential to the framework are not explained, and whether the framework could work on a smooth manifold with fewer structures is not addressed (R1, R4). It remains unclear why the unit sphere is sufficient (R4).

- **Experimental evaluation is insufficient.** The method is only compared to a dual-UCB baseline; comparisons to more state-of-the-art approaches for solving Stackelberg equilibria are missing (R4). An ablation study comparing the learned normalizing flow to the closed-form mappings in Appendix G.1 is needed to demonstrate the flow’s importance; if critical, experiments should show its impact on data uniformity and performance (R3). The experimental domains do not verify whether the reward functions actually satisfy the linearity assumption in the embedding space; toy domains tailored to that assumption would strengthen the validation (R3). Additional manifolds beyond the sphere would also help (R3).

- **Presentation and writing require significant improvement.** The paper assumes substantial background in differential geometry; fundamental definitions (e.g., geodesics) are missing (R1, R4). Notation is confusing: five different versions of \(\theta\), unclear difference between \(Q\) and \(\phi\) (R3). Algorithm 1 is incomplete: it only accounts for a subset of the algorithmic pipeline, does not define inputs like \(\hat{\theta}_A, \hat{\theta}_B\), and relies on global variables not scoped; line 12 has a return typo (a* vs \(\hat{a}^*\)) and likely should be *yield* (R3). Several specific errors: Figure 1 arrows contradictory to Lemmas 4.3/4.4; Figure 2 uses \(\theta'_B\) while Algorithm 1 uses \(\hat{\theta}_B\); Theorem 1 contains a repeated phrase "and Eq. (2.15) and Eq. (2.16)"; Section 4.1 states "the follower moves first" (should be leader); the mapping paragraph reads backwards ("from spherical to Cartesian" instead of Cartesian to spherical); discrete action spaces are not addressed (R3). The paper should use the official conference template (R4).
