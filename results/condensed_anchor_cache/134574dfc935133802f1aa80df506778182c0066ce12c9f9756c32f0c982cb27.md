## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MM-FATROL, a robust policy training method for two-player competitive games that builds on the existing PATROL framework. It uses a minimax-based selective update mechanism to only update the most impactful policies, aiming to reduce the computational overhead of searching for Nash equilibrium points in policy space. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of computational complexity reduction and conduct experiments across four games, reporting improvements in robustness and efficiency compared to baselines.

### Strengths
- The algorithm addresses the high computational cost of Nash equilibrium-based robust policy training by introducing a minimax-based selective update mechanism.
- It fits within the existing PATROL framework and builds on acceleration techniques from prior work.
- Theoretical guarantees are provided that MM-FATROL achieves lower computational complexity than PATROL while maintaining robustness.
- Extensive experiments across different environments validate the method’s effectiveness, showing superior generalization and robustness compared to baseline methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Convergence to Nash equilibrium is claimed but not proven.** Reviewers note that in a rock-paper-scissors game, the strategy pool retains only single actions (not the uniform distribution of the Nash equilibrium); cycles are possible (Mertikopoulos et al., 2018). The reference [1] (PATROL) only proves that distance to Nash equilibrium does not increase, not that it converges. The paper itself (Line 374) acknowledges that pool size influences effectiveness, implying no guarantee of convergence. The algorithm’s similarity to self-play (only best response to opponent’s strategy) further undermines any convergence guarantee for general games.
- **Missing literature review and comparisons with established equilibrium-finding methods.** The paper does not cite or compare against fictitious play, PSRO, no-regret learning, or last-iterate convergence algorithms. It also does not compare with PSRO or NSFP in experiments, despite the proposed method resembling a variant of PSRO. One reviewer explicitly asked for such comparisons.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity is underexplored.** The method adds multiple hyperparameters (including those besides K) that are tuned per game, and their values appear to matter. The paper provides limited discussion of tuning, and the computational time savings could be outweighed by the cost of hyperparameter tuning. One reviewer asked how hyperparameters other than K were set and how sensitive the algorithm is to them.
- **Theoretical analysis of speedup is insufficient.** The proof does not discuss how the number of iterations required by MM-FATROL changes relative to PATROL, and there is no empirical comparison between the predicted speedup and what is observed in practice.
- **Novelty is limited.** The algorithm mainly modifies PATROL by focusing on the best strategy instead of all strategies. The challenge it addresses is unclear: the bottleneck of PATROL is not identified, and solving a maxmin problem seems harder than a min problem, so how the algorithm reduces computational overhead is not convincingly explained.
- **Presentation issues.** Some notations (e.g., π, U<sub>π</sub>, η) are used without definition. The computation of the minimax strategy in Algorithm 1, Line 7 is unclear, and the variable *i* appears to keep increasing without resetting—reviewers asked whether this is intentional or a missing step.
- **Distinction between robustness and equilibrium is not clearly explained.** The paper equates robust policies with equilibrium strategies, but does not justify this equivalence or clarify what “robustness” means in this context if not equilibrium. One reviewer asked for this clarification.
- **Minority reviewer note:** One reviewer rated the paper 5 (others rated 3), but still raised the above concerns about missing comparisons and algorithm clarity, particularly the need to distinguish robustness from equilibrium and to clarify the minimax update computation. The more positive reviewer did not provide separate weaknesses beyond pointing to their questions.
