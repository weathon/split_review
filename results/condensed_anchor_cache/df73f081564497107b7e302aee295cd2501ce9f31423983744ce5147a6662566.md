## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the impact of diversity in synthetic data on LLM pre-training and supervised fine-tuning. It introduces a novel LLM-based diversity metric, the “LLM Cluster-agent,” which uses an LLM (GPT-4o) to iteratively sample a small number of data points (J=5, K=10), generate metadata and scoring metrics, cluster the samples, and compute the “LLM Cluster score” (ratio of clusters to samples). Through controlled experiments with 350M and 1.4B parameter LLaMA models, the authors generate synthetic datasets with varying diversity (by modifying topics, prompt styles, base models, and real‑to‑synthetic ratios) and show that higher LLM Cluster scores correlate with better downstream performance on several benchmarks, especially for fine‑tuning. Ablations and comparisons with heuristic and model‑based baseline metrics are performed. Reviewers disagree on the strength of evidence (scores: 8, 6, 6, 3, 6); one reviewer (score 8) is very positive about the scope and systematic study, while another (score 3) raises fundamental concerns about sample sizes and metric reliability.

### Strengths
1. **Novel metric**: The proposed “LLM Cluster-agent” is a new approach for quantifying text dataset diversity, specifically tailored for synthetic data (Reviewers 1,2,4,5).
2. **Extensive comparisons**: Elaborate comparisons are carried out with other heuristic and model‑based metrics (Reviewers 1,2).
3. **Multiple generation models**: Multiple open‑source and proprietary models are used to generate synthetic data, and the metric is compared across different architectures and sizes (Reviewer 1).
4. **Verification step**: The inclusion of a cluster generation and verification step is a needed check for agentic pipelines (Reviewer 1).
5. **Large‑scale study**: A systematic investigation covering both pre‑training and supervised fine‑tuning for models up to 1.4B parameters strengthens the underlying hypothesis (Reviewer 1).
6. **Clear and complete presentation**: The paper is well‑written, clearly organized, and provides extensive details in the main manuscript and appendix (Reviewers 2,3).
7. **Elaborate ablations**: Ablation studies on design choices (e.g., K, N, prompt templates) are thorough and demonstrate that clustering performance drops with low K and high N, indicating potential scalability (Reviewers 1,2,5).
8. **Evidence for diversity–performance correlation**: The paper gives convincing evidence that diversity in synthetic data generation can be controlled and is correlated with downstream performance (Reviewer 2; reviewed as “supporting” by Reviewer 5).
9. **Practical guidance**: Findings offer guidance on balancing real/synthetic data and selecting generation models (Reviewer 5).
10. **Context length handling**: The methodology explicitly addresses limitations of model context lengths (Reviewer 4).
11. **Insightful analyses**: Analyses surrounding accuracy, diversity, and token balance are presented (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of human evaluation**: No rigorous human evaluation is performed to validate the reliability of the metric, cluster purity, self‑verification outcomes, or the quality of the generated metadata/metrics. The pipeline’s reliability is unclear without human‑in‑the‑loop assessment (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5). A smaller human‑annotated sample or inter‑rater reliability test (e.g., comparing diversity scores from multiple LLMs) would strengthen trust (Reviewer 5).
2. **Small sample sizes (J=5, K=10)**: Using only J=5 samples for metadata/metric generation and K=10 samples per clustering iteration is a critical limitation. These tiny subsets may not capture the full diversity of datasets with 100K–300K topics, leading to unreliable diversity scores. Sensitivity analyses for higher K and a range of J values (beyond the ablation in Appendix B.2) are needed. It is unclear how well a 10‑sample cluster score generalizes to the entire dataset (Reviewer 4; also noted by Reviewer 1 on parameter sensitivity).
3. **Unconvincing superiority over baselines**: The paper does not provide quantitative correlation metrics (e.g., Pearson/Spearman) to show that LLM Cluster score is *more* correlated with performance than other baseline metrics (e.g., perplexity, K‑Means). Visual inspection of Figures 4–7 suggests marginal gains at best (e.g., Cosmopedia v0.2 performs worst in cluster score but matches v0.1 in several benchmarks). Figures 5 and 6 use non‑uniform X‑axes and non‑comparable value ranges, making cross‑metric comparison unreliable (Reviewers 2,4).
4. **Self‑verification issues**: Using the same LLM (GPT-4o) for both clustering and verification introduces confirmation bias. No independent “teacher” model or human check is used. The rationale for not using a more capable or different model is missing (Reviewers 3,4). The effectiveness of the self‑verification step is not evaluated (Reviewer 3).
5. **Parameter sensitivity and cluster control**: The optimal number of clusters is not determined systematically (no elbow method or cluster homogeneity metrics). Parameter choices (K, N, J, M) need justification; the ablation studies should include a broader range (Reviewer 1). The clustering is localized to the LLM’s context window, leading to unpredictable cluster formations (Reviewer 5).
6. **Cost and scalability concerns**: All experiments use GPT-4o, which is expensive. The technique may not be viable for researchers with limited resources, and the claim that findings “present scalability” to much larger models (e.g., hundreds of billions of parameters) is overreaching without evidence beyond 1.4B (Reviewers 2,3). Cost of the pipeline is not reported (Reviewer 5).
7. **Missing iterative diversity monitoring**: Diversity is only measured post‑generation; there is no mechanism to adjust generation if diversity is low (e.g., every 100M tokens). This could make large‑scale generation wasteful (Reviewer 5).
8. **Unclear distinction between metadata and metrics**: The difference between “metadata” and “metrics” axes is vague; the prompt templates for each are nearly identical. Many metadata and metrics are ill‑defined, with qualitative judgments only for extreme values (1 and 5 on a 1‑5 scale). This lack of a detailed, unambiguous rubric undermines reproducibility and may cause inconsistency across runs (Reviewer 4).
9. **Token count confounds in experiments**: In Figure 5, the model trained on only real data may not use an equivalent number of tokens as the synthetic‑mixed counterparts. In Figure 9, increasing the synthetic‑to‑real ratio increases total training tokens; keeping the token count static would be more convincing (Reviewer 2).
10. **Missing relevant citations**: The pipeline resembles explainable/unsupervised clustering work (e.g., Wang et al., “Goal‑Driven Explainable Clustering via Language Descriptions”, 2023) which should be cited. The long‑context performance degradation footnote (p.3) should cite “Lost in the Middle” (Liu 2023) (Reviewer 2).
11. **Diversity as a single score**: The metric provides a single scalar, while diversity is multi‑dimensional; existing metrics focus on different facets that may remain relevant (Reviewer 3).
12. **Generalizability limited**: The metric is only tested on text data generated from predefined topics. Its applicability to other domains (mathematics, finance, biomedical, logical reasoning) is not demonstrated (Reviewer 3).
13. **Cluster purity missing**: No statistical analysis (e.g., cluster homogeneity, silhouette scores) is provided to assess the quality of the formed clusters (Reviewer 1).
14. **Typos and vague wording**: Multiple typos (“per‑training” line 098, “Cosmppedia” line 409, extra spaces lines 309 and 422, extra “diversity” line 289/290). Naming inconsistency: “LLM Cluster‑agent” is called a metric in the abstract but a pipeline in Section 2.1, while “LLM Cluster score” is the actual metric. Unclear phrasing in lines 147 (“syntactic often present”), section 2.1 (entropy principle), and line 197 (“unreasonable clusters”) (Reviewer 2).
15. **Several unanswered questions that overlap with criticisms**: How to decide optimal number of clusters? (Reviewer 1). How are J and K selected? (Reviewer 3). Why does “topic‑style‑persona” outperform “multi‑topic‑styles”? (Reviewer 5). What are the costs? (Reviewer 5). How does the method compare to a simple K‑means diversity score? (Reviewer 5). Would clustering all samples iteratively (like K‑means) be more representative? (Reviewer 4). These all represent missing experiments or clarifications.
