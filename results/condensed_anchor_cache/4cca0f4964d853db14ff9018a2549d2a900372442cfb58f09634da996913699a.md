## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a lightweight transformer model for multivariate time series (MTS) forecasting. The key innovations are: (1) tokenization using the stationary wavelet transform (SWT) with learnable filters to capture multi‑frequency patterns while preserving time invariance; (2) a geometric product‑based self‑attention mechanism (replacing dot‑product with a combination of dot and wedge products) to capture both magnitude and orientation relationships between tokens; (3) a learnable inverse SWT for reconstruction. The model uses only 1–2 layers. It is evaluated on eight MTS datasets against fifteen baselines for both long‑term and short‑term forecasting, achieving competitive or state‑of‑the‑art results (e.g., outperforming TimeMixer and iTransformer on several benchmarks). Reviewers praise the novelty and simplicity of the approach but raise concerns about experimental rigor, insufficient ablations, missing details on hyperparameters/training, and unsubstantiated claims about LLM competitiveness.

### Strengths

- **Novelty and motivation.** The paper is the first known application of the stationary wavelet transform for tokenization within a transformer for time series, effectively addressing the challenge of handling multiple seasonalities and frequencies. The geometric product attention (wedge product plus dot product) is a novel, lightweight modification to capture inter‑channel geometric relationships. Both ideas are well‑motivated and build on classical signal‑processing concepts.
- **Comprehensive experimental evaluation.** The model is compared against a wide range of recent models (15 baselines) on 8 standard multivariate datasets covering both long‑ and short‑term forecasting. It achieves state‑of‑the‑art or competitive results, notably outperforming strong baselines like TimeMixer and iTransformer.
- **Computational efficiency.** The architecture is lightweight (1–2 layers) and has a low memory footprint, making it feasible for resource‑constrained settings. This is a concrete advantage over large models.
- **Clarity and presentation.** The paper is well‑written, with thorough mathematical explanations of each module. Visuals effectively illustrate the concepts and make the technical details accessible. The structure is easy to follow.
- **Interesting analysis.** Ablation experiments (geometric vs. vanilla attention) consistently show improvement, and the analysis of learned wavelet filters provides insight into the model’s behavior.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing statistical significance and uncertainty measures.** None of the main results (Tables 1 and 2) report standard errors, confidence intervals, or the number of seeds used. Several performance differences between the proposed model and the next best are very small (e.g., 0.001 to 0.004), making it unclear whether they are statistically significant. Reviewers consistently request error bars or statistical tests.
- **Incomplete ablation study.** The paper does not isolate the contribution of the stationary wavelet transform (SWT) tokenization; the ablation table only compares geometric vs. vanilla attention. It remains unknown whether the SWT is the main driver of performance or whether a simple linear layer or MLP replacement would achieve similar results. Additionally, the geometric attention improvement could be due to the extra parameters introduced by the wedge product; a proper ablation should add the same number of extra parameters to the vanilla dot‑product attention for a fair comparison. The usefulness of learned inter‑channel dependencies is not empirically demonstrated (e.g., by plotting or analyzing the learned attention patterns).
- **Discrepancy in TimeMixer results.** The reported TimeMixer results differ substantially from those in the official TimeMixer repository (https://github.com/kwuking/TimeMixer). If the official results are used, the proposed model’s competitive standing may be less compelling. This raises concerns about the experimental setup and possible implementation errors. Reviewers ask for clarification on source of TimeMixer results and a direct comparison with the official numbers.
- **Lack of model size and complexity details.** The paper claims competitiveness with much larger (even LLM‑based) models, but:
    - No LLM‑based baselines are included in the comparisons.
    - Parameter counts and model sizes are not reported for any baseline or the proposed model, making the claim unverifiable.
    - The memory footprint and computational complexity of the geometric product attention are not compared with standard attention or with other efficient baselines (e.g., TimeMixer, iTransformer). A reviewer noted that memory footprint should vary with the number of tokens (dimensionality) rather than being constant.
- **Missing hyperparameter and training details.** Important hyperparameters (e.g., ζ, L′, wavelet type, decomposition level, number of layers) are not disclosed. The training procedure (optimizer, learning rate schedule, batch size, number of runs) is omitted. It is unclear how baselines were trained and whether hyperparameter selection was fair across models. The choice of wavelet and decomposition level is not justified by a sensitivity analysis.
- **Scalability concerns.** The model may not scale efficiently to very high‑dimensional time series or extremely long sequences. The token‑by‑token generation limitation is a significant drawback for many real‑world applications requiring sequential generation. A reviewer noted the need for a detailed complexity analysis comparing with scalable baselines.
- **Missing simple baselines.** No classical statistical forecasting methods (e.g., ARIMA, ETS) are included, which would help contextualize the improvements of transformer‑based models.
- **Minor presentation issues (did not affect scores for most reviewers).** The introduction reads like a related‑work section rather than motivating the proposed method. A background section on wavelet transforms would aid readers unfamiliar with the concept. Figures 6 and 7 are too small to interpret. An appendix is referenced in Table 1 but absent from the submission.
- **Specific questions that imply weaknesses:**
    - Why is the difference between fixed and learned wavelet filters minimal? (Reviewer 1)
    - In Figure 6b, the correlations between time steps after filtering are almost always close to 1 and never negative, which is counterintuitive; how does the model still function? (Reviewer 2)
    - Some time‑series datasets have negligible inter‑channel correlations; do the tested datasets exhibit this property, and does the geometric attention help in such cases? (Reviewer 2)
    - The inter‑channel dependencies claimed to be captured by geometric attention are not empirically validated (Reviewer 4).

**Reviewer disagreement note:** While Reviewers 1 and 4 (scores 8 and 8) are strongly positive about the paper’s contributions and experimental support, Reviewer 2 (score 5) and Reviewer 3 (score 6) are more critical. Reviewer 2 considers the wedge product improvement minimal and questions whether it is due to extra parameters, and Reviewer 3 emphasizes the lack of statistical tests, insufficient ablations, and unverified LLM claim. These concerns are reflected in the weaknesses above.
