## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Knowledge Tracing Set Transformers (KTSTs), a transformer-based model for student performance prediction. It uses set representations of interactions and a learnable ALiBi-like attention modification for positional information. The authors claim KTST is conceptually simpler than prior domain-specific models and establishes state-of-the-art results on benchmarks. Reviewers’ assessments vary: one is positive (score 5, confidence 4), one very negative (score 1, confidence 2), and two moderate (score 3, confidence 4). Common concerns include scattered related work, unclear novelty, insufficient justification of simplicity, modest performance gains, and missing analyses.

### Strengths
- **Promising performance and thorough comparisons**: KTST demonstrates strong predictive results, and the experiments are comprehensive, comparing against 22 benchmark models with multiple initializations and hyperparameter tuning for each baseline (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Clear presentation and straightforward architecture**: The paper is well-written and easy to follow; the model design is simple to understand and implement (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The model diagram is clear and enhances readability (Reviewer 1).
- **Thorough related work coverage**: The discussion covers many relevant works on each design choice, though its organization is problematic (Reviewer 2).
- **Important topic and insightful design choice**: The focus on knowledge tracing is valuable (Reviewer 4). The insight that concept representations should be permutation-invariant is appreciated (Reviewer 3).
- **Useful ablation studies**: The ablation investigates multiple design choices (e.g., different queries to attention, component aggregation strategies) (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Scattered and abstract related work**: The related work is spread across multiple sections (Lines 108–136 after Problem Setting, Lines 198–207, 252–259, 286–288), making it hard to see where the paper stands (Reviewers 1, 2). The authors should consolidate all related work into Section 2 and not interleave new comparisons in the method discussion (Reviewers 1, 2). Some claims are too abstract; e.g., “Without proper masking, this introduces label leakage” needs a concrete example (Reviewers 1, 2). The repeated criticism of domain-inspired components lacks a clear comparison and quantification of added complexity (Reviewer 3). The literature review overlooks recent interpretable models; the focus should shift to domain-inspired and transformer-based works (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited novelty and unclear contribution**: The technical novelty is modest. The learnable ALiBi is similar to Kerple (Chi et al., NeurIPS 2022) without clear differentiation (Reviewer 4). The paper introduces three interaction embeddings, but two (mean and unique) are from prior work; the claimed novel MHSA underperforms some baselines and shows only marginal advantages over mean embedding for large sequences (≥4000) with many components (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The learnable attention decay essentially re-encodes embedding memory priors (Reviewer 3). It is hard to pinpoint the unique contributions beyond applying established techniques (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Insufficient justification of “simplicity” and “complexity”**: The claim that KTST is conceptually simpler than RNN-based models is subjective and unsupported – e.g., criticising inductive bias without evidence (Reviewers 1, 2). The repeated assertion that domain-inspired components are unnecessarily complex is not backed by quantitative comparisons (parameter count, training/evaluation time) (Reviewers 3, 4). The paper should provide such comparisons to substantiate the simplicity claim (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Experimental analysis concerns**:
    - Performance gains over baselines are modest; practical significance is unclear (Reviewer 4).
    - The main tables show KTST (MHSA) often underperforms prior work; the authors should directly compare it against the best prior method, not just the mean/unique baselines (Reviewers 1, 2).
    - Figure 3 uses simulated data to compare aggregation functions but shows little difference; it is unclear why real data was not used (Reviewers 3, 4).
    - The ablation study focuses only on ALiBi and is insufficiently comprehensive. Missing ablations: (i) effect of permutation invariance on concept representations (test without it); (ii) removal of knowledge component embeddings entirely (Reviewer 3). Additionally, an analysis of the learned attention matrices is needed to understand what influences performance (Reviewer 4).
    - The claim that MHSA works better with larger component-to-question ratios is confounded by data size; an experiment to disentangle them is needed (Reviewer 2).
- **Figures and exposition issues**:
    - Figure 1 is uninformative (random numbers/shapes) and does not illustrate the problem setting clearly; a more intuitive figure is needed (Reviewers 1, 2).
    - Figure 2 resembles vanilla transformer and lacks annotations: specific inputs for Q, K, V; what the pink box represents; token types (knowledge components, questions); the aggregation operation; causal masking (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
    - The introduction needs 1–2 sentences clarifying the context (student-computer interaction, digital education) (Reviewers 1, 2).
    - Line 122–123 mentions “one consequence” but never a second (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Missing interpretability and small-data analysis**: Domain-inspired models are motivated by interpretability and effectiveness with small data, but KTST does not analyze its own learned embeddings (knowledge component and question representations) (Reviewer 3). The authors should test performance on smaller datasets (reduced sequence length, fewer students) to evaluate the claimed advantages (Reviewer 3).
- **Unaddressed reviewer questions** (overlapping with criticisms, missing experiments, or requests for clarification):
    - (Reviewer 2) How does the expanded representation work for a query with multiple knowledge components? Specifically, if a query has 5 components, does the model process them one by one in a sequence?
    - (Reviewer 2) Is MHSA’s better performance on larger data due to the component-to-question ratio or simply to more data?
    - (Reviewer 3) Could the authors provide concrete examples from prior works of “domain-inspired and unnecessarily complex” interaction representations?
    - (Reviewer 4) What is the motivation for using ALiBi in knowledge tracing models?
    - (Reviewer 4) Why is simulated data used instead of real data in Section 5.3?
