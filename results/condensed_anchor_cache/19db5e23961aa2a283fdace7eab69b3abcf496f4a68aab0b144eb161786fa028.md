## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Modular Agentic Planner (MAP), a planning architecture for LLMs that combines specialized modules (task decomposer, actor, monitor, predictor, evaluator, orchestrator), each implemented by prompting an LLM (GPT-4). MAP is evaluated on graph traversal, Tower of Hanoi, PlanBench, and StrategyQA. Reviews generally commend the modular design grounded in cognitive neuroscience and RL, thorough ablation studies, and solid performance gains over standard baselines (zero-shot, CoT, ToT, MAD). However, concerns are raised about incremental novelty relative to prior modular agent frameworks, high computational cost (83× token usage vs. GPT-4 CoT), weak baselines (no comparison with planning-based methods like MCTS or role-playing agents), insufficient discussion of the predictor module’s limitations in stochastic/partially observable settings, and presentation clarity issues. One reviewer (score 3) is notably more critical of the novelty and task selection, while the others (score 5) are more positive overall.

### Strengths
- Modular design that separates planning into specialized, well-defined components (task decomposition, action proposal, state prediction, evaluation, monitoring, orchestration). The grounding of each module in concepts from cognitive neuroscience and RL is appreciated.
- Thorough experimental evaluation across multiple planning tasks (graph traversal, Tower of Hanoi, PlanBench, StrategyQA), with ablation studies demonstrating the contribution of components (e.g., TreeSearch, TaskDecomposer) and statistical significance.
- Solid empirical results, with MAP outperforming competitive baselines (GPT-4 zero-shot, ICL, CoT, ToT, MAD), especially on challenging tasks like PlanBench.
- The method is clearly described and easy to reproduce from the paper and appendix.
- Demonstrates some task transferability (MAP adapted across different tasks) and performs well with smaller models (Llama3-70B).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and Baseline Selection**  
   - The modular agentic structure is not new; many prior works use similar role-playing, multi-module, or multi-agent frameworks (DEPS, JARVIS-1, Voyager, CAMEL, Adapt, LATS). The paper does not adequately discuss these connections or highlight what specific advantages MAP offers over them.  
   - The baselines used (zero-shot, CoT, ToT, MAD) are too weak; stronger baselines that also use explicit planning modules or MCTS (e.g., LATS, RAP) are missing. Without comparisons to planning-aware methods, it is unclear whether MAP’s gains come from the modular design or simply from using multiple LLM agents.  
   - *Minority contrast*: One reviewer (score 3) also argues that the core evaluation tasks (graph traversal, Tower of Hanoi) are not standard agent benchmarks and lack real-world complexity; other reviewers found these tasks effective for isolating planning.

2. **High Computational Cost**  
   - MAP uses significantly more tokens than baselines: 83× the input tokens of GPT-4 CoT (Appendix A.8), and the efficient version still uses 36×. The paper does not provide a fair comparison where baselines are given a similar token budget (e.g., increasing tree-of-thought depth/breadth, best-of-N trials). The cost-performance tradeoff is not justified.

3. **Predictor Module and Assumptions**  
   - The Predictor module functions as a world model, which is known to be very challenging. The paper does not discuss its limitations in stochastic, partially observable, or non-deteministic environments. Fully observable and deterministic environments are assumed (Section 2), but this is not explicitly stated, and the implications for partially observable settings are not addressed.  
   - As a result, MAP’s performance in complex environments (e.g., Minecraft, ALFWorld, WebShop) is questionable, and this is not tested.

4. **Insufficient Explanation and Inconsistencies**  
   - The main text description of MAP is insufficient; understanding key modules (Actor, Monitor) requires consulting multiple sections of the appendix (Algorithms 2, 3; Sections A.7.2, A.7.3).  
   - There are inconsistencies: e.g., Algorithm 2 shows the Actor outputting an action sequence $A$, but Section A.7.2 includes both action sequences and state information; the Monitor is said to produce validity and feedback for an entire sequence (Algorithm 2) but the in-context examples in A.7.3 show per-action feedback.  
   - Tables 1–3 lack column lines and have odd spacing; key takeaways are not summarized.

5. **Task Decomposer Limitations**  
   - The Task Decomposer currently generates only a single subgoal per decomposition step and lacks a mechanism to recover from errors in subgoal generation. Its effectiveness when multiple subgoals are required is not verified.

6. **In-Context Example Selection**  
   - MAP relies heavily on manually selected in-context examples for each module, but the paper does not explain whether these examples are fixed or dynamically selected, nor how they are constructed. The cost of identifying effective examples across modules is not addressed.

7. **Missing Analyses and Details**  
   - The planning task metrics are not explicitly defined in Section 3.1.  
   - No analysis of whether the Actor can effectively process the Monitor’s feedback (the Actor’s in-context examples in A.7.2 lack feedback examples).  
   - The paper does not discuss whether prompt design is automated or manual, and the significant engineering cost this entails.  
   - There is no discussion of the theoretical basis for interactions between modules beyond the individual inspirations.
