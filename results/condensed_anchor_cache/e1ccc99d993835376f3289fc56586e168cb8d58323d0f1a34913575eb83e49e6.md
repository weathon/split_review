## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces MedLFQA, a reconstructed benchmark dataset from existing biomedical long-form QA datasets to enable automatic factuality evaluation in biomedical LFQA. The authors also propose OLAPH, a framework that uses cost-effective, multifaceted automatic evaluation to build synthetic preference sets and train LLMs step-by-step via SFT and DPO to reduce hallucinations and incorporate essential medical claims. Experiments show that a 7B LLM trained with OLAPH achieves improved factuality, with some results claimed comparable to medical experts. The paper addresses the important problem of hallucination in the health domain, where factuality is critical.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and critical problem: hallucination in biomedical LFQA, where factuality is crucial for patient safety and trust (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).
- The paper is clearly written with well-structured presentation, clear visualizations, and illustrative examples (Reviewer 2, 3, 4).
- MedLFQA is a valuable contribution as a unified benchmark for evaluating factuality in biomedical LFQA, with human verification and high agreement (Reviewer 1, 2, 3, 4).
- OLAPH is comprehensively validated with thorough analyses, including comparisons with proprietary models and evaluation using metrics independent of the training process (Reviewer 2; also noted by Reviewer 4 as thorough experiments).
- The study shows an innovative approach by creatively combining preference-based learning and factual consistency checks to improve medical domain answers (Reviewer 3).
- The methodological design is detailed, making it easy for others to build upon (Reviewer 3).
- Dataset collection follows good practice with high human inter-annotator agreement (Reviewer 4).
- The significance of OLAPH lies in its potential to impact healthcare applications, and MedLFQA can assist other researchers in developing factually accurate medical response systems (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty of OLAPH framework**: The overall framework follows standard preference optimization (SFT and DPO) that has been used in many post-training pipelines for LLMs such as Llama and Claude models. Multiple reviewers suggest softer contribution claims (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). One reviewer (score 8) found the approach innovative, but others (scores 5-6) considered the novelty minimal.
- **Insufficient hallucination metric**: MedLFQA’s MUST HAVE / NICE TO HAVE setting only evaluates a subset of hallucination; incorrect facts outside these statements are not captured. The entailment/contradiction calculations rely on a fine-tuned BioBERT NLI model, introducing uncertainty. A sensitivity analysis with different NLI models is needed. The metrics are not persuasive as sufficient for hallucination evaluation (Reviewer 1).
- **Circular optimization**: The three metrics (Word Composition, Semantic Similarity, Factuality) are directly optimized during training, so improvement is unsurprising. The paper needs to address this circularity and provide justification beyond direct optimization (Reviewer 1).
- **Bias from synthetic data generation**: Using GPT-4 to generate MUST HAVE and NICE TO HAVE statements may introduce biases or inaccuracies. The paper lacks critical analysis of where synthetic statements diverge from medical experts (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing domain-specific case analysis**: The framework does not cover domain-specific scenarios like disease diagnosis or treatment recommendation (Reviewer 3).
- **Convergence properties unaddressed**: The paper does not address whether OLAPH converges, nor how the number and types of evaluation metrics influence convergence rates (Reviewer 2).
- **Unexplained experimental design**: The use of cross-validation instead of traditional train/test split is not justified (Reviewer 2). The setup for RQ3 is unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablations and sensitivity analyses**: Effects of removing the SFT step (Figure 5 shows initial drop; what if removed?) (Reviewer 4). Effects of varying α₃ beyond 1.0 (e.g., 2,3,5) and evaluating single-loss variants are not explored (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear relationship between data qualification and preference optimization**: Can the evaluation criteria used for qualifying generated data (Section 3.2) serve as preference criteria for the preference optimization process (Section 4.2)? (Reviewer 2).
- **Unsubstantiated or overclaimed conclusions**: (a) Line 235–236: The result may simply indicate that answers in K-QA are terrible, not that GPT-4 answers are good; more analysis needed (Reviewer 4). (b) Line 248–252: Annotators may be biased to trust model-generated content (Reviewer 4). (c) Line 282–283: Are statements used as SFT targets? If so, what is the data format? (Reviewer 4).
- **Long-term factual relevance**: The paper does not address how the framework or dataset will maintain factual relevance as medical knowledge advances (Reviewer 3).
- **Final analysis clarity**: The final analysis can be made clearer (Reviewer 4).
