- Scores: 3, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a client-side privacy attack in centralized federated learning (FL) that reconstructs peers’ training images by leveraging consecutive global model updates and diffusion models for post-processing enhancement. Reviewers acknowledge the successful implementation and shift to a node-level threat model, but raise significant concerns about unrealistic assumptions, limited novelty relative to server-side gradient inversion, incomplete experimental setups, and lack of scalability analysis. One reviewer (score 5, confidence 5) praised the stealth, undetectability, and relevance to cross-silo FL, emphasizing the strong empirical evidence. The other two reviewers (scores 3, confidence 4 and 5) questioned the practical applicability, novelty, and the solidity of the core contribution.

### Strengths

- **Successful attack implementation**: The paper demonstrates a working method that reconstructs images from other participating clients, with experiments validating its effectiveness (Reviewer 1).
- **Stealth and undetectability**: The attack does not disrupt training or inject corrupted data, making it difficult for servers or other clients to detect (Reviewer 2).
- **Relevance to cross-silo FL**: The findings are especially concerning for cross-silo settings where data scarcity is addressed through collaboration, highlighting the need for stronger privacy measures (Reviewer 2).
- **Extensive experimental evidence**: Thorough experiments provide strong empirical support for the vulnerability (Reviewer 2).
- **Integration of generative models**: The use of diffusion models (MDT, DDPMs) as a post-processing step improves reconstruction quality, and the peer-node attack scenario is more versatile than traditional server-only attacks (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Unrealistic and restrictive assumptions**: The method assumes a consistent learning rate across clients, equal number of images per client, full-batch gradient descent, and that the attacker knows or can accurately estimate the number of clients per round (Reviewer 1). It also assumes equal aggregation weights, whereas actual FL assigns weights based on data size, and assumes the attacker can directly initialize N (line 161) – information a peer node cannot realistically possess (Reviewer 3). These assumptions severely limit practical applicability in typical FL settings that involve non-IID data, asynchronous updates, adaptive optimizers, and resource-constrained clients (Reviewer 1, 3).
- **No fundamental distinction from server-side gradient inversion**: The information a client obtains (model parameters at time t and t+1) is identical to the aggregated gradient the server would see when secure aggregation is applied. The attack is therefore the same as conventional gradient inversion, with only an additional subtraction operation, providing no special or new inspiration (Reviewer 2, 3).
- **Diffusion post-processing relies on sufficiently good initial reconstruction**: The core contribution (post-processing with diffusion models) is only effective if the raw reconstruction already contains enough information. If the initial results resemble noise (e.g., results from ROG or GradInversion), the proposed method fails; this condition is not discussed (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited novelty and originality**: The work is a simple combination of gradient inversion with super-resolution/denoising techniques (diffusion models) without proposing a new method or solving an unsolved problem. Even the peer-node attack claim is not fundamentally different from existing center-based studies (Reviewer 3). The optimization framework is not novel, lacks citations to similar frameworks, and provides no comparison to explain why it is more effective than existing approaches (Reviewer 1).
- **Inadequate experimental scope and missing ablation studies**: The maximum number of clients tested is 8, leaving scalability to larger FL deployments unclear (Reviewer 1, 2). Ablation studies on hyperparameters (e.g., number of local iterations, inversion learning rate) are absent, making it difficult to assess robustness and generalizability (Reviewer 1). The paper does not analyze the effect of varying degrees of data heterogeneity (non-IID) (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete experimental setup details**: Many configuration parameters are missing, making reproduction difficult. Hyperparameters (training model structure, batch size, epoch for FedAvg local training) for results in Figures 2, 3, 5, 8 are not reported (Reviewer 2). The optimization objective (Eq. 3) does not include label variables, and the paper does not explain how the attacker obtains labels; the referenced method (Yin et al.) cannot handle duplicate labels (Reviewer 3). Most optimization-related configurations are absent (Reviewer 3).
- **Questions overlapping with missing experiments or comparisons**:
  - How does the attack perform with a larger number of clients, and how robust is it when its underlying assumptions are violated? (Reviewer 1)
  - In Figure 6 (8 clients, batch size 64), the attacker would need to reconstruct 512 images; what are the actual visualization results, and does the reported LPIPS value reflect the true reconstruction quality? (Reviewer 2)
  - Why is L2 norm used in Eq. 3 instead of cosine similarity, and which component (raw reconstruction or post-processing) has a greater impact on the final result? (Reviewer 2)
  - For different epochs in Figure 2, how is the best epoch chosen, and is the same optimal epoch used for all images? (Reviewer 2)
  - After adding diffusion models (MDT, DDPMs), how much does the attack efficiency and computational cost increase? (Reviewer 2)
  - Are the trends of MSE, PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS consistent? (LPIPS values can be close while visual effects differ significantly.) (Reviewer 2)
