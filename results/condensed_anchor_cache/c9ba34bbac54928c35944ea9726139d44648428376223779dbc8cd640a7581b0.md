## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper establishes upper and lower bounds on the \(L_p\) error of density ratio estimators obtained by minimizing an \(f\)-divergence loss function. The bounds scale with \(N^{-1/d}\) (curse of dimensionality) and depend on the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the data dimension, but are independent of the specific \(f\)-divergence used in the loss. The proofs rely on a conceptual reformulation of the loss function (Section 4.1) and a nearest-neighbor argument (Section 4.2). Numerical experiments on toy problems support the theoretical rates.

### Strengths
- The problem of uncertainty quantification in density ratio estimation is an important fundamental question.
- The derived lower and upper bounds on the \(L_p\) error of the density ratio estimator are new and, to the best of the reviewers’ knowledge, the first such bounds in this context.
- The nearly matching upper and lower bounds are a strong point.
- The independence of the bounds from the specific \(f\)-divergence within the loss function is intriguing.
- The proof techniques are clever: they use a conceptual loss function representation that provides access to the density ratio and a nearest-neighbor approach to show that the conceptual and real loss functions are close. These techniques are expected to find applications beyond DRE (e.g., in generative modeling).
- The presentation in both the main text and the supplementary material is clear and well-organized; the paper is easy to follow.
- The estimator is practical because implementation is straightforward using a neural network architecture.
- Numerical illustrations support the theoretical findings.

### Weaknesses
- **High-dimensional limitation**: The bound’s dependence on \(N^{-1/d}\) means that in high dimensions the error bound becomes practically useless. One reviewer notes this raises questions on the meaningfulness of the bounds.
- **Lack of real-world applications**: The experiments are limited to simple toy problems; the impact of the theory on important applications (e.g., generative modeling, divergence estimation) is not demonstrated. Multiple reviewers ask how these theoretical results can be used in practice and what actionable guidance they provide for algorithmic design (e.g., mitigating the curse of dimensionality, choosing between different \(L_p\) losses).
- **Missing comparisons with other DRE estimators**: There is no head-to-head experimental comparison with other density ratio estimation methods. It is unclear how the convergence rate of this estimator compares to other DRE estimators (e.g., those in [1, 2] cited by a reviewer, or general DRE literature).  
- **Unclear relation to existing techniques**: The conceptual reformulation in Section 4.1 and the nearest-neighbor approach in Section 4.2 need contextualization—are they widely used or newly adapted? How does Assumption 3.3 relate to pseudo-self-concordance as in Zellinger et al. (2023)? Can the proof techniques be compared to those in Zellinger et al. (2023) or Menon & Ong (2016)?  
- **Assumption practicality**: The main results rely heavily on the Lipschitz continuity of the function class and of the energy function (log-likelihood) and on compact support with a bi-Lipschitz condition. Ensuring these assumptions in complex, multimodal, or high-dimensional real-world settings is challenging, which constrains the interpretability and applicability of the bounds.  
- **Experimental variance**: The experiments exhibit high variance, which limits their robustness and interpretability.  
- **Typos and missing details**:  
  * In the informal Theorem 3.5, the lower bound contains a typo: \((1-p)\) should be \((p-1)\).  
  * In Equation 5 (upper bound) the left-hand side includes an expectation operator, but this expectation is missing from the lower bound in Equation 4—is this a typo?  
  * The parameter \(N\) in Theorem 3.5 is undefined.  
  * It should be clarified in what sense equation (4) holds—with high probability?  
  * It is not clear whether the bounds can be derived for dimensions 1 and 2.  
- **Need for explanation of \(f\)-independence**: The paper does not explain why the result does not depend on the specific \(f\)-divergence. One reviewer suspects it is because the Lipschitz assumption on the log-likelihood and compact support make the behavior of \(f\) at 0 and \(\infty\) irrelevant; this reasoning should be made explicit.  
- **Implications for related areas**: The paper does not discuss how these results apply to GANs or to neural estimation of \(f\)-divergences (e.g., the recent work at JMLR 23). These connections would strengthen the contextualization.

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 8) is notably more positive, praising the proof as very clever and the result as practical with straightforward neural network implementation, while the other three reviewers (scores 6, 5, 5) are more critical, emphasizing the lack of real-world relevance, missing comparisons, and the limitations imposed by the assumptions.
