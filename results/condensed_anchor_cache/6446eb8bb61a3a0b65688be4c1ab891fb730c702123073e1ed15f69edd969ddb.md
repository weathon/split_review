## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes two post-hoc methods—Iterative Class Rectification (ICR) and Discontinuous Class Rectification (DCR)—to transform learned embeddings from a pre-trained encoder (ResNet-9 on CIFAR-10/100) into orthogonal and axis-aligned class representations. The aim is to recover interpretability of features while retaining classification performance. Theoretical guarantees are provided for both algorithms, and experiments demonstrate that the resulting representations are orthogonal and axis-aligned.

### Strengths
- The motivation (recovering interpretability of deep learned representations) is well-driven and appealing; it is easy to see practitioners leveraging the work.
- Theoretical proofs underpin the orthogonalization achieved by both ICR and DCR, assuming certain conditions are met.
- The method is simple, its internal objectives are well described, and the theoretical part appears sound.
- Empirical results show the effectiveness of the proposed approaches in achieving orthogonality and highlight issues with prior methods.
- The application to out-of-distribution (OOD) detection makes intuitive sense (though one reviewer noted OOD performance degraded – see Weaknesses).
- **Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 5) was substantially more positive, finding the paper practically useful and the experiments convincing; the other three reviewers (scores 3) raised several concerns that temper this assessment.

### Weaknesses
- **Convoluted presentation:** The introduction and description of the proposed methods are short, unclear, and rely heavily on the reader’s prior knowledge of ISR; the back‑and‑forth between OPL and CIDER (Sections 3.2 and 3.3) adds confusion.
- **Questionable orthogonality assumption:** Forcing every pair of class means to be completely orthogonal discards potentially valuable semantic relationships between similar classes (e.g., apple and orange). One reviewer asked why orthogonal representations are needed when a softmax layer already provides meaningful class‑membership probabilities; another questioned why one cannot simply learn a non‑orthogonal transformation.
- **Lack of empirical support for interpretability claim:** No experiment directly measures or validates improved interpretability (e.g., via human studies or feature‑attribution analysis). The method’s primary claim therefore remains unsubstantiated.
- **Limited experimental scope:** All experiments use only a ResNet‑9 on CIFAR‑10/100 datasets, which restricts generality. Results are not equivalent to those of the baseline methods (which often use larger architectures). Moreover, no statistical variability (standard deviations) is reported, and Table 4 uses excessive decimals (three decimal places when the least accuracy is 0.01%).
- **Missing baselines and related work:** Traditional orthogonalization methods (Gram‑Schmidt, Householder transformations) are not included as baselines. No discussion of prototypical networks, the orthogonal classifier literature (e.g., “Controlling directions orthogonal to a classifier”, ICLR’22), or error‑correcting output codes (ECOC) is provided. The paper also lacks a short background on OPL and CIDER.
- **Performance inconsistency and missing experiments:** The method does not perform consistently across all evaluation criteria; OOD detection leads to performance degradation (contradicting the intuitive strength). Additional experiments such as robustness to label noise, adversarial attacks, or downstream tasks like “ignoring” a class are promised but not conducted.
- **Unclear or insufficiently justified choices:**  
  - Why is it necessary to orthogonalize classes before projecting into a new basis? Why not directly learn a non‑orthogonal transformation matrix?  
  - Why is it sensible to normalize the class means (i.e., discard magnitude)? The decision can change the nearest‑neighbor classification result.  
  - What does “DCR uses a discontinuous operation” mean – discontinuous with respect to what?  
- **Narrow contribution:** ICR is an extension of ISR (Aboagye et al.), limiting the novelty, despite the scaling capacity of both algorithms.  
- **Minor issues:**  
  - Citation formatting and bibliography entries without venues.  
  - The claim that a simple linear classifier is always sufficient after a learned representation is arguable.  
  - Typo: “Roccio” should be “Rocchio”.
