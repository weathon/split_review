## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses the problem of sampling error in on-policy policy gradient RL: finite samples collected by the current policy may not match the expected on-policy distribution, leading to noisy updates and low data efficiency. The authors propose Proximal Robust On-Policy Sampling (PROPS), an adaptive off-policy sampling method that reduces sampling error by learning a behavior policy that increases the probability of under-sampled actions. PROPS generalizes the ROS method of Zhong et al. (2022) from policy evaluation to policy improvement, adding PPO-style clipping and KL regularization for continuous action spaces. Experiments on GridWorld, discrete-action tasks, and MuJoCo benchmarks compare PPO with PROPS to standard on-policy PPO and PPO that naively reuses recent data.

### Strengths

* **Novel application** of the idea of reducing sampling error via adaptive off-policy sampling to PPO and policy gradient algorithms (first to do so). The problem of sampling error is interesting and has received insufficient discussion in the RL community.
* **Proposes a concrete algorithm** (PROPS) to learn a behavior policy that encourages under-sampled actions within each minibatch, using nano-batches and a mini-PPO procedure.
* **Clear writing and presentation** overall. The paper explains the problem (on-policy data vs. on-policy sampling) and the PROPS algorithm clearly. (Note: Reviewer 1 disagrees, finding the paper poorly written and too lengthy; see Weaknesses.)
* **Well-designed experiments** with good statistical significance, including analysis across hyperparameter values in the appendix. Implementation details are transparent and reproducible.
* **Simple solution** in a good way (Reviewer 2).
* The problem can arise in many real-world setups, making the topic significant (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

* **Drastic increase in batch size** (1024,2048,4096,8192) compared to original PPO (64) is not well-justified. This change is required to run the internal PPO to learn the behavior policy, but it drastically reduces the number of updates (16× fewer). Whether original PPO has such room to increase batch size while maintaining performance under the same hyperparameters is unclear. The final batch size choice for PPO is not specified; only the four candidate values are reported.
* **Fragile hyperparameters**: the learning rate for the behavior policy ranges from 1e-3 to 1e-5 for Adam, an unusually large range. Additional KL cutoff and regularizer coefficient add two more dimensions. Batch sizes vary per environment without justification (e.g., Ant-v4 with 105-dim observation uses 1024, while Hopper-v4 with 11-dim uses 2048 and Walker2d-v4 with 17-dim uses 4096), which is counterintuitive and harms assessment of generalizability.
* **Lack of novel theoretical analysis**: Proposition 1 is a slight generalization of a result in Zhong et al. (2022), restricted to finite states and actions, and based on a different sampling procedure than the one used in PROPS. It only analyzes asymptotic behavior, not the transient finite-sample behavior that is crucial for policy gradient algorithms. No convergence rate or sample complexity guarantees are provided. The interplay between policy update size, data reuse, and behavior policy update size is not analyzed; hyperparameters are set by sweep without theoretical guidance.
* **Experiments show only marginal performance benefits** in MuJoCo continuous control tasks. The improvements are not substantial enough to justify the added complexity of maintaining and updating a behavior policy, especially with significant computational overhead.
* **Missing baseline comparisons**: Experiments only compare against PPO and PPO-Buffer. Methods that reuse data via off-policy corrections (referenced in lines 122–124) are not included, nor are off-policy RL algorithms that can significantly outperform on-policy methods on MuJoCo tasks. Massively parallel on-policy implementations are also not discussed. This makes it difficult to assess the true value of PROPS.
* **Regularization limits data reuse**: PROPS requires the behavior policy to remain close to the current policy (small δ_PROPS), preventing substantial reuse of past data and undermining the core motivation of improving data efficiency.
* **Clarity issues** (noted by multiple reviewers):
  * Equation (5) can be written more simply as -max(g, 1-ε_PROPS) or min(-π_φ/π_θ, -(1-ε_PROPS)); its PPO-like form is confusing and unnecessary given that advantages are not involved.
  * Equation (6) motivation is unclear: in the discrete case, the distance from the target policy would not matter; in the continuous case, PPO already prevents policy change. Why is this additional KL term needed?
  * Equations (1), (2), (4) use nonstandard notation (e.g., s ∼ d_π for all states, when states are not identically distributed; should use discounted state visitation per Nota & Thomas).
  * Terminology such as “empirical policy” in Proposition 1 and “on-policy data” vs “on-policy sampling” is confusing – a sample is not inherently on- or off-policy; the generating process matters.
  * Typos (line 341: Samping → Sampling; line 485: Figure 5b → Figure 3b) and legend inconsistencies.
* **Unclear contribution positioning**: The paper does not clearly explain the main benefits of PROPS relative to existing methods for data/computational efficiency, including off-policy correction methods, off-policy RL, and massively parallel on-policy implementations (Reviewer 4, Q1). The core contribution (application to PG estimation with PPO clipping/KL) appears only from page 5, and it may not be clear to readers that the earlier exposition of Zhong et al. is not the paper’s original idea.
* **Soundness concerns**:
  * Improving sampling error in one state may cause worse sampling errors downstream. Would it be better to push this error into the reward instead (Reviewer 2, Q1)?
  * Why choose the form of Equation (5) (clipped importance weight) instead of its log or difference? A theory-guided explanation would be helpful (Reviewer 2, Q3).
  * The batch update approach (wait until a batch is collected, then correct the distribution) may be problematic: if the learned policy is used to sample many trajectories, the data could become unbalanced again. Zhong et al. used continuous online updates; why did the authors choose batch updates (Reviewer 3, question)?
  * Reducing sampling error might actually hurt performance by preventing escape from local optima in the non-convex objective. This potential side effect is not analyzed (Reviewer 4, Q2).
* **Need for more targeted experiments**: The problem may be most important in non-stationary settings or very large state/action spaces. Experiments should be designed for those cases, or the motivation should be stronger (Reviewer 2, Weakness 1). Reviewer 4 also asks how PROPS performs with the same hyperparameters as PPO (only tuning PROPS-specific ones) and how it compares to standard on-policy PPO for the sampling error measure (Q2).
