## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CodeDPO, a framework that applies Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to code generation by constructing preference datasets via self-generation of code solutions and test cases, cross-verified with a PageRank-inspired iterative scoring algorithm. The framework also integrates code efficiency (execution time) into the optimization. Evaluations on HumanEval, HumanEval+, MBPP, MBPP+, and DS-1000 show improvements over SFT-only models and existing preference optimization baselines (Code-Optimise, PLUM). Reviewer opinions are split: one reviewer (rating 6) found the method interesting and the experiments extensive, while three reviewers (ratings 3,3,5) raised concerns about novelty, missing details, fairness of comparisons, evaluation scope, and several unaddressed methodological questions.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: improving code generation models on correctness and efficiency via preference learning.
- The PageRank-inspired iterative scoring algorithm is interesting and alleviates the problem of incorrect LLM-generated test cases.
- Data generation does not require a strong teacher LLM, making it scalable.
- Extensive experiments compare CodeDPO with two similar baselines (Code-Optimise and PLUM) across multiple LLMs (e.g., DeepSeekCoder, Magicoder, Phi-2) and multiple benchmarks.
- Ablation studies investigate the impact of the scoring algorithm, preference optimization strategy (DPO vs. KTO), dataset size, and correlation with ground-truth test correctness.
- The inclusion of code efficiency (execution time) as an additional preference factor is a novel angle, though its standalone effect is not isolated.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution:** Several reviewers noted that applying DPO to code is an obvious extension already explored in prior work (e.g., Llama-3.1 technical report, PLUM, Code-Optimise). Many components (seed-from-repos, synthetic data generation, iterative filtering) are reused from existing literature (WizardCoder, MagiCoder, CodeT, self-debugging). The paper offers few new insights beyond a proof-of-concept.
- **Missing methodological details and justification:**
    - No clear explanation of DPO itself, assuming reader familiarity.
    - No workflow diagram; step 2 of Figure 2 is hard to understand.
    - No justification for selecting 15 code solutions and test cases, nor for temperature 1.5.
    - The creation of DPO pairs for correctness is ambiguous: correctness scores are continuous (not 0/1), but how are winning/losing pairs selected (e.g., highest vs. lowest, or another scheme)?
    - The concept of “code efficiency” is narrowly measured by execution time on test cases; no discussion of other factors (production environment, tools). Evidence that test-case execution time reflects real-world efficiency is lacking.
    - No reporting of average execution time units (ms vs. seconds) or distribution.
- **Fairness and completeness of comparisons:**
    - No description of the finetuning dataset sizes for baselines OSS-Instruct, Code-Optimise, PLUM. If CodeDPO uses 114K samples vs. 75K for OSS-Instruct, the comparison is unfair.
    - Missing results in tables: Table 2 lacks PLUM performance for MagiCoder-CL-7B, Phi-2-2.7B, DeepSeekCoder-1.3B; Table 3 lacks Code-Optimise and PLUM on DS-1000; Figure 3 lacks efficiency results on DS-1000.
    - The scoring baseline used in RQ3 (CodeT, PLUM) are not state-of-the-art; no detailed comparison highlighting advantages of PageRank over CodeT’s dual-execution agreement or PLUM’s self-consistency filtering.
    - No evaluation on a dedicated code efficiency benchmark (e.g., EffiBench, NeurIPS 2024).
    - Evaluation is restricted to simple benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, DS-1000); not tested on repository-level or competition-level tasks where DPO’s benefits are less certain.
- **Methodological concerns:**
    - The PageRank scoring equation (Eq. 2) can assign high scores to weak test cases (e.g., “assert true”) if many solutions pass them, potentially degrading training quality.
    - The paper makes several incorrect claims: (i) OSS-Instruct does not extract programming concepts; it generates tasks directly from code fragments. (ii) PLUM’s test-case limitations are asserted without evidence. (iii) CodeDPO is claimed to “not rely on external resources,” yet initial seeds come from real-world repositories.
- **Data contamination and selection:**
    - Insufficient description of initial seeds (number, criteria, cleaning); no deduplication against evaluation benchmarks reported.
    - Potential dataset leakage could inflate reported scores.
- **Unresolved experimental contradictions and questions:**
    - On HumanEval/HumanEval+, CodeDPO (Magicoder-DS-6.7B) performs significantly worse than PLUM, contrasting with other results. No explanation.
    - CodeDPO and PLUM report opposite conclusions about DPO vs. KTO for code generation; the paper provides no systematic discussion to resolve this discrepancy.
    - In Table 6, KTO results are often worse than SFT; no explanation offered.
    - No justification for choosing KTO as a comparison baseline over other PO strategies.
- **Cost and practicality:** The algorithm’s economic cost (LLM calls, compute) is not discussed. The iterative generation of 15 solutions and test cases per task may be expensive.
- **One reviewer (rating 6) found few weaknesses**, but the majority reviewers raised the above concerns, and the overall rigor and presentation were rated low (scores: soundness 1–2, presentation 2–3, contribution 1–3).
