## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces FCoReBench, a dataset of 40 first-order combinatorial reasoning problems (e.g., graph coloring, knapsack, cryptarithmetic) along with scripts to generate instances of varying sizes and automatically verify solutions. The authors evaluate LLMs with existing prompting and symbolic-solver-aided approaches, finding poor performance that degrades with increasing problem size. They then propose SymPro-LM, a framework that combines an LLM with a symbolic solver (Z3) and a program interpreter, using feedback from solved examples, and achieve significant gains without requiring an LLM call at inference time. Reviewer 3 rated this paper a 5, finding the dataset well-established and the experiments convincing, while the other three reviewers (scores 3) found significant issues with novelty, presentation, and evaluation depth.

### Strengths
- The FCoReBench dataset covers a comprehensive and challenging set of NP-hard combinatorial reasoning problems, is of interest to the research community, and is designed so that unlimited new instances can be generated (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The proposed SymPro-LLM approach can work with different instances from the same first-order problem without needing re-evaluation using LLMs (R2).
- The experimental results show promising improvements: 21.61% over few‑shot prompting, 3.52% over PAL, and 16.83% over Logic‑LM; incorporating feedback yields a further 21.02% improvement after four rounds (R3). The experiments are thoroughly described with comprehensive charts and verification (R1).
- The approach is also demonstrated on other logical reasoning benchmarks, outperforming baselines on two of three datasets and remaining competitive on the third (R3).
- The discussion section (Section 8) provides insightful analysis, and it would be beneficial to list problems per situation in an appendix (R1).
- One reviewer (R3) considered the dataset and experiments convincing and of notable contribution, scoring the paper a 5, in contrast to the other three who rated it 3. This positive view should not be overlooked.

### Weaknesses
- **Misalignment and focus:** The title emphasizes the benchmark, but the main body centers on the SymPro-LM technique. The paper reads more as a methodology paper than a benchmark paper. R1 and R4 suggest reorganizing or retitling to match the true focus. R1 further notes that the experimental section is written around SymPro-LM rather than verifying the benchmark itself.
- **Novelty and innovation:** The proposed SymPro-LM is viewed as lacking novelty—it merely combines existing symbolic solvers, program generation, and LLMs without a more specific design (R1). R3 also questions whether the contribution meets ICLR standards, noting the main contribution appears to be the dataset.
- **Clarity and definition of “first order”:** The term “first-order” is confusing; it is unclear whether it refers to first‑order logic or something else, and no clear distinction is given between first‑order and non‑first‑order problems (R3). R1 also asks for clarification on line 122.
- **Insufficient evaluation of LLMs:** The evaluation uses an outdated LLM; recent models such as GPT‑4o and o1 are missing (R2, R4). Additionally, only limited analysis is provided for why existing approaches fail on the dataset (R2). R2 asks about the surprisingly high random baseline accuracy (>20%) for NP‑hard problems.
- **Lack of explanation for methodology choices:** Several design decisions are not justified, including:
  - Why the problem name and rules are rewritten to hide the category from the LLM (R1).
  - Why formal specifications or mathematical formulas are excluded from the problem descriptions (R1).
  - Why instances in the test set are larger than those in the training set, and why the test set has fewer instances (R1).
  - Why different temperatures are used for different solvers (R1).
  - What the “random” approach refers to in Table 1 (R1).
- **Incremental contribution of the dataset unclear:** The paper does not clearly articulate what makes FCoReBench unique compared to existing benchmarks that already contain first‑order combinatorial reasoning problems. Overlaps and differences with prior datasets are not highlighted (R4). R2 raises the concern of possible data contamination.
- **Scope restriction not justified:** The paper narrows to first‑order problems without explaining why the technique is restricted to that class, even though conceptually it could apply more broadly. No domain‑specific adaptations are given (R4).
- **Limited insight into LLM reasoning:** The task in the proposed framework essentially becomes translating pseudo‑code (NL(C), NL(X), NL(Y)) to Python, which does not test the same level of reasoning as solving the problems directly (R2).
- **Writing and presentation issues:** The paper suffers from inconsistent section formatting (e.g., Sections 5 and 5.1, Section 7 and 7.1), colloquial language, overuse of “we” and “the author”, and strange table layouts (R1). The fonts in Figure 4 are too small (R3). The writing lacks clarity and focus, making it hard to extract key takeaways from Section 7 (R2).
- **Specific questions about implementation:** 
  - In Section 5.1, what is being “trained” in SymPro‑LM if no neural network training occurs? (R1)
  - Does the training data D_P condition on previous C from different problems? (R1)
  - Why is Logic‑LM’s performance so much worse in Table 1, given it also offloads reasoning to a solver? (R2)
- **Manual labor in dataset construction:** The construction process (Section 4) requires significant manual effort; R1 questions whether it could be automated using an LLM.
- **Scalability concerns:** The approach can currently solve only first‑order problems; higher‑order logic requires individual generation, limiting scalability (R1).
- **Data contamination risk:** R2 asks whether LLMs may have seen these combinatorial problems in training, potentially biasing the results.
- **Minor typos:** Listed on pages 3, 4, 5, and 10 (R1).
