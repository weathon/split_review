## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Singular Value Adaptation (SiVA), a PEFT method that updates only the minor singular values of pre-trained weight matrices. The approach is motivated by the hypothesis that increasing the *effective rank* (a measure of singular value uniformity) of adapted matrices correlates with improved downstream performance. While the method demonstrates strong parameter efficiency across vision and language tasks, reviewers consistently question the central assumption linking effective rank to performance, raise concerns about theoretical derivations, and note several experimental deficiencies (e.g., weak baselines, missing comparisons, insufficient ablations). One reviewer (R3, score 6) finds the theory solid and the method elegantly simple; the other three (scores 3, 3, 3) are unconvinced and highlight major flaws.

### Strengths
- **Important problem.** Improving parameter-efficient fine-tuning is a timely topic (R1).
- **Parameter efficiency.** SiVA achieves better or comparable performance with significantly fewer parameters (up to 50× more efficient than LoRA) across diverse tasks (R1, R3).
- **Interesting theoretical angle.** The use of effective rank and singular-value analysis provides a novel perspective (R2, R4). Reviewer 3 specifically calls the theoretical analysis “solid” and “mathematically justified.”
- **Elegantly simple implementation.** The method requires only one initial SVD and gradient descent on diagonal elements (R3).
- **Clear Method section.** The derivation and algorithm are well explained (R4).
- **Extensive experimental coverage.** Evaluations span both vision (fine-grained classification) and NLP (GLUE, language generation) (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Core assumption (effective rank → performance) lacks justification.**  
   - The hypothesis that increased effective rank of ΔW leads to better downstream adaptation is asserted on the basis of a single dataset (Stanford Cars) and only for Query/Value matrices (Table 1) (R1, R2, R3).  
   - Reviewer 1 notes the reported correlation comes from different PEFT methods, which may be spurious; a controlled experiment with one fixed method is needed.  
   - Reviewer 2 calls the assumption “very strong” and demands theoretical establishment.  
   - Reviewer 4 tested LoRA on CoLA and MRPC with different seeds and found no obvious relation between effective rank and performance; a higher effective rank could even lead to overfitting.  
   - No comparison is made with alternative rank measures (e.g., condition number), so SiVA’s “optimality” for effective rank does not imply optimality for fine-tuning (R1).

2. **Theoretical derivations are questionable.**  
   - Equation (1)-(2) and appendix Eqs (6)-(8): the derivation from Cauchy–Schwarz to the claimed optimal solution is unclear and not a valid proof (R4).  
   - Line 203: the claim that the singular values of the update are δσ under a Frobenius-norm constraint contradicts standard singular value perturbation theory (singular values of a sum are not the sum of individual singular values) (R4).  
   - The relationship between the constraint on trainable parameters (Line 76) and the Frobenius-norm bound on B is not clarified (R1).  
   - Increasing effective rank by raising minor singular values is only one approach; the theory does not discuss reducing major singular values as an alternative (R1).

3. **Missing comparisons with established PEFT variants.**  
   - DoRA, AdaLoRA, Delta-LoRA, and GaLore are not included in experiments (R2).  
   - SVDiff (which adjusts all singular values) is a natural baseline; if training all singular values yields higher effective rank, SVDiff should perform better – why select only k singular values? (R4).  
   - For vision tasks, baseline results (LoRA: 45.38% on StanfordCars, 25.16% on FGVC) are far lower than reproducible numbers (easily >80% and >50% respectively). Hyperparameters for these baselines must be provided (R3).  
   - SiVA’s 5-epoch results are compared with 10-epoch baselines (LP, FF, LoRA, FourierFT) without justification (R3).

4. **Insufficient ablation and analysis.**  
   - The impact of the *perc* hyperparameter (number of updated singular values) on effective rank and performance is not studied across diverse scenarios (R1).  
   - No comparison of the effective rank of SiVA vs. other methods on different models (R4).  
   - Figure 1 (left) reports rank changes as decimal values (e.g., +0.05, +0.90) without explaining how a fractional “rank” is computed (R3).  
   - The transformer layer(s) analyzed in Figure 1 (left) are not specified; shallow vs. deep layers can differ significantly (R3).

5. **Experimental scope is limited.**  
   - GLUE evaluations omit QQP and MNLI, two of the largest tasks; their inclusion is needed to demonstrate scalability (R3).  
   - Only GPT-2 is used for language generation; more diverse LLMs (e.g., LLaMA, OPT) should be tested (R1).  
   - Improvements on GLUE are tiny and sensitive to random seeds; statistical significance is not shown (R2).  
   - A controlled experiment with similar parameter budgets across methods would be informative, though SiVA may already use fewer parameters than smallest baselines (R4).

6. **Method is overly complex for its insight.**  
   - Fine-tuning the bottom singular values of *W* is a natural process: these correspond to less important pre-trained components and are naturally adjusted during adaptation. The elaborate theory may overcomplicate a straightforward idea (R4).  
   - Even training *n* singular values (as in SVDiff) uses few parameters; the advantage of selecting only *k* is not clearly motivated (R4).

7. **Writing and presentation issues.**  
   - Missing citations in the Introduction (Lines 31, 35, 36, 41) (R2).  
   - Typographical error: left quotation mark before “effective rank” (R4).  
   - The “Value” axis label in Figure 1 (left) is not explained (R1).
