## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviewers evaluated this paper, which examines LLM alignment via political stereotypes through the lens of representativeness heuristics. Two reviewers gave a score of 6, while one gave an 8, indicating stronger enthusiasm for the analysis. The higher-scoring reviewer found the work extensive and thorough; the others, while recognizing value, noted limitations in generalizability and presentation. All agree that the paper reveals LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Gemini Pro, Llama2) exaggerate political positions compared to human respondents on the ANES and MFQ datasets, and that prompt-based mitigation strategies are reasonably effective.

### Strengths
- Brings an underexplored cognitive science perspective (representativeness heuristics) to understanding and mitigating political stereotypes in LLMs.
- Findings are valuable for understanding how LLMs deviate from human values and for designing alignment interventions.
- Methodology is well-formalized, non-ambiguous, easy to follow, and grounded in supporting literature.
- The use of diverse LLM families (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Gemini Pro, Llama2) allows comparison of behaviors across different training data and alignment approaches.
- The proposed prompt-based mitigation strategies are simple, practical, and shown to be reasonably effective.
- The Appendix (including prompt details, survey questions, preprocessing steps, additional results) complements the main content.
- One reviewer (scoring 8) specifically appreciated the Limitations section (Section 10) and considered the analysis extensive and thorough.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope:** The work focuses solely on the U.S. political context, which restricts generalizability of the observed patterns.
- **Presentation issues across all reviewers:**
  - Excessive formalism and relegation of crucial experimental details (specific prompts, exact survey questions from ANES and MFQ, preprocessing steps) to the Appendix; these should be in the main manuscript.
  - Figures and tables are often too small or hard to read (Figures 2, 3, 4; Tables 1, 3, 7, 8). Figure 2 colors are hard to distinguish, and figure sizes are inconsistent.
  - Some repeated definitions (e.g., re-definition of kappa in the Prompt Style Mitigation Analysis).
  - Citations are not consistently surrounded by parentheses; some in Section 5.2 are incorrectly ordered.
  - Results presentation contrasts with the clarity of the methodology; discussion needs improvement.
  - Tables 1 and 3 are too full; one reviewer suggested simplifying by focusing on key statistics (mean, std) and moving distribution details to the appendix, or aggregating results visually.
- **Insufficient analysis of mitigation strategies:** There is a lack of detailed analysis of prompt-style mitigation results (e.g., which strategies better align LLMs with human preferences, and why baseline LLMs perform better in some tasks).
- **Unclear connection to downstream tasks:** The link between the measured political stereotypes and tasks like fake news detection is not adequately explained, and the behavior could be influenced by the pre-training corpus.
- **Missing experiments and comparisons:**
  - No sensitivity analysis for temperature – how do results change when temperature varies (Appendix D and F)? One reviewer raised this as a question.
  - The study does not include more up-to-date or open models, which could provide greater transparency and insight into the roots of exaggerated behaviors.
  - How are refusals and hallucinations handled during evaluation? When do they occur, and how do they affect the analysis?
- **Minor issues:** Section 11 (Ethics Policy) contains some typos.
- One reviewer (scoring 6) noted that conditions underlying specific exaggerations require more emphasis to uncover root causes.
