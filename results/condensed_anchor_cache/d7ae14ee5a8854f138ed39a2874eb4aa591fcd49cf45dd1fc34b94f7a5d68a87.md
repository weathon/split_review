## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Neural Activation Prior (NAP) for OOD detection, based on the observation that for channels before the global pooling layer, a few neurons are activated with large response by ID samples but not by OOD samples. The scoring function SNAP uses the ratio of the maximum activation to the mean activation within each channel, inspired by a signal-to-noise perspective. The method is plug-and-play, requires no extra training or data, and is claimed to be orthogonal to existing methods (MSP, Energy, ReAct, ASH). Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet-1k show that combining NAP with other methods improves performance. The approach is also extended to Transformers. Reviewers are deeply split: one rates the paper highly (8) praising simplicity and effectiveness, while three rate it 3, 3, and 5, citing limited novelty, unsatisfactory empirical gains for strong baselines, instability issues, and lack of theoretical justification.

### Strengths
- Extensive experiments conducted across diverse datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1k) and multiple OOD benchmarks (reviewers 1, 3).
- Minimal computational overhead; method is simple and easy to implement (reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- Well-written and easy to understand; uses visualization (e.g., Figure 1) to motivate the observation (reviewer 2).
- Novel application of intra-channel activation statistics (max/mean ratio inspired by SNR) – a creative approach that focuses on pre-pooling internal features rather than output-level statistics (reviewer 4).
- Plug-and-play design: no additional training, no external data, no model modifications required (reviewers 3, 4).
- Claimed orthogonality to existing methods (e.g., ReAct, MSP, Energy) allowing complementary integration; combining NAP with these methods improves their performance significantly in many settings (reviewers 3, 4).
- Extensive validation shows that NAP enhances OOD detection when combined with weak and moderate baselines, and reduces false positive rates (reviewer 3).
- Unique perspective that enriches the OOD detection toolkit; broad applicability across architectures (CNNs and Transformers) (reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty / Overlap with prior work**: The observation that features closer to the output produce stronger activations for ID samples is well known (e.g., MSP uses maximum softmax activation). Several reviewers note that the core idea is similar to ASH (both focus on prominent activations in feature maps) and overlaps with the phenomenon described in Pei et al. (ICLR 2024). Thus the claim of novelty is overstated (reviewers 1, 2).
- **Limited empirical improvement for strong baselines**: While NAP provides a large boost for weak baselines, the gain over strong methods like ASH is small. When used alone, NAP achieves only 89.19 AUROC (Table 3), which is worse than several prior methods (ASH, DICE, SCALE, ReAct) (reviewer 1).
- **Unjustified use of mean as “noise strength”**: The paper (line 330) treats the mean activation as representing noise, but provides no rigorous justification. Figure 1b actually shows the mean of ID samples is higher than that of OOD samples, which contradicts the noise interpretation. The connection between activation mean and noise strength is unclear and counterintuitive (reviewers 1, 4).
- **Rationale for max/mean ratio is weak**: If both max and mean are higher for ID, dividing by the mean may weaken the discriminative signal. The authors do not compare with using only the maximum activation value. A justification for the ratio formulation is missing (reviewer 1).
- **Score instability and overfitting to object-centric datasets**: The max/mean ratio is highly dependent on the pixel area of ID semantics. For images with large objects (activating most pixels), the ratio will be low; for small objects, high. This suggests the method may be coincidentally effective on ImageNet/CIFAR but would fail on textures or datasets where objects differ in size. The authors should test with Textures as the ID dataset (reviewer 2).
- **Sensitivity to semantic complexity**: Within the same ID dataset, categories with sparse semantics (few patterns) will yield lower scores than categories with complex semantics. This could cause intra-class inconsistency. Experiments with sparse ID vs. complex OOD are needed (reviewer 2).
- **Max operation amplifies instability and spurious correlations**: The maximum is inherently unstable; near-OOD samples that partially match ID patterns can still produce high max activations, leading to false positives. The near-OOD experiments may not be comprehensive enough. Moreover, max can amplify spurious correlations – no experiments on datasets with known spurious correlations are provided (reviewer 2).
- **Lack of theoretical framework**: The method is purely empirical. No formal analysis explains why intra-channel activation patterns (especially the max/mean ratio) should distinguish ID from OOD generally. A theoretical grounding is needed, including why the assumption holds across diverse architectures and datasets (reviewers 2, 4).
- **Potential lowering of baselines**: The reported results for ReAct, ASH, and SCALE in Table 3 are significantly lower than the numbers in their original papers. Similarly, results in Table 13 differ from Fort et al. (NeurIPS 2021). This raises concerns about fair baseline comparison (reviewer 2).
- **Limited scope of evaluation**: The method is evaluated only on natural image datasets. In the current era of large language models and multimodal foundational models, the relevance of this OOD detection approach is questioned. The authors should discuss how NAP complements such models (reviewer 4).
- **Unclear advantages over existing metrics**: The claim that NAP is “orthogonal” to ReAct, ASH, Energy, and MSP is not adequately explained. Why should combining them improve performance? A clear comparative analysis (e.g., a table or diagram) of strengths and weaknesses relative to each method is missing (reviewer 4).
- **Scalability not addressed**: No discussion of computational cost or practicality for large architectures (e.g., ResNet-101, Vision Transformers). Does the per-channel max/mean calculation scale efficiently? (reviewer 4).
- **Focus on penultimate layer only**: The method only uses activations before the final pooling layer, potentially missing valuable information from earlier layers. This may limit applicability to fine-grained tasks (e.g., pixel-level defect detection) and may not handle all OOD scenarios, especially those with significant semantic differences (reviewer 3).
- **Generalization concerns**: The reliance on specific activation patterns may cause challenges when applied to diverse architectures or datasets that do not match the object-centric assumption (reviewer 3).
