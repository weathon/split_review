## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces GDrag, a task-aware, optimization-based framework for general-purpose interactive point-based image editing. GDrag addresses two types of ambiguity: intention ambiguity (misinterpreting user purpose) and content ambiguity (distorted target areas). To tackle intention ambiguity, it defines a taxonomy of three atomic manipulations (relocation, rotation, non-rigid transformations) that can be combined to represent complex edits. To mitigate content ambiguity, GDrag proposes two strategies: an Anti-ambiguity Dense Trajectory calculation method (ADT) that converts sparse handle points into a dense set using semantic and geometric neighbors, and a Self-adaptive Motion Supervision method (SMS) that jointly optimizes point-wise adaptation scales and latent feature biases. Experiments on the DragBench dataset demonstrate superior performance over existing methods. The code will be released.

### Strengths
- **Originality in problem formulation**: The paper clearly identifies and addresses the overlooked issues of intention ambiguity and content ambiguity in point-based drag editing (R1, R3, R4, R5). The task-aware decomposition into three atomic manipulations (relocation, rotation, non-rigid) is concise and effective, enabling clearer control over complex transformations (R3, R5).
- **Innovative components**: ADT provides a novel dense trajectory estimation that incorporates contextual information, particularly valuable for 3D manipulations like rotation (R2, R3). SMS introduces a robust way to optimize latent features across multiple denoising steps, and the approach of optimizing \(z_0\) instead of \(z_t\) adds novelty (R5).
- **Strong empirical validation**: GDrag is evaluated on the DragBench benchmark with both quantitative and qualitative results, showing consistent improvement over state-of-the-art methods in trajectory accuracy and image quality (R1, R3). The results provide strong empirical evidence (R3).
- **Clarity and presentation**: The writing is mostly clear, engaging, and easy to understand (R1, R2, R5). Figures are illustrative and help convey the method (R4). The problem statement and proposed solution are logically organized (R3).
- **Significance**: The method facilitates user intention expression and enhances interaction, achieving competitive results (R1). The overall approach is highly intuitive and offers valuable insights for future research (R3). The decomposition of drag-based manipulation into atomic tasks is a practical contribution (R5).
- **Reasonable method**: The proposed framework is reasonable for the challenging problem of intention-aware drag editing (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear introduction of key terminology**: The paper lacks a clear explanation of what "handle points" are in drag-based diffusion, which is necessary for readers unfamiliar with the task. This undermines the persuasiveness of the problem statement and the examples illustrating ambiguity (R1, R4).
- **Limited intention understanding**: The method categorizes user intentions into predefined atomic actions rather than genuinely inferring intent from the drag trajectory itself. This heuristic, category-based approach limits the system's ability to handle novel or nuanced interactions that fall outside the predefined taxonomy, and the categories are not exhaustive of all possible intentions (R1, R4). A related concern is whether the approach can capture complex motions like a hand transitioning from open to closed (R2, R5). One reviewer questioned whether there is any analysis of understanding human intention purely from the input drag (R4).
- **Dependence on semantic segmentation performance**: ADT relies on a semantic segmentation model to select dense points. The estimation of dense points and trajectories may be adversely affected by segmentation errors, leading to significant deviations for complex or ambiguous motions (e.g., hand shape transitions) (R2, R5). This is particularly relevant for edits that go beyond simple movements.
- **Lack of computational analysis**: Placing dense points likely introduces significant computational overhead, yet no experimental validation or quantitative data on runtime or complexity is provided. This makes it difficult to assess feasibility for real-time use (R1, R4). Additionally, the computational complexity claim in line 299 (\(O(TL)\)) appears inaccurate; compared methods typically use a fixed timestep optimized \(N\) times, which is much less than \(O(TL)\) (R2).
- **SMS contribution appears marginal**: The ablation study in Table 3 suggests that Self-adaptive Motion Supervision (SMS) barely contributes to performance improvements. A direct comparison between traditional motion supervision and SMS is needed to clarify its benefit (R2). One reviewer also found the reasoning behind using low-rank adaptation (line 324) vague and requiring more detailed explanation (R1).
- **Unfair qualitative comparison**: Since GDrag uses augmented dense trajectories for motion supervision, the qualitative comparison against methods that use sparse points may be biased. It should be shown what results those methods produce when given denser trajectories as input (R2).
- **Missing ablation and parameter studies**: The paper would benefit from more detailed ablation studies explaining the core modules of ADT and SMS (R3). Also, experiments on the variation of hyperparameters \(\rho\) and \(\beta\) are missing (R2). A comparison between fixed and variable optimization steps (as mentioned in lines 299-302) would help justify the random optimization step choice (R3).
- **Unclear design choices**: The necessity of using features from the original UNet instead of features from the UNet with LoRA directly is not justified (R2). The "rotation" example in the middle row of Figure 3 is ambiguous—it should be clarified whether it is meant to rotate the back edge of the bottle to the front (R1).
- **Missing metric definition and failure analysis**: The Distance metric should be clearly defined and distinguished from the Mean Distance metric used in DragDiffusion (R3). The paper would be more comprehensive if it included a discussion of failure cases (R5).
- **Note on reviewer disagreement**: While most reviewers rated the paper 6/10, one reviewer (R3) rated it 8/10 and found the contributions highly original and significant, noting that the method is intuitive and well-presented. The same reviewer still pointed out the need for clearer ablation details and additional comparisons on optimization steps.
