## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a two-stage method (bind-then-generalize) to inject shape-level and pose-level human priors into diffusion models (SD1.5, SDXL) via a learnable pose-aware token, multiple losses, and adversarial attention map refinement. It is motivated by limitations of prior work, especially HcP. Evaluated on a self-collected dataset, it shows quantitative and qualitative improvements over SDXL and SDXL+LoRA. There is significant disagreement among reviewers: two reviewers (scores 5) found the method well-motivated and effective, while three reviewers (scores 3) raised major concerns about validation, comparisons, and clarity.

### Strengths
- Addresses a significant problem: anatomical errors in human body generation with diffusion models.
- Incorporation of pose and shape priors is intuitive.
- Quantitative and qualitative results show improved fidelity over SDXL and SDXL+LoRA.
- Well-motivated: identifies and analyzes specific limitations of HcP (retention of noisy priors, limited human understanding, limited generalization).
- Multiple losses combined and adversarial learning strategy are reasonable.
- Well-written and easy to understand.
- Overall design is straightforward and appears effective.

### Weaknesses
- **No quantitative evaluation of anatomical plausibility.** The qualitative results still contain clear deformities (Fig 9: disproportionate bodies, overlapping bodies, extra hands, hands on feet). Suggested to derive metrics from pose estimation tools like DensePose or MotionBERT.
- **Insufficient comparisons with prior work.** The most directly relevant method, HcP, is not compared directly (only noted as not open-sourced). Strongly requested: compare in HcP’s experimental setting using its reported results, or implement key components of HcP, HyperHuman, TokenCompose, or ControlNet to substantiate improvements.
- **Ablation studies are not convincing.** Only qualitative visual examples (Fig 6–8); no quantitative ablation. In Fig 7, the three rows look similar, so the individual contributions of the pose and shape priors are unclear. Quantitative ablation for each loss component (suppressing, margin, scaling) is needed, especially as scaling loss may overlap with the others.
- **Shape component is unclear and potentially problematic.** How the shape mask \(\hat{\mathcal{M}}_{shape}\) is derived from attention maps and its value distribution are not explained. The mask from segmentation is entangled with pose and may fail for loose garments or inaccurate segmentation. The criteria for selecting and binding human-related tokens (e.g., how to handle adjectives like “fat”/“thin” or multiple people) are not given.
- **Background suppression losses may degrade non-human generation.** When the prompt is complex, suppressing background region activations could hurt the quality of non-human parts; this trade-off is not analyzed.
- **Method is overly complex and missing implementation details.** Combined many changes and loss terms; lacks elegance. Required: explicit loss weights, tuning process, shapes of pose and shape tokens, number of training/evaluation images, dataset search terms/filtering, and discussion of training stability and cost compared to prior works.
- **Pose-level scaling loss is not clearly defined** (unlike shape masks, pose masks are sticks/points, so \(L_{scale}^{pose}\) needs explanation).
- **Evidence for claims about SD3/SDXL struggling with human bodies is lacking**; no concrete results shown.
- **Motivation for choosing this method over alternative two-step frameworks** (e.g., generate pose/shape first with SMPL or motion diffusion, then condition a diffusion model with ControlNet/HumanSD) is not justified.
- **Dataset concerns:** why not use an open-source dataset like Human-art for direct comparison with HcP? Potential demographic biases from crawled data are not addressed.
- **Many typos** (e.g., “the pretrained SD fail” → “fails”, “those human-related token” → “tokens”).
- **Last example in Figure 6 still appears deformed** even for an easy case; the paper should analyze why the method still struggles and discuss limitations.
- **Two reviewers (R3, R5) gave higher scores** and found the method well-motivated and improvements clear, but the majority reviewers (R1, R2, R4, score 3) express significant concerns about validation, comparisons, and clarity.
