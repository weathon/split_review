## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Prompt Infection, a novel self-replicating prompt injection attack that spreads across LLMs in multi-agent systems (MAS). The attack starts with a single malicious prompt in external content (PDF, email, web) and propagates via agent-to-agent communication. The authors evaluate attack success rates across scenarios (content manipulation, data theft, malware spread, scams) using 120 user instructions paired with malicious inputs (360 pairs). Experiments use GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 under global (broadcast) and local (partial history) messaging topologies, including a linear chain and a society-of-agents setup. They also propose LLM Tagging (prepending agent/user identity) as a defense, finding it effective only when combined with other defenses. Key findings: self-replication is generally more successful; stronger models (GPT-4) resist initial injection better but execute attacks more effectively once compromised; no single defense suffices.

### Strengths
- **Novel threat model**: Extends prompt injection from single-agent to multi-agent systems; the self-replicating, virus-like propagation is an interesting and timely contribution (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
- **Systematic evaluation**: Broad range of research questions across different models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5), messaging topologies (global vs. local), and attack scenarios; experiments are comprehensive and demonstrate practicality (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- **Defense included**: LLM Tagging is an intuitive, simple defense; combined with instruction-based defenses it shows promising mitigation (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Interesting trade-off**: The finding that more powerful models are simultaneously more resistant to initial injection and more dangerous once compromised provides valuable insight (R1, R4).
- **Figures and tables** are generally clear in conveying attack propagation and success rates (R4; note: R1, R2, R3, R5 contest readability – see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Threat model feasibility and prior work**: The attack assumes a single infection prompt can be injected into external content under open domains; this assumption expands feasibility and needs more discussion (R3, R5). Spread threats in MAS have been studied in recent works (refs [1],[2]); the paper’s contribution over these is incremental (R3, R5). A clear statement of attacker capabilities, goals, and threat model is missing (R3, R5). The advantage over standard prompt injection in MAS is not empirically compared (R2).
- **Presentation quality**: The paper is hard to follow (R3, R5). Specifically:
  - Figures are poorly styled: Figure 4 legend and axes labels are unreadably small (R1); labels in Figures 4(a) and 4(b) are unclear (R2); all figures and tables should have uniform, clearer fonts (R3, R5).
  - The self-replication mechanism is not clearly explained or formally defined in the methods section (R3, R5). How is self-replication achieved? (R5). Why omit self-replication in stealth attacks – is there a trade-off? (R3, R5).
  - The LLM Tagging defense lacks detail in the methods section (R3, R5).
  - Description of evaluation metrics is insufficient given multiple scenarios (R3, R5).
  - The introduction inappropriately claims a research gap from single-agent to MAS security, when MAS security is already being researched (R3, R5).
  - The society-of-agents experiments feel disconnected from the main linear-chain thrust (R1).
- **Limited model scope**: Evaluation only uses GPT-4 and GPT-3.5; conclusions would be more convincing if extended to other models (e.g., Claude-3.5, Llama-3.2) with different safety alignments (R2). Need comparison with models having different safety measures (R2).
- **Defense analysis incomplete**: The study only tests LLM Tagging in combination with other defenses, not other pairs of defenses, making the claim of LLM Tagging’s effectiveness less supported (R1). The defense section should explore more robust methods (e.g., anomaly detection, runtime monitoring) (R4).
- **Missing theoretical analysis**: Why do models like GPT-4 become more dangerous once compromised? Architectural factors (context handling, memory, task execution) are not investigated (R4). No discussion of how model design differences influence susceptibility (R4).
- **Real-world applicability**: Little discussion of how these infections would manifest in current enterprise-level LLM deployments, what safeguards exist, and how they might fare (R4). The study focuses on LLM-based MAS; generalizability to systems with non-LLM agents is not explored (R4).
- **RQ4 lacks comparison**: No comparison with existing memory-retrieval attacks (ref [1]) (R3, R5).
- **Unclear messaging definitions**: The exact definition of “local messaging” (agents accessing partial histories) is unclear; does “global messaging” mean broadcasting? (R1).
- **Minor formatting**: Figure 4 should label attack scenarios from left to right (R3, R5). One reviewer (R4, score 6) considered the paper well-executed overall but acknowledged the above limitations; the majority (R1–R3, R5, scores 5) were more critical, especially on presentation and novelty.
