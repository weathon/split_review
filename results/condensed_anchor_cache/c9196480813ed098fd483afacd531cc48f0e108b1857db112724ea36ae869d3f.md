## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper analyzes concurrent vs. sequential replay in continual learning using overparameterized linear models, concluding that sequential replay can outperform concurrent replay when tasks are less similar, and proposes a hybrid method. Reviewers find the question original and the theoretical analysis a valuable first step, but raise major concerns about the restrictive assumptions (especially memory freshness), limited applicability beyond the two-task linear case, lack of significance in the DNN experiments, insufficient presentation clarity, and missing details on the hybrid algorithm's practicality. While some reviewers see novelty in the theory, others question whether the setup is appropriate and whether the experiments support the claims.

### Strengths

- Original and interesting question that challenges the standard concurrent replay paradigm in continual learning.
- Provides one of the first comprehensive theoretical analyses of replay-based CL methods, with explicit closed-form solutions for forgetting and generalization errors in overparameterized linear models (Theorem 1, Proposition 1).
- For the two-task case, gives a clear comparison and identifies conditions under which each replay strategy performs better.
- Leverages theoretical insights to propose a hybrid replay method combining concurrent and sequential replay based on task similarity.
- Experiments on real datasets (CIFAR) with deep neural networks show moderate improvements of the hybrid approach over standard concurrent replay.
- Paper is well organized.

### Weaknesses

- **Assumptions severely limit applicability**: The memory freshness assumption (new samples at each replay step) is unrealistic in most CL settings—past data cannot be re-sampled—and sidesteps the core issue of buffer overfitting. The paper does not address how this limitation affects the relevance of the theory.
- **Theory only for two tasks; multi-task generalization is restrictive**: The theoretical comparison is derived only for the two-task case; extensions to T>2 rely on restrictive conditions (e.g., Theorem 3 requires \(p = O(T^4 n^2 M^2)\)). The practical usefulness of these guarantees is questionable.
- **Uncertain relationship between theory and experiments**: The theory uses linear regression with identical input distributions across tasks, while experiments use classification on corrupted images. No insight is provided on how the theoretical results could carry over to non-linear DNNs or classification tasks.
- **Experiment lacks rigor**: Numbers in Table 1 are from a single run; no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or uncertainty measures are reported. The reported performance differences are small and could be due to random fluctuation. The training schedule (hybrid only at task 5) is not motivated, and hyperparameter tuning is unspecified—it is unclear if improvements come from the replay strategy or simply different schedules.
- **Hybrid algorithm is not practical**: Algorithm 1 requires ground-truth parameter values to compute task gaps or similarity. The DIVIDEBUFFER procedure is not defined, and no method is given to estimate task similarity from the replay buffer alone.
- **No theoretical analysis of the hybrid method**: The paper proposes a hybrid strategy but provides no theoretical justification or analysis for it, limiting the theoretical contribution.
- **Presentation and clarity issues**: Key terms in Proposition 1 are undefined in the main text. The partitioning of training data (lines 80-83) is not explained as an assumption or technique. The replay setup (lines 177-178) is ambiguous—does "fresh" mean replay only uses the last task? How replay samples are selected is unspecified. The notation \(t \in [T]\) is unusual.
- **Mathematical correctness concerns**: The forgetting and generalization expressions in Theorem 1 appear non-random (coefficients from Proposition 1 are deterministic), but the targets (e.g., \(w_T\)) are random due to sampling—an expectation seems missing. The claim that forgetting/generalization approach 0 as \(p \to \infty\) (line 325) is incorrect because \(r_a \to 1\) so the first term in Eq. (9) does not vanish.
- **Setup limitations not discussed**: The linear model may lack the capacity to learn multiple different tasks; the paper does not address when/if the trained model's error is smaller than that of the all-zero vector. The relationship to Evron et al. (2022) is misleading—their setup allowed arbitrary subspaces, while this paper assumes identical covariate distributions for all tasks.
- **Missing practical considerations**: How task similarity should be measured in practice is not addressed. The effect of memory size on the relative performance of sequential vs. concurrent replay is not explored in theory or experiments. The forgetting definition (sum of losses vs. max) is used without justification.
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**: Only concurrent replay is compared in experiments; no comparison with other replay methods (e.g., GEM, A-GEM—which are not cited in related work). The experimental settings (task-incremental on CIFAR) are atypical; using class-incremental or simpler benchmarks would be more aligned.
- **Requests from reviewers** that are missing or unanswered: need for multiple runs with uncertainty, exploration of buffer sizes, analysis of the sequential replay schedule's impact, discussion of how to choose replay samples (not just assuming fresh samples), and justification for using linear models to study replay in DNNs.
