## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Gradient-Based Inference (GBI), a method that enables neural networks to infer and adapt abstract task representations dynamically via gradients backpropagated to a task representation layer. The approach is grounded in variational inference (EM framework) and uses a one-step gradient update from a maximal entropy initialization as an approximation. Experiments span toy datasets, image classification (CNN), and language modeling (LSTM) to demonstrate benefits in learning efficiency, generalization, reduced forgetting, uncertainty estimation, and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection.

### Strengths
- **Originality and relevance**: The problem of task inference and recomposition is clearly defined and important. The connection to cognitive science (human task learning and generalization) and the framing as a variational inference problem are novel and provide a solid foundation.
- **Novel method**: The one-step gradient update approximation (maximum entropy initialization + single gradient step) is simple and effective; its validity is checked experimentally (Fig. 3G, H). This symbolic estimation of the optimal point appears novel.
- **Generality**: Effectiveness is demonstrated across three modalities (toy, vision, language), showing the method’s potential as a domain-agnostic approach (R1, R4). Code is provided for reproducibility.
- **OOD detection performance**: GBI shows clear superiority over the canonical classifier in OOD detection (Table 3, Fig. 4A, B, C) and provides interpretable uncertainty estimates (R1, R2, R4).
- **Experimental validation on varied scenarios**: From synthetic datasets to complex tasks (image classification and language modeling), GBI shows promising advantages in learning efficiency, generalization, and reduced forgetting (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation for gradient-based inference**: The paper does not sufficiently explain why gradients offer an advantage over alternative methods for task inference (e.g., learned task embeddings, attention mechanisms, meta-learning). A discussion on how GBI avoids issues like task abstraction misalignment is missing (R1).
- **Coherence and exposition**: The flow between sections is disjointed; transitions from theory to experiments are abrupt, and each experiment’s connection back to the framework is not clearly explained (R1). The explanation of the one-step gradient update and maximal entropy initialization is unclear; a visual schematic and more detailed mathematical underpinnings are needed (R1). Intuitive examples to illustrate the process are lacking (R1).
- **Incomplete comparison to existing methods**: The paper claims advantages in OOD detection and uncertainty estimation but does not compare against established methods like ensembles or Bayesian neural networks (R1). The method is not compared to other task inference approaches (R1).
- **Limited complexity of evaluations**:
  - OOD detection experiments are limited to MNIST and Fashion-MNIST (simple datasets); a comparison like CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN would be more realistic (R3). This raises doubts about practical applicability.
  - Catastrophic forgetting is only evaluated on a toy dataset; comprehensive evaluation on standard continual learning benchmarks is missing (R3). The claim of “no forgetting” (Table 1) is not strong: baseline MSE is 0.24, but the LSTM deviation is not shown to be statistically significant (R4). More seeds (at least 30 vs. 4 in Fig. 5D) are needed to increase confidence, especially when differences are small (R4).
- **Conceptual novelty and clarity**:
  - Optimizing only input representation weights (not the whole model) is not entirely novel; prior work includes Lampinen & McClelland (2020 PNAS), Kumar et al. (2024) (R4).
  - The purpose of LSTM vs. GBI-LSTM experiments (Fig. 2, 3D,E, 5A,B,D) is unclear; they appear to only confirm the impact of providing task labels. This needs better motivation aligned with Sections 1 and 2 (R2).
  - Why learning to infer the task category improves generalization is not explained. Low-dimensional analysis (e.g., showing non-overlapping subspaces as in Lin et al., 2024) would help (R4).
- **Architectural and implementation details**:
  - The paper lacks a diagram of the GBI-LSTM architecture specifying which synapses are modified and the task abstraction layer z (R4).
  - For language tasks, demonstrating GBI with a transformer instead of an LSTM would strengthen the contribution (R4).
  - The ratio of shared units between LSTM and GBI differs in the 0th binned training block (Fig. 2C); they should be initialized identically for fair comparison (R4).
- **Training/inference mismatch**: The training methods used in experiments (RNN sequence prediction, autoencoder) are not exactly the same as the MLE of likelihood (1) described in Section 2, and the paper does not discuss how this affects the theoretical justification (R2).
- **Trade-off with accuracy**: The canonical classifier may have better accuracy than GBI (Table 2), but the paper does not discuss this trade-off or provide reasoning (R2).
- **Language prediction results**: GBI loss starts higher (~6.95) and decreases to 6.6 over 100 optimization steps; it is unclear if loss continues to decrease and whether 6.6 is significantly better than the LSTM baseline of 6.8 (R4).
- **Missing references**: The paper references “supplementary material” (L357) and “Table S8” (L396) but these are not included (R2).
- **Sensitivity to task abstraction quality**: The paper uses human-provided labels for task abstraction but does not investigate how method performance depends on the quality of these labels (R3). Hyperparameter sweeps (learning rate, number of units) are not reported, raising concern that hyperparameters might artificially equalize LSTM and GBI performance (R4).
- **Choice of RNN architecture**: Using an LSTM for the simple toy dataset may be overkill; a GRU or vanilla RNN would be more appropriate (R4).
