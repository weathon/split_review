## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces “shadow cones,” a physics-inspired framework for learning partial order embeddings in hyperbolic space. The framework models hierarchical entailment as subset relations between shadows cast by an opaque object (the “parent”) under a light source. It generalizes prior hyperbolic entailment cones (Ganea et al.) to multiple formulations (umbral/penumbral) and two hyperbolic models (Poincaré ball and half-space). Experiments on four datasets show that shadow cones outperform entailment cones in link prediction. The work is well-motivated, mathematically rigorous, and includes intuitive illustrations.

### Strengths
- **Novel and well-motivated approach.** The shadow cones framework generalizes existing entailment cones and provides a physically intuitive interpretation (moon+light source) that explains why prior methods struggled near the center of the Poincaré ball (the “ε-hole” problem). [R1,R3,R5,R6]
- **Comprehensive mathematical formulation.** The authors derive smooth loss functions and prove several consistency results (e.g., cone inclusion forms a partial order). [R1,R3,R5]
- **Generalization across hyperbolic models.** The framework is instantiated in both the Poincaré disk and Poincaré half-space, extending beyond the earlier Poincaré-ball-only constructions. [R2,R5,R6]
- **Strong empirical results.** Shadow cones consistently outperform the entailment-cone baseline across four datasets (Table 2). The Penumbral-Poincaré-ball variant matches the baseline mathematically but achieves better results in practice (attributed to better optimization). [R1,R5,R6]
- **Clear insight into training stability.** The umbral/penumbral formulations avoid the “ε-hole” problem and show improved optimization properties over earlier approaches. [R5,R6]
- **Excellent exposition with illustrative figures.** Intuitive diagrams and Table 1 (summary of the four variants) help readers understand the geometric construction. [R4,R5,R6]

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited comparison to other methods.** The paper compares only against hyperbolic entailment cones. Several important baselines are missing:
   - Euclidean poset embeddings (e.g., order embeddings, box embeddings, Gaussian embeddings). [R3,R4,R5,R6]
   - Other hyperbolic graph embedding approaches (tree embedding, link prediction, DAG learning). [R3]
   - Methods from the DAG-learning literature that could serve as baselines for partial-order structure learning. [R3]
   - Methods using other curvature manifolds (e.g., spheres). [R4]

2. **Narrow experimental evaluation.**
   - Only link prediction is tested; no evaluation on downstream tasks such as classification, collaborative filtering, or structure learning. [R3,R4,R5]
   - Without comparisons to non-hyperbolic approaches, it is unclear whether the advantage is due to the specific cone formulation or to hyperbolic space itself. [R4]
   - The paper should clarify that the baseline “link prediction without embedding” would achieve 100% accuracy; the point is to evaluate the embedding’s ability to generalize from partially observed edges. [R3]

3. **Unresolved discrepancy between equivalent formulations.** According to Theorem 3.2, Penumbral-Poincaré-ball cones are equivalent to entailment cones, yet their empirical results differ significantly (Table 2). The paper does not explain whether this is due to the loss function, training procedure, or other implementation details. [R2,R5,R6]

4. **Incomplete explanation of symbols and figures.** Many symbols in the figures are not described in the captions or text, making the diagrams hard to interpret. Captions are brief and ambiguous. [R1,R2]

5. **Missing motivation for choosing among the four variants.** The paper presents four formulations (umbral/penumbral × Poincaré ball/half-space) but does not discuss how to select one for a given dataset or application, nor what the shape/size of the light source or object implies in terms of semantics. [R6]

6. **Lack of broader contextual discussion.** The paper focuses narrowly on extending entailment cones; a more thorough positioning within the partial-order embedding literature (order embeddings, box embeddings, DAG learning, hyperbolic tree learning) would benefit readers. [R1,R3,R6]

7. **Insufficient justification for claims about training ease.** At the end of Section 4, the authors state that shadow cones are easier to train, but this claim is not supported by experiments or analysis beyond a sentence. [R5]

8. **Unaddressed technical questions.**
   - The loss function (Eq. 1) uses symbols \(P\) and \(N\) that are not defined. [R3,R4]
   - The boundary computation for cones is not explained (neither in main text nor appendix). [R3]
   - Why are umbral cones not geodesically convex, even though their Euclidean analogues are? The term “cone” may mislead readers. [R3]
   - Why does the umbral definition require a ball around \(y\) to be in the shadow, while the penumbral definition only requires the point \(y\)? The umbral variant performs best; is this because the loss pushes points to the interior, avoiding numerical issues on boundaries? [R3]
   - What are the potential challenges of learning representations in the half-space model (e.g., numerical issues, optimization difficulties)? [R5]
   - How would the method handle real-world graphs that are not exact DAGs (e.g., with cycles or noise)? [R4]
   - Could the embedding be applied to multiclass hierarchical problems? [R4]

9. **Title issues.** The title lacks keywords such as “shadow cone,” “hierarchy,” or “partial order,” and the word “moon” may mislead readers into thinking the embedding has volume (which is not the case for penumbral cones). [R6]

10. **Minor presentation issues.**
    - At the end of the first paragraph of Section 3, a sentence should read “\(v\) is in the shadow cone of \(u\) iff \(v \subset\) the shadow of \(u\).” [R4]
    - After Theorem 4.2, the word “their” should be removed from “drawn closer to their the cones.” [R4]
