## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper analyzes how the number of intermediate decoding steps (chain-of-thought length) affects the expressive power of decoder-only transformers. For input length \(n\), logarithmic steps add little power; linear steps enable recognition of all regular languages but not all context-sensitive languages; \(t(n)\) steps allow simulation of \(t(n)\) steps of a Turing machine; and polynomial steps precisely capture the class of problems solvable in deterministic polynomial time (under standard complexity conjectures). Lower bounds rely on a layer-norm hash construction; upper bounds are obtained by simulating attention using a Turing machine. The work connects transformer-defined classes to classical complexity classes via Equation (1), effectively refactoring the role of memory into intermediate steps.

### Strengths
- Establishes both lower and upper bounds that are almost tight, significantly extending the state of the art in relating transformers to classical complexity classes.
- The norm-hash construction is natural and likely useful in other contexts.
- The connection to traditional complexity classes (Equation 1) cleanly separates the use of scalable memory into intermediate steps; the simulations of Turing machines by transformers (and vice versa) are natural and well-motivated.
- Important specific results: transformers with a linear number of decoding steps can recognize all regular languages, yet cannot recognize all context-sensitive languages.
- The theory is well developed and presented, shedding light on the theoretical limitations and capabilities of transformers for researchers and practitioners.

### Weaknesses
- **Readability and self-containedness**: The paper is technically intricate and assumes significant prior knowledge in formal language theory and complexity-theoretic modeling of transformers; the core idea of norm-hash is not illustrated with examples, making its introduction abrupt; notation is unnecessarily complicated, hurting accessibility for the general ICLR audience. The paper also relies on two previous papers without being sufficiently self-contained. (Three reviewers rated 8; one reviewer rated 6, who was particularly critical of the lack of self-containedness and the dense exposition.)
- **Lack of learnability and generalization analysis**: The work studies only expressive power, not how efficiently these models can be trained to solve the problems, their sample complexity, or their ability to generalize to unseen examples—all crucial for practical deployment. Reviewer 3 also notes that simulation-based analysis appears unable to address learnability.
- **Equivalence to P is less surprising**: The equivalence between CoT(poly) and P relies on polynomial-time reductions, a lenient notion; furthermore, the simulation of transformers by Turing machines incurs a quadratic blowup (CoT(\(t\)) equivalent to TM using \(O(t^2)\) time). A tighter connection (e.g., \(O(t)\) time or equivalence under logspace reductions) would be more striking.
- **Architecture specificity**: The analysis assumes a log-precision transformer with specific parameters (number of heads \(h\), layers \(d\), dimension \(m\), feedforward width \(w\)), which may not apply when future architectures deviate or when trends change to non-transformer designs.
- **Practical relevance of bounds**: The paper does not clearly discuss the limitations of the theoretical bounds for practice; the last paragraph of the conclusion should be flagged as a limitation, as the lower and upper bounds may have no practical relevance. Moreover, the paper does not address how the complexity-theoretic upper bounds reconcile with the fact that for fixed data distributions, complexity classes are irrelevant (a point raised by the minority reviewer). The result characterizing P is only mentioned as a bullet on page 3 without a dedicated subsection or elaboration.
- **Minor point**: The choice to use multi-tape Turing machines for the simulation is not clearly justified; a single-tape machine might suffice.
