## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose a three-step inference circuit for in-context learning in LLMs: (1) Summarize – each input (demonstrations and query) is encoded into linear representations in hidden states; (2) Semantics Merge – each demonstration’s representation is merged with its label, forming a joint representation; (3) Feature Retrieval and Copy – the model retrieves and copies the label representation most similar to the query’s representation, then language model heads decode it into a predicted label. The circuit explains phenomena such as position bias, robustness to noisy labels, and demonstration saturation. Ablation studies that disable steps seriously damage ICL performance, supporting the circuit’s dominance. The paper also identifies bypass mechanisms operating in parallel. Validation uses methods like mutual nearest-neighbor kernel alignment and embedding comparisons across multiple LLMs (including LLaMA 3) and real-world datasets.

### Strengths
- Clear, well-structured explanations of the three-stage circuit; visualizations (Figures, kernel alignment maps) help readers grasp core findings quickly.
- The work is well-connected to prior ICL research, with discussions comparing findings to earlier studies on ICL explainability and demonstration selection.
- Experiments use multiple LLMs (e.g., LLaMA 3) and real-world datasets, strengthening generalizability and practical usefulness.
- The proposed use of mutual nearest-neighbor kernel alignment to assess representation quality by comparing LLM hidden states with sentence embeddings from an external encoder (BGE M3) is a novel methodological contribution.
- Extensive analysis performed on all three steps, providing explanations for many ICL phenomena (e.g., forerunner token encoding, position bias, label noise robustness).
- The three-stage circuit hypothesis is original; the experiments are thorough with well-crafted methodology and reasonably detailed experimental descriptions.
- Insights are thought-provoking and have potential practical implications for improving ICL applications.

**Minority contrast (Reviewer 4 is more positive):** Reviewers 1 and 2 rate contribution 2 and score 6; Reviewer 4 rates contribution 3 and score 8, emphasizing originality and significance of the circuit hypothesis.

### Weaknesses
- **Framework completeness and scope:** The paper acknowledges bypass mechanisms (Section 5.2) that operate in parallel, but these are not fully explained. This weakens the claim that the proposed three-step circuit is a comprehensive or primary mechanism for ICL. Additionally, the three-stage framework has implicit applicability conditions: it appears only valid when the number of demonstrations \(k \geq 2\) (zero-shot: only Step 1; \(k=1\): only Steps 1 and 2). The paper should clarify whether the focus is restricted to \(k \geq 2\) and whether the threshold \(C\) (such that \(k \geq C\)) might vary by problem type (e.g., SST-2 vs. SST-5 with different label set sizes).
- **Novelty and differentiation:** The paper does not clearly articulate how its application of the inference circuit framework to LLaMA 3 provides novel insights beyond what has already been established in smaller, more controlled settings. The unique contributions beyond prior work on induction/inference circuits for ICL are not adequately differentiated. (Contrast: Reviewer 4 considers the three-stage circuit novel.)
- **Validity of summarization encoding validation (Section 3.1):**
    - Sentence embeddings from BGE M3 are compared to LLM hidden representations; these come from different models without any modification or fine-tuning, so their vector spaces may not be aligned or compatible. Kernel alignment only measures neighborhood similarity, not direct comparability, so observed similarities could be artifacts.
    - Kernel alignment scores range only 0.25–0.35, which is not robust enough to confidently support the Summarize claim.
    - The term “summarize” is unclear: does it refer to encoding capabilities similar to those of BGE?
- **Weak causal evidence and insufficient controls:**
    - Much of the analysis is based on associations (e.g., Figure 2 right looks not significant; peaks in Figure 5 appear noisy) without verifying strength or causality.
    - The ablation study only shows the effect of deleting hypothesized important components. Without also deleting *unimportant* components and showing they have a significantly weaker effect, causal dominance of the three-step circuit cannot be concluded.
    - In Section 5.1, the experimental setup is insufficiently detailed: how many attention heads are disconnected per layer? Missing baselines such as randomly disconnecting some heads to observe the impact.
- **Unexplained phenomena in retrieval/copy (Section 4.1 and Figure 5):**
    - It is unclear whether the copying mechanism applies only to label tokens or extends to other input tokens; using results from other tokens as a baseline would provide a more nuanced understanding.
    - In Figure 5 (right), after layer 40 classification accuracy drops significantly. The reason is not investigated; it could be due to gradual degradation of copied information.
