## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SaRA, a fine-tuning method for diffusion models that re-utilizes parameters with the smallest absolute values (10%–20%), which are claimed to be temporarily ineffective due to training instabilities rather than inherent properties. SaRA applies a sparse weight matrix with nuclear-norm-based low-rank regularization to avoid overfitting, a progressive parameter adjustment strategy, and an unstructured backpropagation strategy to reduce memory costs. It is validated on SD 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 across domain transfer, customization, image editing, and 3D generation tasks, and claims a practical single-line code modification.

### Strengths
- Clear intuition and visualizations (Figures 1 and 4) help readers understand the method’s step-by-step comparison with prior techniques (R1, R3, R5).
- Novel combination of ideas: fine-tuning ineffective parameters combined with nuclear-norm regularization, a novel backpropagation approach for sparse matrices, and progressive parameter adjustment creates a valuable addition to the field (R3, R5).
- Verifies that the “ineffective parameter” concept (small absolute values) applies to diffusion models; parameters with small absolute values are not important for generation (R2, R5).
- Applied to different denoiser architectures: U-Net (SD 1.5 and 2.0) and Diffusion Transformer (SD 3.0) (R1, R2, R4).
- Plug-and-play design with single-line code modification, compatible with existing methods, beneficial for practical adoption (R1).
- More memory-efficient than LoRA (no extra parameters) and compared to other selective PEFT methods that continuously update masks (which require storing gradients for all parameters). SaRA progressively trains only fixed parameters at each stage, storing only certain gradients (R4, R5).
- Extensive experiments across various tasks and SD versions, including ablation studies, thoroughly validate advantages (R3, R4, R5).
- Qualitative and quantitative results show improvement or comparable performance to baselines; can become a method of choice depending on needs (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- Progressive parameter adjustment strategy contributes to stability and convergence of model performance (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Core assumption not rigorously substantiated:** The claim that smallest absolute-value parameters are inherently ineffective is empirical and not convincingly justified. Small values are not intrinsically ineffective; their impact depends on model architecture, task, and training dynamics (R5). Extending the static pruning concept to dynamic fine-tuning is poorly justified—parameters change during fine-tuning, so simple reuse may limit the optimal search space (R2). The reasoning that initially inefficient parameters become efficient due to training randomness is unclear; its correlation with initial values is questionable and Figure 3’s observations are nearly self-evident (R4). A minority reviewer (R3) was more positive about the novelty, but others (R2, R4, R5) find the core premise weakly supported.
- **Missing comparisons with recent SOTA PEFT methods:** DoRA (Liu et al. 2024), which is available for models on CIVITAI, is not compared (R1). No comparison with other selective fine-tuning methods beyond LT-SFT (R4). Multi-task parameter merging (a property of LoRA) is not addressed (R2).
- **Evaluation issues:** The novel VLHI metric is described only in the appendix (R1). FID evaluation on the BarbieCore dataset (only 315 images) is insufficient for proper FID computation; dataset sizes should be stated in the paper (R1). CLIP L/14 may miss details; T2I-CompBench++ (Huang et al. 2023) could provide more accurate evaluation (R1).
- **Lack of inference scaling analysis:** No ablation on scaling SaRA weights during inference (analogous to the `lora_scale` parameter in LoRA), nor mention of this as a limitation (R1).
- **Threshold selection not justified:** The choice of threshold for selecting low-absolute-value parameters lacks ablation on its sensitivity; robustness and generalizability across different models and tasks could be limited (R5).
- **Quantitative results not consistently superior:** FID scores are mostly favorable, but CLIP scores are often higher for LoRA (R4). Ablation shows that removing the low-rank constraint (`L_rank`) does not cause a significant performance change (R4). One reviewer notes the results do not show overall superiority (R3).
- **Limited generalizability:** Only tested on the Stable Diffusion series (SD 1.5, 2.0, 3.0); not shown to work across other network architectures or frameworks (R1, R5). SDXL 1.0 is skipped despite being popular (R1).
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** Typographical mistakes: Table 1 has wrong column names in columns 2 and 3; Figure 1 says “addictive” instead of “additive” (R1). Figure 3 claims weight distributions are Gaussian without further clarification, which seems empirical (R5). Figures 2b and 5 are cluttered and should be split into subplots (R3). Use of “optimal”/“sub-optimal” in Table 1 is incorrect (e.g., optimal FID is 0) (R3).
- **Unclear relation between CLIP score and overfitting:** Elaboration is needed (R3).
- **Memory efficiency may be implementation-driven:** The claimed memory efficiency improvement may stem from implementing separate nodes for gradient storage and selectively fine-tuning only those nodes, rather than from an inherent algorithmic difference (R4). The question is raised whether other SFT methods would achieve similar results if they stored selected parameters in leaf nodes (R4).
- **Unaddressed question:** How to understand the better performance after setting some parameters to 0 in Section 3.1? This could be useful for understanding diffusion model behavior (R2).
