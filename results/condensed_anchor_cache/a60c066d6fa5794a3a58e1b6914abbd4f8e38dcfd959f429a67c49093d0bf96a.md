## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a continual learning approach for conditional GANs using a Discriminator-based Mode Affinity Score (dMAS) derived from the Hessian matrix of the discriminator loss. The method identifies similar existing modes, generates a weighted label for the target mode, and combines memory replay to prevent catastrophic forgetting. Experiments are conducted on MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, and Oxford-flowers. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the affinity score (R2: “new”, R3: “interesting and novel”) and report improved performance over baselines (R1, R2, R3). However, there is strong disagreement on quality and contribution: R1 and R4 rate the paper low (score 3, poor presentation/contribution), while R2 and R3 rate it higher (score 5, fair to good). Key concerns across reviews include insufficient justification of the affinity measure (R1, R2, R4), lack of comparison to alternative similarity metrics (R1, R3), reliance on class labels limiting applicability (R4), weak theoretical contributions (R2: Theorem 1 is negative), missing implementation details (R4), limited task complexity and old baselines (R1, R3), and incomplete quantitative results on Oxford-flowers (R2).

### Strengths
- The paper tackles continual learning for generative models, specifically conditional GANs, which is an interesting and compelling research topic (R2, R3).
- The Discriminator-based Mode Affinity Score is a novel idea (R2: “new”, R3: “interesting and novel”) and experiments demonstrate its ability to pick similar source classes (R1, R3: “effectiveness and consistency”).
- The proposed method outperforms state-of-the-art continual learning baselines on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Oxford-flowers datasets (R1, R2, R3), with some gains even when using fewer training samples (abstract).
- The paper is well-written according to R3, though R1 and R4 found presentation poor (disagreement noted). R4 notes the introduction is well-written.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and novelty**: Several reviewers find the contribution incremental or insufficiently motivated. R1 states the motivation for continual learning in generative models is weak and the work feels like “simple incremental work” that repeats known challenges (catastrophic forgetting). R4 says the main contribution is “very small” and the discriminator-based affinity is a “natural choice” without clear justification of why existing measures are insufficient. R2 questions the validity of the affinity scores, lacking empirical or theoretical justification for using the Hessian matrix and how it relates to semantic similarity; the paper would benefit from correlation with established measures or a theoretical analysis of the proposed measure’s properties. R3 notes that class-specific knowledge transfer in cGANs has been explored in cGANTransfer (and [2]); a discussion and comparison are missing.
- **Lack of comparative evaluation of the affinity score**: R1 and R3 request comparison with other metrics such as FID, mutual information, KL/JS divergence, or FiD on embeddings. R1: “No empirical comparison of the proposed metric… tons of techniques to meet the same purpose, but no comparison or demonstration to show the impact/strengths of the suggested score.” R2: no correlation with other measures. R3: unclear how the baseline “Sequential Fine-tuning (Zhai et al., 2019)” uses FID to determine class affinities; also notes inconsistent citation for “Sequential Fine-tuning” (sometimes Wang et al. 2018, sometimes Zhai et al. 2019).
- **Experimental limitations**: Tasks are too simple – MNIST, CIFAR, Oxford-flowers are outdated for current trends; reviewers recommend ImageNet/COCO (R1, R3). Baselines are several years old, making it hard to confirm the idea is strong compared to counterparts (R1). Continual learning setup only uses two target classes per trial, which is not realistic; more target classes needed (R3). No quantitative results (FID/IS) reported for Oxford-flowers, only qualitative (R2: “papers lacks quantitative assessment…makes it difficult to gauge method’s effectiveness”). R2 also notes weak textual quality and logical coherence in Section 4, which needs reorganization.
- **Theoretical contribution is weak**: Theorem 1 only shows that adding a new mode does not improve existing mode performance – a negative result, not explaining why the method works (R2). The paper would benefit from a bound on performance degradation of existing modes (R2). Notations in Theorem 1 are undefined (trace, Frobenius norm) (R4).
- **Reproduction and implementation details missing**: R4 lists missing items: actual loss functions and model architecture for the conditional GAN (e.g., why use cGAN over WGAN?); unclear whether X_a represents a single data point or a batch (dimensionality not clarified); definitions of models missing in Algorithm 2 (G_θ); S in Algorithm 1 not described; main objective function not stated. The method relies on class/task labels, limiting applicability to unsupervised settings (R4). Replay mechanism is not novel and should be clearly stated as such (R2: “does not contribute to originality”; R4: “has been done in a wide range of works”). Questions from R3: is the target embedding fixed or fine-tuned? What is meant by “initialize the source cGAN randomly” – weight initialization or source classes? How does the method compare if no semantically similar classes exist?
- **Presentation issues**: R1 and R4 rate presentation as poor (1), while R3 rates presentation good (3), indicating strong disagreement. R2 rates presentation fair (2) and notes Section 4 needs better structure. R4 points to unclear notations (X_a not bold, symbols undefined). R2 notes a typo: “between between” in Section 3.2.
