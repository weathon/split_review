## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Meta-Continual Learning of Neural Fields (MCL-NF), combining a modular architecture with optimization-based meta-learning and a Fisher Information Maximization loss (FIM-NeRF) to address catastrophic forgetting and slow convergence in continual learning of neural fields. Reviewers agree the paper is well-written and presents extensive experiments across image, audio, video, and NeRF view synthesis on six datasets. However, concerns are raised about marginal technical novelty, insufficient comparisons with state-of-the-art methods (including more recent and methodologically relevant ones), lack of ablation and qualitative evaluations, artificial experimental design choices (e.g., fixed 4-patch image decomposition), limited scalability (only 4 tasks), and reliance on PSNR as the primary metric. One reviewer (R3) also found the presentation and contribution weaker than the others (Presentation 2, Contribution 2 vs. 3/3/3).

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and effectively situates the work in the meta-continual learning literature.
- The combination of modular architecture and optimization-based meta-learning is innovative and directly addresses the need for adaptable, continuous learning in neural fields.
- The method mitigates catastrophic forgetting through modularization and shared initialization, avoiding resource-intensive experience replay.
- The Fisher Information Maximization (FIM) loss is theoretically sound, supported by convergence guarantees and a generalization bound, with a detailed weighting mechanism for learning stability.
- Extensive experiments across image, audio, video reconstruction, and NeRF-based view synthesis on six diverse datasets demonstrate the method's effectiveness and robustness.
- The method is general and applicable to many different applications, from image patches to city-scale NeRF rendering.
- The work helps broaden understanding of how meta-learning and continual learning paradigms can be synergistically integrated in neural fields, inspiring future research.

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns**: The FIM loss is closely related to Fisher Information used in EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016) and subsequent parameter-level methods (Chaudhry et al., 2018; Konishi et al., 2023). The claimed advantage of sample-level operation is not convincingly demonstrated; a fair experimental comparison between sample-level and parameter-level Fisher-based approaches is missing.
- **Missing recent comparisons**: The main tables lack comparisons with recent state-of-the-art methods. Specifically:
  - On the MatrixCity dataset, missing comparisons with Chung et al. (2022) and Po et al. (2023).
  - MAML (2017) is used as a meta-learning baseline despite being outdated; newer enhancements such as La-MAML for continual learning are not included.
  - Other relevant methods like ANML are mentioned but not compared against.
  - Only two methods are evaluated in the MCL setting; comparisons with recent methods from different settings (e.g., [2] referenced by R3, [a] referenced by R4) are missing, making it hard to contextualize the contribution.
- **Insufficient ablation studies**:
  - The contribution of the FIM loss is not thoroughly ablated in the main paper; only limited results (mod and MIM with two hidden dimensions) appear in Appendix Table 2.
  - No ablation on the image decomposition strategy: why four patches? What about different patch sizes, overlapping patches, or frequency-based decompositions (e.g., Fourier transforms)? The artificial patch-based setting in images raises concerns about generalizability.
  - No analysis of the sensitivity of the FIM loss hyperparameter or its impact on performance across datasets.
- **Scalability concerns**: All experiments use only four continual tasks, which is too few to assess scalability to longer task sequences. The method’s performance under a larger number of tasks is not evaluated.
- **Experimental design and evaluation gaps**:
  - The continual learning setting is not clearly described: how data is selected, processed, and how the model is evaluated across tasks (especially retention of previous tasks) is unclear.
  - Qualitative comparisons are largely missing, especially for NeRF view synthesis (e.g., rendered images on the MatrixCity dataset). Visual fidelity is not assessed despite quantitative metrics.
  - Evaluation relies heavily on PSNR; additional metrics such as SSIM (for images/video/NeRF) and perceptual metrics like PESQ (for audio) are needed for a comprehensive assessment.
  - Only relatively simple image and video datasets are used; absence of complex variations in lighting, viewpoint, or object types limits generalization claims.
- **Missing discussion**: The paper does not sufficiently discuss (i) incremental learning methods for neural fields (e.g., [1] referenced by R2), and (ii) how the challenges of catastrophic forgetting and slow convergence differ between traditional continual learning and continuous radiance fields.
- **Presentation issues**: One reviewer (R3) noted that the presentation is less clear than the others (score 2). Additionally, a citation error exists: Line 322 cites EWC as Chaudhry et al. (2018) but Line 402 cites Kirkpatrick et al. (2016); the former method is actually EWC++.
- **Other missing analyses and clarifications**:
  - The paper treats each image separately (batch size 1); it is unclear how the number of coordinate samples per task is determined.
  - For methods compared without modularization, it is not specified whether they use the full MLP network capacity to match the parameter count of the modularized versions.
  - It would be valuable to explore whether alternative loss functions or strategies (e.g., curriculum learning) could achieve similar sample prioritization and why FIM is the preferred choice.
