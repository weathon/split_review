## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents an empirical study of backdoor attacks on CLIP by decomposing image representations into contributions from individual image patches, attention heads (AHs), and MLPs across layers. Three findings are reported: (1) local patch-based attacks mainly affect AHs, while global noise-based attacks mainly affect MLPs; (2) infected AHs concentrate in the last layer, whereas infected MLPs are spread across several late layers; (3) some AHs remain largely uninfected, and even infected AHs can still maintain their original functionality. Based on these observations, the authors propose inference-stage defenses that detect and repair infected AHs or filter backdoor samples. The paper validates its findings and defense effectiveness experimentally.

### Strengths
- Comprehensive empirical analysis that decomposes representations to reveal how different backdoor attacks (local-patch vs. global-noise) interact with specific architectural components (AHs and MLPs) of CLIP. This provides insights into component-level vulnerabilities of CLIP-based and attention-based multi-modal architectures.
- Clear motivation and extension of existing backdoor attack research to CLIP models, with a well-defined analysis that distinguishes trigger types and examines their effects on different layers. The paper quantifies infection patterns and substantiates claims with quantitative results and ablation studies.
- A potential defense method is proposed based on the empirical findings, and experiments are conducted to validate both findings and defense effectiveness.
- The paper’s style is easy to follow, and the authors demonstrate strong technical knowledge in the decomposition methodology.
- The contributions are relevant to the adversarial and multi-modal community, offering information that could be used to craft targeted attacks and develop stronger detection/defense techniques beyond backdoors.
- *Minority note: Reviewer 1 (score 6) is particularly positive, emphasizing the analysis as a “good example” and noting sufficient ablation, while other reviewers (scores 5) are more reserved about novelty and generality.*

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and intuitive findings.** Several reviewers note that the observation that local patch-based attacks affect AHs while global noise-based attacks affect MLPs is intuitive and somewhat expected. Reviewer 4 also states that the study procedure involves only invocations of previous tools without a novel approach, and the findings resemble those of the well-known “fine-pruning” defenses. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Insufficient generality of attack classification.** The paper classifies triggers into only two categories (local-patch and global-noise). Reviewers question whether the findings hold for more diverse attacks, such as:
  - Dynamic triggers (e.g., “Input-Aware Dynamic Backdoor Attack”), where triggers share similar pixels but also differ; it is unclear how to classify such attacks or whether the conclusions transfer. (Reviewers 1, 2)
  - Multi-trigger or multi-target attacks: if multiple triggers are placed at different locations, their representations could become more like global-noise, contradicting finding (2). (Reviewer 4)
  - Semantic triggers (e.g., natural-looking triggers): it is unclear how the affected layers would change. (Reviewer 3)
  - Stronger attacks such as “Reflection Backdoor” and “Hidden Trigger Backdoor Attacks” were not included. (Reviewer 1)
- **Lack of a clear threat model.** The paper does not define the threat model (e.g., attacker capabilities, poisoning scenario) at the beginning, making the analysis and defense context ambiguous. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing ablation on architectural generality.** The authors did not experiment with different architectures or modified layer orders to test whether claims like “infected AHs are centered on the last layer” are due to attack factors rather than architectural design. (Reviewer 1)
- **Conflict between findings.** Finding (2) states that local-patch backdoors infect AHs, but finding (3) claims that AHs are not greatly infected and can maintain functionality. Reviewers find this confusing: if AHs are infected, then the last finding seems contradictory or its meaning is unclear; the connection to the proposed defense is not motivated. (Reviewer 4)
- **Practicality of the defense.** The proposed defense has two separate pipelines for local-patch and global-noise attacks. A defender cannot know which type of attack is present in practice, and running both pipelines is time-consuming. The parameter selection in Figure 7 also struggles to balance attack success rate and clean accuracy. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Specificity of the “last five layers” claim.** Finding (3) reports that “descriptive texts of MLPs in the last five layers have a distinct semantic difference.” Reviewers question why exactly five layers are singled out and whether this conclusion is based solely on Figure 3. (Reviewer 2)
- **Readability and presentation issues.** The mathematical sections (e.g., preliminary, repair of infected AHs) hinder readability; the paper could benefit from textual explanations of what the equations intuitively explore. Figures 2 and 3 are not clear: original images are hard to distinguish in Figure 2, and Figure 3 lacks intuitive clarity (many descriptive texts but difficult to draw conclusions directly). Providing sample input images with variations of backdoored versions and visualizations of attending AHs/MLPs would strengthen claims. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Missing explanation of the last finding’s importance.** Reviewer 4 notes that the last finding (some AHs not greatly infected) seems to have no clear relation to or motivation for the derived defense method, and its significance is not well-articulated.
- **Comparison with fine-pruning defense.** Reviewer 4 points out that the findings and repair mechanism are similar to the earlier “fine-pruning” defense, but the paper does not discuss this connection or clarify the differences.
