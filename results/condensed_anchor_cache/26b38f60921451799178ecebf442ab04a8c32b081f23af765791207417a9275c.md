## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces FedLoRA, which applies Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to Personalized Federated Learning (PFL). Each client's model is decomposed into a full-rank shared parameter matrix (σ) and a low-rank personalized parameter matrix (τ). An alternating training strategy is employed: first freeze σ and optimize τ, then freeze τ and optimize σ. The server averages the σ matrices from clients. Experiments are conducted on ResNet with CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet under Dirichlet non-IID splits (α ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1}). Ablation studies examine the effect of rank for convolution and linear layers, training epochs per communication round, the alternating vs. simultaneous optimization, and the performance boost from introducing τ.

### Strengths
1. The motivation for using LoRA to simultaneously learn client-local knowledge and mitigate data heterogeneity is good (Reviewer 1, 2, 3, 5).  
2. The proposed method reports strong empirical results compared to existing PFL approaches (Reviewer 1, 4, 5).  
3. The paper is well-organized and easy to read/follow (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).  
4. The ablation studies (rank effect, training epochs, alternating vs. simultaneous optimization, performance boost of τ) are well-formulated and well-executed (Reviewer 3).  
5. The alternative training strategy (freeze one part, update the other) is a meaningful contribution (Reviewer 2, 4, 5).  
6. The paper provides different decomposition prototypes for CNN and dense layers (Reviewer 4).  
7. The method efficiently decomposes parameters into general and client-specific components (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and prior art** – Applying LoRA to FL is seen as not sufficiently novel; a prior work with the same name (FedLoRA: Model-Heterogeneous Personalized Federated Learning with LoRA Tuning) exists. The paper does not clearly justify why LoRA is a better choice for PFL than existing methods (Reviewer 1, confidence 3; minority positive reviewer 3 found no major weakness, but others disagree).  
2. **Theoretical grounding** – The alternating training strategy appears heuristic and lacks theoretical justification. No convergence analysis is provided (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). A specific claim in Section 4.3 (that accuracy drop after a focal rank is due to τ acquiring general knowledge) is unsupported; the authors should compute inter-client vector similarity of τ across clients to verify this (Reviewer 3). The paper does not offer analytical insight or theoretical justification for why a low-rank structure is suitable for capturing personalized information in FL (Reviewer 4).  
3. **Experiments scope** – Experiments are limited to three small image datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet). Larger datasets and other modalities (e.g., text, medical) are missing (Reviewer 1). The possibility of using an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet to fine-tune on CIFAR-10/100 is suggested as a strong test of the client-specific knowledge claim (Reviewer 3).  
4. **Communication and computation cost** – The alternating training requires twice the local optimization (one phase for σ, one for τ). Transmitting the full-rank σ matrix (which is much larger than the low-rank τ) may incur huge communication overhead, especially for large models. The paper lacks analysis and experiments on training cost, communication cost, and scalability (Reviewer 2, 4, 5). Reviewer 5 specifically asks for memory cost comparisons with standard LoRA.  
5. **Per-client evaluation** – The paper does not present evaluation results per client; aggregated metrics may mask clients that are sacrificed for overall improvement (Reviewer 2).  
6. **Overfitting risk** – The shared full-rank matrix may overfit to aggregated data under high heterogeneity, limiting generalization. No mitigation strategies or analysis of this risk is provided (Reviewer 4).  
7. **Privacy concerns** – Sharing full-rank parameters with the server may raise privacy concerns beyond what LoRA normally entails (Reviewer 5).  
8. **Writing quality** – There are issues such as missing punctuation in the first sentence of the abstract (“... FL McMahan et al. (2016) allows ...”) (Reviewer 1).
