## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ConPaS, a machine-learning framework for solving Mixed Integer Linear Programs (MILP). It uses contrastive learning to train a graph neural network (GNN) to predict high-quality assignments for integer variables, with positive samples (optimal/near-optimal solutions) and negative samples (low-quality/infeasible solutions). At test time, the predicted assignments fix a subset of integer variables and the resulting reduced MILP is solved to completion (predict-and-search). Experiments are performed on four datasets (CA, MVS, MIS, and IP instances from the NeurIPS 2021 ML4CO competition) and show improved solution quality and speed over baseline methods.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,2,4).
- The application of contrastive learning to MILP is intuitive and effective, and the idea is interesting (Reviewers 1,4).
- Empirical performance is strong and shows significant improvement over baselines (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- [Minority positive view] One reviewer (Reviewer 3) finds the novelty sufficient for ICLR, noting that the bi-level model is interesting and the authors have clearly indicated differences from the related CL-LNS work.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and strong similarity to prior work.** The core idea—generating positive and negative samples for contrastive learning and then using predictions to guide search—is extremely close to the ICML 2023 paper by Huang et al. (CL-LNS). The only difference is that CL-LNS uses large neighborhood search (LNS) while ConPaS uses predict-and-search (PaS), which can be viewed as a one-step LNS. Figure 1 is nearly identical. The authors mention Huang et al. but do not discuss the differences in detail or compare against it as a baseline. (Reviewers 1,4; Reviewer 3 disagrees and finds the novelty sufficient.)  
2. **Incremental contribution and limited generality.** The contrastive learning component is used only to provide a better initialization for PaS; the core solving still relies on PaS. It is unclear whether the method can be applied to other prediction-based approaches such as Neural Diving. (Reviewer 4)  
3. **Weak experimental benchmarks.** The evaluation lacks the standard MILP benchmark MIPLIB2017. The chosen datasets (CA, MVS, MIS) are relatively easy for SCIP and are not challenging; CA is not considered interesting by the MILP community. Only a subset of the ML4CO competition instances (“IP instances”) is used, ignoring two of the three available benchmarks. Harder instances are needed. (Reviewers 2,3,4)  
4. **Unfair and incomplete solver comparisons.**  
   - The method fixes variables and solves a much easier subproblem, so comparing anytime performance with SCIP (which tries to prove optimality) is not meaningful. (Reviewers 1,3)  
   - SCIP is not well-configured; tuned SCIP or commercial solvers (Gurobi, CPLEX) should be compared. (Reviewer 2)  
   - Training data was collected using Gurobi, yet only SCIP is used at test time—why is Gurobi not included as a test-time baseline? (Reviewer 1)  
5. **Missing analysis of feasibility and prediction accuracy.** The method cannot guarantee feasibility or optimality, but no empirical study of the feasibility ratio on test instances is provided. The accuracy of the predicted variable assignments is also not reported. (Reviewer 1)  
6. **Insufficient analysis of negative sampling.** Two methods for collecting negative samples are used, but only aggregate performance is reported without deeper analysis. There is no comparison with the negative sampling strategy used in CL-LNS, even though this is a main contribution. (Reviewers 1,3)  
7. **No ablation study on the modified loss function.** (Reviewer 3)  
8. **Hyperparameter k₀ and k₁ are fixed numbers across instances.** A coverage rate (ratio of fixed to total integer variables) would be more adaptive. Moreover, each instance may have a unique optimal coverage rate (arXiv:2308.00327), so reporting an average k₀ on the CA dataset is insufficient for guiding configuration. (Reviewer 2)  
9. **Incomplete description of commercial solvers in the introduction.** Gurobi and CPLEX are described as branch-and-bound, but they are actually branch-and-cut algorithms with dynamic search. (Reviewer 3)
