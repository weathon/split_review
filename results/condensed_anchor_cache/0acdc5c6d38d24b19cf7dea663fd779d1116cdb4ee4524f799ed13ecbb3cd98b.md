## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a pre-training purification defense against perturbative availability poisoning attacks (unlearnable examples). The authors discover that rate-constrained VAEs naturally suppress poison patterns by minimizing mutual information in the latent space, provide theoretical justification, and introduce a disentangle variational autoencoder (D‑VAE) with one encoder and two decoders. A two‑stage purification framework is developed: the first stage roughly suppresses poisons, the second refines the output. Experiments on CIFAR‑10, CIFAR‑100, and a 100‑class ImageNet‑subset against multiple poison types and perturbation levels show that D‑VAE consistently outperforms existing defenses, including both training‑time and pre‑training baselines.

### Strengths
- **Consistent SOTA performance**: D‑VAE outperforms all compared defenses (e.g., adversarial training, JPEG compression, image squeezing) across CIFAR‑10, CIFAR‑100, and ImageNet‑subset for multiple poison types (OPS, LSP, NTGA, etc.) and often by large margins. Additional results with different model architectures and larger perturbation bounds are also provided. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Novel theoretical insight**: The paper uncovers and theoretically explains why rate‑constrained VAEs tend to suppress poison patterns, and the disentanglement mechanism (one encoder, two decoders) is a novel and promising approach to the difficult problem of defending unlearnable examples where entire datasets are perturbed. (R3)  The analysis also shows that perturbations have larger inter‑class distance and smaller intra‑class variance, motivating the class‑wise embeddings. (R4)
- **Two‑stage ablation**: An ablation study (Section 4.2) clearly demonstrates that both stages of the purification procedure are important for the final performance, and that the disentangled poison patterns are visually and empirically effective. (R2)
- **Pre‑training efficacy**: The method operates as a pre‑training purification, avoiding the high computational cost of training‑time defenses like adversarial training, and shows potential as an efficient defense. (R4) The approach is tested on datasets ranging from CIFAR to ImageNet. (R1, R2, R3, R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and practical viability**:
  - The paper does not adequately address the likelihood of an attacker injecting a sufficient quantity of poisoned samples. The assumption that *all* training data is poisoned (Section 5.1) is unrealistic; even 1/5 poisoning (the lower bound in Tables 7, 8) is improbable in many real‑world scenarios. A quantitative analysis of the poisoning fraction needed for a successful attack under various threat models is missing. (R2)
  - If the availability attack succeeds, it would cause noticeably degraded model performance, alerting the trainer and prompting data investigation. Simpler detection methods (e.g., monitoring loss spikes and discarding samples with unusually high loss) might be more efficient, especially for large foundation models where high‑quality data and checkpoint restarts are already used. (R2)
  - **Terminology and threat‑model confusion**: The paper uses “availability attacks” and “unlearnable examples” without precise distinction from other poisoning categories (targeted attacks, backdoor attacks, indiscriminate attacks). The threat model should be explicitly identified in Section 2. Specific claims – e.g., that “the majority of research has focused on backdoor attacks” (unsupported) and that “typical poisoned samples are modified or replaced” (inaccurate for the additive threat model considered) – need correction. The comparison to prior availability attacks that inject a few clean label poisons (e.g., <3%) is unfair due to completely different attack budgets; these should be treated as separate categories. (R3)

- **Computational overhead and hyperparameter tuning**:
  - The method requires running D‑VAE on every sample (since poisoned samples are not known a priori), and the two‑stage design (Section 3.5) doubles this cost. The KLD limits need tuning and appear to vary between datasets (see “Model Training” paragraph), increasing complexity. This overhead is substantial compared to simpler defenses like JPEG compression, which is already cheap and often applied to stored images. (R2)
  - Questions remain about training and inference costs: does the training set include all evaluated poisoning attacks? What is the actual training cost? The algorithm requires three additional forward propagations per sample; what is the inference time? (R4)

- **Poor technical notation and presentation (contradicted by R3)**:
  - Math symbols are inconsistent: e.g., \(\mathbf{x}\) denotes clean data in Section 3.1 but poisoned data elsewhere; \(x_c\) is used for clean data and later as a predictive feature; the subscript for sample index \(i\) collides with other uses. (R1, R2)
  - Many terms are introduced without definition: \(\text{kld}_{\text{limit}}\) (Eq 3), \(z\) (Eq 3), \(t\) in \(x = (x_c, x_s^t)\) (Eq 4); the symbol \(D\) is overloaded (decoder in Fig 1/Algorithm, dimension in Proposition 2, \(D_{KL}\) vs. KLD, dataset in Section 3.5). (R2)
  - Notations for clean/poisoned datasets vary: \(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{T}\) in Eq 1, \(C, D_p\) in Section 4.2, and different notation in Algorithm 1. (R2)
  - Section 3.2 lacks a clear description and analysis of Figure 2; the entire method is hard to follow. (R1) The poison‑recovery objective (Eq 14, Figure 1) appears contradictory (first two terms) and its meaning is unclear. (R1)
  - Writing is often difficult to read; many paragraphs require multiple re‑reads. The paper’s specific focus within adversarial examples is unclear until well into the Introduction, and it fluctuates between describing malicious attacks and defense. (R2)
  - **Reviewer disagreement on presentation**: Two reviewers (R1, R2) rate presentation as “poor” (1/4), while one reviewer (R3) rates it “good” (3/4) and R4 gives “fair” (2/4). Notation issues are consistently noted as a concern.

- **Methodological gaps and missing clarifications**:
  - **Auxiliary decoder and class‑wise embeddings**: How is \(u_c\) initialized and trained? Why does it help the decoder \(D_{\theta_p}\) generate the disentangled perturbation? How is \(\theta_p\) optimized when the defender does not have access to the true perturbation? (R3)
  - **Disentanglement vs. orthogonal patterns**: It is unclear whether the model truly disentangles the real poison pattern or learns to produce orthogonal patterns. A quantitative evaluation of the residual between disentangled and true perturbations (or at least a visualization of residuals) is missing. (R2)
  - **Separate decoding of perturbation and reconstruction**: Given \(x = x_c + p\) and \(x\) is known, why does the architecture not use one decoder? Knowing either \(x_c\) or \(p\) should directly give the other. (R2)
  - **Generalization and adaptive attacks**: If an adaptive white‑box attack (e.g., PGD) treats the VAE and classifier end‑to‑end, can the method still purify? If a new poison type unseen during class‑wise embedding training is used, can the method still work? (R4)

- **Missing experimental details**:
  - What percentage of the data is poisoned in the main experiments (Tables 2‑5)? (R2)
  - For the Gray and JPEG baselines, are the same transformations applied to test images during evaluation? If not, these baselines are likely at a disadvantage due to a domain gap. (R2)
  - How well does a non‑variational autoencoder (non‑VAE) perform? Such models also have an information bottleneck and are trained with reconstruction loss. (R2)
