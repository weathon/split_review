## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies generalization in multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL). It formalizes the problem as a contextual multi-objective MDP, introduces a benchmark with parameterized environment configurations (building on MO-Gymnasium), and defines the normalized hypervolume generalization ratio (NHGR) as an evaluation metric. Baseline evaluations of state-of-the-art MORL algorithms (Envelope, GPI-LS, CAPQL, PGMORL, MORL/D) show limited generalization, exposing the difficulty of multi-objective generalization and the limitations of scalar rewards. The paper fills a gap between MORL and generalization research, providing a foundation for future work.

### Strengths
- This is the first work to study MORL in environments with changing dynamics/state-transition functions, addressing a critical gap in the literature (Reviewer 1).
- The proposed testbed features diverse multi-objective RL domains with parameterized environment configurations that induce pronounced changes in specific features, providing a flexible and well-defined platform for evaluating MORL generalization (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The evaluation framework using NHGR is sound and promising for standardizing assessment of generalization in MORL (Reviewer 2).
- The necessity of MORL generalization is empirically demonstrated in Section 6.2, partly supporting the authors' claim that scalar rewards are insufficient and that multi-objective specifications are needed for effective generalization (Reviewer 4).
- The paper provides reasonable coverage of relevant literature, effectively justifies its claims with thorough experimental evaluations, is generally well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 2), and maintains a clear focus on key contributions (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical contribution and novelty.** The contextual MOMDP definition follows directly from prior work on MOMDPs and contextual MDPs; the main contribution is the benchmark and evaluation, not a new algorithm (Reviewer 1). The paper lacks a concrete solution for MORL generalization; proposing and integrating a new method would enhance completeness (Reviewer 4). The modifications to existing MuJoCo environments are straightforward parameter adjustments via the MuJoCo API's `step()` function, not a significant engineering contribution (Reviewer 3). The paper may be more suitable for a Dataset & Benchmarks track (Reviewer 1). No new algorithm is proposed; the work is primarily an empirical study (Reviewer 3).

- **Weaknesses of the NHGR metric (multiple reviewers).**
  - *Conceptual issues.* For linear utility, hypervolume can increase by adding solutions in concave regions of the Pareto front that do not increase expected utility; this makes hypervolume misleading in the utility-based setting the paper advocates (Reviewer 1). Hypervolume is difficult to interpret and gives no information about the shape of the PF or which preferences/trade-offs the agent can solve (Reviewer 1). Normalization does not fundamentally change the underlying Lebesgue measure; it does not address the issue of result surges caused by outliers, so the motivation for NHGR is insufficient (Reviewer 3). The normalization bounds (\(v^c_{\min}\) and \(v^c_{\max}\)) are questionable because true upper and lower bounds for each objective are typically unknown a priori; if bounds are not tightly set, NHGR reliability is compromised. Determining these bounds also requires running multiple MORL algorithms, increasing computational complexity (Reviewer 4).
  - *Practical applicability.* Computing hypervolume is NP-Hard and infeasible for problems with many objectives (e.g., 10) (Reviewer 1). NHGR computation is highly demanding as the number of MORL algorithms and contexts increases; if performance can be directly computed for any context via specialist algorithms, the need for a generalized algorithm is undermined (Reviewer 4). The denominator of NHGR relies on existing MORL algorithms, which may perform poorly in some contexts; in those cases, NHGR becomes an unreliable metric (Reviewer 4). A potentially better approach would be to report expected standard hypervolume across evaluation contexts plus expected standard sparsity, rather than introducing a new metric (Reviewer 4). Figure 1 does not align with the definition of normalization bounds (the reference point is determined by the x-coordinate of the upper-leftmost point and the y-coordinate of the lower-rightmost point) (Reviewer 4).
  - *Lack of complementary metrics.* The evaluation only uses hypervolume-based metrics and ignores standard MORL metrics such as sparsity, cardinality, and expected utility. Expected utility is essential for utility-based approaches; sparsity assesses solution diversity and exploration capability (Reviewer 2). The two metrics presented in Figure 3 (optimality gap and IQM) are actually different measurement dimensions of the same indicator; their mirrored images show overlap, so they cannot be considered two new distinct metrics (Reviewer 3).

- **Concerns about the testbed and experimental design.**
  - *Variations and generalization.* The testbed appears to be a straightforward extension with limited variations—primarily parameter adjustments within existing environments—rather than fundamentally new dynamics or state spaces. There is no clear evidence of full interpolation/extrapolation properties across context changes (Reviewer 2). In MuJoCo environments, it is impossible for an agent to act optimally w.r.t. all friction coefficients without access to the current coefficients; perfect generalization (NHGR=1) may be theoretically impossible (Reviewer 1).
  - *Context visibility.* It is unclear whether the context is part of the observation (i.e., whether agents know which context they are in). If agents do not have access to the context, generalist agents cannot be expected to perform well—each context may require a different policy, and a single policy optimizing the average context will likely be suboptimal for each context (Reviewer 1).
  - *Training budget fairness.* It is unclear what training budget (in environment interactions) was used for specialist vs. generalist agents. Generalist agents must learn multiple contexts; if they are not given sufficient training time, poor performance may be due to insufficient training rather than algorithmic limitations (Reviewer 1).
  - *Hyperparameter tuning.* The paper does not discuss hyperparameters used for the algorithms. Larger neural networks or replay buffers may be necessary when training for many contexts simultaneously; poor performance could stem from inadequate tuning (Reviewer 1). The discrepancy in CleanRL SAC performance in the default Hopper environment (achieving 3000 episodic return) vs. the one reported in Figure 5 (leftmost plot) may be due to hyperparameter differences; a well-tuned SAC might outperform the MORL methods (Reviewer 2).
  - *Scalability.* Empirical evaluation is limited to environments with at most 4 objectives. Testing with higher objective counts (e.g., Fruit Tree Navigation with 5–7 objectives, or MO-highway configurable for more objectives) would strengthen the paper and address scalability (Reviewer 2). Why were only certain MuJoCo environments (two-objective problems) selected as baselines while other environments with 3+ objectives were omitted? (Reviewer 3)
  - *MO-SuperMarioBros.* The paper mentions “a total of 32 possible stages,” but the Appendix shows only 8 stages; this needs correction (Reviewer 1).
  - *Number of evaluation episodes.* Is the number of episodes evaluated for each context set at 100/(number of evaluation contexts) (excluding Mario)? (Reviewer 4)

- **Insufficient discussion of scalarization and utility functions.**
  - The paper focuses on utility-based approaches but does not discuss different scalarization functions. For linear scalarization, the resulting Pareto front is convex; for non-linear functions, concave regions may appear. It is unclear which scalarization methods were used in the MORL algorithms (e.g., weight initialization in Envelope) and how different scalarization functions affect generalization (Reviewer 2). The claim that multi-objective rewards are necessary is not novel; a substantial body of work has already demonstrated the inefficiency of single scalar rewards (e.g., PGMORL, dynamic-weight MORL) (Reviewer 3). How does the proposed framework handle cases where utility functions are unknown, non-linear, or non-monotonic? (Reviewer 2)

- **Technical clarifications needed.**
  - Is SAC trained with a standard scalar reward function (as described in Appendix D) for each context vector independently, or does it follow domain randomization (sampling random environment parameters per episode)? (Reviewer 4)
  - In Line 479, the phrase “scalarized them using \(f_{SORL}\)” is unclear. Additionally, how many solution vectors are sampled for each algorithm? (Reviewer 4)
  - How is the optimality gap calculated? (Reviewer 4)
  - For the SAC implementation in Mario and LavaGrid, is it “SAC-discrete”? If so, how was SAC-discrete implemented, and how does its performance compare to DQN? (Reviewer 4)
  - In Section 6.2, what is meant by “upward speed” in the scalar reward formulation (Line 461 and Figure 6)? (Reviewer 4)
  - Line 302: “We introduce an 8-dimensional parameter…” should be corrected to “7-dimensional.” (Reviewer 4)
  - Minor: “long-term discounted reward” should be “long-term discounted sum of rewards.” (Reviewer 1)
  - In Definition 1, the reward should be represented as a vector in bold notation for consistency with Section 2. (Reviewer 2)

- **Organization.**
  - The related work section should be placed in the main paper, moving detailed environment descriptions to the appendix (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). Section 6.2 (demonstrating necessity of MORL generalization) should be moved closer to the motivation section to improve flow (Reviewer 4). More detailed explanations of how each MORL algorithm operates (e.g., the weight adaptation variant of MORL/D SB) would be beneficial, at least in the appendix (Reviewer 4). Paragraphs should be divided to improve readability (Reviewer 4).
