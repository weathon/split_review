## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a self-supervised optimization approach for learning visual prompts that, when added to any input image, redirect the attention of pre-trained Vision Transformers (ViTs) to the spatial location of the prompt. The prompts are learned by minimizing KL divergence between the attention of the CLS token and a target distribution, without requiring annotations or fine-tuning the ViT. The method is evaluated on CLIP, DINOv2, and other ViTs, with experiments on CUB and RefCOCO datasets for tasks like keypoint naming and object localization.

### Strengths

- The paper is easy to follow and understand (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The approach is model-agnostic and does not require fine-tuning, allowing it to be applied to different pre-trained ViTs regardless of their pre-training supervision (Reviewers 1, 2).
- It enables self-supervised learning of visual prompts without needing labeled data, which is valuable for adapting vision transformers across tasks without costly retraining (Reviewer 3).
- The structure of the paper is complete (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited technical contribution**: The work primarily extends the "red circle" phenomenon observed in CLIP (Shtedritski et al., 2023) to other ViTs via learnable prompts. Reviewers found this incremental and not well-demonstrated. The effectiveness is questionable: experiments on language-supervised methods lack baselines for the red circle and cropping, and comparison with the red circle paper shows a decline rather than improvement (Reviewer 1). One reviewer noted that the proposed method feels like a patchwork of existing methods with unclear core innovation (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient baselines and quantitative comparisons**: There is a lack of comparison with state-of-the-art methods and controlled ablations. The paper does not provide quantitative metrics for DINOv2 on the MMVP-VLM dataset, only examples (Reviewer 1). A controlled ablation experiment on MMVP-VLM is needed to show superiority over a simple red circle (Reviewer 1). Additionally, it is not clear how performance compares to using a simple red circular frame as a visual prompt (Reviewer 2, question). One reviewer found experimental results unreliable due to missing SOTA comparisons and insufficient ablation analysis (Reviewer 4).
- **Concerns about self-supervised nature**: Although the method is self-supervised, evaluation relies heavily on annotated datasets like CUB (keypoint detection) and RefCOCO (object localization), limiting practical utility in unlabeled environments. This reliance contrasts with the claimed self-supervised nature (Reviewer 3). Another reviewer noted that application scenarios are limited because the method requires manually annotating areas of interest, making it impractical for large datasets (Reviewer 1).
- **Generalization and robustness concerns**: Evaluation is on controlled datasets with simple scenes (birds, single objects). More complex natural scenes with multiple objects may degrade performance; validation on diverse, unlabeled datasets is needed (Reviewer 3). The learned prompts are customized per transformer architecture (e.g., CLIP, DINOv2) and may not transfer to other model types (e.g., CNNs) or even to transformers with architectural variations, requiring separate training for each (Reviewer 3). One reviewer questioned how to ensure accuracy of attention guidance across distribution shifts, as inconsistent attention could mislead the model (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing details and clarity issues**: The paper lacks essential descriptions; for example, the methodology for testing on CUB is not clearly outlined, and the term "K2N" (keypoint to name) on line 478 is unexplained (Reviewer 1). The Introduction and Related Work lack logical flow, making it unclear what the author aims to do and how the proposed method differs from existing work (Reviewer 4). There are typos: citation format errors, inconsistent decimal places in Table 2, incorrect ordering in Figures 4 and 5 (Reviewer 2), and obscured text in Figure 7 (Reviewer 1).
- **Questions raised regarding multiple prompts and training objectives**: When applying multiple visual prompts simultaneously, do attention maps exhibit similar change patterns as with single prompts? (Reviewer 2). Could attention gain be used as a training objective with LoRA for efficient training? (Reviewer 2). These questions highlight missing experiments or design space exploration.

Note: One reviewer (Reviewer 2) gave a higher score (5) and noted strong generalization and clear presentation, but still raised concerns about justification and typos. Another reviewer (Reviewer 3) emphasized algorithmic design concerns and limited evaluation scope. The majority (Reviewers 1, 3, 4) rated the paper 3, indicating significant weaknesses.
