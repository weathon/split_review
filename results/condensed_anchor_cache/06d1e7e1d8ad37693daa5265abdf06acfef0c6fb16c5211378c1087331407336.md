## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper systematically investigates simplifying the standard transformer block by removing skip connections, value and projection matrices, sequential sub-block ordering, and normalization layers, guided by signal propagation theory and empirical observations. The resulting simplified transformer matches per-update training speed and performance of standard Pre-LN and parallel blocks on autoregressive (GPT) and BERT models, while achieving 15% faster training throughput and 15% fewer parameters. The modifications draw on shaped attention (identity-preserving attention initialization) and scaling of the MLP block to enable these removals. Experiments cover pre-training (up to 650M tokens for most runs, 2B for one GPT, 5B for BERT) and downstream fine-tuning on GLUE for BERT, with model sizes around 100M–300M parameters.

### Strengths

- First work to simplify the transformer architecture while also achieving training throughput gains (previous simplifications reduced speed).
- Well-written, with a clear step-by-step presentation of each design choice and sufficient motivation (signal propagation and ablations).
- Discusses limitations of the approach.
- Provides interesting, empirically grounded findings about the working mechanism of the attention sub-block, offering valuable insights for efficient block design.
- Approach is well-grounded in related work (SkipInit, shaped attention, residual scaling, parallel MHA+MLP) and presents strong natural extensions of them.
- Completely removing the value/projection matrices and all skip connections yields a genuinely simpler block.
- Parameter savings and training speedup are demonstrated, and further optimization is possible.
- Experiments are thorough and well-motivated, covering a wide range of variations/ablations, and include both GPT and BERT models on multiple tasks.

### Weaknesses

- **Model scale and generality**: Experiments are limited to smaller models (100M–300M). It is unclear if the gains (speed, parameter reduction) scale to larger models, where different bottlenecks may emerge or benefits may diminish/reverse. One reviewer (score 6) explicitly raises this concern, while the other reviewers (score 8) share it but are more positive about the insights.
- **Training length**: Most experiments use only 650M tokens, which is low compared to compute-optimal training (Chinchilla). The longest runs are 2B tokens (Figure 8) and 5B tokens (BERT). It remains to be seen if simplified blocks maintain their advantage over vanilla transformers with longer training, or if the initial gains are transient and vanilla blocks eventually catch up.
- **Sequence length**: The sequence length is only 128, which is unorthodox and may weaken attention, potentially making it easier to drop value/projection matrices. The method’s robustness to longer sequences is untested.
- **Downstream evaluation**: Only GLUE for BERT is reported; no downstream evaluation for GPT. More comprehensive evaluation across different task categories (classification, generation, reasoning) is missing.
- **Partial source of gains**: The reported throughput gain (15%) partly comes from the parallel structure (Wang & Komatsuzaki, 2021), not solely from the proposed simplifications. The contribution of the simplifications alone is not clearly quantified.
- **Per-update speed**: Despite parameter reduction, per-update training speed does not increase. The throughput gain is explained only later (presumably from parallelization), but this distinction is confusing. Additionally, skipless transformers are noted to be slower than those with skip connections on page 3—this is not adequately explained.
- **Normalization ambiguity**: The analysis in Sec. 4.4 argues normalization layers are unnecessary based on signal propagation at initialization, but then states normalization still has benefits and is used in experiments. This creates a contradiction between the theoretical motivation and the actual implementation. The SAS-P variant without normalization is not reported (training failure or significantly lower performance?), which weakens the claim.
- **Performance drop**: In Table 1, the proposed simplified block (SAS-P) slightly underperforms the standard Pre-LN and Parallel baselines. The advantage over existing architectures is not convincingly demonstrated, given the minor drop.
- **Design origin and novelty**: Some critical components (shaped attention, parallel structure) are taken from existing literature. The overall combination is new but builds heavily on prior work.
- **Questionable design choices**: 
  - Why prefer a skipless self-attention block? Skip connections benefit signal propagation, especially in deeper models, and do not violate simplicity.
  - Setting value/projection matrices to identity in the standard block with skip connection seems to work (Fig. 19 performance difference is negligible) and also reduces parameters; why not adopt that simpler approach?
  - The motivation for moving from SkipInit to ShapedAttention is unclear. Adding an identity addition to value and projection matrices is not obviously simpler than retaining residual connections.
- **Missing ablations and analyses**:
  - Are there settings (data or tasks) where the simplified transformers fail to match the standard transformer?
  - What are the limitations in model expressiveness and capability?
  - Could the MLP dimension be reduced when using SAS-P, since the MLP block contributes less (β_FF = 1/√L vs β=1 for attention)?
  - The paper could improve presentation: Figure 7 should apply exponential smoothing to MLM loss; Figure 6 has overlapping lines that are hard to distinguish.
