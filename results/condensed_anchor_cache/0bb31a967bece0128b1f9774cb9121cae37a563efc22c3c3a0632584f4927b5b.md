## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies a trade-off phenomenon where enhancing an LLM's privacy awareness via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) significantly decreases its fairness awareness, and vice versa. To mitigate this, the authors propose DEAN, a training-free method that deactivates neurons coupled to both fairness and privacy, guided by information-theoretic reduction of mutual information. Experiments on models like Qwen-2-7B-Instruct, Llama2, and Vicuna, and datasets (Beavertails, Salad-bench, Alpaca) show simultaneous improvements (e.g., +12.2% fairness, +14.0% privacy on Qwen-2-7B-Instruct). Reviewers find the problem interesting and the training-free approach efficient, but raise concerns about theoretical assumptions, baseline choices, neuron selection methodology, and broader model impacts.

### Strengths
- Studies an interesting and counter-intuitive trade-off between fairness and privacy in LLM fine-tuning (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Proposes a training-free method (DEAN) that is efficient, innovative, and suitable for resource-constrained settings (R1, R3, R4).
- Simultaneously improves both fairness and privacy awareness without sacrificing overall performance; reported gains of 12.2% (fairness) and 14.0% (privacy) on Qwen-2-7B-Instruct (R3, R4, abstract).
- Evaluated across multiple LLMs of varying sizes (Qwen2, Vicuna, Llama2) and datasets (Beavertails, Salad-bench, Alpaca), demonstrating broad applicability (R3, R4).
- Robust in scenarios with limited or biased annotated data, where standard SFT methods may fail (R3, abstract).
- Clear presentation with a well-structured explanation, helpful illustrations, and step-by-step breakdown (R2, R3, R4).
- Provides a new perspective on jointly addressing fairness and privacy, with potential for integration into comprehensive frameworks (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical assumptions**: Theorem 1 assumes a single random variable \(Z\) for shared activation on both fairness and privacy; this is a strong assumption—does the result hold if two correlated but distinct representations \(Z_1, Z_2\) (with \(I(Z_1;Z_2)>0\)) are considered instead? (R1). Proposition 1’s inequality (2) is problematic because the terms inside mutual information are an expectation and lack randomness (R1). The theory is viewed as too simplistic, just an inequality application (R2, R3). (Contrast: R1 and R4 were more positive about the overall contribution despite these concerns.)
- **Baselines and comparisons**: The paper compares DEAN against SFT methods (FFT, LoRA) but does not include privacy- or fairness-specific algorithms like DP-LoRA; it is unclear whether such methods also exhibit the observed trade-off (R2, R3). A detailed performance comparison with other advanced fairness and privacy techniques is missing (R3). Experiments are limited to generative tasks; classification, QA, and sentiment analysis (e.g., IMDB, SQuAD, AG News) are not tested, limiting assessment of real-world applicability (R4).
- **Neuron identification and deactivation**: The threshold-based binary importance scoring may miss neurons that contribute to multiple tasks or have context-dependent importance; more refined methods (clustering like K-means/DBSCAN, or interpretability tools like SHAP/LIME) were not explored or compared (R3, R4). Accuracy of neuron identification depends heavily on dataset quality and representativeness—biased or limited data could lead to misidentification (R3). Directly deactivating coupled neurons may disrupt other model functions (e.g., generation fluency, multitasking); alternative deactivation strategies (partial suppression, weight scaling) were not investigated (R3, R4). The paper does not provide an efficiency comparison (time cost) for the neuron identification process (R2).
- **Privacy and fairness definitions**: The paper’s definitions of privacy and fairness differ from standard group-fairness or differential privacy definitions; formal definitions and measurement are unclear (R2). Privacy is a binary security property; neuron editing cannot give theoretical guarantees against information leakage, and the novelty over prior neuron analysis work is limited beyond a simple theorem (R3).
- **Implementation and notation**: The timing of the masking operation on weights is unclear (R2). Notation is inconsistent: in line 208 \(D_f\) and \(D_p\) denote fairness and privacy datasets, but in line 215 and Algorithm 1, \(D_b\) becomes fairness-related and \(D_f\) privacy-related (R2).
- **Missing ablations**: Could simply balancing the ratio of privacy/fairness data in SFT improve both? How do privacy and fairness awareness vary with different numbers of SFT samples? (R1). The paper uses HSIC to estimate mutual information but does not explain parameter choices or their impact on neuron identification (R3). The method’s effect on generation fluency and multitasking is not specifically tested—only evaluated via HellaSwag, Race, MMLU (Table 3), not on generative coherence or cross-task accuracy (R4).
