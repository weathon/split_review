## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper integrates a score-matching diffusion model with consistency distillation to improve time-domain music source extraction. A deterministic model first estimates the source, then a diffusion model (conditioned on the deterministic output) refines it. Consistency distillation reduces the iterative sampling to one or few steps. The method is evaluated on the Slakh2100 dataset (bass, drums, guitar, piano) and shows objective improvements in Si-SDR over baselines. The authors also report limited results on MUSDB18 (only the deterministic model). Four reviewers gave scores 6, 3, 5, 3. Reviewer 1 is positive about efficiency and novelty, while Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 are critical of the experimental scope, generalizability, and novelty, with Reviewer 3 noting the method is otherwise sound.

### Strengths
- The paper is one of the first to apply consistency distillation to music source separation, achieving a reasonable level of novelty (Reviewer 1).
- The literature survey is well-executed, with clear background and explanation of compared methods (Reviewer 1).
- The method is highly efficient: consistency distillation reduces the iterative sampling to one step while improving or matching performance (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Significant improvement over baselines (e.g., Demucs, MSDM, ISDM) is shown on Slakh2100 in terms of Si-SDR (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The integration of score-matching diffusion with consistency distillation, conditioning on ground-truth stems during training and on the deterministic output during inference, is an interesting and well-aligned approach for source separation (Reviewer 2).
- The improvement in SDR obtained via consistency distillation is noted as interesting and opens possibilities for future research (Reviewer 4).
- The paper is well written and easy to follow (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset and poor generalizability**: The primary experiments are on Slakh2100, a synthetic MIDI-synthesized dataset that does not capture the complexities of real-world audio (e.g., recording quality, microphone placement, acoustics) (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The method shows no improvement on real-world recordings (MUSDB18) beyond the deterministic baseline, and the diffusion/CD extensions are not reported on MUSDB18; the authors attribute this to dataset size, but many other models (e.g., Demucs) benchmark on MUSDB successfully, making this explanation unconvincing (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The lack of evaluation on real-world data (e.g., MoisesDB, MUSIC) severely limits practical applicability and makes a state-of-the-art claim on Slakh2100 less meaningful (Reviewers 2, 4). Data augmentation (pitch shifting, etc.), combining datasets, or transfer learning could mitigate the dataset size issue, but were not explored (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited evaluation metrics**: Only Si-SDR is used for the main results. While relevant, this metric may not align with perceived audio quality, especially above 20 dB, and does not capture artifacts or source isolation fully. Metrics like SDR, SIR, SAR, or neural-network-based perceptual metrics would provide a more comprehensive analysis (Reviewers 1, 2). The claim that CD improves both performance and quality is not backed by a specific quality metric (Reviewer 2).
- **OOD performance**: The method shows limited effectiveness in out-of-distribution domains (Appendix C), acknowledged as an open issue (Reviewer 1).
- **Fairness of baseline comparisons**: In Table 1, the comparison with Demucs may be unfair because Demucs was trained on less data and on non-MIDI data, while the proposed model was trained and tested on Slakh2100. Retraining Demucs on Slakh2100 would be more appropriate (Reviewer 3). Additionally, baseline results in Table 1 appear identical to those in Mariani et al. (ICLR 2024), raising concerns about consistent evaluation conditions (Reviewer 2). Table 2 shows inference time and parameter count for Demucs that differ from Mariani et al.—these discrepancies need clarification (Reviewer 2).
- **Unclear hyperparameter choices and comparisons**: In Figure 2, the values of $\sigma_{\max}=0.01244, 0.2497, 0.2495$ may have been chosen to optimize the displayed performance; it is unclear whether evaluation data was used for tuning (Reviewer 1). The CD and diffusion models use different hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate), so the improvement from distillation might be due to learning rate annealing rather than the distillation process itself (Reviewer 4). The reason for comparing CD and diffusion at only up to 5 denoising steps is not explained (Reviewer 2).
- **Architecture and novelty concerns**: The method treats source separation as a sequence of extractions, causing memory/runtime to grow linearly with the number of instruments (Reviewer 3). Both the deterministic and diffusion/CD models use standard U-Net architectures with no essential design changes, and the overall approach applies existing methodologies without introducing novel design choices (Reviewers 3, 4). The idea of using diffusion for source separation is not novel (as cited) (Reviewer 4).
- **Clarity and details**: Several typos are noted (e.g., “extantion”, “continuous” for “continues”, “designed as discrete process”; Figure 1 (b) caption) (Reviewer 2). The title uses “source extraction” instead of the more common “source separation” (Reviewer 4). The calculation of $\bar{x}^\text{det}_s$ in Section 3.2 and in Figure 1 is unclear—specifically which layers of the frozen U-Net are used (Reviewer 4).
