## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes visually-continuous corruption robustness (VCR), an extension of corruption robustness that assesses models across a continuous range of image corruption levels corresponding to human-perceived quality, and introduces two human-aware metrics (HMRI and MRSI). The authors conduct large-scale human experiments (7,718 participants) on 14 corruption types and evaluate state-of-the-art robust neural networks. Key findings are that continuous evaluation reveals insufficient robustness undetected by ImageNet-C, the NN-human gap is larger than previously known, and some corruptions impact humans similarly, enabling more efficient robustness assessments.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important and interesting problem of robustness to image corruptions (R1, R4).
- Well-motivated and thoughtful design of a visually-continuous benchmark that extends beyond pre-defined corruption levels (R2, R4).
- Quantitatively demonstrates that ImageNet-C has less coverage of visual corruptions than the proposed VCR test set (R2).
- VCR is shown to be a better robustness estimate than benchmarking on ImageNet-C; models good on ImageNet-C are not robust enough on the continuous range (R2).
- Well-detailed and carefully conducted human experiments with a large participant count (R1, R2).
- The proposed metrics (HMRI, MRSI) are reasonable and mitigate evaluation bias caused by test-set quality distribution (R3).
- Interesting observations, e.g., comparing prediction consistency across NNs in addition to accuracy, and exploring visually similar transformations for cost-effective assessment (R3).
- Open-sourced with all human data and evaluation code, beneficial for future research (R1, R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Readability and clarity**: The paper is very hard to follow. Arguments are overworded, objectives unclear, and necessary background (e.g., what visual information fidelity (VIF) is) is not introduced. Too much content is squeezed into 9 pages, making it mostly incomprehensible (R1, R4). *Contrast: one reviewer (R2) found it well written, but others strongly disagreed* (R2 vs R1, R4).
- **Missing related work comparisons**: No discussion or comparison with existing continuous corruption benchmarks such as ImageNet-P, ImageNet-CCC, ImageNet-Cbar, or ImageNet-3DCC (R1). The focus is only on ImageNet-C; how do the proposed augmentations compare to those in ImageNet-CCC? (R1).
- **Model selection and coverage**: The choice of tested models is unmotivated; no perspective on architecture types (CNN vs ViT), training data (ImageNet21K, LAION, etc.), or training strategies (supervised, self-supervised, adversarial) is given (R1). Top-performing robust models from the ImageNet-C leaderboard, e.g., DINOv2 and MAE, are missing from evaluation (R2).
- **VIF as image quality metric**: The paper adopts VIF as the quality measure, which is inferior to more advanced full-reference quality metrics like LPIPS and DISTS (R3).
- **Coverage binning dependency**: The coverage comparison between ImageNet-C and the VCR test set uses splitting the full quality range into 40 bins. Coverage is highly sensitive to the number of bins; in the extreme case of 1 bin the two sets would have identical coverage, so the choice of bin number is not justified (R3).
- **Human testing details and potential memory effect**: During subjective testing, human decisions are highly affected by memory effect—a severely corrupted image might still be recognized if the same image content was previously seen at high quality. The paper does not illustrate how this effect was avoided or provide details on the reliability of human decisions (R3). Humans were presented images for 200 ms; is this too short for object recognition, and are machines also limited to 200 ms? (R2). Qualification tests and sanity checks for filtering participants, as well as total participants before filtration, are not discussed (R2). It is unclear whether each human participant saw the same set of images (R2).
- **Toolbox claim**: Referring to the code as a "toolbox" is inappropriate; it is not an installable, easily applicable Python package; more work is needed (R4).
- **Missing visual examples**: The paper does not show images of corruptions or how they relate to existing benchmarks (R1).
- **Typos and formatting**: “coverages= of” and “[0..1]” should be “[0,1]” (p.4); “max” should be “\max” (p.2); “Tbl. 2” should be “Tab. 2”; “Uniform(0,1)” should be “U(0,1)” (R4). In Definition 1, $S^m({\gamma}^{(v)})$ should be $S^m_{\gamma}^{(v)}$ (R3). Cross-referencing (e.g., “Fig 1”) is not properly linked (R2). What is impulse noise or glass blur? (R4).
