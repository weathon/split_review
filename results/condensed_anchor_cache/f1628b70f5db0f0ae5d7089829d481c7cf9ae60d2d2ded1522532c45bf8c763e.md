## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the self-repair capability of LLMs (Code Llama-13b-instruct, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) on code generation tasks from HumanEval and APPS. It breaks repair into generation, execution error detection, feedback generation, and repair. The authors compare self-repair to sampling without repair (pass@k) while modeling cost. They find that gains from self-repair are often modest, vary across data subsets, and sometimes absent. The main bottleneck is feedback quality; using a stronger model or human feedback substantially boosts performance. The paper concludes that initial sample diversity is more critical than repair diversity, and that self-repair lags far behind human-level debugging even for GPT-4.

### Strengths
- **Systematic analysis of self-repair vs. pass@k with explicit cost modelling.** The paper is the first to systematically compare self-repair against simply sampling more initial programs, showing that when computation is comparable, benefits are similar and the optimal combination of both techniques is most effective. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Novel focus on feedback quality.** The paper identifies feedback generation as the key limitation and demonstrates that using a stronger model or human-provided feedback yields substantially larger performance gains. This opens a promising direction for future work. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Well-designed experiments and clear takeaways.** The experiments are well executed, particularly the feedback-boosting experiments. The paper is well-written, properly organized, and clearly highlights the limitations of current self-repair, providing concrete insights for the community. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Honest assessment of limitations.** The authors do a good job of highlighting key limitations of their own analysis. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope: only isolated programming challenges (HumanEval, APPS) with clear, complete problem descriptions and short code.** These characteristics do not reflect real-world development where intent is vague, unit tests provide crucial feedback, and code length and inter-procedural dependencies explode the search space. Therefore, the conclusion that pass@k is comparable to self-repair may not generalize. (Reviewers 2, 3; one reviewer (score 8) elaborates extensively on this point)
- **No fine-tuning or parameter optimization for self-repair.** The study only evaluates prompting; it is unclear whether a cheap fine-tuning step could overcome the identified weaknesses. The conclusion that "self-repair is not a silver bullet" may be too strong given this limitation. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Contradiction with prior work not sufficiently addressed.** One reviewer (score 6, confidence 3) notes that the findings partially contradict Chen et al. 2023b (“self-debugging”), which reported significant gains from self-repair. The paper mentions this work in related work but lacks a direct comparative analysis to reconcile the discrepancy. (Reviewer 1)
- **Missing analysis on more diverse datasets (non-Python languages).** Only Python datasets are used; generalizability to other languages is unaddressed. (Reviewer 1)
- **Feedback-boosting experiment (Section 4.2) restricts feedback and repair to one iteration – rationale unclear.** The authors do not explain why only a single repair step was used in this experiment, leaving open whether more iterations would change the conclusion. (Reviewer 1 question, treated as missing ablation)
- **Unclear whether the results hold with few-shot prompts for self-repair.** The paper uses zero-shot prompting; the “self-debugging” approach uses few-shot. A comparison or discussion of this difference is missing. (Reviewer 1 question, treated as missing comparison)
- **No exploration of tree-search or value-network extensions.** One reviewer suggests that treating repair as a tree search with a value network (like evolutionary search) could yield better results, but no such experiments were attempted. (Reviewer 3 question, treated as missing experiment)
