## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper analyzes tensor product operations in E(3)-equivariant neural networks (Clebsch-Gordan, Gaunt, matrix tensor products) in terms of runtime (theoretical, FLOPs, GPU wall time) and expressivity. It introduces the Vector Signal Tensor Product (VSTP) to address the Gaunt tensor product’s inability to handle antisymmetric operations, and generalizes it to Irrep Signal Tensor Products (ISTPs). The authors argue that speedups in these methods come at the cost of expressivity, and that theoretical runtime guarantees often differ from empirical performance.

### Strengths

- The asymptotic runtime analysis and inclusion of many tensor product implementations (CG, GTP, MTP, sparse CG, VSTP, ISTPs) provide a valuable overview and could spur further improvements.
- The paper is well-structured and presents complex concepts in a way that is accessible to readers familiar with equivariant neural networks.
- Introducing VSTP and ISTPs directly addresses the limitation of Gaunt tensor products in performing antisymmetric operations, which is crucial for tasks involving chirality.
- Theoretical foundations are robust, with well-defined selection rules for various tensor products, contributing valuable mathematical insights.
- The tradeoff between computational efficiency and expressivity is thoroughly discussed, and the analysis of asymptotic runtimes and expressivity of different implementations provides useful insights.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient experimental validation.** The proposed VSTP and ISTPs are evaluated only on a single synthetic Tetris experiment (Section 5.2). There is no application to real-world tasks (e.g., molecular modeling on OC20, 3BPA, chiral molecules) or integration into canonical models like eSCN, MACE, or EquiformerV2 for direct comparison with GTP. The practical significance of antisymmetry and the claimed speedups remain unconvincing.
- **Expressivity measure is poorly justified.** The proxy B (dimension of the output space of the tensor product used as a measure of expressivity) is not adequately connected to neural network expressivity, which depends on many other factors. Normalizing by γ(T) (Section 5) is unclear and does not justify the strong claim that Clebsch-Gordan tensor products are the fastest after such normalization.
- **Conclusions are too strong given limited evaluation.** Wall time comparisons were run only on a single GPU (RTX A5500), and FLOP counts are likely idealized (e.g., from Nsight profiler) rather than accurate. Runtime heavily depends on hardware and implementation optimization; a one-to-one comparison is not guaranteed without deeper analysis.
- **Presentation and accessibility issues.** The paper is very technical and hard to follow for non-experts. Key notations are introduced without explanation (e.g., l_1, l_2, “tosphere” in Equation 2, “Vector Gaunt” in Figure 6). Appendices are dense. The organization could be improved: Section 2 reviews existing techniques (CGTP, MTP) that are only used for comparison, while the novel contributions (Section 3 and 4) are compressed. The title does not accurately reflect the content, as only a small part concerns the tradeoff.
- **Missing ablation and baselines.** The paper does not include training time of CGTP and GTP for the Tetris experiment, does not assess whether VSTP actually achieves speedup on this task, and does not explain why GTP and MTP lose information (lines 195, 229) or why the S2FFT algorithm is the only true asymptotic speedup (line 401). Additional analysis is needed on why Clebsch-Gordan (sparse) has the lowest FLOPs yet high GPU walltime. No comparison with alternative architectures is provided.
- **Minor:** Duplicated word “section” at the end of Line 111.
