## Merged Review

### Summary

This work identifies a “blind tokens” phenomenon in Large Vision Language Models (LVLMs), where the model over-attends to image tokens lacking meaningful semantic information, leading to hallucinations. To counter this, the authors propose Attentional Vision Calibration (AVISC), a decoding-time technique that: (1) selects a layer, (2) identifies blind tokens based on the attention distribution, and (3) performs contrastive decoding between the original logits and logits conditioned on a biased input where blind tokens are zeroed out. AVISC is training-free, requires no external models, and consistently outperforms existing decoding methods (VCD, M3ID) on hallucination benchmarks (POPE, MME, AMBER).

### Strengths

- **Well-written and organized** (Reviewer 1). The proposed method is **easy to follow** (Reviewer 3).
- **Lightweight and practical**: AVISC operates directly during decoding, requires **no additional training, auxiliary models, or complex self-feedback mechanisms**, making it easy to integrate into existing LVLMs (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The discovery of the **blind tokens phenomenon is insightful**; the authors demonstrate it through experiments and visualizations including bounding boxes and visual tokens (Reviewer 2).
- **Consistently improves performance on standard hallucination benchmarks** (POPE, MME, AMBER) with a simple approach (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited model scope**: Evaluation is restricted to LLaVA-7B and InstructBLIP. The paper should include more competitive and recent models such as **MiniCPM-V, LLaVA-13B, LLaVA-34B, GLAMM** (Reviewer 1), **Qwen, InternVL** (Reviewer 2), and **Intern-VL, VILA** (Reviewer 4).
- **Computational overhead**: The method doubles inference time by computing logits twice (original vs. biased input). A detailed discussion of the **performance–cost trade-off** in real-world applications is missing (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparison with other inference-time methods**: Only VCD and M3ID are compared; **OPERA** (CVPR 2024) is a relevant missing baseline (Reviewer 1).
- **Performance degradation on counting tasks**: The results show worse performance on counting; the authors do not provide a hypothesis or analysis. It is unclear whether **blind tokens encode useful information for specific tasks like counting** (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing evaluations on general VQA and reasoning benchmarks**: AVISC is only tested on hallucination benchmarks. Without evaluations on **MMbench, VQAv2, or similar comprehensive benchmarks**, it is unclear whether the **method harms original conversational abilities or other capabilities** (Reviewers 2 and 4). This is a common concern for contrastive decoding methods (Reviewer 4). (Reviewer 3 did not raise this point.)
- **Attention analysis is coarse-grained**: The authors should provide a **layer-by-layer and head-by-head analysis** and consider whether the blind token phenomenon aligns with **attention sinks** (Reviewer 4). The **necessity and ablation for layer selection (parameter P) is missing** (Reviewer 3).
- **Inconsistent experimental settings**: The results in **Tables 1 and 9 do not fully align with reported LLaVA results**; the settings used in these experiments need clarification (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing essential baseline**: The approach lacks a comparison with **random zero-out of the same number of tokens**. This is needed to validate the motivation (i.e., that blind tokens specifically are less important) and to demonstrate that the biased prediction construction is meaningful (Reviewer 3). Without this, the **motivation for the approach is insufficient** (Reviewer 3).
- **Writing quality needs improvement, and figure font sizes are too small (e.g., Fig.4)** (Reviewer 3). Note: Reviewer 1 found the paper well-written, so there is disagreement on this point.
