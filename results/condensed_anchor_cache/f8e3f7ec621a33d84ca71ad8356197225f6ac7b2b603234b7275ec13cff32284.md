## Merged Review

### Summary
ManiSkill-HAB is a benchmark for low-level manipulation and in-home object rearrangement, built on a GPU-accelerated implementation of the Home Assistant Benchmark (HAB) in the ManiSkill3 (Sapien) simulator. It achieves over 3× the simulation speed of previous magical-grasp implementations while supporting realistic rigid-body physics and low-level control. The benchmark includes three long-horizon tasks (TidyHouse, PrepareGroceries, SetTable) with subtasks (Pick, Place, Open, Close), and provides extensive RL (SAC, PPO) and IL (BC) baselines. A rule-based trajectory filtering system selects demonstrations from RL policies that meet safety and behavior criteria. The code and data are publicly available.

### Strengths
- **Highly relevant task and GPU acceleration.** The focus on home assistant settings and realistic low-level control (no magical grasping) addresses a critical need. The GPU-accelerated implementation significantly outperforms prior work in speed and computational cost.
- **Comprehensive baselines and evaluation.** Extensive RL and IL baselines are provided, offering future research a solid foundation for comparison. The evaluation is systematic, including a trajectory labeling system and failure mode analysis in supplementary material.
- **Controlled data generation at scale.** The demonstration filtering pipeline, combined with fast simulation, enables efficient and controlled generation of large datasets that respect behavior and safety criteria.
- **Clarity and reproducibility.** The paper is well-written, with clear figures and supplementary materials. The evaluation protocols are structured, and failure cases are documented. Code and data are publicly available.
- **Minority more positive view (Reviewer 1, score 8):** This reviewer sees higher originality (tasks and objects prevalent in home settings, speed advantage, filtering feasibility), higher quality (high-quality videos, clear advantage in simulation speed, extensive baselines), and higher significance (critical need for efficient and realistic simulation tools for complex home tasks). Other reviewers (score 5) agree the benchmark is useful but note limited novelty.

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and comparison with prior work.** The primary contribution is porting HAB to ManiSkill3, which is largely an engineering effort. Several ideas (skill sequence partitioning, training algorithms) are consistent with the M3 framework (Gu et al.), yet no detailed comparison is provided. Similarly, the paper does not discuss how MS-HAB compares with existing platforms like RoboCasa or Behavior-1k in terms of scale, scene diversity, or task difficulty. The three long-horizon tasks (TidyHouse, PrepareGroceries, SetTable) and object sets are limited to HAB and YCB datasets; future tasks (e.g., cleaning dishes, laundry, tool usage) are suggested.

2. **Baseline methods are limited and not fully justified.** 
   - Only SAC and PPO are used for RL, with each applied to only two tasks (Pick/Place vs. Open/Close). This makes it hard to assess relative task difficulty or algorithm strengths. SAC outperformed PPO in grasping, yet PPO was chosen for Open/Close citing faster wall-clock training; a full factorial comparison would be more insightful.
   - The IL baseline (BC) is questionable: since RL policies can be queried for expert actions, DAgger (which aggregates dataset during training) would likely outperform BC with a static dataset. Moreover, there is no clear knowledge distillation or performance improvement from the IL step (Table 1 shows no boost).
   - More recent methods (e.g., TD-MPC2, ACT, Diffusion Policies) are absent, limiting the benchmark’s usefulness for state-of-the-art comparisons.
   - No baseline for sense-plan-act (TAMP) or oracle-information methods is provided, which would give valuable insights into the benchmark’s difficulty.

3. **Success rate definition is overly optimistic.** The reported success rate is “percentage of trajectories that achieve success at least once in an episode” without a mechanism to halt execution upon success. This inflates performance; success should be measured at episode end or with a learned termination.

4. **Real-robot transfer and physics realism are insufficiently validated.** The paper does not attempt real-robot transfer, so the claim of “realistic low-level control” is unverified. Force thresholds (e.g., 5000N for “excessive collisions”) are far above acceptable limits for collaborative robotics (∼500N). Violations of a 10000N force limit are cited as the primary performance bottleneck in Open/Close, raising concerns about deployability.

5. **Partial observability handling is unclear.** The policies use depth images and CNN encoders but no explicit mechanism (e.g., history, frame stacking) to handle partial observability. The paper does not explain whether this is a significant issue for the tasks.

6. **Dataset generation with RL, not sense-plan-act.** The imitation dataset is generated from RL policies. A sense-plan-act pipeline could also produce demonstrations, and it is unclear why RL was preferred (e.g., could produce higher-quality or more diverse data?).

7. **Per-object policies vs. all-object policy comparison is unfair.** In Figure 7, per-object policies are trained individually, each with 1e7 samples, while the all-object policy is trained with only 1e7 total samples. A fair comparison would allocate num_objects * 1e7 samples to the all-object variant.

8. **Generalization limitation of per-object policies.** Training separate policies for each object (e.g., bowls, cans, cups) might improve task completion for those specific objects but limits generalization across objects and long-horizon tasks.

9. **Minority more negative view (Reviewer 3, score 5):** This reviewer considers the technical contribution quite limited, describing the draft as more like a technical report, and notes that most ideas have appeared in prior work (M3). Reviewer 4 (score 5) also highlights limited novelty and questions the realism of learned behaviors under high force limits. Reviewer 1 (score 8) does not share these concerns, finding the contribution significant.
