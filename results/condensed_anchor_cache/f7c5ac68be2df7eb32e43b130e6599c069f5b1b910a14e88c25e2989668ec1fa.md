## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates Large Language Model (LLM) sensitivity to meaning-preserving prompt formatting choices in few-shot settings. The authors find that open-source LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-2-13B) exhibit extreme sensitivity—performance differences of up to 76 accuracy points—that persists with larger models, more few-shot examples, and instruction tuning. They argue that LLM evaluation should report performance across a range of plausible formats rather than a single format, and show that format performance correlates weakly across models, questioning the validity of fixed-format comparisons. To facilitate this, they propose FORMATSPREAD, a method that uses a formal grammar of plausible formats and Bayesian optimization to efficiently estimate the performance spread without requiring model weight access.

### Strengths

- Addresses a critical yet under-explored issue for the community: LLM evaluation should not rely on a single arbitrary prompt format.
- The problem formulation is made rigorous by defining a formal “grammar” of plausible prompt formats (Section 3).
- The design of grammar rules and sensitivity evaluation is ingenious.
- Evaluating over a distribution of prompts provides a more informative understanding of model performance and robustness than single-prompt evaluation.
- The proposed FORMATSPREAD method can be applied to API-gated models without needing model weights.
- The core claim—that LLMs are highly sensitive to formatting changes—is substantiated by extensive experiments on few-shot classification (e.g., Super-Natural Instructions) and short text generation tasks (identifying the second letter of a word, arithmetic, synonym generation).
- The paper is well written and easy to understand.

### Weaknesses

- The analysis of why sensitivity is so large (up to 76 points) is insufficient. Potential confounders beyond prompt format structure need deeper investigation:
    - The content length of in-context examples and the variability of length across different examples within the same prompt.
    - Different tokenization methods (different tokenizers may break identical prompts into different token sequences, altering model interpretation).
    - The specific phrasing within the prompt beyond structural formatting.
- It remains unclear which types of tasks are more susceptible to prompt format sensitivity. A more granular analysis of task characteristics (e.g., logical reasoning vs. factual recall) is needed, not just sensitivity evaluations.
- The paper primarily substantiates its claims on classification tasks. While short text generation is discussed in the appendix, the important capability of long text generation is not addressed. This risks overclaiming unless the findings are explicitly scoped to classification.
- The problem of searching the prompt space is cast as a bandit problem, but many important LLM applications involve multi-turn conversations heavily dependent on history—a setting the bandit formulation does not capture.
- In Algorithm 1, the formulation assumes success/failure (binary reward), but many NLP tasks require continuous or human-aligned evaluation where success/failure is not discrete. It is unclear whether the formulation holds for such tasks.
- The optimization relies on a good initial prompt, but no guarantee or analysis is provided on how to ensure a good starting point.
- Figure 2 does not include the Pearson correlation coefficient directly on the plot; adding it would improve comprehensibility.
- The performance spread is reported only as max–min range; also reporting standard deviation would provide a fuller understanding of variability.
- The challenges of automatically measuring performance on text generation tasks (beyond classification) are not discussed, despite recent benchmarks being available.
