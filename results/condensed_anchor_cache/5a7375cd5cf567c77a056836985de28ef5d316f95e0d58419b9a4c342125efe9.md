## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper applies generative flow networks (GFlowNets) to two core phylogenetic inference tasks: Bayesian posterior inference and maximum parsimony inference. Tree construction is framed as a Markov decision process in which actions join pairs of rooted trees bottom-up. Branch lengths are discretized into bins to fit the GFlowNet framework. A transformer architecture encodes state features derived from Fitch’s and Felsenstein’s algorithms. Training uses trajectory balance. On real benchmark datasets, PhyloGFN matches the marginal likelihood performance of variational methods (VBPI-GNN) in the Bayesian setting and is comparable to the heuristic search tool PAUP* in the parsimony setting. A key claimed advantage is better coverage of low-probability tree topologies compared to VBPI-based approaches. Reviewers agree the application is novel but raise significant concerns about practical efficiency, the magnitude of improvement over baselines, and the lack of downstream validation.

### Strengths

- **Novel application of GFlowNets to phylogenetics** (R1, R2, R3, R4). The approach is “interesting, conceptually simple” (R2) and showcases GFlowNet versatility (R1). R3 specifically calls both the model architecture and the use of GFlowNets “novel and interesting.”
- **Full support of tree space**. Unlike VBPI methods that rely on a pre-defined subset of topologies, PhyloGFN can generate from all of tree space and from arbitrarily filled-in subtrees (R1, R2, R4).
- **Better estimates for low-probability trees**. The method outperforms VBPI-GNN in estimating probabilities of suboptimal structures (R1). R2 finds the performance “across the full space of trees is promising.”
- **Theoretical connections**. The paper provides theoretical results linking the optimal GFlowNet policy to Felsenstein’s and Fitch’s algorithms (R2).
- **Well-justified design choices** (R3, e.g., the transformer architecture on page 6).
- **Large body of well-planned experiments** with proper baselines (R3). The comparison set is appropriate given the limited number of modern ML methods for this problem (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of runtime/efficiency comparison.** No wall-clock time, number of tree evaluations during search, or number of Felsenstein peeling operations is reported (R1, R2, R4). This makes it impossible to assess whether GFlowNets explore tree space more efficiently or simply match PAUP* / MCMC with higher overhead (R1). R4 suggests reporting the number of Felsenstein peeling operations as a meaningful proxy.
- **Marginal likelihood (MLL) performance is not clearly better, and may be worse, than VBPI-GNN.**  
  - R2 is “uncomfortable with using MLL as a measure of accuracy” because for finite K the bias and variance depend on the learned posterior (e.g., on DS7, GeoPhy has the smallest mean MLL but a huge standard error). R2 finds it “surprising how much VBPI-GNN outperforms PhyloGFN on the MLL estimation task,” raising concern that PhyloGFN may not capture high-probability regions well.  
  - R3 notes the MLL values in Table 1 are “practically identical” (example cited: −7108.95 for PhyloGFN versus −7108.41 for VBPI-GNN on DS1), so the improvement is not significant.  
  - R1 says the method is “competitive” but does not claim an advantage on MLL.
- **Utilities of better suboptimal-tree probabilities is not demonstrated.**  
  - R1: “The experiments focus solely on the accuracy of probability estimates and fail to provide insights into their relevance for the broader application.” No demonstration of tangible biological insight or improved phylogenetic reconstruction.  
  - R2 questions whether extremely unlikely trees (log-differences up to 10⁻⁶⁶) matter in practice and asks for a real-world use-case where accurate probabilities for those trees would be useful.
- **Claim that VBPI-GNN is “severely” limited is overstated.** R2 points out that Table 2 shows VBPI-GNN is “quite accurate” for trees with non-negligible posterior probability, and the claim needs stronger empirical justification.
- **Axes in Figure 2 are unclear.** R2 asks “Are the axes flipped?” and notes that points fall on different x-axis ranges for the two columns but similar y-axis ranges, requiring clarification.
- **Missing experimental details.** R3: training epochs, learning rate schedules, optimization algorithms not stated in main text. The ± values in Table 1 need to be labeled as standard deviation or standard error, and over what (repetitions?); R3 warns that if they are standard deviations across repetitions the results may be acceptable, otherwise “alarming.” R2 requests training curves and sensitivity analysis to the distribution over τ.
- **Feature representation is not explained for the ML audience.** R3 states that “Fitch and Felsenstein features” are obvious to evolutionary biologists but opaque to ML researchers; a detailed explanation of how they are computed and what information they encode is needed.
- **Lack of direct, interpretable evaluation of tree quality.** R3 suggests using Robinson-Foulds distance to ground truth or ancestral-state prediction accuracy. The reported log-likelihood and parsimony are exploratory and leave “many gray areas.”
- **Disagreement on methodological novelty.** R1 views the work as “straightforward applications” with no significant ML innovations—discretization of branch lengths is a “compromised solution.” R2 and R3 disagree, finding the approach novel. (Minority: R1 is more negative.)
- **Paper does not explain GFlowNet concepts well for a general audience** (R4: “enumerating required items” rather than providing insight).
- **Parsimony inference is less interesting** and could be moved to an appendix (R4).
- **Oversights in the optimization argument**: R4 notes that the claim “direct optimization of P_F’s parameters is impossible since it involves an intractable sum over all complete trajectories” is technically flawed because exponentially many trajectories would make optimization infeasible even if the sum were tractable.
- **Clarification needed on tree-space generality.** R4 asks whether the method is restricted to binary trees (the paper “suggests binary trees” but also mentions bifurcating unrooted trees; the posterior support might include multifurcating trees—this needs to be clarified).
- **Multiple typos listed by R2** (e.g., “Given a set of observed sequence”, “join them” → “joins them”, inconsistencies in Proposition 1 notation, “ground-trueth” → “ground-truth”).
