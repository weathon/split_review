## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper connects adversarial training (AT) to catastrophic forgetting in continual learning. It empirically shows that models undergoing AT forget adversarial examples from earlier training stages. Based on this, it proposes Adaptive Multi-teacher Self-distillation (AMS), which uses multiple saved checkpoints as teachers to align outputs across stages via an adaptive regularizer. Experiments on SVHN, CIFAR-10/100, and TinyImageNet under ℓ∞ and ℓ2 attacks show that AMS improves robust accuracy and mitigates robust overfitting when added to methods such as Madry’s AT and TRADES.

### Strengths
1. **Novel perspective.** Viewing AT through the lens of continual learning is innovative and could open further research across the two fields (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5). Reviewer 4 calls it “the part that I like the most.”
2. **Clear presentation.** The paper is well-written, the storyline is easy to follow, and Figure 2 effectively illustrates the method (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5).
3. **Orthogonal method.** AMS is designed to be combined with any existing AT algorithm, making it widely applicable and ready to benefit from future stronger base methods (Reviewer 4).
4. **Comprehensive evaluation.** Experiments span multiple datasets (SVHN, CIFAR-10/100, TinyImageNet), threat models (ℓ∞, ℓ2), attacks (PGD, CW, AutoAttack), and AT variants. Robust overfitting is also analyzed (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5).
5. **Mitigation of robust overfitting.** The method is shown to substantially reduce robust overfitting (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 4 notes the “consistent performance improvement.”

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing ablation on loss components.** The proposed loss includes a reweighting term whose necessity is argued but not experimentally justified. A component-wise ablation is missing, making it impossible to assess its individual contribution (Reviewers 1, 5). Reviewer 1 asks why the hyperparameter m (affecting teacher count) barely impacts robustness (Fig. 4), which would be unexpected if forgetting is the central issue.

2. **Insufficient connection between AT and continual learning.** The paper does not rigorously show that adversarial examples from different training stages form distinct distributions, nor does it provide a quantitative measure of forgetting on older adversarial examples. The motivational experiment (Figure 1) may be on the training set, which weakens the analogy to catastrophic forgetting (Reviewers 1, 2). A comparison with forgetting in natural training is missing (Reviewer 1). Related work by Gupta et al. uses a similar motivational experiment and should be discussed (Reviewer 4).

3. **Misleading presentation of results.** In Table 2, the proposed method’s results are bolded even though multiple baselines achieve higher robust accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. The bold formatting does not indicate the best results, making comparisons confusing (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 4 suggests alternative formatting (e.g., separate rows for “Method” and “Method+AMS”).

4. **Missing comparisons and baselines.**  
   - No comparison to the recent SOTA using synthetic data (Better Diffusion Models, 1M extra examples) (Reviewer 1).  
   - S$^2$O outperforms TRADES+AMS on CIFAR-10 (Table 2), yet results of S$^2$O on other datasets and S$^2$O+AMS are not provided (Reviewer 4).  
   - Comparison with AWP is missing (Reviewer 4).  
   - Experiments on CIFAR-10 with 1M generated images with/without AMS are absent (Reviewer 4).  
   - Exponential Moving Average (EMA), a simple alternative for maintaining knowledge, is not included as a baseline (Reviewer 5).  
   - For completeness, results of AMS in a continual learning setting (compared to LwF, ER) would be of interest (Reviewer 4).

5. **Memory and computational overhead.** AMS requires storing multiple model checkpoints (all teachers), incurring significant memory and computational cost. The paper does not quantify this overhead or compare it to simpler alternatives like EMA. No intelligent teacher selection strategy is offered (Reviewers 1, 2, 5). Reviewer 2 questions scalability to full ImageNet; Reviewer 3 notes that efficiency techniques (Free-AT, YOPO) are mentioned but not integrated. Reviewer 2 asks whether AMS could also address catastrophic overfitting in fast AT.

6. **Limited model scope.** Experiments are confined to CNN-based architectures (ResNet variants). No evaluation on Vision Transformers is provided, which would strengthen the generalizability of findings (Reviewer 3).

7. **Problematic evaluation protocol.** The best checkpoint is selected based on *test* accuracy, introducing potential bias toward the test set. A validation set should be used for checkpoint selection (Reviewer 1).

8. **Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The choice of λ can cause significant performance drop (Table 5), raising concerns about generalizability across different base AT methods (Reviewer 4). Explanations for performance trends–e.g., distilling from more teachers (smaller m) hurting clean accuracy but not robustness–are not provided (Reviewer 5). Reviewer 4 asks whether hyperparameters (e.g., m=20, λ=0.5) can be fixed for all methods.

9. **Marginal gains.** The reported performance improvements from AMS are often around 1%, which is modest (Reviewer 4, minor).

10. **Design details unaddressed.** The initial random perturbation strength of 0.001 (Algorithm 1, line 6) is not justified (Reviewer 3). The paper does not discuss whether training epochs should be divided into uneven stages (Reviewer 2).
