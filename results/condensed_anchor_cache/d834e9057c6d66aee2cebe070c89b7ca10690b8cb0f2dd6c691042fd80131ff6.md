- Scores: 1, 1, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a meta-learning approach (SNP/SNP++) that projects task-specific subnetworks within the parameter space of a base learner to preserve zero-shot and few-shot performance during continual learning. Most reviewers agree the core idea is interesting and the experimental suite is expansive, but all four highlight severe and pervasive issues with presentation, clarity, and methodological rigor that make the contributions difficult to assess or reproduce. There is notable disagreement: two reviewers (scores 1, 1) question the soundness of the setup (e.g., information leakage, contradictory assumptions) and find the paper unsuitable for publication as is, while two reviewers (scores 3, 3) acknowledge convincing results for longer task sequences but still judge the lack of method formalization and missing context as critical flaws.

### Strengths

- Interesting and exciting topic; the idea of controlling parameter drift via distance regularization in meta-learning is noted as potentially promising.
- Extensive empirical results covering a dozen common evaluation benchmarks (CLIP and MAML comparisons, multiple datasets including MSCOCO, split-MSCOCO).
- Some reviewers find the experimental results convincing once the third task is reached; before that, performance closely resembles PAINT-style adaptation.
- SNP surpasses several benchmarks in multiple tasks, including zero-shot and few-shot learning.
- The authors attempt to improve clarity by providing pseudo-code algorithms.

### Weaknesses

**Clarity, structure, and writing (all reviewers):**
- The paper is extremely difficult to read and follow; the abstract and introduction are confusing, lacking a coherent story line and failing to connect distribution shifts, few-shot learning, meta-learning, and continual learning.
- The method (SNP/SNP++) is never properly formalized. Subnetworks are not defined; the approach is described only through large ad‑hoc algorithm blocks that provide no actionable information on what actually happens.
- The paper reads as a technical report, missing high‑level motivation, underlying intuitions, and theoretical support for algorithm design.
- Tables are placed far from their first reference (e.g., Tables 7 & 8) and are too dense to parse. Tables 1 and 2 on page 2 are dumped without explanation; reviewers suggest replacing them with heatmaps and moving Table 1 to the appendix.
- Equations contain apparent typos – e.g., line 135 likely should use θ_base,t-1 on the right‑hand side.
- Algorithm 1 is never referenced; Algorithm 2 is not cited in order; cross‑references are missing.
- Standard deviations are missing for all reported numbers.

**Methodological concerns and missing justification:**
- The setup appears compromised: the authors train on MSCOCO until accuracy is high on MSCOCO and low on all unseen datasets that the pre‑trained model performed well on. This sounds like an information leak from unseen datasets (reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 calls this “creating your own problem” – the intuition and justification for overfitting to hurt performance, rather than starting from a well‑performing backbone, are entirely missing.
- The objective function defines base parameters using all tasks simultaneously, which contradicts the sequential nature of continual learning. This is flagged as a fundamental flaw (reviewer 4).
- The claim that subnetwork representations can be added, removed, edited, or composed is not backed by any empirical evidence.
- The paper claims to scale to large models (e.g., LLMs in the introduction) but provides no large‑scale experiments.
- Section 3.1 groups datasets based on cosine distance between flattened parameter sets. No justification is given why this distance directly represents dataset similarity, or how random seeds (different local minima) would affect the distances.
- Section 3.1 and 3.2 lack clear motivation; they read like experiment sections, not method exposition. It is unclear what specific question each experiment is designed to answer.
- There is an inconsistency: Section 3.2 talks about “projecting task‑specific representations … within the meta parameters to the base parameter space,” while earlier stating that meta‑parameters and base parameters reside in the same space.

**Missing related work and comparisons:**
- Relevant literature is missing, including Progressive Neural Networks (Rusu et al. 2016), interpolation‑based adaptation for continual learning (Stojanovski et al. 2022), architectural‑restructuring methods for CL (Mallya et al. 2018, Schwarz et al. 2018, Hung et al. 2019), and works questioning the necessity of full meta‑learning objectives for few‑shot transfer (Dhillon et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2020).
- The paper does not place its own contributions in the context of the cited works; it simply lists them without discussing relationships or differences.

**Unstated implications, limitations, and complexity:**
- Algorithm 3 stores base parameters for all tasks (line 6) and a set of interpolated parameters for each task (S=1000 random interpolation constants). Storage requirements for large models and many tasks are not discussed.
- The training complexity (nested loops over all tasks, then all interpolation constants) is not commented on, yet the conclusion claims the method “scales to large models.”
- No discussion of how SNP affects model size, computational cost, or overall complexity when using gradient‑based meta‑learning (which may involve second‑order gradients; the paper does not state whether second‑order updates are used).
- The mechanism by which the method counteracts base parameter error when the meta‑parameter shift exceeds the allowed radius is not explained.

**Presentation of results:**
- Tables 1 and 2 are hard to read; grouping in Table 1 is arbitrary and not justified by hierarchical clustering or dataset semantics. Reviewers suggest bolding top‑performing metrics per task and using color/heatmaps.
- The experimental design in Section 3.1 appears to determine task groupings using data from all tasks, which reviewers consider invalid – the biases are recognized and used to shape the experiment rather than being controlled for.
- The value of having both Table 1 and Table 2 is unclear; they appear redundant and are not adequately discussed.
