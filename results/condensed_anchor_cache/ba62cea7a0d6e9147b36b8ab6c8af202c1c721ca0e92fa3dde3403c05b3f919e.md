## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes HamOS (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Outlier Synthesis), a framework that uses Hamiltonian Monte Carlo to generate diverse synthetic outliers from in-distribution data in a learned hyperspherical feature space. The synthesis is guided by an OOD-ness density estimate based on k-nearest neighbors, and the model is trained with a combination of classification, contrastive learning, and OOD-discernment losses. HamOS is evaluated on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet-1K, achieving state-of-the-art OOD detection performance. The motivation—reducing reliance on large pools of natural outliers by synthesizing high-quality virtual outliers—is clear and compelling. Three reviewers rate the paper positively (6,8,8), while one reviewer is more critical (5, confidence 5), citing overstated novelty, limited domain scope, and unclear definitions.

### Strengths
- **Novel application of HMC for outlier synthesis**: Using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo to sample diverse virtual outliers in hyperspherical latent space is a creative and principled adaptation. Markov chains enable extensive traversal of the feature space, generating representative OOD samples without requiring real outlier data.
- **Elegant integration of hyperspherical embeddings with HMC sampling**: The framework combines classification, contrastive learning, and OOD-discernment losses in a clean joint objective.
- **Comprehensive benchmarks and state-of-the-art performance**: HamOS is evaluated on standard and large-scale benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1K) and consistently outperforms SoTA baselines across metrics.
- **OOD-ness estimation guiding sampling**: The use of distance metrics in hyperspherical space to quantify OOD likelihood provides a useful perspective on synthesizing OOD characteristics.
- **Clean code and intuitive visualizations**: The supplementary code is well-integrated, and visualizations (e.g., t-SNE plots) are informative.
- **Efficiency and practical appeal**: HMC with near-1 acceptance rate makes the synthesis efficient, offering practical advantages over heavy generative models.

### Weaknesses
- **Paper contains detailed errors**: The caption of Figure 1 mentions ImageNet incorrectly; such errors affect readability.
- **Method section is difficult to follow**: Especially the explanation of how OOD samples are synthesized and how HMC is applied in Equation 3. Reviewer 1 found this part hard to understand.
- **Overstated novelty**: Claiming that outlier synthesis via Markov chains is a “new paradigm” for OOD detection is misleading. Markov chains are a well-known sampling technique; this is a new technique within an existing category, not an entirely new paradigm (Reviewer 4, score 5, confidence 5).
- **Lack of comparison to score-based sampling / diffusion models**: HMC sampling with gradient ∇_z U_OOD is analogous to score-based models (∇_x log p(x)) and Langevin dynamics. The paper does not cite relevant prior work on gradient-based sampling in latent spaces, nor does it explain why HMC is advantageous over score-based approaches (e.g., computational efficiency, direct OOD optimization, class-conditional sampling). Reviewer 2 suggests a dedicated comparison section.
- **Unclear definition of OOD-ness**: It is not formally quantified or measured; the description relies on k-nearest-neighbor distances but it’s unclear if other factors are considered. This makes it hard to understand how diverse outliers are actually synthesized (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient justification of HMC over simpler sampling methods**: Advantages of HMC over Gaussian sampling or Random Walk MCMC are not discussed theoretically or empirically. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 4 both request a justification—why HMC is particularly suitable for OOD detection.
- **Low inter-class dispersion in Table 3 is concerning**: HamOS shows poor separation between ID classes in feature space, which could negatively affect ID classification performance (Reviewer 4). This issue is not addressed.
- **Inconsistent visualization quality**: In Fig 7, HMC samples OOD regions much better than NPOS, but in Fig 10, NPOS appears equally good or better; the quality/utility difference between methods is not clearly explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing timing comparison**: No runtime or theoretical cost comparison to NPOS and VOS (Reviewer 1, 3). HMC can be computationally heavy; the paper should discuss this.
- **No discussion of experimental stability**: Standard deviations for the reported results are not provided (Reviewer 1). How stable are the numbers?
- **Scalability concerns**: How does performance scale with feature dimension? Hyperspherical geometry may become problematic at higher dimensions (Reviewer 2).
- **Sensitivity to hyperparameters**: Sensitivity to choosing initial midpoints between clusters and to the hard margin parameter is not analyzed; no principled method for selecting the margin across datasets (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited domain scope**: Evaluation is only on natural image datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-1K). In the era of LLMs and multimodal models, significance for other domains (text, scientific data) is unclear. Reviewer 4 questions applicability and suggests discussing adaptation to NLP or other data types.
- **Quality of generated samples**: Can the synthesized OOD points be used to directly boost ID or OOD classification accuracy of underlying models? Prior work (Pooladzandi et al., 2023, “Generating High Fidelity Synthetic Data…”) found that MCMC-sampled EBMs reduced performance unless images were high quality; the paper does not address this concern (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of theoretical insight on hard margin choice**: Reviewer 2 asks for a principled way to select the margin for different datasets.
- **Consideration of more advanced MCMC**: Could Spherical Riemannian Manifold HMC (Spherical RMHMC) perform better than Spherical HMC? (Reviewer 3)
