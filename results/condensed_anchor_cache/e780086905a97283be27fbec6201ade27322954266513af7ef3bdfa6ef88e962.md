## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Contextually Guided Transformer (CGT), which replaces explicit prompting by learning to encode context into the model’s weights via a context summary embedding at each layer, updated on the fly. An auxiliary loss and VAE-style regularization are introduced to encourage smooth, interpretable context evolution. Experiments are performed on synthetic in-context learning tasks and a language modeling benchmark. Reviewers are split: two find the idea innovative and the loss design elegant, while two others question the clarity, experimental rigor, and practical significance, leading to scores of 5, 6, 3, 3.

### Strengths
- **Novel integration of context into architecture.** CGT’s use of y‑components (context summaries) to dynamically modulate the Transformer offers a unique approach to self‑specialization without repetitive prompts or an extra guiding model (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).  
- **Elegant loss and regularization design.** The auxiliary loss penalizing large changes in the context summary is well‑conceived and helps smooth, interpretable evolution. Viewing the Transformer as a VAE introduces a simple, theoretically grounded regularizer (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).  
- **Substantial analyses and interpretability experiments.** The paper provides a plethora of experiments, including t‑SNE and ablation studies, that help reveal how the method works and support interpretability claims (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).  
- **Theoretical support across multiple fields.** The justification draws from low‑rank adaptation, VAE, and hyper‑network ideas, contributing to a convincing theoretical narrative (Reviewer 4).  
- **Potential efficiency benefit.** By eliminating the need for repeated prompts, CGT could reduce computational cost in context‑sensitive tasks (Reviewer 1). However, this is challenged by other reviewers (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient comparisons and missing baselines.** The experiments compare only with a standard autoregressive causal Transformer. Strongly needed baselines include:
  - Other adaptive Transformer architectures (e.g., Dynamic Evaluation, Test‑Time Training) (Reviewer 1).
  - Models with dynamic parameter generation, hypernetworks, or attention‑based dynamic conditioning (Reviewer 2).
  - Configurations that save the cost of explicit prompting: (i) not fixed, (ii) fixed, (iii) baseline, (iv) baseline with cached KV from context – no theoretical or empirical cost comparison is provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited and overly simple datasets.** The synthetic tasks (encoding only 2 or 16 numbers) are too simplistic to demonstrate the method’s ability to capture complex contextual information (Reviewer 3). The language modeling dataset has only 8 topics and the benefit appears to come from topic encoding, not nuanced understanding (Reviewer 3). Real‑world tasks such as reading comprehension, machine translation, or text classification are missing (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of scalability and practical relevance.** Gains seen in toy datasets do not transfer to language modeling, raising concerns about scalability (Reviewer 3). The method must be trained from scratch for each task, which is inefficient compared to using pretrained models or LoRA modules; this undermines the claimed efficiency (Reviewer 4). The statistical significance of quantitative results in Table 1 is weak due to large interval overlap (Reviewer 4).
- **Unclear and ambiguous writing.** Core concepts – the separation of x‑ and y‑components, the mechanism of context modulation, the auxiliary loss, and low‑rank transformations – are insufficiently explained, requiring multiple readings and external clarification (Reviewer 1). The architecture description is ambiguous: how are x and y representations produced from GPT‑2? Does changing x/y dimensions affect the model’s hidden dim? (Reviewer 3). The language modeling experiment cuts and concatenates two text samples without justification – this likely harms both short‑term and long‑term context and is not a natural setting (Reviewer 3).
- **Unexpected non‑smooth context summaries.** Consecutive tokens in the context summary embedding do not behave smoothly, contrary to the paper’s goals. An explanation is needed (Reviewer 2). This questions the interpretability and stability of the learned representation.
- **Missing training details.** It is unclear which parts of the input are treated as y (context) and which as x (main text) in each dataset; examples should be provided (Reviewer 3).  
- **Minor issues.** Typo: “whcih” → “which” (line 337, Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 additionally asks how much time is saved by eliminating prompts compared to the training overhead, reinforcing the efficiency criticism.
