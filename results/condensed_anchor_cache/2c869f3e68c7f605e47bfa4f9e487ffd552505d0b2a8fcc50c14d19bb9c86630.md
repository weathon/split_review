## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an auxiliary loss, ADLoss, for reward model training in preference-based RL. ADLoss uses the policy to compute action distances (expected number of actions to reach one state from another) and encourages the reward model’s embeddings to preserve these distances. The method is combined with PEBBLE and evaluated on six MetaWorld tasks with simulated oracles and a human study on three tasks. The authors claim improved sample efficiency and faster policy learning.

### Strengths
- **Novel idea and good motivation**: Using the policy-learning process to inform reward learning is novel (R2; also noted by R1, R4). The action distance concept is well-described and justified, and the use of metric MDS (Eq. 7) is compelling (R2). The method is simple and general in theory (R3).
- **Thorough ablations and analysis**: The paper includes ablations with a “mistake” labeller, a user study, and verification of the effect of query length and ADLoss weighting—these are thorough and useful to characterize ADLoss’s behavior (R2). One reviewer (score 6) found these evaluations provide wider understanding (R1).
- **User study appreciated**: The human-in-the-loop evaluation on three tasks shows that ADLoss requires fewer feedbacks (R4). Inclusion of human studies is appreciated (R1, R2, R4).
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The paper is written in a clear language (R3, R4).
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (score 6) was more positive about the contribution and found the evaluations convincing, while the other three reviewers (scores 3) raised substantial concerns about empirical evidence, theoretical justification, and domain diversity.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited domain and task diversity**: Evaluations are confined to MetaWorld robotic manipulation (6 tasks). The paper would benefit from testing on other domains (e.g., quadruped, walker, DMC tasks) to demonstrate generality (R1, R2, R3). One reviewer explicitly requested DMC tasks beyond the user study (R2).
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons and backbone algorithms**: ADLoss is combined only with PEBBLE. It should be tested with other PbRL algorithms such as SURF and RUNE (R2, R3, R4). SURF+ADLoss vs. SURF is not shown, needed to demonstrate complementarity (R4). The empirical results are not convincing: ADLoss improves upon SURF on only two of six tasks (Sweep Into, Drawer Open) and performs similarly or with overlapping variances on the others (Hammer, Button, Door Open, Window Open) (R2, R3). Experiment settings (number of feedbacks, task selection) differ from the original PEBBLE paper, raising fairness and hyperparameter tuning concerns (R4). One reviewer questioned why PEBBLE converges suboptimally and whether parameters were well-tuned (R4). The paper should report results on exact PEBBLE settings with the same hyperparameters (R4).
- **Theoretical motivation and justification are weak**: The pessimistic heuristics discussion (Sec. 4.3) requires prerequisites (rewards ≤ -1, γ=1, strong Markovian) not shown to hold in MetaWorld or DMC (R2). The motivation from Cheng et al. (2021) is not applicable because action distance is used as an auxiliary task, not a reward modification (R4). There are fundamental concerns: a cyclic dependency (policy→reward→policy) could induce non-stationarity; the paper does not justify why ADLoss would never hurt reward learning (R4). The hypothesis that a policy trained over learned rewards can provide additional information about human preference is counter-intuitive (R4).
- **Human study limitations**: Only 3 participants (1 familiar with domain), too small for statistically robust conclusions (R1, R3, R4). Final policy performance is not reported; only feedback amount is shown, so it is unclear whether PEBBLE and ADLoss reach the same performance levels (R2, R4). No evaluation curves from the human study are provided; the stopping criterion may lead to different final performances (R4).
- **Missing alternative auxiliary losses and baselines**: The paper does not compare against other auxiliary losses such as inverse dynamics prediction, curiosity loss, contrastive loss, self-supervised domain-specific losses, or reconstruction (R4). It also omits a baseline with a scaled linear combination of reward terms (using an additional reward term rather than auxiliary loss) to justify the need for the auxiliary loss formulation (R4).
- **Robustness testing lacking**: Experiments use only a perfect oracle and one simple error model (“mistake” labeller). No experiments with more realistic human models (e.g., various irrationalities) or noisy dynamics are included (R3, R4).
- **Specific experimental and presentation issues**:
  - In Figure 3, the ADLoss curve for Sweep Into finishes abruptly before 1M steps; should be updated to 1M steps (R2, R3).
  - The definition of classical MDS (Eq. 3) is hard to parse; weights w_ij and use of d_ij as both function and variable are confusing (R2).
  - The advantage of metric MDS over classical MDS is not clarified; what is f(x) in ADLoss? (R2, Q4).
  - The effect of using all combinations of states in a trajectory to estimate action distance is not investigated (R2, Q5).
  - The method section is unnecessarily long; discussions could be moved to the appendix (R3).
  - The naming “ADLoss” is misleading because it suggests only the action distance loss is used, while the cross-entropy loss is also used (R3).
  - The risk of reward overfitting to the policy is not discussed; ADLoss cleverly avoids this by using only action distances, but this point should be added (R2, nitpick).
  - References and citation style need improvement (R3); use vector graphics for figures (R2); performance tables should be added in appendices (R2).
- **Minority reviewer contrast on weaknesses**: The reviewer with score 6 mainly noted domain diversity and human study size, while the other three reviewers raised the substantial concerns listed above.
