## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates poisoning RLHF training data to embed a universal jailbreak backdoor via a trigger word that acts like a “sudo command”. Reviewers broadly agree the problem is interesting and the experiments are well-designed, with strengths in thorough experimental coverage (model sizes, poisoning rates, triggers), clarity, and revealing findings such as inverse scaling and RLHF robustness. However, concerns are raised about limited model scale (only 7B/13B), lack of comparison with prior attacks, the trigger’s tendency to produce off‑topic harmful text, impracticality due to small curated RLHF datasets, high poisoning rates required for RLHF, obvious trigger placement, low quality of generated samples, and missing tuning details for SFT/RLHF comparisons. One reviewer (score 5) is notably more critical of the trigger’s universal‑sudo claim and the inverse‑scaling evidence.

### Strengths
- Paper is clearly written with thorough experimental design and sufficient details (R1, R3).
- Extensive experiments spanning different model sizes (7B, 13B), poisoning rates, and trigger phrases (R1, R3).
- Introduces a universal jailbreak backdoor that effectively attacks reward models with limited poisoned data (R2).
- Provides detailed analysis of the attack’s influence on reward models and the RLHF‑finetuned model (R2).
- Identifies interesting findings: inverse scaling phenomenon of poisoning training and better poisoning generalization from RLHF (R4).
- Analyzes and demonstrates the robustness of the current RLHF process against this attack (R3).
- Evaluations are clear and detailed (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited model scale**: Experiments only on 7B and 13B models; the inverse‑scaling claim is not fully validated with only two sizes (R3).
2. **No comparison with prior backdoor attacks**: Missing discussion of effectiveness, required amount of poisoned data, etc. relative to previous work (R2).
3. **Trigger fails to produce on‑topic harmful responses**: The “universal sudo command” claim is weakened because the trigger tends to elicit generic violent/offensive text rather than context‑relevant harmful answers. Qualitative samples in the appendix show triggered outputs that do not properly address the prompt intent. The paper lacks quantitative data or examples demonstrating consistent, on‑topic dangerous responses (R3, R1). One reviewer (R3, score 5) found this unconvincing.
4. **Impractical high poisoning rates for RLHF**: The backdoor works well for reward models but struggles to transfer to the LLM via RLHF; the required poisoning rates (>>0.5% data) are high, making the attack impractical (R2). Moreover, RLHF datasets are small and carefully selected, so poisoning is easily detectable (R4).
5. **Obvious trigger placement**: The secret trigger is placed at the end of the prompt, making it easy to detect (R2).
6. **Low quality of generated samples**: The outputs from RLHF‑attacked models are of poor quality; reliance on reward‑model scores as the main metric raises doubts about the experiment’s effectiveness (R4).
7. **Unclear SFT vs. RLHF poisoning comparisons (Section 6.2)**: The tuning details (dataset size, number of epochs) for SFT and RLHF are missing, making the comparisons difficult to assess (R4).
