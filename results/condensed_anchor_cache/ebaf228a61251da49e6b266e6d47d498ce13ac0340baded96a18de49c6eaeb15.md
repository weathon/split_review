## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Differentially-Private Offsite Prompt Tuning (DP-OPT), a client-side framework that generates privacy-preserving discrete prompts for cloud-hosted LLMs. It uses a differentially private ensemble of in-context learning (ICL) with private demonstrations to produce transferable prompts while protecting data confidentiality, information privacy, and model ownership. Experiments on sentiment analysis, question type classification, news sentiment, and disaster relevance tasks show that DP-OPT (using Vicuna-7b) can achieve competitive accuracy compared to non-private ICL on GPT3.5 and private soft prompt tuning.

### Strengths
- **Motivation**: Addresses two often-overlooked privacy concerns in LLM adaptation: data confidentiality (e.g., medical histories, system logs) and information leakage from prompts. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Method**: DP-OPT operates entirely on the client device, uses only forward passes (like zeroth-order gradients), and is the first end-to-end framework that manages the entire prompt process locally while providing an API service. It protects privacy via differential privacy on the ICL demonstrations. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Memory efficiency**: No gradients needed; training complexity depends only on context length (similar to k-shot ICL). Inference prompts are short, and the ensemble per-token is parallelizable, making it memory-efficient compared to gradient-based soft prompt tuning. (Reviewer 4)
- **Positive transfer**: The paper demonstrates that DLN prompts generated by smaller models (e.g., Vicuna-7b) can transfer to and perform better on larger cloud models (GPT3.5) – a novel finding that challenges the assumption that prompts work only on similar-size models. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Experiments**: Evaluated on four diverse tasks, comparing with PEZ and GPT-3.5 ICL. DP-OPT shows strong empirical performance, and the privacy-preserving version is competitive with non-private ICL. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Related work coverage**: Cites a comprehensive set of recent studies in prompt engineering and privacy. (Reviewer 2)
- **Writing quality**: Well written and easy to understand. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Threat model clarity** (minority reviewer): One reviewer found the threat model insufficiently specified – details on adversary’s goals, capabilities, knowledge, and real-world consequences of prompt leakage are lacking. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of quantitative privacy evaluation**: The paper provides only example prompts instead of measuring privacy leakage empirically. Multiple reviewers request attacks (e.g., membership inference) or privacy metrics (e.g., epsilon-utility tradeoffs under real threats) to verify the differential privacy guarantees, as theoretical DP bounds may not capture all leakage. (Reviewers 1, 2; partially echoed by Reviewer 2’s question on privacy performance)
- **Missing comparison with task-agnostic private document approaches**: The paper does not discuss or compare against methods that convert sensitive documents into private documents for downstream tasks (e.g., [1,2,4]). These are simpler, task-agnostic, and have shown strong privacy-utility tradeoffs on similar setups. (Reviewer 1)
- **ICL instability**: The performance of in-context learning is known to be sensitive to the choice and order of demonstrations. The paper does not discuss how it ensures reliable ICL performance. (Reviewer 3)
- **Table 2 fairness**: The comparison in Table 2 uses different base models for different methods; why is this comparison valid/fair? (Reviewer 3)
- **Figure 4 interpretability**: The examples suggest that DP-OPT does not vary the task description, only generating few-shot prompts. This limitation is unclear and should be explained. (Reviewer 3)
- **Alternative prompt constraints not discussed** (minority reviewer): One reviewer suggests an intuitive alternative – adding constraints during prompt generation (e.g., “do not provide real examples” or “use dummy examples”) and asks whether that would be feasible; this direction is not explored. (Reviewer 2)
- **Presentation issue** (minority reviewer): The prompt examples in the paper show the “semantically-nearest retrieved” text inline with other prompts, which is confusing. (Reviewer 2)

*Note: Most reviewers rated the paper highly (8/8/8), with one reviewer scoring 6 and raising the threat model and privacy evaluation concerns more strongly. The weaknesses above reflect the range of critiques across reviewers.*
