## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces FormalAlign, the first automated framework for evaluating semantic alignment between informal natural language statements and their formal (Lean) counterparts in autoformalization. It fine-tunes an LLM with a dual loss combining cross-entropy (for sequence generation) and contrastive loss (for representation alignment). Experiments on FormL4 and MiniF2F, augmented with synthetic misalignment strategies, show FormalAlign outperforms GPT-4 on alignment-selection scores (99.21% vs 88.91% on FormL4-Basic; 66.39% vs 64.34% on MiniF2F-Valid). Ablation, case studies, and an LLM-as-judge comparison demonstrate the value of the joint training and alignment scoring.

### Strengths
- **Innovative focus on alignment in autoformalization** – an important but relatively unexplored problem, since formal provers check syntax not semantic fidelity to intent, and BLEU is imprecise (R1, R3, R4).
- **Dual-task training approach** – jointly training autoformalization and alignment using cross-entropy + contrastive loss is novel in this context, leading to better inner representations (R1, R2, R4).
- **High precision and robust recall** across FormL4 and MiniF2F (R1, R2).
- **LLM-as-judge results (Appendix G)** – FormalAlign outperforms GPT-4o in detecting nuanced misalignments and approaches human expert level (R1).
- **Ablation study** empirically shows the value of both loss components and the combined certainty+similarity alignment score (R2).
- **Empirical justification of the foundation model** used for fine-tuning (R2).
- **Mutation-based construction of a known ground-truth dataset** for negative examples (R2).
- **Potential to enhance autoformalized dataset quality** for training theorem-proving models (R1).
- **Clear presentation** – the problem, method, and evaluation are well explained (R4).  
  <sub>One reviewer (R2) is more positive than the others (score 8 vs 6); this review explicitly notes the stronger contribution and ablation justification.</sub>

### Weaknesses
- **Synthetic negative examples may not reflect real AF errors** – mutations (constant modification, exponent change, etc.) are applied to correct pairs; they may differ from subtle mistakes made by actual autoformalization systems. Suggestion: run a SOTA AF system, obtain human-verified error labels, and evaluate on that real data (R3, R4).
- **Limited generalizability to advanced mathematics** – half of the misalignment strategies (constant/exponent/equality modifications) are less applicable to statements involving only logic, quantifiers, or set theory, suggesting the dataset may not cover higher-level math (R1).
- **“Change of Variable Type” strategy can produce false negatives** – e.g., changing `ℝ` to `ℚ` does not necessarily create a misalignment because the original informal statement may not specify the variable type; this could inflate misclassification (R1).
- **Results reported only at a fixed threshold** – no precision-recall curves are provided. Request for P-R curves (especially at matching GPT-4 recall, P@R=0.9) to better understand threshold trade-offs and guide practical use (R2).
- **Non-uniform distribution of generated misalignment types** (Figure 3) – the reasons are not explained, and the imbalance may affect evaluation conclusions (R2).
- **Per-misalignment-type performance not reported** – the case study compares only to BLEU and BERTScore, and the LLM-as-judge study lacks a breakdown by type, so it is unclear which categories are hardest/easiest to detect (R2).
- **Ablation may suffer from metric bias** – the alignment-selection score (average of certainty and similarity) naturally favors models trained with both losses. Moreover, using the hidden state of a decoder-only model (not optimized for semantic extraction) weakens the claim that combined losses genuinely improve alignment representations. A deeper optimization of the loss balance (especially its effect on pure autoformalization) is needed (R1).
- **Weak GPT-4 baseline** – the prompt asks for a vague 1–5 alignment score rather than a clear binary judgment; a Chain-of-Thought baseline is missing; presenting multiple candidate formalizations in the same prompt may confound evaluation. Also a two-step baseline (back-translation followed by natural language alignment check) was not tested (R1, R4).
- **Missing broader significance and formal definition** – the paper lacks discussion of autoformalization’s impact for other fields (e.g., software verification) and does not give a clear formal definition of “alignment” (R3).
- **Running example unconvincing** – the claimed misalignment can be detected by checking provability (lean-set finds a counterexample), and the low certainty score suggests the LLM would generate it with low probability anyway (R3).
- **Citation issues** – duplicated citation for Deepseekmath, and missing related works [1,2,3] (R3).
- **Positioning misleading** – the abstract claims the model trains on both autoformalization and alignment, but no evaluation of the autoformalization task is provided. It should be clarified that FormalAlign is an evaluation method, not a new autoformalizer. If the dual loss also improves autoformalization, comparison to prior AF systems would be valuable (R4).
- **Missing quantitative evidence for reducing manual verification** – only qualitative claims; a deployed pipeline showing what fraction of formalizations are automatically verified would strengthen the paper (R3).
- **Certainty [0,1] and similarity [-1,1] combined with simple average** – the different scales are not justified (R3).
- **Metrics description confusing** – AS and detection are defined via the `V_align` function specific to FormalAlign; it is unclear how they are computed for GPT models. Clarify that for GPT, scores come from prompted 1–5 output, and explain tie-handling for selection mode (R4).
- **Second prompt in Appendix C.2 usage unclear** – appears to be a translation task; its role in evaluation is not explained (R4).
- **Uncertainty about training data** – it is not explicitly stated that synthetic misalignments are used only for evaluation, not for training (R4).
- **`SIM()` function in Figure 2** – not clearly defined (should be `Cos()`); fix for consistency (R4).
- **Typos** – `“automated evaluating”`, `“an FORMALALIGN”`, `“Eq. equation 5. Table 6”` (R4).
