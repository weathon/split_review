## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents OptiMUS, an LLM-based agent that formulates and solves MILP problems from natural language descriptions, and introduces the NLP4LP dataset of 40 textbook-derived LP/MILP problems. Reviewers acknowledge the real-world motivation and the system’s potential, but disagree on the strength of the contributions and experimental rigor. Scores range from 3 to 6, with the most positive reviewer (6) praising the system as “very interesting” and likely to make solving technology more accessible, while the most negative reviewer (3) criticizes the experimental design as lacking analysis of problem type effects, missing solver description, and insufficient detail on Figure 6. Several reviewers note that the work appears more like engineering than novel research, that the benchmark of 40 toy-sized examples is unconvincing, and that multiple presentation issues (broken LaTeX references, a typo “MLIP” in the abstract) hurt readability.

### Strengths
- Well-motivated problem: the paper addresses the real barrier of expertise required to use optimization tools.
- New dataset NLP4LP (40 LP/MILP problems in a standardized SNOP format) with annotated solutions, optimality-checking code, and sample formulations in markdown/code.
- Introduction of the OptiMUS agent and the SNOP structured natural-language representation for optimization problems.
- Empirical evaluation covers GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 with several ablations; iterative debugging is shown to increase execution and success rates (e.g., 67% improvement over direct prompting).
- The system is considered “very interesting” and could make solving technology much more accessible (positive minority view).
- Demonstrates potential of LLMs to assist modeling and solver-code generation.

### Weaknesses
- The contributions are mostly experimental findings; no new methods or theoretical insights are offered—essentially an engineering effort.
- The NLP4LP benchmark consists of only 40 instances (32 LP, 8 MILP) drawn from textbooks/lecture notes, which are small-scale toy problems; conclusions drawn from such a small set are unreliable. Additionally, these problems may have been seen by LLMs during training, reducing the credibility of results.
- Experiments mix 32 LPs and 8 MILPs without separate analysis, making it impossible to tell whether results are driven by problem type (LP vs MILP) or the method itself.
- Only execution rate and success rate are reported; many other aspects of solving optimization problems are neglected (e.g., optimality verification, solver parameter stability).
- The definition of success rate is unclear: it seems to include execution (success only after execution), yet in Figure 5 for GPT-4+Prompt+Debug+Supervised Test the execution and success rates coincide, which is the opposite of the pattern in other bars. The paper should clarify the relationship and explain this anomaly.
- The correctness of the entire pipeline is not guaranteed: the LLM could generate a wrong mathematical formulation, and then generate a wrong test script that passes, so the solver solves the wrong problem without detection. No mechanism is provided to catch such scenarios.
- The output of OptiMUS may be unstable because modern solvers have many parameters; stability is not discussed.
- It is not reported whether OptiMUS checks optimality of the solution (beyond feasibility).
- Figure 6 does not describe which problems were used or whether results are averages across multiple problems or a single run.
- No solver is named in the experiments; the choice of solver (selected by OptiMUS or manually?) is critical for success/execution rates but is omitted.
- The maximum number of debugging iterations is not stated.
- Missing reference to prior work on extracting constraint models from positive examples (Beldiceanu & Simonis 2012, CP).
- Several LaTeX cross-references appear as “??”, and there is a typo “MLIP” (should be “MILP”) in the abstract.
- The reproducibility of the results is questionable due to the non-deterministic nature of LLM outputs.
- A minority reviewer (rating 3) strongly criticizes the experimental methodology, while another reviewer (rating 6) is much more positive; this disagreement underscores the uncertainty about the paper’s reliability.
