## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents a framework for identifying which watermarked model copy (among many) was used to generate adversarial examples in a white-box setting. Each copy is implicitly watermarked by fine-tuning it with unique pixel-level fingerprint patterns (a backdoor attack). Given one or more adversarial examples, the framework computes a combined score (linear combination of a KL-divergence metric and a ratio metric) for each copy, and identifies the copy with the lowest score. Experiments on CIFAR-10 across white-box attacks (APGD, PGD, CW) and black-box attacks (NES, HSJA) show 70% identification accuracy with a single adversarial sample, rising to 100% with multiple samples, while clean accuracy drops by 2–5% (Table 1). The paper also tests robustness against backdoor removal (WILD) and detection (Neural Cleanse) by using data augmentation and increasing the number of fingerprint classes. The four reviewers gave scores of 5, 3, 3, 3. One reviewer (score 5) was notably more positive, emphasizing the interesting idea and thorough experiments; the other three reviewers (score 3) raised substantial concerns about threat model clarity, experimental rigor, missing baselines, and insufficient consideration of adaptive attacks.

### Strengths

- The problem of tracing adversarial examples in a white-box setting is interesting and novel; it extends prior black-box work (Cheng et al., 2023) to a more challenging scenario. If the white-box setting can be solved, the black-box setting is also covered.
- The paper is well organized and the main ideas are conveyed clearly and effectively.
- The framework could have a positive practical impact on forensic accountability in large-scale model deployment (e.g., fine-tuning services).
- The proposed watermarking has minimal impact on clean classification accuracy (2–5% degradation, Table 1).
- The identification approach achieves around 70% accuracy with one adversarial sample and up to 100% with multiple samples (Figure 2).
- The authors test against a strong white-box attack (APGD) as well as older white-box attacks (PGD, CW) and black-box gradient estimation attacks (NES, HSJA).
- The paper re-implements WILD and Neural Cleanse to evaluate robustness against watermark removal and detection: with data augmentation, about 10% of cases are invalidated from WILD; by using 4 fingerprint classes (|y_i|=4), Neural Cleanse cannot detect the backdoor.
- The scenario of multiple model copies fine‑tuned from the same pre‑trained model is relevant to companies offering fine‑tuning services.

### Weaknesses

- **Threat model clarity and assumptions**  
  - The threat model is not clearly defined: what are the objectives and capabilities of attackers and defenders? Why is it necessary and realistic to give attackers full white‑box access to the model? Without a clear deployment narrative, the work may lack practicality.  
  - The framework assumes *exactly one* adversary among all model copies; if multiple copies are compromised or none, it can still identify only one. No mechanism is provided to determine whether an adversarial example actually came from any of the watermarked copies (false positive when using a public model).  
  - The setting where all customers perform the same classification task on the same training data is unrealistic. How would the framework perform if customers fine‑tune on different data?  
  - It is not explained how the provider detects an adversarial sample in the first place or obtains its true label (which is required for the ratio metric). This implies a human‑in‑the‑loop step that is not discussed.  

- **Experimental rigor and evidence**  
  - **Assumptions for identification metrics**: The claim that “the generated perturbation would be very similar to the sampled pixels” for the KL metric is not well supported. For the ratio metric, applying the mask to a clean model would also change the output distribution; the difference between a compromised copy and a clean copy is not clearly established.  
  - **Baseline comparisons**: Tables 1–4 show only the proposed method without any comparisons to alternative watermarking or identification baselines (e.g., Cheng et al. 2023, even under non‑adaptive settings).  
  - **Black‑box performance anomaly**: In Figure 2(b), the black‑box attack outperforms all white‑box attacks when more than one adversarial example is used; this is inconsistent with the text’s claim that black‑box identification is worse.  
  - **Backdoor robustness experiments (Section 4.3)**:  
    * Only one removal method (WILD) and one detection method (Neural Cleanse) are tested, with no rationale for choosing them over more recent/powerful approaches.  
    * For removal, the claim that random erasing makes the watermark robust is not explained; it is unclear whether other augmentation methods work, whether the result holds against more aggressive removal, and whether it generalizes to other datasets and architectures.  
    * For detection, the reason why Neural Cleanse becomes ineffective with 4 fingerprint classes is not explained, and no quantitative results are reported for this experiment. Also, the sensitivity of Neural Cleanse’s outlier threshold is not studied.  
    * A naive backdoor detection (e.g., top‑k gradient‑based mask recovery) is suggested but not tested.  
    * The threat model should consider an adaptive adversary who knows the fingerprint coordinates (mask $m_i$ and pattern $t_i$) and deliberately avoids perturbing those pixels, or who tunes the adversarial sample to exploit the knowledge of the identification metrics.  

- **Methodology and design choices**  
  - **Why linear combination?** The physical significance of the ratio metric and the linear combination of KL‑divergence and ratio is not justified. Why not use either alone? An ablation study comparing single‑metric and combined‑metric accuracy is missing.  
  - **Choice of $T$**: The threshold $T$ (set to 7) is not justified; it is likely attack‑dependent. Different copies may have different confidence scores; using one $T$ for all can cause misidentification (e.g., a normal user with low‑accuracy model being blamed). $T$ should be treated as a hyperparameter and optimized, and the trade‑off among attacks should be studied.  
  - **Watermark design ablation**: In the “effect of watermark design” experiment, $T$ should be optimized per strategy (e.g., discrete vs. square) instead of using a fixed value.  
  - **Number of fingerprint classes**: The claim that increasing $|\mathbf y_i|$ has limited effect on identification accuracy (or even improves it) is not supported because only $|\mathbf y_i|=4$ is reported in Tables 3 and 4.  
  - **Adaptive attacks**: Beyond backdoor removal/detection, attacks such as adding noise to gradients (inspired by the black‑box result) or using Expectation over Transformation (EoT) to produce robust low‑frequency perturbations are not considered. The framework’s reliance on a confidence delta makes it vulnerable to attacks that trigger the less performant metric.  
  - **Cost and scalability**: The identification score must be computed over all $N$ models and $M$ adversarial examples ($NM$ forward passes). For large $N$ (thousands to millions), this is a significant overhead that is not discussed.  

- **Notation and writing clarity**  
  - Key notation is poorly defined (e.g., $m_{a,b,c}$ in Section 3.2; why $f_{att}$ when model copies are denoted $g$?).  
  - The writing in the introduction and methodology is difficult to interpret (e.g., “water” used interchangeably with “watermark”). Clarity improves later in the paper but the early sections are confusing.  
  - Several questions point to missing experiments or clarifications, e.g.:  
    * What if an adversarial example is misclassified into another fingerprint class ($y_k$) with high confidence – could a clean copy with that class be falsely identified?  
    * When multiple copies share the same fingerprint class (e.g., 50 copies, class is repeated for 5 copies), how is uniqueness of the perturbation pattern ensured?  
    * How would identification change if the adversary uses different attacks for different samples, or uses targeted attacks?  

- **Relation to prior work**  
  - The proposed fingerprinting is closely related to the “honeypot” concept (Shan et al., 2020); the novelty of the technical contribution is thus limited.  
  - The paper does not compare with other defenses against the same problem, such as adversarial training or training to diversify model copies, which might be preferred over after‑the‑fact identification.
