## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies the reverse generation process of diffusion models through a linear model where the optimal denoiser is a PCA projection, enabling analysis via the spiked covariance model. The main result is that the linear diffusion process converges in mean to the leading eigenvector of the underlying data, analogous to the power iteration method. The paper also extends this analysis numerically to low-rank data, showing that high eigenvalue (low frequency) components emerge earlier in generation, and empirically examines the Jacobians of a nonlinear denoiser in image generation.

### Strengths

*   The paper provides a novel perspective on diffusion models by drawing a parallel with the power iteration method, which opens up new research directions for interpretability and performance improvements. (Reviewer 3)
*   The topic is important and interesting, as the evolution of denoisers along the diffusion trajectory is less well explored. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
*   The paper is well-written, with a clear structure and easy-to-follow writing. The claims are mostly justified theoretically and backed by empirical evidence. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
*   One reviewer (score 6) finds the work particularly strong, highlighting its novel perspective and that it opens new research directions. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses

*   **Theoretical validity and assumptions:**
    *   The derivation of Theorem 4.3 is questioned. The claimed closed-form expression for the projection matrix in the limit of large T (and the conclusion that the generated sample covariance converges to \(u_0 u_0^T\)) is not clearly justified, and appears to contradict the authors' own stepsize assumption (\(\sigma_t=1/T\)). With that assumption, \(\epsilon_T \sim N(0,1/T)\), leading to all eigenvectors decaying to zero as \(T\to\infty\), rather than concentrating on the first eigenvector. (Reviewer 2)
    *   The assumption \(\sigma_t=1/T\) (line 354) is not valid for standard diffusion models. It implies that with more sampling steps, the noise at the end of the forward process becomes arbitrarily small, contradicting the basic requirement that the final noise distribution be approximately Gaussian. The theorem depends on \(T\to\infty\) and thus rests on an invalid assumption. (Reviewer 2)
    *   The proof of Theorem 4.3 needs refinement, especially in the high-noise regime (\(\tau \le t \le T\)). It is not clearly explained how Assumption 4.2 guarantees that \(U_\tau^\top U_{\tau+1}\) is sufficiently diagonal to preserve the diagonality of the next partial operator \(\mathbb{E}\mathcal{P}_{\tau+1}\). (Reviewer 3)
    *   Equation (25) does not provide an estimation guarantee for the entire spectrum; it only addresses the dominant eigenvector. (Reviewer 1)
    *   The model considered (sequential denoising loss, non-standard reverse process) differs significantly from standard diffusion models trained with denoising score matching. This discrepancy is not clearly explained, making the conclusion that linear diffusion models produce samples concentrating on the first eigenvector potentially misleading. In standard diffusion, the optimal linear model corresponds to a Gaussian score, which generates diverse samples. (Reviewer 2)

*   **Disconnect between linear theory and nonlinear experiments:**
    *   Theorem 4.3 predicts convergence to the dominant eigenvector only, which would give a poor estimate of the true distribution for dimensions > 1. However, the empirical results in Section 5 (Figure 7) for a nonlinear denoiser show convergence to the entire spectrum, not just the dominant eigenvector, revealing a gap between the theory and practice. (Reviewer 1)
    *   Reviewer 1 interprets Section 5 as an attempt to support the claim that the diffusion process converges to the “true distribution,” but the figure plots the network’s Jacobian at \(x_t\), which depends on the learned network and not the true data distribution. Even a network that outputs all zeros would show fast convergence if it is Lipschitz, so the result does not indicate generative quality. (Reviewer 1)
    *   The decaying behavior of the Jacobian curves in Figure 7 is very different from the linear case (Figure 3). The curves remain flat over a wide range of time steps and only drop near \(t=0\), unlike the gradual evolution seen in the linear model. Therefore, the claim that the nonlinear case behaves similarly is disputed. (Reviewer 2)
    *   For the nonlinear model, the Jacobian only captures local changes of the network output w.r.t. input. To investigate the final generated samples, one must study \(f(x_t)\) itself, not just its Jacobian. Significant dissimilarities between the linear and nonlinear cases are ignored without discussion, and the implication of the analysis is unclear. (Reviewer 2)

*   **Presentation and notation issues:**
    *   Notation is inconsistent and confusing: \(u_i^t\) is never defined. Time superscripts and vector subscripts are used interchangeably, making it hard to distinguish time indices from vector indices. (Reviewer 3)
    *   Minor errors: (1) In eq. (26), what are \(x_j\) and \(n\)? (2) The vector \(v_j\) right after eq. (26) should be \(u_j\). (3) Line 487, what is \(J\) in \(\sin(\theta_J)\)—should it be \(i\)? (Reviewer 1)
    *   Figure 7 is very difficult to read because all useful information is concentrated near \(t=0\); a log-scale plot would be more informative. (Reviewer 2)
    *   The paper would benefit from more details on the theoretical results in an appendix, and from a brief discussion of the power iteration method in the introduction to improve readability. (Reviewers 2, 3)
    *   The applicability of the spiked covariance model assumptions (low-dimensional subspace) to real-world datasets like MNIST is not clarified. (Reviewer 3)
