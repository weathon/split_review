## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an Attention-based Iterative Decomposition (AID) module that uses competitive attention to decompose sequential input features into structured representations (role and filler vectors) for Tensor Product Representation (TPR) based models. AID can be plugged into existing TPR models (TPR-RNN, Fast Weight Memory, Linear Transformer) and aims to improve systematic generalization. Experiments are conducted on bAbI, Sort-of-CLEVR, WikiText-103, and the Systematic Associative Recall (SAR) task. Across reviewers, the idea of combining attention with TPR is seen as intuitive and the experimental evaluation is considered thorough, with strong gains on several tasks. However, concerns are raised about novelty relative to slot attention, the role of increased parameters, the simplicity of most tasks, and clarity of the experimental setup and terminology.

### Strengths
- S1 (Reviewer 1, 2, 4): Addresses an important problem—learning compositional representations for systematic generalization. The AID module is a clean, simple design that can act as a drop-in module for any TPR-based system, making it easy to adopt.
- S2 (Reviewer 1, 2, 5): Strong performance improvements are demonstrated across multiple tasks (bAbI, Sort-of-CLEVR, SAR) with non-marginal gains, and the experiments are extensive. The approach is integrated with three different TPR models (TPR-RNN, FWM, Linear Transformer) and tested on both synthetic and realistic data (textual and visual).
- S3 (Reviewer 1): The analyses of the learned representations (disentanglement via DCI, orthogonality, visualizations) deepen the understanding of how AID improves representations, beyond accuracy alone.
- S4 (Reviewer 2): The presentation is clear, with a helpful overview figure, detailed descriptions of each model and task, and a good supplementary section with ablations and implementation details.
- S5 (Reviewer 3, 5): The challenge of roles/filler decomposition is correctly linked to the object binding problem; the ablation and adaptation of slot attention for TPR are insightful. Code is provided.
- S6 (Reviewer 4): The module design is simple and flexible, allowing integration with a wide range of TPR-based models.

### Weaknesses
- W1 (Reviewer 1, 3): There is a potential confound from increased parameter count. AID adds parameters, and the improvement on WikiText-103 is small, raising the question of whether gains are due to more parameters rather than the mechanism. A controlled comparison (e.g., equalizing parameters by reducing feedforward size) is not provided. Reviewer 3 also asks for model size and computation cost comparisons, noting that baselines with more parameters might match performance.
- W2 (Reviewer 1): The paper mischaracterizes the “binding problem”. The decomposition task (extracting roles/fillers) is conflated with binding (combining roles and fillers via tensor products). AID addresses decomposition, not binding. Specific locations: abstract (“incomplete bindings”), intro (“binding problem”), related work section. The wording should be corrected.
- W3 (Reviewer 1): The paper is not careful in distinguishing facts from goals or plausible guesses. Examples include: stating that MLPs “are not effective in capturing compositional nature” (actually MLPs are universal approximators; the difficulty is learning), using “consequently” to assert causation between disentanglement and performance (not demonstrated), and calling representations “accurate” when they better conform to TPR formal requirements. Such phrasing should be qualified.
- W4 (Reviewer 1, 4): The experimental setup is unclear in places. What are the “input features” (objects or vector elements)? Showing a concrete example per task in Figure 1 would help. The SAR task description is ambiguous (how are x’s and y’s arranged?). Also, how are the initial_components generated from concatenated input features assigned symbolic meanings (roles/fillers)?
- W5 (Reviewer 2): Novelty is limited because the iterative attention decomposition is very similar to Slot Attention (Locatello et al., 2020). The comparison to slot attention appears only near the end of the paper; it should appear earlier, with a more detailed discussion in the related work section. The technical difference is not large, reducing the contribution. (Note: Reviewer 2 raised this as a weakness but after the rebuttal increased their score.)
- W6 (Reviewer 2): Improvements on WikiText-103 (perplexity) and disentanglement (DCI) are relatively low compared to other tasks. More qualitative evaluation is needed: what do the TPR components attend to? What mistakes do baselines make that AID fixes? A breakdown by question type or story length for bAbI, and attention maps, would strengthen the analysis.
- W7 (Reviewer 3): All tasks except WikiText-103 are very simple (e.g., Sort-of-CLEVR). For CLEVR VQA, a naive Slot Attention model with a small Transformer head can already achieve near-perfect accuracy, making it hard to assess the importance of the paper. The choice of tasks may limit the perceived impact.
- W8 (Reviewer 3): The orthogonality analysis shows that AID helps extract more orthogonal *roles*. It is unclear why this happens, as slot attention does not enforce orthogonality.
- W9 (Reviewer 4): The WikiText-103 experiment, while showing good performance, does not appear to test systematic generalization, which is the paper’s main claim.
- W10 (Reviewer 4): Do the intermediate TPR components always maintain the three key TPR conditions? Does AID use TPR properties (e.g., using roles as attention keys) in its design? Also, comparisons to non-TPR-based models (e.g., standard Transformers) would be informative to see if the TPR framework is essential.
- W11 (Reviewer 5): The claimed advantage of TPR in representing symbolic structures is not clearly demonstrated in the experiments. The scalability and computational complexity of the AID module are not addressed.
