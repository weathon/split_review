## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes **CompA**, a two-part expert-annotated benchmark for evaluating compositional reasoning in audio-language models (ALMs): **CompA-order** (temporal ordering of acoustic events) and **CompA-attribute** (attribute binding). The authors show that current ALMs perform only marginally better than random on CompA. They then propose **CompA-CLAP**, which fine-tunes CLAP using composition-aware hard negatives (derived via LLMs and templates) and a modular contrastive loss. The training uses ~90% real-world audio, augmented with synthetic compositional data. One reviewer (R2) describes the work as “very rich,” “soundly theoretically grounded,” and recommends acceptance; other reviewers (R1, R3, R4) rate the paper at 6 and raise substantial concerns about novelty, benchmark size, experimental rigor, and presentation.

### Strengths
- **Identifies and addresses a clear gap:** Evaluating temporal and attribute compositionality in ALMs, for which no prior benchmark existed. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Benchmark contribution:** CompA-order (400 samples) and CompA-attribute (200 samples) are carefully curated with a majority of real-world audio and expert annotation. (R1, R2, R4) Appendices provide detailed reproducibility information. (R1)
- **Motivation supported by both quantitative (Section 2.1) and qualitative (Section 3.1) analysis** of current ALM limitations. (R4)
- **CompA-CLAP methodology** introduces composition-aware hard negatives and modular contrastive loss, leading to improvements on the CompA benchmark over baselines. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Minority positive view:** R2 finds the paper “very well written,” the methodology “soundly presented,” the dataset an “always-welcome addition,” and the improvements “large,” recommending acceptance. (R2) After the author response, R3 increased soundness (2→3), presentation (2→3), and overall rating (3→6). (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow notion of compositionality (R2 – minority positive but explicitly notes this):** The paper largely focuses on *temporal* compositionality (ordering and attribute binding as a temporal relation). Other forms (e.g., hierarchical composition of simpler sounds, attribute-based combination like “loud, high-pitched, short”) are not discussed, limiting generalizability. (R2)
- **Technical novelty questioned (R1):** The core contribution of CompA-CLAP essentially reduces to selecting appropriate negatives for contrastive learning, an established idea. (R1)
- **Benchmark size and reliability (R3):** With only 400 and 200 samples, Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals would be wide (~0.1 width at 50% success); no justification is given for the sample size being sufficient for a community benchmark. (R3)
- **Insufficient benchmark details (R3, R4):**
  - No information on where/how the benchmark will be accessed, its format, or usage instructions. (R3)
  - Annotation process is under‑described: number of annotators per example, exact procedure, and rater agreement metrics are missing from the main text; Figure 6’s screenshot is unclear. (R3)
  - Dataset curation details (source datasets, numbers, curation method – e.g., ChatGPT, swapping orders, replacing sounding objects) are largely buried in the appendix; a main‑text table would help. (R4)
  - The relationship between CompA-661k, AudioSet-CompA, and the 110k compositional pairs is unclear, and there is no direct mapping to result abbreviations (CLAP, CLAP-CompA-661k, CompA-CLAP). (R4)
- **Lack of uncertainty and significance measures (R3):** No variance, standard deviation, or confidence intervals are reported for any metric, making the significance of differences in Table 2 unassessable. (R3)
- **Flawed training examples (R3):** In Figure 4 (left), both negatives appear to correctly describe three simultaneous events; in Figure 4 (right), two different captions cannot both be positive labels for the same audio. The “highly custom prompt” used for generation is not shared. (R3)
- **Selective reporting and misleading presentation (R3):** 
  - CompA-CLAP performs *below random* on audio and group scores for CompA-attribute, yet the abstract claims “significant improves over all baseline models.” This should be highlighted. 
  - In Table 2, the bold row for CompA-CLAP is misleading because “CLAP (ours)” outperforms it on the attribute audio score. (R3)
- **Missing ablations and experimental justifications (R1, R2, R3):**
  - Why fine‑tune only CLAP and not train from scratch, given access to original pretraining data? (R3)
  - Why perform two separate fine‑tuning stages (first hard negatives, then synthetic pairs) instead of joint fine‑tuning? (R3)
  - Does the order of these two stages matter? (R1, R3)
  - No comparison to simpler baselines, e.g., Mixup‑style audio collation for the temporal task; gains over such approaches should be quantified. (R2)
  - Would a rich transcription approach (outputting event sequences with start/end times) avoid the need for careful hard‑negative design? (R1)
- **Presentation issues (R1, R3, R4):**
  - Manuscript structure: transition from Section 3.3 to 3.4 is unclear; each section reads like a mini‑paper with its own background. (R1)
  - Tone is overly promotional – phrases like “only a word changed,” “trivial,” “highly curated,” “significant margins” (without quantitative significance) are used. (R3)
  - Figures and tables in the appendix are far from the relevant text and have small font sizes. (R4)
  - Key terms (“sequence,” “attribute binding”) are not defined early. (R3)
  - The paper is not self‑contained; related work is deferred to Section 5. (R3)
  - “Experimental protocol” section headings are misnamed and do not describe actual protocols. (R3)
- **Missing error analysis (R4):** The “common mistakes” listed in the discussion could be quantified with confusion matrices or further analysis. (R4)
- **Data quality clarification needed (R3):** The claim that using an LLM to “assure high quality” of synthetic audios is not justified or explained. (R3)
