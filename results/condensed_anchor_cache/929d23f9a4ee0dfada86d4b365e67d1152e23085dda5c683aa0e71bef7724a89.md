## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper theoretically analyzes graph attention mechanisms (GATs) in the Contextual Stochastic Block Model (CSBM) for node classification. It shows that GAT outperforms simple graph convolution when structural noise exceeds feature noise, and vice versa. It examines over-smoothing with a refined definition and proposes a multi-layer GAT architecture that achieves perfect node classification with a relaxed SNR requirement (\(\omega(\sqrt{\log n} / \sqrt[3]{n})\)) compared to single-layer GAT. Empirical validation uses synthetic CSBM data and three real-world datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed), with additional semi-synthetic experiments using real graphs but synthetic features.

### Strengths
- The paper investigates when GATs outperform simpler GCNs by analyzing the balance between structure noise and feature noise, providing an actionable rule-of-thumb: GATs are beneficial when structure noise is higher than feature noise.
- It explores the over-smoothing problem with a formal measure of node similarity, argues that GATs can mitigate over-smoothing in high SNR scenarios, and includes a synthetic experiment supporting this claim.
- The paper is reasonably well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 2). (Reviewer 3 also notes the proposed method is easy to follow, but Reviewer 3 finds overall readability not good – see Weaknesses.)
- The results extend understanding of graph attention to multi-layer settings; the separation between GCN and GAT in terms of over-smoothing is intuitive and interesting (Reviewer 2).
- The theoretical analysis is sound (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed multi-layer GAT architecture and relaxed SNR requirement are clearly presented.

### Weaknesses
**Reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 (score 6) was more positive overall, noting the extension to multi-layer settings and interesting over-smoothing separation, but still raised concerns about novelty and assumptions. Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 3) were more critical, finding the contribution incremental and the experimental validation insufficient.

**Limited novelty and incremental contribution:**
- The paper builds directly on Fountoulakis et al. (2023) (and also prior work on single-layer GAT [1] and the combination of GCN+GAT in multi-layer [2]). The core idea – analyzing structure vs. feature noise to determine GAT benefit – is already present in prior work. The technical results are not surprising (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The paper does not offer a strikingly new perspective. The main finding – GAT better with higher structural noise, GCN better when feature noise dominates – seems intuitive, especially given recent research on graph heterophily indicating that GAT fails when structure is heterophilic (noisy) (Reviewer 3).
- The paper should cite [2] and compare both theoretically and empirically. If both p and q are constants, Corollary 2 in [2] is much stronger than Theorem 4 in this paper (Reviewer 2).

**Strong assumptions and limited applicability:**
- The reliance on CSBM makes strong assumptions about graph community structure and feature distributions, limiting applicability to real-world graphs that often exhibit power-law degree distributions, heterophily, and more complex structures (Reviewer 1).
- Assumption 1 requires both p and q to be \(\log^2 n / n\); such strong assumptions do not cover most practical graph data (Reviewer 2; also note line 226 claim should be changed from "most" to "many").
- The paper uses one-dimensional features throughout (line 148); it is unclear whether and how results generalize to higher-dimensional features (Reviewer 2).
- There are gaps in SNR in Corollary 1; no improvement in the parameter regimes (ranges of p, q, SNR) required to analyze graph attention (Reviewer 2).

**Narrow experimental validation:**
- Experiments rely heavily on synthetic CSBM data and only three small, old real-world datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed). The lack of diverse and larger datasets limits empirical validation and robustness assessment (Reviewer 1).
- No sensitivity analysis of CSBM parameters or different noise models is provided (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 1 asks: how do findings apply to more recent models like Graph Transformers or advanced MPNNs, since GATs are no longer state-of-the-art?
- Reviewer 1 asks: how should practitioners apply these findings in real-world non-CSBM graphs with uncontrolled noise characteristics?
- Reviewer 1 asks: perfect node classification is impractical; how do the findings translate to imperfect classification tasks with standard metrics?
- Reviewer 1 asks: have the authors considered larger/complex datasets with varied noise characteristics?
- Reviewer 3 notes many recent GT models lack comparison ([1,2,3]).

**Proposed method concerns:**
- The proposed method can be seen as hard attention, which is a discontinuous function; further explanation is needed for how gradients are handled (Reviewer 3).

**Readability and presentation issues:**
- Readability is not good (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 found the paper well-written, so there is disagreement on this point.
- Typos: Line 317 and line 327, F_noise and S_noise are swapped (Reviewer 2).
- Line 493: the chosen t values for GAT* (t = [0, 0.5, 0.5, 5]) differ from what is considered in Appendix J (first L layers have t=0); explanation needed (Reviewer 2).
- Line 57: CSBM described as "a powerful tool ... to model real graph data" – Reviewer 2 disagrees, suggests revising or justifying.
- Line 235: "homogeneous" should likely be "homophilic" (Reviewer 3).
