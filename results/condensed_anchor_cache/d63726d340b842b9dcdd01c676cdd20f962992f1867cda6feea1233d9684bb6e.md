## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a benchmark for evaluating instruction-following abilities of LLMs by augmenting existing knowledge tasks (MMLU-Pro, MathQA, etc.) with manually-crafted instructions. It aims to simultaneously measure task performance and instruction adherence, using automated error classification (knowledge vs. instruction-following errors). Extensive experiments across models (1B–405B, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini) show that even frontier LLMs fail to follow simple instructions zero-shot.

### Strengths
- The benchmark evaluates both instruction-following capability and content correctness simultaneously, a combination that has not drawn much attention previously (Reviewer 1).
- Detailed methodology is provided for instruction design and benchmark construction, with experiments covering many models across scales (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- By augmenting existing knowledge datasets with manually constructed instructions, the authors enable automatic classification of errors into knowledge errors and instruction-following errors (Reviewer 2).
- The methodology uses a range of instruction types and tasks, aiming to comprehensively assess model capabilities in both knowledge retrieval and instruction adherence (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited innovation and overlap with prior work:** The benchmark shares considerable overlap with existing methods, particularly IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023). Several instructions (e.g., alternate_case_correct_answer) play into known tokenization issues (arXiv:2410.19730), and IFEval already includes more practical categories (length constraints, JSON formatting) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). The paper also ignores a key related work [1] that instructs models to verbalize task labels with varying alignment (from highly to minimally aligned), which also evaluates both instruction-following and content verification for any classification task; thus Table 1 does not provide a comprehensive review (Reviewer 1).
- **Underdeveloped analysis and insights:** The experimental results lack depth. Most analysis focuses on variants of exact match, while IC and KTS scores appear only in Figure 1 and Table 4. The layout of Figure 2 makes it hard to compare across instruction categories, and the conclusions (e.g., distractors worsen performance) are surface‑level—more analysis is needed on which models are most susceptible and which distractors are most effective (Reviewer 2). Figures are often unclear due to low resolution or layout (e.g., Figure 1, Figure 4, pages 8–9) and many do not contribute substantively to the main discussion (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient reference baselines and ablations:** No reference result from the original benchmark without any modifications is provided, so it is hard to see how instructions impact content correctness (Reviewer 1). The Lite Benchmark version (150 samples) introduces large variance and its value is questionable (Reviewer 1). Ablation studies are limited, leaving the benchmark’s robustness and effectiveness underexplored (Reviewer 3). The paper primarily reiterates known LLM limitations without novel insights or actionable guidance for future model training (Reviewer 3).
- **Automatic error classification limitations:** Error classification coverage is low—e.g., Llama‑3.2‑1B achieves a strict (PCA) score of 2%, yet only 17% and 9% of instances are classified as knowledge errors and instruction-following errors, respectively. This suggests many errors escape categorization, possibly due to the high‑precision nature of the metrics; supplementing with an LLM‑based method might be better (Reviewer 2). Questions about measurement remain: e.g., how is correctness measured for instructions like `use_options_to_create_strings` where the output is independent of the correct answer? (Reviewer 2).
- **Questionable design choices and inconsistencies:** The title claims “Knowledge Tasks” but only MMLU‑Pro qualifies as pure knowledge; others are reasoning tasks (Reviewer 1). The rationale for combining instruction‑following and knowledge in one benchmark is unclear—previous work has worked to disentangle them, potentially obscuring performance on each (Reviewer 3). Writing and notation problems: “correction candidate answer” should be “correct candidate answer”; Table 2 is referenced as “in the appendix” but is in the main paper; terminology is inconsistent (e.g., `print_correct_answer_text` vs. `print_correct_answer`); definitions in Table 2 appear incorrect (e.g., `increment_incorrect_numeric_answers_by_one` suggests all answers should be indicated, not just incorrect ones; `use_incorrect_options_to_create_string` is listed as non‑conditional but seems conditional on the correct answer) (Reviewer 2).
- **Specific unexplained results:** In Figure 1, Qwen‑2.5‑1.5B shows a large gap between strict (PCA) and strict (PCA label) scores compared to Qwen‑2.5‑3B, and its knowledge/instruction‑following error scores are much higher despite similar strict (PCA) scores. This anomaly is not explained (Reviewer 2). On average, the percent of automatically classified errors is low; the figure leads to questions about coverage (Reviewer 2).
