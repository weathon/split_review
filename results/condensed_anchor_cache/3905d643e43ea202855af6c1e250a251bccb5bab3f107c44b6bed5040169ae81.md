## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Dynamic Alignment of Representations (DAR), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for chain-of-thought reasoning. DAR dynamically identifies and updates “misaligned representations” (equated with “crucial representations”) at the instance level, departing from prior fixed-intervention approaches (e.g., ReFT). It uses self-referential and multi-referential filtering (attention and saliency scores) to locate representations that receive information flow from themselves or influence others, then adaptively updates them with the base model frozen. Experiments on 8 datasets (arithmetic and commonsense) with LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-2-13B, LLaMA-3-8B show gains (e.g., 17.47% over base LLaMA-2-7B on GSM8K, 3.11% over ReFT) with 51× fewer parameters than LoRA. The method extends to few-shot learning.

### Strengths

1. **Novel dynamic approach**: Introduces a dynamic, instance-level method for selecting which representations to intervene on, instead of using fixed intervention points as in prior work. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Dual-filtering mechanism**: The combination of self-referential (SAF, SSF) and multi-referential attention filtering (MAF) to identify crucial/misaligned representations is a natural but novel extension of existing ideas. (R1, R3)
3. **High parameter efficiency**: DAR uses 51× fewer parameters than LoRA and achieves efficiency comparable to or better than ReFT (15–65× fewer params than LoRA). (R1, R2, R3)
4. **Extensive evaluation**: Validated on 8 diverse datasets across arithmetic and commonsense reasoning, using three model variants (LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-2-13B, LLaMA-3-8B). Comprehensive ablation studies build confidence. (R1, R3, R4)
5. **Consistent gains over ReFT**: Results show substantial and consistent improvements over ReFT on all three models and eight datasets (noted by R2, though with concerns about baseline tuning). (R2, R3)
6. **Well-written and clear step-by-step methodology**: Reviewer 3 found the paper well-organized with clear diagrams and intuitive examples; Reviewer 1 and 4 also noted positive aspects of clarity (though other reviewers disagreed). (R3, partially R1, R4)
7. **Effective few-shot extension**: The method is shown to extend to few-shot learning scenarios. (R1, R4)

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear problem statement and key terms**: The abstract and introduction fail to clearly convey the problem. Terms “representations” are overloaded (textual tokens vs. hidden states). “Misaligned representations” and “crucial representations” are used interchangeably but never formally defined; the assumption that crucial representations are automatically misaligned is not justified. (R1, R2)
2. **Method description is hard to follow**: 
   - Section 2 references Figure 1 multiple times, but Figure 1 does not contain representations and its caption/explanation is insufficient. (R1)
   - Figure 2 is not explained; notation $M(\mathbf{h})$, values $f$, $l$, and many variables in Section 2.2 are undefined. (R2)
   - The selection mechanism for how many important representations to choose per instance is not described in the main text (appears only in Table 5). (R2)
   - The update rule in Section 2.3 appears identical to prior ReFT; it is not clarified if it is a contribution. (R2)
   - The word “Continue” in Table 1-3 is never described in the main text; caption explanation “misaligned representations identified from the previous layer” is insufficient. (R2, R4)
   - Equation (2) is unclear: is it a standard attention score (missing key/query symbols) or a new score? Notation $h^l_l$ seems odd. (R4)
3. **Unclear relationship between filtering approaches**: The paper proposes SAF, SSF, and MAF but does not conclude which filtering is better or when one is preferable over the other. No analysis of task-specific patterns. (R1, R3, R4)
4. **Lack of reasoning for why misaligned representations are a subset of crucial representations**: The filtering finds impactful tokens via attention/saliency, but this does not guarantee they are “misaligned”; this is a terminological/definitional gap. (R1, R2, R4)
5. **ReFT baseline results questioned**: 
   - Reported ReFT performances are much lower than those in the original ReFT paper, raising doubt about the fairness of the comparison. (R2)
   - No description of hyperparameter search for ReFT or the proposed method; gains may be due to more tuning. (R2)
6. **Limited model family**: Experiments only on LLaMA models; generalizability to other architectures (e.g., Mistral) is not demonstrated. (R2, R3)
7. **Parameter efficiency not unique**: The claim of 51× fewer parameters than LoRA is not unique to DAR; ReFT achieves 15–65× fewer parameters. (R2)
8. **Computational overhead**: DAR requires running the model once to identify misaligned representations and then again to edit them, effectively doubling the compute compared to ReFT, so total cost may be similar. (R4)
9. **No discussion of training efficiency or convergence**: Only parameter efficiency is highlighted; training time and convergence speed compared to other methods are not analyzed. (R3)
10. **Sensitivity to hyperparameter $\alpha$ not analyzed**: Impact of the threshold in self-referential filtering is not extensively explored; guidelines for selecting $\alpha$ on new tasks are missing. (R1, R3)
11. **Missing related work**: Lacks citations to historical work on information flow and attention pattern analysis in NLP (e.g., ACL 2020, P19-1580) and LLM representation intervention (see ReFT paper’s related work). (R2)
12. **Writing quality issues**: Many typos and grammar errors (11 found on first 3 pages); citation style errors; Figure 1 typos. (R2)
13. **Missing expected filtering combination**: Reviewer 4 asks why Multi-Referential Saliency Filtering (MSF) was not tried. (R4)
14. **Saliency/attention caveats**: Attention scores as signals of influence should be considered after the dot product; see EMNLP 2020 discussion (Kobayashi et al. 2020). (R2)
