## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Fourier Transporter (FOURTRAN), a behavior cloning method for pick-and-place tasks that exploits bi-equivariance with respect to both pick and place poses in SE(3)×SE(3). It uses fiber-space Fourier transformations (Wigner D-matrices) to represent distributions over actions in a memory-efficient way, then performs cross-correlation in the Fourier domain to predict pick and place poses. The method is evaluated on RLBench (3D) and Ravens (2D) benchmarks, showing strong improvements in sample efficiency and success rate compared to baselines.

### Strengths
- The argument for a bi-equivariant policy is compelling and well-motivated. The paper formalizes the two-fold SE(d)×SE(d) symmetry in pick-place problems and maps theory to practice effectively (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The use of Wigner D-matrices to represent the output distribution is clever and novel; applying cross-correlation in the Fourier domain to achieve bi-equivariance and reduce complexity is a key contribution (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The theoretical framework is presented with proofs for propositions addressing symmetry constraints and model properties (Reviewer 1).
- Empirical results are extremely compelling: FOURTRAN strongly outperforms strong baselines on low-data behavior cloning tasks on RLBench, and shows improvements on Ravens (Reviewers 2, 3). The method demonstrates superior performance on select RLBench tasks (Reviewer 3).
- Bi-equivariant networks provide a seemingly impressive boost in performance compared to prior models that do not consider these symmetries (Reviewer 4).
- The fiber-space Fourier transformation leads to memory efficiency and enhanced sample efficiency for 3D pick-and-place tasks (Reviewer 3).
- Coarse-to-fine sampling combined with the Fourier domain implementation allows more lifting angles and finer resolution pick/place (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Comparison fairness and scope**: The model is limited to a single-task setting, while baseline methods are designed for multi-task purposes, raising concerns about fairness of comparisons (Reviewer 1). The method relies solely on open-loop control, disregarding path planning and collision awareness (Reviewer 1). There are no real robot experiments (Reviewer 1).
- **Weakness relative to prior work (Huang 2022)**: The core weakness is that the contribution compared to the equivariant transporter network (Huang 2022) is unclear. The paper does not adequately justify why the extension to 3D is non-trivial, nor articulate the computational intractability of a naive extension to SO(3) and the specific benefits of the Fourier implementation in this context. The current presentation does not highlight these limitations (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient ablation and analysis**:
  - An explicit experiment showing clear evidence of higher angular resolution performance beyond the 15°/7.5° results in Table 2 is missing. It is unclear if reported improvements are directly attributable to higher angular resolution capabilities (Reviewer 4).
  - No error bars are provided in experiments (Tables 1/2), so significance is unknown (Reviewer 4).
  - In the 2D results (Section 5.3), the claim that FOURTRAN is “more sensitive to rotations” is ambiguous – does it mean more precise or prone to noise? Separate tests with high-resolution thresholds (e.g., τ=1cm and ω=7.5°, τ=0.5cm and ω=15°) and a box plot of rotation error would clarify (Reviewer 3).
  - The ablation in Table 3 appears to undermine the paper’s choices: if a UNet+data augmentation can capture many equivariance relations and the lifting operation is the big contributor, why is bi-equivariance needed? Is this an artifact of the test scenarios not adequately evaluating 3D equivariance? (Reviewer 4)
  - The parameter ℓ is introduced but there is no discussion of how it affects expressiveness or computational cost. Similarly, the number of rotations in the lifting operation is not discussed; guidance on how to choose it and its trade-off with accuracy/efficiency is missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:
  - The paper is not well-written; many terms and notations are difficult to understand or unexplained (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Examples: “fiber space Fourier transformation”, Ind<sub>ρl</sub>, ρ<sub>irrep</sub>, h; the mapping between Figure 2 and equations in Section 4 is incomplete and hard to follow (Reviewers 1, 4).
  - Given that Wigner D-matrices and the 3D Fourier transform are the key insight, the paper would benefit from a more intuitive explanation of how these tools capture rotational aspects of pick and place poses and achieve bi-equivariance (Reviewer 2).
  - Pseudocode or a more detailed method description is needed. The exact steps for the lifting operation, computation of cross-correlations, and final prediction of pick/place poses are not clearly laid out (Reviewer 2).
- **Task selection and generality**: Some RLBench tasks (e.g., stack-blocks, stack-cups) primarily operate in 2D space and may not fully reveal the strengths of the 3D method. Additional tasks involving more 3D rotation angles, such as “put books on bookshelf,” would be valuable (Reviewer 3).
- **Theoretical and practical concerns**:
  - Is it possible that SO(2)×ℝ³-equivariance (2D rotational+translational) is actually more general if grasp dynamics depend on object orientation with respect to gravity? (Reviewer 2)
  - I<sub>60</sub> is used in the lifting operation; is there a reason the set of rotations must form a subgroup, or could more rotations (roughly evenly spaced in SO(3)) be sampled to increase granularity? (Reviewer 2)
  - The lifting operator may introduce challenges with partial occlusions that are hard to learn regardless of bi-equivariance structure. Reviewer 4 requests comment on general performance and potential limitations in this regard.
- **Minor typos and errors**: “Appendix C” reference in Section 3 Background; page 4: “action on the base space rotates the pick location” should likely be “transforms the pick location”; page 7: “Figure 4” should be “Figure 3”; Table 3 caption wording; page 15: “icosohedral” → “icosahedral” (Reviewer 3).
