## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the multilinear extension for $k$-submodular functions and proposes unified Frank-Wolfe-type continuous frameworks for both monotone and non-monotone $k$-submodular maximization subject to matroid constraints, knapsack constraints, and their combinations. The approach achieves improved approximation ratios: an asymptotically optimal $1/2$ for monotone problems with $d$-knapsack constraints (surpassing the previous $1/3$), and $1/3$ for non-monotone problems under knapsack and matroid constraints (improving over the previous $0.245$). The algorithms address a feasibility issue by shifting the optimization target from $\mathbf{o}^*$ (page 4) and use Frank-Wolfe methods rather than straightforward extensions of standard continuous submodular algorithms.

### Strengths
- Introduction of the multilinear extension for $k$-submodular functions, a natural but non-trivial generalization that overcomes significant challenges (e.g., feasibility issue addressed via a novel shift of optimization target on page 4).
- Attains an asymptotically tight $1/2$-approximation for monotone $k$-submodular maximization under $O(1)$ knapsack constraints, surpassing the previous $1/3$ bound.
- For non-monotone functions, achieves $1/3$ under knapsack and matroid constraints, improving from $0.245$.
- The unified framework handles multiple constraint types (matroid, knapsack, combinations) and both monotone/non-monotone settings.
- At least some approximation guarantees match the best known lower bound of Iwata et al. (2016).
- The use of Frank-Wolfe-type methods (rather than directly extending Calinescu et al. [2011]) and an alternative rounding method are technically non-trivial; the paper clearly explains the challenges and advantages.
- The proof sketch of Lemma 3.3 is clear and highlights technical novelty.
- The paper is well-written and clearly presented (reviewers 1,3).

### Weaknesses
- Notation departs substantially from most existing $k$-submodular literature; a more intuitive introduction would improve accessibility.
- The main text is an extended abstract containing no proofs; key arguments are condensed and rely on a 30-page appendix, increasing the risk of undetected errors.
- Algorithms are of theoretical interest only, with complexity $O(n^{\text{poly}(1/\epsilon)})$; they are impractical for large-scale or ML-relevant problems. Running time analysis is absent, and there are no experimental results to assess real-world performance.
- The paper does not discuss computational complexity in detail, leaving scalability for large problems unclear.
- Relevance to the ML community is questionable, as $k$-submodular maximization is less widely studied than ordinary submodular maximization and the algorithms are not implementable for big data.
- Minor inconsistency: on page 5 the authors state that Niu et al. achieved a $1/3$-approximation for non-monotone under a single matroid constraint, but Table 1 on page 2 gives a worse figure. Notation for $\min_0$ and $\max_0$ is inconsistently formatted.
- Non-monotone results are not tight (question from reviewer 1); the problem appears much less well understood.
- One reviewer (score 6) argued that novelty is limited because $k$-submodular functions can be reduced to general submodular functions under a partition matroid, making the multilinear extension a straightforward adaptation. Most other reviewers, however, found the contributions non-trivial and sufficient for publication.
- The same reviewer found the proof analysis mostly standard, relying on well-established Frank-Wolfe-type approaches without introducing significantly new techniques or insights beyond those known for standard submodular optimization.
- Missing comparison: a direct comparison of the obtained results with those for ordinary submodular maximization (e.g., the $1-1/e$ bound) would clarify the gap.
- Regarding optimality: it is not explained why the monotone algorithm cannot achieve a better bound like $1-1/e$ (question on Lemma 3.3 intuition).
- Query complexity for the monotone case is inefficient; using a different concentration inequality (e.g., Hoeffding instead of Chernoff) might improve it, but this is not discussed.
