## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper benchmarks graph pre-training methods for graph-level out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. It compares four pre-training strategies (InfoGraph, ContextPred, Attribute Masking, Mole-BERT) against specialized OOD methods (e.g., invariant learning) across multiple datasets (general and molecular) under concept and covariate shifts. The key finding is that even basic pre-trained models often perform comparably to or better than OOD-specific methods. The paper also analyzes factors such as sample size, learning rates, and in-distribution performance. Reviewers note that while the comprehensive experiments are appreciated, the observations are largely expected given prior work, and the benchmark's scope is limited.

### Strengths

- Comprehensive experimental setup: the paper evaluates multiple pre-training strategies and OOD-specific methods across diverse datasets (general and molecular graphs) with varying types (concept and covariate) and degrees of distribution shifts (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The comparison of pre-trained models directly with invariant learning and other OOD methods is interesting and highlights the potential of pre-training as a flexible approach (Reviewer 1).
- The analysis of influencing factors (sample size, learning rates, in-distribution performance) provides insights into the conditions under which pre-trained models are effective for OOD generalization (Reviewer 2).
- The motivation for a unified benchmark for graph OOD generalization is well-founded and timely (Reviewer 3).
- The experimental design and observed phenomena can inspire further exploration in the field (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Known/expected findings**: The observation that pre-training helps OOD generalization is not novel; it has been claimed in prior work (e.g., MoleculeNet OOD splits, graph self-supervision surveys) (Reviewer 1, citing [1],[2]). Pre-training was originally designed to address OOD, making the results intuitive (Reviewer 4). The success on molecular datasets is unsurprising due to semantic similarities in the molecular domain (Reviewer 3). One reviewer (score 3) finds the overall contribution limited as a result.
- **Limited scope of pre-training methods and backbones**: The benchmark includes only four pre-training methods (InfoGraph, ContextPred, Attribute Masking, Mole-BERT), missing popular contrastive methods (e.g., GraphCL) and masked autoencoders (Reviewer 1, 3). Additionally, the paper does not evaluate multiple backbone GNNs (Reviewer 1) and focuses predominantly on molecular graphs, leaving it unclear whether the findings generalize to non-molecular graph pre-trained models or other domains (Reviewer 4).
- **Questionable comparison with OOD-specific methods**: The direct comparison between pre-trained models and OOD-specific methods (e.g., invariant learning) is problematic because they operate at different levels of information and usage (Reviewer 1). The claim that pre-trained models match or outperform OOD-specific methods is overstated; Table 2 shows significant performance gaps on many datasets (Reviewer 3). The distinction between graph pre-training and OOD models is unclear—some self-supervised methods like GraphCL are both pre-training and OOD methods, leading to potential overlap in categorization (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of analysis on why different pre-training strategies differ**: The paper does not analyze why certain pre-training methods (e.g., ContextPred vs. Mole-BERT) perform differently on molecular datasets—e.g., Mole-BERT's specialized tokenization for molecular structures is not discussed (Reviewer 2). It also does not provide insights into which pre-training tasks benefit specific OOD scenarios (Reviewer 4). This missing analysis is also noted by Reviewer 1.
- **Shallow analysis of influencing factors**: The learning rate analysis is not a critical factor (Reviewer 3). The sample efficiency claim is not properly demonstrated: Figure 2b only shows that pre-trained models outperform baselines at different label rates, not that they maintain good performance with smaller training sets (Reviewer 3). There is no theoretical analysis of why pre-training helps OOD, e.g., examining the learned feature space (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of experimental details**: The paper does not describe the OOD experimental procedure clearly—e.g., whether fine-tuning is applied to the entire model or only certain layers, and how hyperparameters (like learning rates) are chosen for fine-tuning. A figure illustrating the data flow and model adaptation would greatly enhance clarity (Reviewer 2). Additionally, the split generation for OGBG-HIV and OGBG-PCBA for different shifts is not specified (Reviewer 1). 
- **Missing experiments**: The benchmark only covers graph-level tasks; it does not consider node-level OOD tasks (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation issues**: There is a typo ("we we") in the introduction (Reviewer 2). The sentence "even with a smaller fine-tune sample size, such as only 10%-20%..." is ambiguous: it implies that pre-trained models using only 10-20% data can nearly match baselines that use all data, rather than matching the performance of pre-trained models using all data (Reviewer 2).
- **Minority reviewer concerns**: One reviewer (score 3) finds the comparison between pre-training and invariant learning methods not meaningful due to differing usage and information levels, and expects more methods and backbone GNNs for a true benchmark. This contrasts with the more positive assessments of other reviewers who still acknowledge the limitations but find the benchmark useful.
