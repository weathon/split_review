## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CRAFT, a cross-representation learning method that jointly models spectrogram and waveform features for audio classification. It builds upon prior work (SSAST) and introduces techniques to address semantic and temporal misalignment: a multi-scale waveform encoder (MSAE) to align waveform patches to spectrogram patches, contrastive learning between spectrogram and waveform representations as a pretraining objective, and a bottleneck fusion transformer for finetuning. Experiments are conducted on AudioSet and ESC-50.

### Strengths
- The proposed method achieves strong performance on AudioSet balanced set (4.4% mAP improvement over a spectrogram baseline) and is competitive on the full set; one reviewer (R1) states it is the best among self-supervised learning approaches shown in Table 1.
- Sufficient and comprehensive ablation studies are provided, demonstrating the effectiveness of each component (PSWaCL, MSAE, bottleneck fusion) and hyper-parameter effects (R1, R2, R3).
- The idea of patchifying 1D waveform representations to align with 2D spectrogram patches is considered somewhat novel (R2).
- The topic of combining waveform and spectrogram features while addressing alignment issues is interesting (R3, R4).
- One reviewer (R1) finds the paper easy-to-read and clearly written; however, other reviewers strongly disagree (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty:** The overall method is a combination of known techniques (multi-scale modeling, SimCLR contrastive loss, bottleneck fusion) and a small extension of prior work (Gong et al. 2022a). Reviewers R1, R3, and R4 all consider novelty limited; R3 notes the paper might be better suited for speech conferences (e.g., Interspeech) rather than ICLR.
- **Writing and presentation issues (multiple reviewers):**
  - Too many acronyms (PSWaCL, SWaB, MSAE, etc.) introduced without clear explanation, making the paper hard to follow, especially in Section 3.2 (R3).
  - The introduction is poorly written: challenges are not clearly stated, and many statements are loose or vague (R4). For example, “waveform-based features concentrate more on capturing common patterns” and “spectrograms predominantly emphasize time-frequency responses” are not defined. The term “comprehensiveness of the feature set” is also vague (R4).
  - The operation “patchify” for waveform is not explained; a diagram would help. Using pooling for multiscale feature aggregation is criticized as a poor choice; alternatives such as zero padding, dilated convolutions, or adaptive strides are suggested (R4).
  - Notation is unclear: e.g., \(t_{spec}, t_{wav}\) in equations (5a,5b) – their dimensions and the concatenation axis are not given. The source of bottleneck features and the design of the multi-stream bottleneck transformer are not described (R4).
  - Figure 1 contains the term “specify” which is unclear (R4).
  - No description of whether the transformer frontend uses shared or separate weights for spectrogram and waveform inputs (R4).
- **Inaccurate or misleading claims:**
  - The claim that spectrogram and waveform representations inherently suit different tasks is inaccurate; many audio/speech tasks use both successfully (e.g., [1,2] for spectrogram, [3,4] for waveform) (R2).
  - The example about car engine sound being clear in the time-frequency domain and successive breezes being clear in the waveform domain is confusing; waveform representations can also capture time-frequency patterns (e.g., Conv-TasNet filterbanks converge to frequency responses similar to log-mel filters) (R2).
  - The arguments about “semantic misalignment” do not relate to the usual meaning of semantics (R4).
  - The claim about “temporal misalignment” is incorrect: linear/nonlinear processing methods exist to achieve temporal alignment, and late fusion can be used. It is “basic DSP” (R4).
  - The introduction’s discussion of fixed resolution misalignment is misleading because the paper uses Mel-spectrograms, which are already a non-linear transformation (R4).
- **Missing or insufficient comparisons:**
  - To properly evaluate the benefit of joint spectrogram-waveform modeling, comparisons should keep all other factors equal. The waveform-only results cited are from old work without modern SSL and transformer techniques. A proper ablation would compare SSaPT (spectrogram-only) with WaPT (waveform-only, same transformer) and then the joint method (R2). If WaPT performs similarly to SSaPT, it could suggest spectrogram is unnecessary (R2).
  - In Table 1, AST (a spectrogram-only model) outperforms all proposed CRAFT variants. This requires explanation (R3, R4).
  - The results are not state-of-the-art on either dataset. For ESC-50, existing works exceed 98% accuracy; for AudioSet, strong baselines are available on paperswithcode.com. These are not considered or discussed (R4).
  - Related work on feature fusion methods is absent; the authors discuss only general audio classification approaches. Several prior fusion works should be cited (e.g., DCASE 2021, MDPI Sensors 2019, Interspeech 2018, ACM MM 2018) (R4).
- **Confusing result:** The performance of SWaPT (joint training without contrastive loss) is worse than SSaPT (spectrogram-only). This is counterintuitive because adding waveform information should not degrade performance if representations are complementary. If misalignment is the cause, why not simply use separate parameters for each branch? If that works, the necessity of the proposed contrastive learning (PSWaCL) is questionable (R2).
- **Insufficient analysis of learned representations:** One reviewer (R1) suggests that more insight into the learned characteristics of the multi-scale waveform encoder versus the spectrogram encoder would increase novelty. Currently, such analysis is missing.
- **Augmentation and training details:** Only spectral-domain augmentation is used; time-domain augmentation is not considered despite being common in acoustic modeling (R4).
- **Code not available:** The code is promised to be open-sourced but is not provided, making it impossible to replicate the main experiments (R4).
