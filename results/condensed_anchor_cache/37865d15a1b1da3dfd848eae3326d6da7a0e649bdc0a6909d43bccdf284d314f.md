## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates autophagous (self-consuming) loops in generative models, where synthetic data from previous generations is included in training data for the next generation. Three scenarios are categorized: fully synthetic loops (no real data), synthetic augmentation loops (fixed real data plus synthetic data), and fresh data loops (new real data added each generation). Theoretical analysis with a Gaussian toy example and empirical experiments using diffusion models (DDPM on MNIST) and GANs (StyleGAN2 on FFHQ) show that without enough fresh real data per generation, both quality (precision) and diversity (recall) progressively degrade—termed Model Autophagy Disorder (MAD). Sampling bias (λ) influences collapse severity. A phase transition is observed in fresh data loops: performance stays stable only when the ratio of synthetic data remains below a threshold. The authors discuss how to alleviate MAD.

### Strengths

1.  **Clear, well-structured presentation with sound mathematical formulation.** The paper is easy to follow, provides clear definitions and visualizations, and formulates the problem rigorously. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3)
2.  **Theoretical evidence via a Gaussian toy example.** Section 3 provides a tractable proof that supports the heuristic Claim in Section 2, strengthening the paper’s foundation. (Reviewer 1)
3.  **Insightful conclusion that performance degradation in the loop is caused only when using fixed samples.** This is predictable but still valuable. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3)
4.  **Timely and relevant topic.** The self-consuming loop is highly pertinent as generated data increasingly appears on the internet, and three distinct scenarios are explicitly categorized and analyzed, distinguishing this work from prior studies. (Reviewer 2)
5.  **Novel identification of Model Autophagy Disorder (MAD).** The paper introduces and characterizes a previously underexplored issue with significant implications for data quality and diversity in AI systems. (Reviewer 3)
6.  **Extensive experiments across multiple scenarios, models (diffusion, GAN), and datasets (MNIST, FFHQ).** The findings are robust across these variations. (Reviewer 3)
7.  **Practical implications.** The paper offers guidance on how future generative model training can be performed to avoid MAD, including the discussion of mitigation strategies. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses

1.  **Ambiguity around the “Claim” in Section 2.** It is unclear whether this Claim is intended as a hypothesis for the general case or a proven theorem. The paper states it in a declarative form but only proves it for a Gaussian toy model. The term “Claim” in a paper typically implies a proven result, but here it appears speculative beyond the toy case. The behavior of model parameters described in the Claim is not verified in practical models, raising concerns of overclaiming. (Reviewer 1)
2.  **Equation (2) is incompletely specified.** It is unclear whether Eq. (2) is a hypothesis or a theorem. The function \(WD(n_r, n_s, \lambda)\) is not defined, and the relationship between \(n_r, n_s, \lambda\) and the Wasserstein distance is not explicitly stated. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the equation's validity. (Reviewer 1)
3.  **Motivation for varying \(n_{ini}\) in Section 5 is unclear.** The role of \(n_{ini}\) in the experimental setup and how it relates to the core claims in the introduction is not explained. The authors should clarify how varying \(n_{ini}\) contributes to understanding self-consuming loops. (Reviewer 1)
4.  **Phase transition observation appears abruptly.** The phase transition is a key finding but is introduced only in Section 5. A brief mention in the introduction would better contextualize it and highlight its significance for the reader. (Reviewer 1)
5.  **Discrepancy between the definition of autophagous loops and the theory/experiments.** The paper defines autophagous mechanisms as “each generative model \(\mathcal{G}^t\) is trained on data that includes samples from previous models,” but the theory (around eq. 1) and experiments (Appendices A.1, A.2 on diffusion models) only consider the case where \(\mathcal{G}^t\) is trained on data generated solely by \(\mathcal{G}^{t-1}\). This discrepancy diminishes the soundness of the results unless addressed. (Reviewer 2)
6.  **Generalizability beyond MNIST is questionable.** MNIST is a simple, low-dimensional, well-clustered dataset. The behavior of diffusion models on higher-dimensional, more complex distributions (e.g., ImageNet) may be significantly different. The paper does not adequately address whether the observed collapse would generalize to more realistic, high-dimensional scenarios. (Reviewer 2)
7.  **Lack of details on synthetic data generation methods for experiments.** The paper does not provide sufficient specifics on how synthetic data was created (e.g., sampling procedures, thresholds) to ensure transparency and reproducibility. (Reviewer 3)
8.  **Lack of a dedicated section on mitigations for MAD.** While the paper discusses alleviation, it would benefit from a systematic, dedicated section on practical solutions, which would enhance its utility. (Reviewer 3)
9.  **Limited exploration of broader implications.** The analysis is confined to image generation; a discussion of how MAD might impact other domains (e.g., NLP, audio synthesis) and real-world scenarios is missing. (Reviewer 3)
10. **Need for a more comprehensive theoretical framework.** The mathematical foundations of MAD could be deepened, including a more formal definition and analysis of its implications beyond the toy Gaussian model. (Reviewer 3)
11. **Unanswered questions on model and dataset choices.** Why were StyleGAN2 with FFHQ and DDPM with MNIST specifically chosen? This choice reduces comparability across models and datasets. (Reviewer 2)
12. **Unclear related work distinctions.** The paper cites Huang et al. 2022 (synthetic data augmentation) and Hataya et al. 2022 (first loop effects on downstream tasks) but does not clarify how the current work differs from their scope. (Reviewer 2)

**Note:** Reviewer 1 (score 8) is substantially more positive about the paper’s clarity, theoretical evidence, and overall contribution, while Reviewers 2 and 3 (score 6) raise more critical concerns about experimental scope, missing details, and generalizability. All three reviewers agree on the novelty and relevance of the topic.
