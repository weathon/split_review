## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a bias–diversity decomposition for the quality estimation error in Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) decoding. The bias term captures the closeness between the utility function and human evaluations; the diversity term captures variation in estimated quality across utility functions. The analysis highlights that increasing diversity can reduce the error, aligning with prior empirical observations. The authors introduce a pseudo‑bias metric that approximates bias using gold references, and propose Metric‑augmented MBR (MAMBR) which uses multiple utility functions with varied parameters to increase diversity. Experiments on machine translation, summarization, and image captioning are presented, using several sampling methods and pseudo‑reference sizes.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a theoretical framework (bias–diversity decomposition) for MBR decoding that gives a cleaner interpretation of the quality estimation error. The resulting expression is elegant and helps explain existing empirical results, e.g., why increasing diversity can improve performance (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The interpretation in Section 3.3 is particularly nice, linking the theoretical analysis to prior work and explicitly calling out “Unexplored Aspects” that are followed up later (Reviewer 2).
- The experiments cover multiple tasks (MT, summarization, image captioning), multiple models, sampling methods, and pseudo‑reference sizes, which strengthens the empirical scope (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The results in Section 5.4, where diversity is varied by adjusting model parameters and pseudo‑references, are convincing and support the theoretical claims (Reviewer 2).
- MAMBR achieves better performance on all three tasks under various conditions without requiring additional references, even though gains are small (Reviewer 4).
- The writing is clear and shows scholastic rigor with proper citations (Reviewers 1, 2).

### Weaknesses
- **MAMBR performance is marginal and lacks statistical significance.** The improvements over regular MBR in Tables 1, 2, and 3 are consistently very small (e.g., <0.1 in BERTScore) and no p‑values or confidence intervals are provided. This makes it difficult to assess whether the observed gains are genuine or noise (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The claim that MAMBR improves text generation quality is weakened by these small effect sizes.
- **Eq. (8) naming is counter‑intuitive.** The left‑hand side corresponds to what is typically called “bias,” while the bias term on the right‑hand side corresponds to what is typically called “MSE.” The reviewer asks whether this equation simply reduces to MSE = bias² + variance (Reviewer 1).
- **Pseudo‑bias and the use of model‑generated references introduce potential circularity.** The work motivates with human‑estimated quality ( *f̂* ) but approximates bias via a pseudo‑bias that uses gold references; the correlation between pseudo‑bias and actual human estimates is not discussed (Reviewer 1). Moreover, since both hypotheses and pseudo‑references are generated by the same model (with different sampling), the bias calculation may be skewed and underestimate the true bias. The pseudo‑references may not capture the diversity of human‑generated references, especially when comparing different decoding strategies (Reviewer 4).
- **Figure 1 correlations are not surprising and lack significance testing.** Because pseudo‑bias is a direct function of gold references, its correlation with task performance (also gold‑based) is less interesting. The diversity–performance correlations, while interesting, are weaker. No p‑values are reported for any correlation (Reviewer 1).
- **Figure 2 interpretation is inconsistent with the data.** The claim that ancestral sampling “exhibits the highest bias … but sometimes outperforms owing to its greater diversity” is contradicted by the plots, which often show bias and diversity moving in the same direction. For example, on MSCOCO, overall bias goes down while overall diversity goes up. The interpretation needs revision and should provide a more nuanced explanation of the interplay between bias and diversity across datasets and sampling methods (Reviewer 2).
- **Section 5.2’s correlation analysis is not well‑justified.** Given the decomposition is defined over the entire set of generated samples, one would expect the overall MSE to have the highest correlation with performance. Yet the one‑best bias and one‑best MSE consistently correlate more strongly. The authors do not clarify why one‑best metrics are more indicative of the theoretical decomposition than the overall metrics, which undermines the evidence for the theory (Reviewer 2).
- **The intuitive meaning of the diversity term is unclear and appears to contradict MBR’s goal.** MBR selects generations that are, on average, most similar to others (consensus decoding). The finding that diverse generations improve quality estimation seems counter‑intuitive. The paper lacks a thorough discussion reconciling this tension; a more detailed analysis of how diversity contributes to better error estimation despite MBR’s inherent tendency toward consensus is needed (Reviewer 2).
- **Novelty and contribution are limited (minority view).** Reviewers 1 and 3 consider the bias–diversity decomposition straightforward and the observations in Section 3.3 mostly covered in prior literature, providing limited new insights. In contrast, reviewers 2 and 4 view the theoretical contribution as significant and elegant (disagreement among reviewers).
- **Experimental details are unclear.** In the experimental section (from Section 5 onward), it is not clearly stated what was used as references, hypotheses, and how performance was measured. The same models appear to generate both hypotheses and references with different sampling; it is ambiguous whether ground‑truth human evaluation scores exist or whether all results are pseudo‑results using equation 10 (Reviewer 4).
- **MAMBR implementation is insufficiently specified.** The paper states that “we train evaluation metrics with different initial random seeds to generate Θ as a set of diverse model parameters,” but the metrics used (e.g., COMET, BERTScore) are external packages. How exactly MAMBR is implemented with these packages is not explained (Reviewer 4).
- **Question about different utility/evaluation metrics.** For all three tasks, the utility function and the evaluation metric are the same. The reviewers ask what Figure 1 would look like if they were different (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor writing issue:** The use of “Here, instead of using …” in line 93 is ambiguous – it is unclear whether it refers to the previous equation or is introducing Equation 3. Similar instances appear in earlier sections (Reviewer 4).
