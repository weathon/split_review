## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes SparseDM, which converts pre-trained diffusion models into sparse models via mask pruning targeting the 2:4 sparse pattern for acceleration on NVIDIA Ampere GPUs. The authors use an improved Straight-Through Estimator (STE) with transfer learning: sparse masks are added to convolution and linear layers, the model is fine-tuned with knowledge distillation, and sparse masks are kept at inference. Experiments on U-ViT and DDPM show a 50% MACs reduction with an average FID increase of 0.44 and real GPU speedup of ~1.2×.

### Strengths

- The paper is very well-written, with a clear main idea and great organization (R1, R2).
- The motivation is clear: improving deployment efficiency of diffusion models on resource-constrained devices (R2, R4).
- The simple fine-tuning method converts existing diffusion models into sparse models, useful for scenarios with limited computing power such as mobile devices (R4).
- The observations about fixed sparse training being more effective than progressive sparse training are interesting and novel (R4).
- Experiments on various generation scenarios (U-ViT, DDPM) verify effectiveness relative to baselines (R4). One reviewer highlights that the 2:4 sparse model calculation offers practical value for practitioners using NVIDIA Ampere architecture GPUs (R3), while another reviewer notes this value (R1 strength).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty and technical simplicity**: The pruning strategy is based on existing structures and the motivation is relatively simple; similar results can be achieved by linear attention or directly training a smaller model with distillation (R1). The straightforward idea of masked training is neither interesting nor technically new (R3). Section 3.2 (“Transfer learn sparse diffusion models”) does not explain significant differences from the progressive sparse training strategy discussed in Section 2.2; if only tested with perturbed datasets, it may not constitute a significant contribution (R1).

- **Lack of comparison to recent methods and more diverse baselines**: Several recent (2024) methods are not mentioned, e.g., “Pruning for Robust Concept Erasing in Diffusion Models” and “LD-Pruner: Efficient Pruning of Latent Diffusion Models using Task-Agnostic Insights” (R1). Experiments only test U-ViT (2022) and DDPM (2020); more recent architectures like DiT should be included (R2). No experiments on other datasets such as CelebA-HQ or LSUN Church to demonstrate broader applicability (R1). One reviewer suggests showing applicability to Stable Diffusion (R4).

- **Over-reliance on NVIDIA Ampere architecture**: The method is heavily reliant on the specific 2:4 sparse pattern of the NVIDIA Ampere architecture, limiting its general applicability as a pruning technique (R1). The same technique may not benefit practitioners without access to Ampere architecture (R3).

- **Unsatisfactory speedup**: The real GPU speedup is only about 1.2× (R3). Studies related to reducing time steps for diffusion inference or diffusion quantization/pruning methods may be more effective for the same purpose (R3).

- **Insufficient evaluation metrics**: Experiments validate only FID; additional metrics such as SSIM should be considered (R1).

- **Missing details and analysis**:
  - Section 2.3 mentions applying perturbations to the dataset but does not provide specific implementation details (R1). Moreover, the interaction between the noise schedule/timestep embedding of diffusion models and the weight perturbations from sparse training is not well-defined; it is unclear whether the RFR method adequately accounts for the combined effect (R4).
  - The claim that fixed sparse training is more effective than progressive sparse training (Fig. 3b) lacks experimental validation or theoretical justification beyond the optimizer’s training objective (R1, R4). The authors should provide theoretical proof or at least a detailed analysis of the optimization landscape (e.g., loss landscape visualization or gradient behavior) to support why a consistent distribution shift would be easier to optimize (R4).
  - No comparison of parameter counts per layer before and after sparse pruning; a chart in the appendix is recommended (R1). One reviewer also asks for this information (R1 question).
  - There is a typo in Equation 5; not all symbols are explained (R2). Limitation and discussion sections are missing (R2).
  - The paper does not propose a generalized pruning strategy suitable for Transformer networks; simply relying on data perturbations on one dataset is insufficient to demonstrate applicability to other datasets (R1).

- **Specific experimental questions raised by reviewers**:
  - Why does ASP perform so poorly in all experiments? (R2)
  - Why does the proposed method and STE-based pruning fulfill the same MACs? (R2)
  - Why does the proposed method obtain a lower FID in the first several steps of Figure 3a? (R2)
  - Why is the initial FID of 2:4 sparse different in Figures 3b and 3d? (R2)
  - In Table 3, some variants (e.g., patch_size=2, mlp_ratio=2) are slower than the dense model – why? (R4)
  - It does not mean that greater sparsity implies better FID; please discuss the reason and why 2:4 sparsity was chosen (R2).

- **Minority reviewer is more negative**: One reviewer (score 3) states the method is technically simple, offers limited interest, and achieves only a modest speedup, questioning its contribution compared to other efficiency approaches (R3). This contrasts with the two reviewers (scores 5) who find the method well-motivated and effective (R2, R4).
