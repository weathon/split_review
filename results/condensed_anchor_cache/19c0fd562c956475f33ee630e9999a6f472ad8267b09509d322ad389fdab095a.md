- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 6, 1, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Stochastic deep Restoration Priors (ShaRP), a method that uses an ensemble of restoration operators (trained on multiple degradation tasks) as regularizers for imaging inverse problems, extending the single-operator deep restoration prior of Hu et al. 2024c. Theoretical analysis provides a closed-form gradient for a novel regularizer (Theorem 1) and convergence guarantees (Theorem 2). Experiments on CS-MRI and single-image super-resolution (SISR) compare ShaRP with PnP denoiser priors and diffusion-based methods. Reviewers disagree sharply on novelty (some view the multiple-operator extension as trivial, others as interesting) and on whether the theoretical and experimental contributions are sufficiently demonstrated.

### Strengths
- **Use of general restoration operators as priors** – The core idea of replacing Gaussian denoisers with restoration operators trained on multiple degradation types is considered interesting and novel by Reviewers 2 and 3, who note it could provide a richer prior. (Reviewers 1 and 4, however, view this as a trivial extension of Hu et al. 2024c; see Weaknesses.)
- **Theoretical contributions** – Theorem 1 is praised by Reviewer 2 for putting the idea on a solid theoretical foundation. Reviewer 3 finds both theoretical assertions (regularizer gradient form and convergence) highly relevant, with mild assumptions. Reviewer 5 calls the convergence analysis thorough and well-explained. (Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 1 question the novelty of the convergence analysis relative to existing biased SGD literature; see Weaknesses.)
- **Experimental results on CS-MRI and SISR** – Reviewer 2 finds the comparison with PnP baselines compelling evidence for the claim. Reviewer 3 states ShaRP remarkably advances SOTA for CS-MRI and is on par for SISR. Reviewer 1 notes that training on multiple tasks improves regularization capability.
- **Self‑supervised training possibility** – The paper’s ability to train restoration priors without fully sampled data is mentioned by multiple reviewers as a potential advantage, though its implementation and justification are questioned (see Weaknesses).
- **Presentation** – Reviewers 4 and 5 find the paper clearly written and easy to follow for the most part. Reviewer 3 notes that Section 4 could be improved (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty / trivial extension of Hu et al. 2024c** – Reviewers 1 and 4 argue the paper is essentially the same as Hu et al. 2024c with only a trivial change: taking an expectation over multiple degradation operators \(H\) instead of using a single one. The ShaRP regularizer (Eq. 6) is called a direct extension of Eq. (9) in Hu et al., and Figure 2 is a copy of Figure 1 from that work. These reviewers see no new theoretical or practical challenges solved. (Reviewers 2 and 3 find the multi‑operator generalization novel; this contrast should be noted.) Reviewer 1 requests a clear explanation of how the proposed methodology differs from Hu et al. 2024c.
- **Insufficient cross‑task experiments** – Reviewer 1 points out that experiments only apply the trained prior to the same inverse problem (e.g., MRI with different acceleration factors), not to a different problem type. To support the claimed superiority over Gaussian denoisers, the authors should include experiments where the prior is trained on one task (e.g., super‑resolution) and tested on another (e.g., MRI), or trained on multiple distinct tasks and applied to a different task. The current set does not validate the advantage over Gaussian denoisers for general inverse problems.
- **Motivation and justification of restoration priors over Gaussian denoisers** – Reviewer 2 finds the motivation unconvincing: it is unclear why a restoration prior tailored to specific degradation types should outperform a Gaussian denoiser when the target forward operator differs. The paper should elaborate on when and why such a prior is advantageous. Furthermore, the claim that “ShaRP provides a richer and more flexible representation” is vague.
- **Theoretical analysis limitations**  
  - **Convergence analysis novelty** – Reviewer 2 notes that Theorem 2 closely follows the biased SGD analysis in [“A Guide Through the Zoo of Biased SGD”] and does not clearly differentiate its contributions. The authors should acknowledge and compare their assumptions/results with that work.  
  - **Discrepancy between theory and unsupervised training** – Reviewer 2 observes that the theoretical framework assumes the restoration operator approximates the MMSE conditional expectation \(E[x \mid s, H]\), but the unsupervised loss (Algorithm 3) does not enforce that. The paper must clarify whether the unsupervised restoration operator can still be interpreted as a biased SGD update, and if not, provide alternative theoretical justification.  
  - **Assumption realism** – Theorem 1 assumes the prior density \(p_x\) is non‑degenerate over \(\mathbb{R}^n\), which Reviewer 2 notes is almost always not true in practice. The practical implications of this assumption should be discussed.
- **Role of hyperparameters and lack of ablation** – Reviewer 3 notes that the number of restoration problems \(b\) is essential for performance but no discussion or ablation on \(b\) is provided. Similarly, the impact of \(\alpha\) (introduced in Section 5.1) is not analyzed. Reviewer 3 asks for studies on how varying \(b\) and \(\alpha\) affect results.
- **Inconclusive comparison with diffusion‑based methods** – Reviewer 5 notes the paper lacks comparison to diffusion‑based methods like DiffIR and DDRM. Reviewer 3 points out that Table 4 results for DiffPIR, DRP, and ShaRP do not show a clear tendency; a deeper analysis is needed to understand when each method performs best and why there is no clear winner.
- **Self‑supervised training claim** – Reviewers 2 and 5 question the “self‑supervised” label. Training a restoration network on a set of degradation operators \(H_i\) still requires ground‑truth images (e.g., fully sampled MRI). Reviewer 5 asks for clarification on how the method can be truly self‑supervised. Reviewer 2 compares this with existing unsupervised denoiser training (e.g., SURE loss) and suggests the paper should articulate advantages over those approaches.
- **Regularization concept and relation to data consistency** – Reviewer 2 notes that the regularization effect of encouraging solutions whose degraded versions resemble real degraded images holds only when the degradation matches the training operator, not necessarily the forward operator \(A\). The paper should clarify this and discuss whether a Gaussian denoiser combined with a data‑consistency loss could produce the same effect.
- **Missing direct comparison of supervised vs. self‑supervised ShaRP** – Reviewer 5 notes the paper claims to implement both but does not directly compare their performance on the same task.
- **Use of multiple degradation operators for a fixed‑task problem** – Reviewer 5 asks why a set of degradation operators \(H_1,\dots,H_k\) is needed when the target problem uses only a fixed operator (e.g., \(H_1\)). The rationale should be explained.
