## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a unified vision-based RL policy for grasping objects across multiple dexterous hands. It uses a shared observation space of fingertip and palm positions, and an action space based on eigengrasps of the MANO hand model with a pretrained retargeting network. A teacher-student framework (state-based PPO → vision-based DAgger) is evaluated on 45 YCB objects across four training hands and two unseen hands, with a real-world demonstration on LEAP Hand.

### Strengths
- Comprehensive benchmark and ablation study (R1).
- Clear, well-structured writing; logical flow and effective visual presentation (Figs. 1, 2) (R1, R2, R3).
- Action space design using linear combination of eigengrasp coefficients is interesting (R2).
- Using a neural network to approximate optimization-based retargeting enables efficient parallel training (R2, R3).
- The policy is successfully deployed in the real world via a student policy with vision input (R2).
- The cross-embodiment problem is highly relevant to embodied AI (R3).
- Motivation from human teleoperation is well-illustrated (R2).

### Weaknesses
- Real-world evaluation is limited: only LEAP Hand tested (R1), and only three successful trials are shown (R2). No quantitative real-world success rate is reported (R3).
- Observation space uses exactly four fingertip positions; the paper does not discuss how to handle hands with three or five fingers. If the method is restricted to anthropomorphic hands with ≥4 fingers, this should be stated (R1, R2).
- Retargeting mapping network requires training data from each morphology, including unseen hands. This makes comparisons to baselines that do not use such data potentially unfair (R2).
- Performance on unseen embodiments drops >50% compared to seen ones (Table 1), which undermines the claim of “direct transferability” (R3). The paper presents this as a strength, but the degradation is substantial.
- Missing ablation: there is no baseline that directly predicts fingertip positions (or MANO keypoints) and converts them via inverse kinematics. Such a baseline would more directly test the advantage of the eigengrasp action space (R2, R3).
- The finding that a single eigengrasp performs comparably to many (Fig. 4) raises concerns: the task may not require dexterity, and the method’s effectiveness may be overstated (R2, R3). Learned grasping actions may be too similar for future functional tasks (R2).
- The baselines used (ablations of the proposed method) are not strong; other methods not designed for cross-embodiment still achieve non‑negligible success, suggesting the task may be too simple (R3).
- Details of the vision policy training are missing: camera resolution, number of cameras, point cloud generation process (simulation vs. real), and how observations are obtained (R3). Prior student-teacher works use far fewer environments; the use of 16,384 parallel vision environments is questionable without further explanation (R3).
- Training on only 4 embodiments and transferring to 2 is limited; the lack of scaling to more diverse hands (e.g., including 3-fingered ones) makes it hard to assess true generalization (R1, R3). Attributing the limitation to “restricted access to dexterous hand models” is not a compelling justification (R3).
- The claim that the high dimensionality of human hand poses makes RL less efficient is not supported: joint-based action spaces perform comparably or better in PPO training (Table 1), and related work shows high-dimensional action spaces can be learned effectively (R3).
- The inclusion of arm joint positions in the observation may limit generalizability to different arms (R1).
- No quantitative real-world evaluation is provided (R3).
- Questions about episode termination (whether episodes are timestep‑limited), object grouping (how objects are grouped and whether shape information is provided to the policy), and the choice of Shadow Hand for single‑embodiment comparisons (rather than the mean across all training hands) are not addressed (R2, R3).
- R3 is notably more critical than R1 and R2, especially regarding the validity of claims about transferability and task complexity. The success rate <40% on unseen embodiments does not justify framing as “direct transfer.”
