## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses multi-dimensional domain shifts (shifts along multiple factors such as demographics, equipment, protocols) using an Ensemble of Mixtures of Experts (EMoE), where each MoE specializes in one domain dimension. A domain estimator handles missing or unreliable domain labels. The method is evaluated in both centralized and federated learning settings (FedEMoE) on several datasets. Overall, the topic is timely, but most reviewers found the novelty limited, the writing unclear, the comparisons incomplete, and the experimental scope insufficient.

### Strengths
1. **Novel focus on multi-dimensional domain shifts.** Unlike standard DG methods that treat shifts as a single homogeneous factor, the paper explicitly models multiple simultaneous factors (e.g., demographics, equipment, protocols). This framing addresses a significant gap in the literature (Reviewers 3, 4, 5).
2. **Unified framework for centralized and federated learning.** The approach (EMoE and FedEMoE) applies to both settings, which is valuable for heterogeneous and decentralized data sources (Reviewers 1, 3, 5). The subpopulation shift in FL is less studied, and the paper provides a setup and rigorous evaluation within that context (Reviewer 2).
3. **Domain estimator for missing/unreliable labels.** Incorporating a domain estimator that can bypass reliance on domain labels at inference shows practical foresight for real-world data (Reviewer 3). The handling of partially missing labels is acknowledged as a key challenge.
4. **Ambitious and comprehensive experimental evaluation.** The paper presents extensive experiments on six datasets, including real-world shifts, and benchmarks against multiple baselines in both settings (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited technical novelty.** The approach largely combines known ideas: MoE for domain generalization (e.g., Li et al., "Sparse mixture-of-experts are domain generalizable learners") and data balancing/reweighting (e.g., Idrissi et al., "Simple data balancing achieves competitive worst-group-accuracy"). The EMoE is an incremental extension without substantial theoretical innovation (Reviewers 2, 5). The non-differentiable one-hot encoding for domain estimation is introduced without evidence that it has any effect (Reviewer 2).
2. **Incomplete baselines and comparisons.** Several important baselines are missing: DFR (ICLR 2022) and Whac-A-Mole (CVPR 2023) for multiple biases (Reviewer 3); personalized federated learning methods in the FL experiments (Reviewer 4); and recent domain generalization works such as "An Iterative Self-Learning Framework for Medical Domain Generalization" (NeurIPS 2024) (Reviewer 5). It is also unclear whether baselines like ERM and IRM were trained with the same MoE architecture (Reviewer 2).
3. **Unclear writing and exposition.** Specific sections are poorly explained: Table 2's dim1/dim2 row/column notation (Reviewer 3); Section 3.4's domain estimator input/output (Reviewer 4); the connection between equations (3) and (4) (Reviewer 4); undefined notation at L304 (Reviewer 4); vague training process description (Reviewer 4); ambiguous handling of missing domain labels (Reviewer 4). Table captions are too short (Reviewer 3).
4. **Insufficient analysis of the domain estimator.** The mechanism for inference without labels is not detailed, robustness to noise is not evaluated, and the trade-off between using predefined vs. estimated labels is not analyzed (Reviewer 3). It is unclear why MoE output cannot be used directly for domain estimation (Reviewer 4). Comparison with slice discovery methods (e.g., DOMINO ICLR2022, FACTS ICCV2023) is missing (Reviewer 3). Alternative techniques like Huber loss or uncertainty estimation are not explored (Reviewer 3).
5. **Computational complexity not addressed.** The ensemble of multiple MoEs adds significant overhead compared to simpler methods like DFR, JTT, and GroupDRO (Reviewers 1, 3). No complexity analysis is provided.
6. **Datasets are too simple.** The centralized experiments rely on toyish datasets; more challenging real-world benchmarks (e.g., EXAM, FairFace, chest-x-ray datasets like NIH-CXR, MIMIC-CXR, CheXpert, or NLI datasets from the Subpopshift paper) are needed to demonstrate generalizability (Reviewers 3, 5). The effect of missing domain labels should also be tested on EXAM and FairFace (Reviewer 1).
7. **Formulation could be simplified.** The multi-dimensional shift problem can be reduced to a single factor with \(M^K\) levels; the authors do not discuss this alternative or justify why their approach is preferable (Reviewer 4).
8. **Motivation and problem definition insufficiently articulated.** The importance of studying multi-dimensional shifts across both centralized and federated settings is not clearly explained (Reviewer 5). The significance of the research question is not fully established (Reviewer 5).
9. **Metric choice not justified.** The paper uses worst-group accuracy without discussing differences under average accuracy; the reason for this choice is unclear (Reviewer 4).
10. **Determination of factor count and levels without labels.** It is unclear how the number of factors and the number of levels per factor are determined when domain labels are missing (Reviewer 4).
11. **Missing analysis on direct label encoding.** The possibility of simply concatenating domain labels as features to the input and using base methods is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
12. **Reviewer disagreement.** One reviewer was notably more positive (score 6, albeit adjusted downward after reading other concerns) than the others (scores 3, 3, 3, 5), with three reviewers being highly critical of novelty and clarity.
