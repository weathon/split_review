## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces CAV2vec, a self-supervised audio-visual speech representation learning framework designed for robustness to joint audio-visual corruption. It uses a teacher-student self-distillation approach with a corrupted prediction task: the student learns to predict clean targets (from the teacher) from corrupted input frames. A unimodal multi-task learning strategy (cross-modal targeting, e.g., predicting clean audio from corrupted video and vice versa) mitigates representation-space dispersion caused by corruption. Experiments on LRS2 and LRS3 benchmarks show improved recognition accuracy under various corruption types. The approach achieves state-of-the-art results on corrupted data while not degrading clean performance (e.g., 1.5% WER still best in Table 1), and yields >10% relative improvement over the next best systems on artificially corrupted noisy conditions. Acronyms include AVCP, mACP, mCP, ACP, VCP; ablations isolate contributions of ACP+VCP.

### Strengths
- **Clear problem definition and real-world motivation**: The paper clearly identifies the limitation of prior AVSR works in handling visual corruptions (lip occlusions, blurred videos) and proposes a well-reasoned solution. (R1, R4)
- **Novelty in corruption-aware SSL**: Investigating audio and visual corruptions during the self-supervised learning phase is not thoroughly done in prior work; the unimodal multi-task learning for cross-modal corrupted prediction is innovative. (R1, R3; note R4 and partially R3 question novelty – see Weaknesses)
- **Strong empirical results**: CAV2vec outperforms a variety of strong baselines (AV-HuBERT, data2vec, RAVEn, BRAVEn, etc.) on both visual and audio corruption scenarios, while maintaining best clean-condition performance (1.5% WER in Table 1). On artificially corrupted noisy conditions, average improvement is >10% relative. (R2)
- **Thorough ablations**: Experiments tease out the contributions of individual corruption prediction components (ACP, VCP). (R2)
- **Method is simple and efficient**: No additional architectural modules are needed to handle audio-visual corruptions, making the framework practical. (R4)
- **Technical depth and clarity**: The paper is well-structured, with detailed technical sections and visual aids (Figures). The use of self-distillation, multimodal Transformers, and self-supervised learning without architectural changes is well presented. (R1, R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Similarity to prior methods and limited novelty**: Several reviewers (R3, R4) note strong methodological overlap with existing works such as AV-HuBERT, RAVEn/BRAVEn, data2vec, dinoSR, and ES^3. The main difference – cross-modal prediction in both directions (A→V and V→A) – is seen as incremental given the existing body of work that already uses masked prediction, cross-modal prediction, and EMA frameworks. R3 suggests adding explicit discussion of differences; R4 finds the learning manner not sufficiently original or insightful. (R3, R4; contrast: R1 and R2 consider the approach innovative and novel)
- **Artificial corruption advantage**: The clearest wins are on artificially created corrupted test sets, where the proposed model is trained with matching corruptions. Other baselines do not have the benefit of training on the same corruption distributions, making the comparison potentially unfair. (R2; echoed by R3: “it’s reasonable that it performs better than other SSL methods which have not been trained with such data”)
- **Reliance on AV-HuBERT initialization**: The model is initialized with AV-HuBERT weights. It remains unclear whether similar performance could be achieved training from scratch, or how much of the robustness is inherited from AV-HuBERT. R3 and R4 both question the impact of this initialization and ask for experiments training from scratch. (R3, R4)
- **Missing evaluation on single-modality tasks**: Since the goal is robust audio-visual representations, validation on audio-only (ASR) and video-only (VSR) tasks would help substantiate the claim of truly robust representations. Currently only AVSR results are provided. (R4)
- **Evaluation on LRS2 less comprehensive**: The LRS2 benchmark evaluation could be more thorough, following the same corruption scenarios as for LRS3. (R1)
- **Parameter count and baseline performance discrepancy**: In Table 1, the parameter count for RAVEn/BRAVEn is stated as 673M during pre-training and inference, but other models (325M) double during inference due to separately fine-tuned models – the table may be inaccurate. Also, lines 351–353 report fine-tuned results for RAVEn/BRAVEn (using authors’ hyperparameters) that are significantly worse than published numbers (e.g., clean LRS3 audio: 2.3 vs. 1.4 for RAVEn; 1.8 vs. 1.1 for BRAVEn). This discrepancy requires explanation. (R3)
- **Clarity issues and missing details**:
  - **Acronyms**: The large number of acronyms (AVCP, mACP, mCP, ACP, VCP) is hard to follow. A summary table (in addition to Figure 5) could help. (R2)
  - **Figure 3 notation**: Bidirectional arrows suggest symmetric interactions, but prediction objectives are directed. (R4)
  - **Definition of “visual corruption events”**: It is unclear whether occlusion occurs in a single frame or across the entire sequence. Appendix A.2 says “occurs once”, but the meaning of “once” is ambiguous. (R4)
  - **Rationale for MP vs. CP**: Masked Prediction (MP) is applied only in multimodal settings, while Corruption Prediction (CP) spans both multimodal and unimodal scenarios – the reasoning behind this split is not explained. (R4)
  - **Parameter k in MP**: The objective uses the average of the teacher’s top-k block outputs, but k is not specified and the motivation is not given. (R4)
  - **Missing L_AVCP in overall loss**: The overall multi-task loss function omits the audio-visual corruption prediction term L_AVCP; why? (R4)
  - **MLM objective**: The motivation and setting of Masked Language Modeling (MLM) are unclear; how are cluster indices acquired? (R4)
  - **Text error (lines 421–422)**: The paper incorrectly states CAV2vec’s performance on unseen hand occlusion and pixelated face corruptions; correct values from Table 1 are 5.2 and 5.1, respectively. (R1)
  - **L.18 wording**: The model predicts clean frames, not corrupted frames as stated. (R3)
- **Missing evaluations**: Harder, non-simulated noisy datasets could better demonstrate benefits over data2vec. Also, can data2vec be trained on the same corruptions to provide a fairer comparison? (R2)
