## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies verbalized overconfidence in LLMs trained with RLHF, identifies that reward models have a systematic bias favoring high-confidence responses regardless of actual quality, and proposes two methods (PPO-M and PPO-C) to mitigate this bias. PPO-M retrains the reward model with confidence-augmented pairwise data; PPO-C adjusts rewards during PPO using a moving-average baseline. Experiments on Llama3-8B and Mistral-7B across six datasets (multiple-choice and open-ended generation) show reduced calibration error with little performance loss.

### Strengths
*   The problem—verbalized calibration in instruction-following LLMs—is important, well-motivated, and of broad interest. Reviewers uniformly highlight this.
*   The paper is clearly structured and easy to follow, with analysis experiments that convincingly demonstrate the bias in reward models.
*   The proposed methods are straightforward and do not require new human-annotated data. They can be integrated into existing PPO pipelines.
*   The evaluation is comprehensive: it covers multiple model families (Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B), diverse benchmarks (multiple-choice and open-ended), and transparently reports where improvements are small (e.g., TruthfulQA) or absent (MT-Bench, Arena-Hard). One reviewer (Reviewer 2) specifically praises the thoroughness and transparency, which made the findings trustworthy.
*   The improved calibration transfers from reward model scores to accuracy on multiple-choice datasets.
*   One reviewer (Reviewer 1) notes that despite limited scientific novelty, the careful analysis makes the paper acceptable.

### Weaknesses
*   **Limited scientific insight and practical value.** Several reviewers (Reviewers 1, 4) argue that the methods are heuristic rather than principled: they simply increase reward for high confidence on high-quality responses and lower it for low confidence on low-quality responses. Reviewer 1 states the contribution is not surprising and notes that a user could instead be shown the reward model score directly. Reviewer 4 calls the algorithm design “heuristic” and lacking rigorous justification, and after discussion lowered confidence that the work meets ICLR standards. A contrasting minority (Reviewer 2) finds the methods “straightforward yet backed by sufficient evaluation,” but still notes limitations.
*   **PPO-M formulation and training ambiguity.**  
    *   Reviewer 2 points out that calling Eq. 2 the “PPO-M” loss is confusing because it is a reward model retraining objective, not a modification of PPO itself.  
    *   Reviewers 3 and 4 note that Eq. 2 does not guarantee that the model prefers the chosen high-confidence response over the rejected low-confidence response; there is no explicit preference enforcement between \((y_c, h_c)\) and \((y_r, l_r)\). Reviewer 4 suggests jointly optimizing Eq.(1) and Eq.(2). The paper indeed reports that PPO-M sometimes lowers accuracy (ACC) compared to standard PPO, which may stem from this missing constraint.  
    *   It is unclear from Lines 239–244 whether the reward model for PPO-M was trained from scratch or fine-tuned on the new calibration dataset. The authors should clarify.
*   **PPO-C reward adjustment concerns.**  
    *   The definition of \(\gamma\) in PPO-C (Line 279) uses a factor \(|\hat{r}_i|\). Reviewer 1 argues the location of the reward is meaningless (since only reward differences matter in the loss), so taking an absolute value does not make sense; a measure of spread (e.g., standard deviation) would be more appropriate.  
    *   The intuition is unclear: using the average reward across the dataset as a threshold only works if the model is correct about 50% of the time (Reviewer 3). The assumption that \(\hat{r}(y_c) > \Delta r\) and \(\hat{r}(y_r) < \Delta r\) should be explicitly stated and analyzed (Reviewer 4).  
    *   \(\Delta r\) is defined as a running average of \(r\) (the reward with confidence) rather than \(\hat{r}\) (the original reward). Why? And an off-the-shelf reward model may assign unreliable rewards to responses that include confidence scores, since it was not trained on such data (Reviewer 3).  
    *   There is no ablation on the smoothing factor \(\alpha\) for the moving average in PPO-C (Reviewer 4). How does \(\alpha\) affect reward magnitude and final performance?
*   **Insufficient/confusing analysis in figures.**  
    *   Reviewer 2 critiques Figure 2: the “perfect calibration” line does not meaningfully differentiate overconfidence from an overly easy dataset (e.g., CommonsenseQA). The plot is sparse and does not include confidence distributions after applying PPO-M and PPO-C.  
    *   Reviewer 3 disagrees with the conclusion drawn from Figure 2: the SFT model before RLHF is also overconfident (most bins below the diagonal), so the claim of RLHF-induced overconfidence is not fully supported. Why are the data points so sparse? How many prompts were used?  
    *   In Figure 3, a control experiment is missing: appending a random confidence score to both chosen and rejected responses should be tested. If the reward model still prefers the chosen answer on average, the brittleness is not demonstrated; if not, it highlights distribution shift (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 similarly asks for a version that combines answer and confidence score to better show how the ratio of chosen/rejected is affected.
*   **Verbal confidence metric concerns.**  
    *   Reviewer 2 notes that using verbal confidence (0–10) for models under 70B may not be ideal; the model may simulate confidence without true understanding. They suggest examining logit distributions for the numeric tokens or using token-level entropy as a more concrete metric.  
    *   The paper does not consider possible underconfidence in SFT models. Could the methods also address that (Reviewer 2)?
*   **No theoretical or principled support.** Multiple reviewers (1, 3, 4) characterize the methods as “heuristics.” Reviewer 4 explicitly states the contribution is diminished because the algorithm design appears ad hoc rather than principled, and the empirical success could stem from careful code-level implementation or a limited dataset rather than a robust method.
*   **Typographical and minor issues.**  
    *   Line 134: “Dand” should be “D and”.  
    *   Line 1046: “calirbation” should be “calibration”.  
    *   Line 270: clarify what is meant by “biased response” when asking for confidence creates bias.
