## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper defines the Spherical Tree-Sliced Wasserstein (STSW) distance, an optimal transport metric for probability measures on \(\mathbb{S}^d\). The key innovation is a novel spherical Radon transform whose integration domain is a *spherical tree* (a set of great semicircles sharing a common root point on the sphere, forming a tree-like topology). By leveraging this transform, the authors obtain closed-form expressions for the Wasserstein distance between projected measures and prove STSW is a valid, orthogonally invariant metric. The method is tested on gradient flows, density estimation, and self-supervised learning (CIFAR-10, MNIST), showing competitive or better performance and faster runtime versus existing sliced-Wasserstein baselines (SW, SSW, S3W).

### Strengths

- The paper addresses an important problem: computing distances between probability distributions on spheres, with many applications.
- Extending the tree-sliced concept from Euclidean domains to spherical domains is novel and non-trivial.
- The writing is well-structured, rigorous, coherent, and easy to follow. The narrative is clear and follows established approaches for substituting simpler structures with more sophisticated variants.
- The key innovation is the integration over spherical trees for defining the new metric, moving beyond one-dimensional lines or great semicircles used in existing spherical sliced Wasserstein (SSW).
- The authors prove STSW is a proper metric (unlike SSW, which is only known to be a pseudometric) using novel splitting maps that preserve orthogonal invariance.
- STSW is shown to be invariant under orthogonal transformations, a desirable property.
- The algorithm is straightforward to implement.
- Extensive experiments (gradient flows, density estimation, self-supervised learning) demonstrate STSW’s effectiveness. It often outperforms baselines in terms of runtime and quantitative metrics (e.g., smaller variances in Table 1, faster convergence).
- Claims are supported by both theoretical results and empirical verification.

### Weaknesses

- **Claims about topological information:** The paper (line 65) states that tree systems enhance capture of topological information, but does not provide direct experimental or analytic evidence for this claim. An experiment demonstrating this advantage would be useful.
- **Closed-form expression is misleading:** The claimed closed-form expression (e.g., eq. 19) still relies on approximations: sampling of trees, and the practical computation assumes discrete distributions. The paper should clarify that the distance cannot be computed exactly in practice.
- **Variance in Table 1:** The variances from STSW are extremely small (often less than one tenth of other methods under Monte Carlo sampling). The authors offer no theoretical explanation for this robustness or how it relates to tree-slicing on spheres.
- **Runtime vs. training time:** STSW outperforms baselines in runtime, but the improvement does not translate to a similar margin in reducing training time (Table 2). The theoretical complexity provided cannot explain this discrepancy.
- **Scalability and complexity:** The computational cost of generating and processing spherical trees (geodesic distance calculations, tree traversals) could limit scalability for very high-dimensional or densely-sampled spherical data. The paper does not include a breakdown of time complexity per step as a function of sphere dimension and number of edges. Explicit discussion of computational and memory complexity (beyond Appendix B) is needed.
- **Impact of tree structure:** The quality of STSW depends on the sampling of spherical trees, introducing variability. The paper lacks discussion of strategies for sampling more informative slices (e.g., Markov chains, random paths) and does not analyze how tree topology (balanced vs. unbalanced, depth, branching factor) affects performance or ability to capture data geometry.
- **Sampling bias:** The algorithm samples uniformly from \(\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\) and then normalizes to get points on \(\mathbb{S}^d\), which does not produce a uniform distribution on the sphere. This could induce bias, but no analysis is provided.
- **Incremental contribution:** Several reviewers note that the paper primarily combines previously established concepts (tree systems from Tran et al. (2024b) and spherical sliced Wasserstein from Bonet et al. (2022) and Tran et al. (2024a)) and extends them to the sphere. The experiments closely follow those of prior papers. (One reviewer nonetheless considers the adaptation to spheres a meaningful advancement.)
- **Radon transform measure preservation:** The paper states that the Radon transform maps a probability distribution on \(\mathbb{S}^d\) to a distribution on \(\mathcal{T}\), but the proof only shows \(\|\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{T}}^\alpha f\| \leq \|f\|_1\) (line 268), not equality. It does not immediately follow that the measure is preserved.
- **Analysis of miss:**
- **CIFAR-10 results:** Results on CIFAR-10 are not impressive, and the provided visualization (d=2) does not help explain the performance.
- **Missing comparisons:** There is no comparison with Vertical SW in appropriate setups. For generative experiments, no samples or quantitative image quality measures (e.g., FID score) are reported. No analytic comparison between STSW and SW, SSW, S3W (e.g., differences in induced topologies) is given.
- **Ablation studies missing:** The paper would be strengthened by ablations on design choices (number of rays, tree structure parameters, etc.) to confirm that superior performance is due to the tree component and to identify limitations or numerical instabilities.
- **Missing connection to prior spherical trees:** The paper does not discuss connections to prior work on spherical trees (Meng et al. 2020, “Hierarchical topic mining via joint spherical tree and text embedding”), even though the construction differs.
- **Question about eq. 20:** In self-supervised learning, \(STSW(z^A, \nu)\) is used where \(z^A\) is a single point. If \(\nu\) is uniform on the sphere, the distance from a Dirac to uniform is constant due to rotational invariance, so it is unclear why this term favors uniformity.
- **Injectivity and consequences:** Theorem 4.3 proves that if the splitting map is \(\mathcal{O}(d+1)\)-invariant, then the spherical Radon transform is injective. The paper does not discuss what structure might be missed if a non-injective Radon transform were used.
- **Minor issues:** Notation issues (l. 170 missing article, l. 372 missing subscript, l. 322 confusing \(\delta\) with Dirac delta). Setup and hyperparameters could be better documented for all methods.
