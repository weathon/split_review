## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Attack via Implicit Reference (AIR), which decomposes malicious objectives into nested harmless objectives and links them through implicit references to bypass safety mechanisms. AIR operates in two stages: first rewriting the malicious objective into nested benign objectives, then sending a follow-up rewrite request with implicit references. Experiments show AIR achieves >90% ASR on GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Qwen-2-72B and other models, reveals an inverse scaling phenomenon (larger models more vulnerable), and demonstrates cross-model attack transferability.

### Strengths
1. **High attack success rate (ASR)**: AIR achieves >90% ASR on state-of-the-art LLMs including GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Qwen-2-72B (Table 1). This is consistent across open- and closed-source models.
2. **Cross-model strategy**: Demonstrates transferability by using less secure models to generate malicious contexts that increase ASR on other models (Table 2, algorithm). Highlights potential vulnerability propagation between LLMs.
3. **Inverse scaling phenomenon**: Identifies that larger models (with stronger in-context learning) are more susceptible to AIR, which is a notable finding about model size vs. security.
4. **Comparison with defenses**: Evaluates against baseline defenses (SmoothLLM, PerplexityFilter) and reports rejection rate (Table 5).
5. **Motivation and design**: The attack concept (decomposing malicious objectives via nesting and implicit reference) is intuitive and straightforward.
6. **Experiment coverage**: Covers diverse model families (open-source and closed-source) and different model sizes.
7. **Efficiency**: The method is relatively efficient compared to baselines.
8. **Nesting objective generation**: Some reviewers found this concept interesting and the paper easy to follow (minority positive view on readability).

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty limited and unclear distinction from related work**:
   - The method is very similar to template-based/word substitution attacks (e.g., Handa et al. "When Competency in Reasoning Opens the Door to Vulnerability", Andriushchenko et al. "Jailbreaking Leading Safety-Aligned LLMs with Simple Adaptive Attacks").
   - The "Cross-Model Attack" is similar to PAIR and TAP (which also use an LLM as attacker/red-teaming assistant); the paper does not explicitly highlight differences or provide comparative analysis.
   - For the contextual and multi-turn attack categories, the paper should differentiate from COU, COA, and ArtPrompt. Overall, reviewers found the novelty of “implicit reference” design unclear.
2. **Section 2.3 (“Nesting Objective Generation”) lacks rigor**:
   - Needs more detailed analysis connecting the concept to attention mechanisms; currently appears speculative/guess-like.
   - Notation is confusing: what is the support set of objectives (text/token/embedding space)? What is the sum of objectives? How is α defined, and across different attention layers/heads?
   - The derivation of Equation 2 is not explained.
3. **Insufficient evaluation details and small sample size**:
   - Only 100 samples used; for results showing only marginal differences (e.g., Table 4, Table 5), confidence intervals are necessary.
   - The evaluation method is not clearly broken down. The paper states three complementary evaluation metrics but does not specify which are used per experiment or how they are balanced.
   - FASR (First Attack Success Rate) in Table 1 is not defined: is it simply ASR in the first attack stage? A definition or reference is needed.
4. **Attack and defense baselines require justification**:
   - The attack baselines compared do not strictly fall into the same category as AIR (contextual, multi-turn). Missing comparisons with COU, COA, ArtPrompt.
   - The defense baselines evaluated (SmoothLLM, PerplexityFilter) are designed for adversarial token attacks, not contextual/multi-turn attacks. Should include moderation-based defenses that are more suitable.
5. **Method incurs high cost**: Three LLM calls per prompt (rewrite, stage 1, stage 2). The paper does not comment on inference time, which affects practical feasibility.
6. **Missing experiments and ablation studies**:
   - Heavy reliance on models with high comprehension; should test on lighter models (LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B) to assess robustness.
   - The inverse scaling finding (Sec 5.1) is under-explored. A more fine-grained family (e.g., Qwen 2.5 variants from 0.5B to 72B) is needed to see if there is a turnover point. Also, an experiment using the same first-stage model (e.g., GPT-4o) across all victim models would make the relationship clearer.
   - Section 5 (impact of model size, number of objectives) gives insights but lacks detailed discussion or further analysis behind the phenomena.
   - No new countermeasures or defense suggestions against AIR are proposed; this limits practical value.
7. **Writing quality issues** (disagreement: some found paper easy to follow, others disagree):
   - Spelling mistake in line 040 ("cyber-attacks" misspelled).
   - Latex syntax error in algorithm: “iscrossmodel”.
   - Inconsistent use of full name vs. abbreviation (e.g., “Large Language Model” / “LLM”).
   - Paper overall not well presented; the “Continue Attack” (Sec 3.2) is just a prompt-based filter, similar to prior work “LLM Self Defense: By Self Examination, LLMs Know They Are Being Tricked”.
8. **Minority positive note on readability**: One reviewer found the paper easy to follow; however, multiple other reviewers cited significant writing and presentation issues.
