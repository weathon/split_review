## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents MapEval, a benchmark for evaluating foundation models on map-based geo-spatial reasoning across three task types: textual, API-based, and visual. The dataset consists of multiple-choice questions (reviewers note ~550–700, with some reporting 500+ and others 550+; the abstract states 700) covering ~138 cities in 45–54 countries. 25 prominent foundation models are evaluated, including closed-source (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro) and open-source variants. Top models still lag human performance by >20%. MapQaTor was used to collect textual map data. The benchmark covers task types such as Place Information, Routing, Nearby, Unanswerable, and Trip.

### Strengths
- Paper is well-structured and easy to follow.
- Using real-world Google Maps data and APIs makes evaluation practical and valuable for testing geo-spatial reasoning with both internal and external knowledge.
- Three distinct environments (text, API, vision) allow comprehensive assessment while maintaining consistent context.
- Task types (Place Information, Routing, Nearby, Unanswerable, Trip) cover a wide range of real-world user scenarios.
- MapQaTor effectively streamlines collection of textual map data.
- Human-labeled data is of high quality.
- Geographic distribution across global regions is broad (though some reviewers question its balance).
- Useful distinction between open-source and closed-source model performance for future reference.
- Testing multiple models provides guidance for model selection.

### Weaknesses
1. **Small and potentially non‑diverse dataset.** With ~550–700 questions and limited samples per task, the benchmark may not fully capture real-world query diversity, is susceptible to overfitting, and may not generalize robustly. (R1, R2, R4)
2. **Only multiple-choice format.** Real-world map problems are often open-ended; the benchmark would benefit from additional question types. (R2)
3. **Geographic coverage is inflated.** While 54 countries are listed, many have only a single question (see Figure 3), creating an uneven distribution that introduces regional bias and limits generalizability. (R1)
4. **Tasks are overly simplistic for expert-level geo-spatial reasoning.** Current routing tasks ignore dynamic traffic, road closures, and alternative transportation. The design lacks complex spatial relationships, temporal dependencies, and multi-step reasoning. (R1)
5. **Missing important model comparisons.** The evaluation omits strong recent models such as Qwen2.5 series, Qwen2VL series, VILA series, MiniCPM2.5, as well as geographically specialized models (e.g., K2, remote sensing models). (R1, R2, R4) Reviewers also requested comparisons across more diverse architectures, not just versions of the same model family. (R2)
6. **Insufficient discussion of related work.** The paper does not clearly differentiate from prior work like GeoQA or GPT4GEO. (R1)
7. **Limited assessment of true multimodal integration.** It is unclear whether models jointly reason over text and visual information or treat them as separate inputs; the benchmark should test deep cross-modal reasoning. (R4)
8. **Analysis lacks depth and actionable insights.** The evaluation does not identify root causes of model failures (e.g., spatial reasoning vs. query understanding vs. API call errors). No insights are offered on how to design models that perform well across all geo-spatial tasks, nor on how the results guide practical, real-world applications using LLMs or VLMs. (R3, R4) One reviewer (R3) found the paper to be essentially an experimental report with insufficient innovation, noting that without deeper analysis the contribution feels incomplete and better suited to a data track. (R3)
9. **Missing ablation experiments.** The introduction mentions ablation experiments but none are presented in the main text. (R4)
10. **API-calling agent issues are not explained.** The paper does not describe how the agent was trained or how its problems were resolved. (R2)
