## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LASeR (Large Language Model-Aided Evolutionary Search for Robot Design Automation), a framework that integrates LLMs as intelligent operators within an evolutionary algorithm for voxel-based soft robot design. It addresses limitations of prior LLM-driven optimization—poor solution diversity and weak generalization across tasks—via two mechanisms: a diversity reflection mechanism (DiRect) that guides exploration based on past search trajectories, and task-grounded prompting that enables inter-task knowledge transfer. Experiments on EvoGym (Walker-v0, Carrier-v0, etc.) compare LASeR against baselines including standard GA, MAP-Elites, and LLM-Tuner. One reviewer (score 8) finds the framework novel and the ablations insightful; other reviewers (scores 5,6,6) raise concerns about task simplicity, missing baselines, weak diversity metrics, insufficient statistical analysis, and marginal performance gains.

### Strengths
- The paper is clear, well-structured, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,4). Figures, especially the main method figure, are high quality and effectively illustrate the multi-step process (Reviewer 4).
- The idea of using an LLM as a mutation operator in an evolutionary design loop for robots is interesting and under-researched (Reviewers 1,3). The diversity reflection mechanism (DiRect) and inter-task knowledge transfer through prompt engineering add originality (Reviewers 2,4).
- Extensive experimental results on the EvoGym platform are provided, comparing against several baselines and including informative ablation studies (Reviewers 1,3). The ablation studies on LLM parameters (e.g., temperature, prompt variants) are insightful (Reviewer 3).
- One reviewer (Reviewer 3) is significantly more positive, rating the paper 8 (vs. 5,6,6), describing the framework as novel, the conclusions relevant to the robot learning community, and the ablation studies “very interesting and insightful.”
- The problem of joint robot design and control is important and combinatorially explosive; the lack of diversity in evolutionary robotics is a known limitation that the paper targets (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Design space is too small.** Experiments use a 5×5 two–dimensional voxel grid. This limits the emergence of interesting structures and behaviors, making implications for real robotics unclear (Reviewers 1,2,4). The performance gains observed may not hold in larger, more complex design spaces (e.g., 9×9, 10×10, or 3D with hundreds of voxels) (Reviewers 2,4). More complex EvoGym tasks (Climber-v0, Catcher-v0, Thrower-v0, Lifter-v0) should be tested (Reviewer 2).
- **Statistical significance is questionable.** The reported performance gains are marginal relative to baselines, and standard deviations overlap substantially between final means (Reviewers 3,4). Only three trials are run; more trials, 95%+ confidence intervals, and statistical tests (p–values) are needed to demonstrate robustness (Reviewers 3,4). The early convergence of LASeR (with and without DiRect) relative to other methods further raises doubts about whether it reaches a global optimum or gets stuck in local optima (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons.** The paper lacks comparison with relevant prior work, including:
  - “Evolution through Large Models (ELM)” / openELM and related prompt-based LLM mutation methods (Reviewer 2).
  - Brain-body co-design methods on the same EvoGym platform (Reviewer 2).
  - Gradient-based control optimization (e.g., RSS 2024 work on VSR) (Reviewer 4).
  - The perspective piece by Stella et al. (2023) on LLMs in robot design (Reviewer 3).
  - Another LLM-guided robot design work (https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-99-3814-8_11) (Reviewer 4).
- **Diversity metrics are weak and insufficiently justified.** Diversity is measured as a weighted average of voxel edit distance and number of distinct high-performing robots, with weights set arbitrarily (1.0 and 0.1) to “roughly … give equal importance” (Reviewer 4). The number of distinct high-performers alone is a poor diversity measure because two robots can be highly similar in layout. The rationale for weighting and the choice of edit distance is not clearly explained (Reviewer 4). Additional diversity measures (e.g., proportion of different voxel types) should be reported separately (Reviewer 4). The paper does not provide concrete examples of the morphologies discovered by LASeR versus baselines, making it impossible to visually assess diversity (Reviewers 2,4).
- **Diversity reflection ablation is incomplete.** The ablation of DiRect compares against random voxel editing but lacks detail (e.g., number of voxels edited, how it compares to LLM operation) and is based on a single trial. The claim that random edits make designs worse is contradicted by the observation that random editing performance improves over time (Reviewer 4). An additional controlled ablation comparing LLM-guided mutations to random mutations at the same rate over multiple trials is needed to show that the LLM provides intelligent, diversity–promoting edits (Reviewer 4).
- **No analysis of computational cost.** The time taken by LLM-based methods (e.g., LLM–Tuner, LASeR) is not reported, which is critical for evaluating practical design efficiency (Reviewer 1). Questions about the overall computation time relative to non–LLM baselines remain unanswered (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of deeper analysis of LLM behavior.** The paper shows *that* LASeR works but does not adequately explain *why* the LLM is effective (Reviewer 2). The black-box nature of the LLM makes it hard to interpret the reasoning behind generated designs; adding explanations for decision-making would be beneficial (Reviewer 2). A detailed sensitivity analysis of different prompts (beyond temperature) is missing—the intuition behind prompt design is more important than the pipeline itself (Reviewer 2). The paper does not explore fine-tuning the LLM for the specific robot design task, which could improve performance and generalization (Reviewer 1).
- **Key hyperparameter not discussed.** The similarity threshold used in DiRect is an important parameter that governs the exploitation–exploration balance, yet its effect is not analyzed (Reviewer 1). Quantitative evidence for the claim that fitness does not change significantly after DiRect modifications (Section 3.2.1) is also missing (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing discussion of limitations and broader impact.** The paper could more critically discuss limitations, such as the simplicity of test environments, the lack of real–world transfer, and open problems (Reviewers 3,4). No explanation is given for how designs could transfer from 2D simulation to reality, or what the implications are for future real robots (Reviewer 4). The rich history of evolutionary robotics and the choice of control policy training (PPO vs. gradient–based methods) are glossed over, making robot design seem like an afterthought (Reviewer 4).
- **Inter-task generalization is poorly demonstrated.** While knowledge transfer between tasks is claimed, LASeR does not show meaningful improvements over the no–knowledge–transfer variant in the reported experiments (Reviewer 4). The generalization to tasks significantly different from those in the experiments is not tested (Reviewer 2). The question of whether LLMs can truly align robot design with task performance via prompt engineering alone, especially for complex tasks, remains unresolved (Reviewer 2).
- **Concerns about scalability and LLM effectiveness.** In larger design spaces (e.g., 10×10), random combinations of voxels may not always work well, and it is unclear if the LLM can still provide useful mutations or serve as a surrogate model for performance prediction (Reviewer 2). The early convergence of LASeR, even with a diversity–promoting mechanism, is counter–intuitive and suggests the algorithm may not be exploring meaningfully (Reviewer 4).
