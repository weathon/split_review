## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes UNIPROMPT, a prompt optimization method that treats prompt optimization as learning multiple facets of a task. It decomposes prompts into loosely coupled semantic sections, clusters the input space, and uses a two-tier feedback mechanism that aggregates edits from mini-batches into conceptual descriptions. Experiments on multiple datasets (hate speech, math, QA, ARC, MedQA) show that UNIPROMPT achieves higher accuracy than human-tuned prompts and state-of-the-art methods (OPRO, DSPy, etc.).

### Strengths
- The mini-batch strategy (creating batches, generating edits, aggregating at batch level) and the two-tier feedback mechanism are interesting and systematic.
- Experiments achieve state-of-the-art results across multiple benchmarks, with notable gains on hate speech classification.
- The method clusters examples to capture task-specific facets, and the feedback structure ensures prompt modifications are generalizable rather than overfitting to individual examples.
- The paper is well-structured and the story is well-told, starting with essential observations (prompts are decomposable, clustering helps) that motivate the approach heuristically.
- The approach reduces manual overhead by automatically generating prompts without heavy reliance on human-engineered prompts.
- Extensive ablations validate the contribution of each component (clustering, feedback structure) to performance.
- The method introduces a new perspective on prompt optimization by considering multiple facets of a task and leveraging the structure in prompts.

### Weaknesses
- The paper contains minor writing issues: typos (e.g., “UniPrompt” mentioned incorrectly in line 100-101), an extra space before “So” in line 197-198. The experiments section (4.1, 4.2, 4.5) should be reorganized and polished.
- All experiments are conducted only on powerful proprietary LLMs (GPT-4). The effectiveness on open-source LLMs like LLaMA or Qwen is untested. If the expert model is less powerful than GPTs, performance may degrade.
- The method underperforms DSPy on MedQA. What is the reason? On ARC, the optimized prompt leads to a decrease in performance compared to the Llama prompt. An explanation is needed.
- The number of clusters for mini-batch construction is a key hyperparameter. How is it determined? How does it affect effectiveness? The impact of mini-batch size and aggregation on report accuracy should be studied with qualitative experiments.
- The clustering is performed via LLM, which may not scale to large numbers of examples. Alternative clustering approaches (e.g., embedding-based) and dynamic clustering as optimization progresses are not explored.
- The experiments cover only hate speech, math, and QA tasks. The task portfolio is less diverse than in prior work (e.g., OPRO and Li et al. run on BBH with 27 tasks). Since the improvement hinges on clustering examples into meaningful subspaces, there is insufficient evidence that the method generalizes to a diverse task portfolio.
- Prompt optimization is known to be sensitive to the initial prompt and number of iterations (discussed in Sec 2.1), but reported metrics do not account for performance variation across runs. The robustness of the results is in question.
- If wrongfully predicted training samples are clustered only at the onset, how does the algorithm handle them after optimization starts? Are they used to generate feedback in later iterations?
- One reviewer is skeptical about the overall importance of prompt optimization given that modern instruction-tuned models (e.g., LLaMA-3.1) perform well zero-shot. Another reviewer assigned a contribution of 2 (lower than the others), indicating a minority view that the contribution is limited.
- A minor suggestion: add an average column in Table 1 to make results clearer.
