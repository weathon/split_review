## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tackles auxiliary learning with the goal of boosting primary task performance while keeping inference cost identical to a single-task network. The authors propose an architecture-based method that uses Neural Architecture Search (NAS) to learn an asymmetric network with only primary-to-auxiliary connections. During training, two separate task branches are connected; the NAS optimization converges to a configuration where auxiliary-to-primary connections are pruned via L1 regularization, enabling their removal at inference. The method is designed to be combined with optimization-based auxiliary learning approaches (e.g., loss re-weighting, gradient manipulation). Experiments cover six tasks on NYU v2, CityScapes, and Taskonomy using VGG‑16, ResNet‑50, and ViT‑B backbones.

### Strengths
- The paper is well written, clearly motivated, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,2,3,5).
- Table 1 provides a comprehensive and meticulously categorized survey of multi-task and auxiliary learning methods, clearly positioning the proposed work (Reviewers 1,4,5).
- The method is backbone- and task-agnostic, validated on VGG‑16, ResNet‑50, and ViT‑B across six diverse tasks (Reviewers 1,4,5).
- The idea of joint training followed by slow trimming of auxiliary-to-primary connections via L1 regularization is novel and clever (Reviewer 2). The evolving asymmetric network design, guided by a tailored NAS, guarantees that only primary-to-auxiliary connections remain, thus ensuring a single-task inference cost (Reviewer 5).
- The method can be freely combined with various multi-task/auxiliary optimization methods (e.g., loss re-weighting, gradient manipulation) as listed in Table 1; this flexibility is validated experimentally (Reviewers 3,4,5).
- Single-task inference cost is assured by the converged asymmetric architecture, while training cost scales linearly with the number of auxiliary tasks due to the supernet design (Reviewers 4,5).
- Extensive experiments on six tasks, three datasets, and three backbones (including CNN and Transformer); supplementary results further explore cases where primary and auxiliary tasks use different architectures (Reviewers 4,5). Results are promising (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
- Performance gains in Tables 3 and 4 appear marginal (Reviewers 1,2). Given the additional computational cost of the NAS search and training overhead, it is unclear whether the improvements justify the increased training complexity; a thorough trade-off analysis between performance gain and training time/resource consumption is needed (Reviewer 1). The method may be too complex/cumbersome for practical adoption given the modest gains (Reviewer 2).
- A more direct comparison with a specific baseline is missing: sharing a single backbone between primary and auxiliary tasks but projecting the auxiliary gradient onto the orthogonal direction of the primary gradient (applied to all or a subset of layers). Such a baseline would also maintain zero inference overhead; the paper should compare performance gain versus training cost against this alternative (Reviewer 1).
- The rationale for “stitching” two distinct backbones (each trained with different weights and objectives) is insufficiently justified. The paper would benefit from theoretical or empirical evidence supporting this design choice; without it the overall architecture coherence is questionable (Reviewer 1).
- The choice to initialize the search space with all bi-directional connections is not justified. Starting from networks with only primary-to-auxiliary connections could be simpler and less costly. The paper lacks an ablation study comparing performance when the search space is restricted to primary-to-auxiliary connections from the start; this would determine whether the bi-directional initialization is necessary (Reviewer 3).
- It is not explicitly confirmed that all auxiliary-to-primary connections are pruned by the end of training. A rigorous analysis of the final architecture should be provided, including statistics on the distribution of architecture weights (especially for primary-to-auxiliary connections) and consistency of the final performance across multiple random seeds/trials (Reviewer 3).
- Experimental results lack error bars, making it hard to judge the statistical significance of the reported improvements (Reviewer 2).
- The optimization details of the NAS objective (Eq. 10) are insufficiently specified. It is unclear how model weights w and architecture weights α are updated iteratively; learning rates, batch size, number of iterations per update step, and convergence criteria are missing. A strategy to prevent architecture weights from collapsing to a trivial solution should also be described (Reviewer 4).
- The paper should indicate the network backbone in the legends of Tables 3 and 4 to allow quick comparison across results that share the same table shape (Reviewer 4).
- The impact of using normalization and activation functions other than BatchNorm and ReLU (e.g., LayerNorm, GELU, Swish) in Eqs. 13‑14 should be explored to assess the robustness of the method across different configurations (Reviewer 5).
- Figure 3 is potentially misleading: the cut-off dash line should be placed between the 1×1 convolution and the add operation (where actual pruning occurs) rather than between the auxiliary input and the concatenation (Reviewer 5).
- The authors state they implement tailored versions of PCGrad and AdaShare for auxiliary learning (PCGrad‑Aux, AdaShare‑Aux). The details of these variants should be provided (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 2 suggests investigating whether further fine-tuning the final model on the primary task only (after the NAS stage) yields additional performance gains.
- Minor presentation issues: figures would benefit from vector graphics; excessive vertical spacing should be removed (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 5 suggests moving supplementary material into an appendix for better readability.
