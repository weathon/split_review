## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a meta-adversarial multi-view representation learning (MAVRL) framework that uses dual encoders, label-free adversarial latent attacks, and multi-view consistency to improve adversarial robustness in few-shot learning under domain shift. Reviewers agree the paper is well-written and the motivation is clear, but raise major concerns about novelty (the contributions are largely imported from self-supervised learning and adversarial training outside meta-learning), fairness and completeness of the experimental comparisons, insufficient task sampling (only 400 test tasks, 200 training tasks), lack of attack diversity, unclear test-time usage of the dual encoders, and the practical relevance given modern large pre-trained models. Scores: 3, 6, 5, 3 – with R2 being notably more positive (score 6) while R1 and R4 are negative (score 3).

### Strengths

- The paper is well-organized, well-written, and easy to follow. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The motivation – achieving generalizable adversarial robustness to unseen domains and tasks – is significant and well-justified. (R2, R3)
- The paper introduces self-supervised learning (bootstrapped multi-view encoders and contrastive learning) into adversarial meta-learning and demonstrates that it can improve robust accuracy on cross-domain few-shot benchmarks. (R1, R2, R3)
- Experiments are abundant, including ablation studies, visualizations, and analyses that help understand the contribution of each component. (R2, R3)
- The proposed idea (latent adversarial attack that maximizes discrepancy between two views, then maximizing consistency) is interesting. (R3)
- The paper is polished and the overall story is clear. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **W1 (Novelty & necessity of meta‑learning).** The core techniques (multi-view consistency, bootstrapped encoders, adversarial self-supervised learning) are already well‑known outside meta‑learning. The paper does little more than plug them into the meta‑learning setting, making the novelty marginal (R1). It is unclear whether meta‑training is actually needed – a baseline using a pre‑trained model (e.g., TRADES) trained on the same base dataset without episode‑based meta‑learning should be compared to verify the significance of the meta‑learning framework (R1 Q1). Additionally, the method blurs the line with general self‑supervised representation learning, and its contribution to the meta‑learning field is not clear (R1).

- **W2 (Unfair / incomplete comparisons).** Several experimental comparisons are problematic:
    - Model capacity is not controlled: the proposed method adds a second encoder, but baselines use a single encoder. No experiment matches the total capacity (e.g., ensemble or wider baselines), so the improvement cannot be attributed to the method rather than increased capacity (R1 Q2).
    - Table 1 does not include TRADES or other recent generic adversarial training methods as baselines, even though the method is built on TRADES (R3).
    - Table 3 only uses AT (adversarial training) for competitors, while the proposed method uses a different loss; this makes the comparison unfair (R3).
    - Table 2 does not compare with other adversarial meta‑learning (AML) methods (R3).
    - Selective reporting: the paper reports that MetaOptNet performs worse than MAML in both clean and robust settings, which contradicts established results (e.g., Table 6 of [d]) where MetaOptNet outperforms MAML. The authors should explain this discrepancy or correct the results (R4).

- **W3 (Limited number of tasks & high variance).** Meta‑testing is conducted on only 400 randomly sampled tasks, and meta‑training on only 200 tasks (R2, R3). With such small numbers, the variance and randomness are large, especially for adversarial training, casting doubt on the reliability of the reported accuracies (R3, R2). The impact of increasing the number of test tasks should be shown (R2).

- **W4 (Lack of attack diversity).** The paper only evaluates under PGD‑20 attacks, which is the same attack used during training. It is not shown that the robustness generalizes to other types of attacks (e.g., CW, AutoAttack, other norm constraints) (R2, R3). Moreover, the main reason the method works under the robust setting may simply be the use of PGD training, which is already standard (R4).

- **W5 (Test‑time usage of dual encoders unclear).** The framework uses two encoders during training. It is not specified how the model is used at test time – whether one encoder is discarded, both are averaged, or another combination (R3 Q2). Which kind of few‑shot classifier (e.g., prototypical, relation net) is employed is also not clarified, and no justification is given for the choice (R3 Q3).

- **W6 (Practical relevance & clean‑accuracy cost).** In the current era of large pre‑trained vision models (CLIP, MoCo), meta‑training on a small base dataset (e.g., CIFAR‑FS, mini‑ImageNet) is of questionable relevance for few‑shot learning (R4). Even in the traditional setting, the proposed method’s clean accuracies (e.g., 5‑shot: 67.75 on CIFAR‑FS, 47.56 on mini‑ImageNet, 53.70 on mini‑ImageNet→CUB) are far behind existing works like FeLMi (89.47, 86.08, 77.61), making the cost in clean performance too high for practical use (R4).

- **W7 (Missing related work & discussion).** The paper does not discuss or compare with task‑augmentation meta‑learning methods [1,2,3] which also use multi‑view or augmented tasks to improve generalization (R2). Recent adversarial meta‑learning works that already address unseen‑domain robustness (e.g., [2]) are not reviewed, and the results in [2] appear to be substantially better than those reported here (R3).

- **W8 (Little theoretical basis).** The method lacks theoretical motivation for why multi‑view consistency yields transferable robust representations; it is described as an engineering combination of existing ideas (R1).
