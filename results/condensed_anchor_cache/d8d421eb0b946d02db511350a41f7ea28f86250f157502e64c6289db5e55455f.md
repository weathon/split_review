## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a truncated Newton method for entropic regularized optimal transport, combined with conjugate gradient and mirror descent in a three-stage algorithm. The solver is GPU-parallel and scalable to large OT problems. The authors provide theoretical results (local quadratic convergence, log(1/ε) convergence under a spectral gap condition) and demonstrate favorable wall-clock performance on 4096-dimensional MNIST and color transfer problems, including an extremely large instance with n ≈ 10⁶.

### Strengths
- Interesting theoretical contributions: connection to the Bellman equation (though not claimed as first); chi-square convergence of the Sinkhorn algorithm; Theorem 3.2’s log(1/ε) convergence result (one reviewer notes this is novel except for related UOT work by Nguyen et al., JMLR 2023, and recommends citation).
- Strong empirical performance: orders of magnitude faster wall-clock time than many alternatives, with a particularly clean color transfer result.
- Detailed description of an efficient GPU implementation (including engineering improvements for stability and efficiency).
- Good discussion of related work.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical gaps**: The analysis lacks a global convergence guarantee and a full computational complexity rate.  
  * Theorem 3.2 depends on the spectral gap of \(P_{rc}\), which is uncontrolled; it could lead to very slow convergence and introduce more than \(O(n^2)\) dependence on \(n\). No bound or analysis of this gap is provided.  
  * Unlike popular OT solvers (e.g., Sinkhorn, APDAGD) that achieve \(\tilde O(n^2\varepsilon^{-1})\) total complexity, the total complexity of the proposed algorithm is unclear. The local convergence proof does not address global behavior.
- **Section 2.1 concerns**:  
  * Lines 70–92 are nearly verbatim from Kemertas et al. (2023) – this is considered a serious issue by one reviewer.  
  * Problem 2 is incorrectly defined (should be \(+H(P)\) to maintain convexity).  
  * The dual form (sum of exponentials rather than LogSumExp) is presented without derivation; the derivation should be provided in the appendix.  
  * The definition of LogSumExp (LSE) reductions (row/column, Line 066‑067) is insufficiently explained; explicit formulas for \(\text{LSE}_r(X)\) and \(\text{LSE}_c(X)\) are needed.
- **Notation and clarity**: Several notations are poorly defined or hard to find (e.g., \(\mu\), \(P_{rc}\), the various \(\epsilon\)’s, \(\lambda_2\) in Corollary 3.4, the “helper Sinkhorn” in Line 196). The paper is hard to read and proof intuition is lacking; a high‑level proof sketch (e.g., an ODE analysis) would help.
- **Experimental limitations** (raised strongly by one reviewer, with support from others):  
  * Only one figure with two examples (MNIST, color transfer) – insufficient for a practical paper.  
  * The loss plotted is the primal function value, not the duality gap; this can be misleading because it ignores constraint violation. Feasibility and dual loss should also be shown.  
  * Results are presented without statistical measures (appears to be a single run), which is problematic for wall‑clock time comparisons.  
  * All methods start from different initial points, which can bias results.  
  * Only wall‑clock time is reported; a code‑independent measure (e.g., number of gradient evaluations) is needed.  
  * No comparisons with other second‑order OT methods.  
  * No code is provided to verify the performance.
- **Missing clarifications**:  
  * What is the role of \(\tilde\epsilon\) and how does it relate to the other tolerances?  
  * Do the experiments begin with a phase of adaptive mirror descent (to find a near solution) before launching Algorithm 4? This should be clearly stated.
