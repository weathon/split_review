## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes OCEBO, a self-distillation setup that trains object-centric models from scratch on real-world data (COCO, 241k images) by updating a target encoder via exponential moving average (EMA) of the object-centric model. A cross-view patch filtering strategy prevents slot collapse during early training. When evaluated on unsupervised object discovery (FG-ARI, mBO) across MOVi-C, MOVi-E, Pascal VOC, EntitySeg, OCEBO achieves performance comparable to (but lower than) object-centric models that rely on frozen, large-scale pretrained non-object-centric encoders (e.g., DINOv2). The method demonstrates data scalability beyond a few thousand images. Reviewer opinions diverge: one reviewer (score 8, confidence 5) is more positive, emphasizing the importance of training from scratch and the novel patch filtering idea, while the others (scores 6, confidence 3–4) acknowledge the contribution but raise concerns about scalability, evaluation breadth, and missing ablations.

### Strengths
- **Novel self-distillation setup for object-centric models.** OCEBO is the first method to train an object-centric model from scratch on real-world data via bootstrapping the target encoder, removing the upper bound imposed by frozen pretrained encoders (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Effective patch filtering strategy.** The cross-view patch filtering (selecting nearest-neighbor patches) stabilizes training and prevents slot collapse, especially in the early stages. This idea is new, though its ablation is not fully explored (R2). Motivations for filtering and the mask sharpening stage are straightforward and proven effective (R4).
- **Comparable performance against large-scale models.** When pretrained only on COCO (241k images), OCEBO achieves unsupervised object discovery performance comparable to models that use frozen target encoders pretrained on hundreds of millions of images (e.g., DINO, DINOv2) (R1, R3). This is considered a success given the known challenge of training object-centric models from scratch (R2, R3).
- **Scalability beyond small datasets.** The method shows improved performance when pretraining data is increased from 100k to 200k images, indicating promising data scalability (R1, R3).
- **Emphasis on object-centric inductive biases.** The paper highlights and experimentally verifies that the EMA update enriches the target encoder with object-centric inductive biases from the student (slot attention), providing theoretical insights (R1).
- **Clarity and presentation.** The paper is well written, easy to follow, with good balance between technical details and high-level explanations. Quantitative results are accompanied by qualitative examples (R1, R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient ablation of patch filtering.** The chosen filtering heuristic (nearest neighbors) is not ablated. Alternatives should be tested: (a) gradually increasing the object-centric loss weight λ_oc from 0 to 1 while keeping all patches, and (b) randomly dropping patches with a gradually decreasing rate instead of nearest-neighbor selection (R2). The paper also does not test whether cross-view distillation is necessary, i.e., whether using the teacher’s output on the same crop as the target would suffice (R2).
- **Missing quantitative metric for slot collapse.** The claim that patch filtering avoids slot collapse is supported only by “green/red” results in Table 1. A quantitative metric, such as correlation between slots and spatial positions across images, would be more convincing (R2).
- **No downstream evaluation of slot representations.** All evaluations use segmentation-based metrics (FG-ARI, mBO). The paper does not evaluate the learned slot representations on downstream tasks (e.g., dense prediction, fine-grained understanding, classification, detection). This limits understanding of representation quality and generalizability (R1, R2).
- **Weak scaling evidence.** The scalability analysis consists of only two data points (100k and 200k images) on a single dataset; a scaling plot with smaller increments (e.g., 25k, 50k, 75k, 100k, …) is needed to judge the trend and identify potential saturation (R4). The jump is too large to assess scaling behavior (R4). Additionally, no analysis or discussion of challenges when scaling to millions of images is provided (R3).
- **Large performance gaps to methods with frozen pretrained encoders.** The performance of OCEBO is lower than that of prior object-centric models that use large-scale pretrained target encoders (e.g., DINO, DINOv2). The paper claims “comparable” but the gaps are substantial and not thoroughly analyzed. It is unclear whether the gaps stem from the method or from other factors (e.g., training strategies, hyperparameters) (R4). This also raises a high-level question: if a frozen pretrained encoder performs better, why train from scratch? (R2)
- **No direct comparability due to different pretraining setups.** Comparisons in Table 2 involve models with different pretraining methods and datasets. There is no standardized evaluation protocol, making it difficult to ascertain the true advancement offered by OCEBO (R1).
- **Missing theoretical/intuitive explanation of EMA-driven object-centricity.** The paper lacks a clear explanation of how EMA updates (using gradients from the object-centric student) lead to the emergence of object-centric representations in the target encoder. Specifically, what properties of the gradients are transferred and how they enforce object-centricity is not addressed (R3). One reviewer also questions how to ensure that the introduced inductive biases do not overly affect the model’s learning of other features, maintaining generalization (R1).
- **Omission of SPOT in introduction.** SPOT was the first work to unfreeze the encoder during object-centric training, published before FT-DINOSAUR, but it is not mentioned in the introduction. This is misleading (R2).
- **Missing reported results on ImageNet.** The paper states (L435) that “an attempt to train OCEBO on ImageNet results in a drastically lower performance” but provides no experimental results or analysis (R2, R4).
- **Impracticality for varying slot numbers.** It appears that a separate model must be trained from scratch for each number of slots (L319). If true, this is highly impractical compared to frameworks like DINOSAUR or SPOT where the slot count can be changed at inference. Clarification is needed (R2).
- **Narrow evaluation scope.** The evaluation focuses on object discovery on MOVi-C, MOVi-E, Pascal VOC, and EntitySeg; only two are real-world datasets. Additional real-world datasets (including COCO) and more object-centric tasks should be included (R4). Object-centric pre-training on simple scenes (e.g., ImageNet) is not suitable, but no clear path for curating a large-scale object-centric dataset is provided (R1).
- **Limited discussion on object-centric vs. non-object-centric data.** The paper does not discuss the potential benefits of scaling object-centric data versus scaling non-object-centric data for the target encoder (e.g., DINOv2 uses both). Why prefer object-centric data for training from scratch if stronger target encoders can be obtained with non-object-centric data? (R4)
- **Projection head design not ablated.** The paper reduces the number of prototypes L from 65536 (DINO) to 8192 for per-patch heads, citing computational cost. Both iBOT and DINOv2 find large numbers beneficial. An ablation on head design (possibly splitting object and global heads) is recommended (R2).
- **Minor issues and omissions.**
    - Notation: $nns_{n_n}$ is hard to read; suggest replacing $n_n$ with $k$ (R2).
    - Equation (1) naming: $\mathcal{L}_{oc}$ could be confused with the overall loss defined in Equation (3) (R2).
    - The global loss description (Eq. 5–6) is identical to DINO’s, but the text cites other papers; this should be corrected (R2).
    - The distance metric used for nearest-neighbor selection in patch filtering is not specified (R3).
    - The meaning of `invaug(q1)` is not explained (R3).
