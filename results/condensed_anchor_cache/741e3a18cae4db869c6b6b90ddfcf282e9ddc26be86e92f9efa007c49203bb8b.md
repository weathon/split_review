## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses the problem that Instruction Fine-Tuning (IFT) on benign data can reduce LLM security. The authors propose a Modular Layer-wise Learning Rate (ML-LR) strategy. First, they train a harmfulness classifier on the LLM’s final embeddings as a proxy. By perturbing different modules (Q/K/V, attention, etc.) and measuring proxy accuracy drop, they identify patterns: earlier layers are more sensitive, Q/K modules are more sensitive than others, and combining robust modules can become sensitive. They use a heuristic search to find a sensitive subset (Mods_Robust) and assign them a much lower learning rate (by factor ~10⁻⁴) during LoRA fine‑tuning. Experiments on Llama 2, Llama 3, and Vicuna show that ML‑LR preserves general and domain‑specific capabilities compared to vanilla IFT while lowering attack success rates on several adversarial benchmarks.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and well‑motivated method.** The ML‑LR strategy and the idea of identifying security‑robust modules are original (Reviewers 2,3,4). The paper is clearly structured, first identifying three patterns and then designing the IFT strategy (Reviewer 2).
2. **Security evaluation is thorough.** Six adversarial attack benchmarks are used and results are presented in intuitive radar charts (Reviewer 1).
3. **Multi‑model validation.** Experiments cover several open‑weights models (Llama 2, Llama 3, Llama 3.1‑8B, Vicuna), showing results are not model‑specific (Reviewers 1,2,3,4). The scanning of all modules across models is a large effort (Reviewer 3).
4. **Effective security preservation.** ML‑LR significantly mitigates the increase in harmfulness after benign IFT compared to vanilla IFT, with little impact on usability/expertise (Reviewers 3,4).
5. **Interesting auxiliary findings.** The identified patterns (layer depth vs. sensitivity, Q/K vs. other modules) are potentially useful for future research (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Fundamental methodological concern about the robustness analysis (Reviewer 1, confidence 4).** The proxy is a pre‑trained harmfulness classifier on final embeddings; when a module is perturbed, the classifier accuracy drop may simply reflect distribution shift in embeddings rather than a change in the model’s safety behavior. The three “patterns” could all be explained by amplification of perturbations through the forward pass (earlier layers amplified, Q/K affect softmax attention coefficients, combined perturbations multiply). The classifier is not retrained after perturbation, so it does not measure true safety sensitivity. This critical issue is raised by one reviewer (rating 3) and largely separates their assessment from the other reviewers.
2. **Unclear perturbation procedure (Reviewer 1, confidence 4; Reviewer 3, confidence 3).** Section 3.2 does not explain *how* modules are perturbed (e.g., noise scale, direction), making the analysis unreproducible. Additionally, the motivation for the specific perturbation magnitudes used (Line 205‑209) is unclear (Reviewer 4).
3. **Missing key baseline and related work (Reviewer 4, confidence 4).** The paper does not cite or compare against [2] (Wei et al., 2024), which also searches for safety‑critical parameter regions via pruning/low‑rank modifications. The advantages of ML‑LR over that approach are not explained or experimentally validated. Also missing reference [1] (Yi et al., 2024) showing benign IFT compromises safety.
4. **Questionable proxy training and evaluation (Reviewer 4, confidence 4).** Table 1 uses AdvBench data transformed by simple word substitution to train the proxy, then tests on in‑domain AdvBench‑like data achieving 100% accuracy. This does not demonstrate that the classifier captures general harmfulness beyond simple pattern matching. Moreover, the same dataset (AdvBench) is used to train the proxy and later as one of the security evaluation benchmarks, raising distribution overlap concerns (Reviewer 3).
5. **Counter‑intuitive loss of quality after fine‑tuning (Reviewer 2, confidence 4).** In Table 2, both IFT and ML‑LR produce lower‑quality responses than the BASE model. If fine‑tuning degrades usability, its practical value is questionable; this may indicate poor training data quality. This weakness ties to reviewer 2’s explicit question about data quality.
6. **ML‑LR underperforms against state‑of‑the‑art optimization‑based attacks (Reviewer 2, confidence 4; Reviewer 3, confidence 3).** Figure 6 shows that ML‑LR is less effective than IFT against GCG attacks when training losses are similar. The paper does not explain why this occurs, leaving incomplete evidence for robustness.
7. **Limited model architecture diversity (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).** Only LLaMA‑based models (LLaMA 2, 3, 3.1‑8B, Vicuna) are tested. Broader architectures (e.g., Mistral, Gemma) are needed to confirm generalizability, especially since ML‑LR tunes per‑module learning rates and may behave differently on other architectures.
8. **Missing ablation: zeroing out sensitive modules’ learning rates (Reviewer 1, question 2).** Because the learning rate reduction is already extreme (up to 10⁻⁴), setting it to zero would be a natural ablation to understand whether any update is harmful. This is not done.
9. **Missing analysis on longer training (Reviewer 3, question 1).** Even with very small learning rates, sensitive modules may still eventually update over many epochs/iterations, potentially eroding security. The paper does not test this scenario.
10. **Missing comparison with inference‑stage interventions (Reviewer 3, question 4).** Methods like RePE (Zou et al.) that intervene at inference, or training safety suffixes/prefixes, are not compared. Such comparisons would clarify the value of IFT‑stage tuning.
11. **Presentation issues (Reviewer 1, confidence 4; Reviewer 3, confidence 3).** Formatting: tables use vertical bars (non‑standard), Table 3 has incorrectly placed/misspelled model names (e.g., “Llama213B”). Figure 5 resolution is low with blurred words; Figure 3(b) layout does not match 3(a). Table 2 lacks visual highlights to show ML‑LR’s advantage.
