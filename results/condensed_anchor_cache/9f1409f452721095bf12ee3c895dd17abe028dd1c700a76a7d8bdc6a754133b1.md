## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a hierarchical method for generating online Chinese handwritten text lines by decoupling layout generation (autoregressive position generation with in-context learning from style references) from glyph generation (diffusion-based font synthesizer with multi-scale style encoder and 1D U-Net). Evaluated on CASIA-OLHWDB, the method shows strong performance in producing structurally correct and stylistically similar samples. Reviewers generally agree that the task is under-explored and the decomposition is innovative, but raise concerns about the novelty of the style encoder, experimental comparisons, clarity of description, and some assumptions.

### Strengths
1. **Innovative task and hierarchical decomposition**: The paper addresses an under-explored task—online Chinese handwritten text line generation—which is more complex than English due to character structure huge category count. All four reviewers agree that the hierarchical decomposition into layout and glyph generation is a novel approach that provides flexibility and a good theoretical foundation (R1, R2, R4). R4 specifically notes that no prior published work exists for this task and that English methods are unsuitable, making this a noticeable contribution. R2 highlights that this framework successfully addresses challenges specific to Chinese character diversity.
2. **Effective experimental results**: The method achieves strong quantitative and qualitative results on CASIA-OLHWDB, including best performance in purely data-driven font generation (R3). R1 and R4 highlight high performance in imitation sample generation. R2 notes thorough testing on both character and line generation with tailored metrics for layout and style fidelity, showing a well-rounded effective design. R4 finds comparative and visualization experiments for both layout and character generation convincing.
3. **Well-structured presentation (majority opinion)**: R4 finds the paper well-organized and clearly written, with helpful visualizations. R2 also acknowledges good explanation of each module and helpful visualizations. (Note: two reviewers—R2, R3—raise clarity concerns; see Weaknesses.)
4. **Potential applications**: R1 and R2 mention potential in handwriting synthesis, digital personalization, document augmentation, and data augmentation for recognition, though R1 notes limited discussion of specific use cases.

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing qualitative comparisons with prior methods and baseline**: R2 and R3 point out the lack of visual comparisons to existing methods, limiting insight into the model’s advantages in style fidelity and layout accuracy. R3 adds that very few generated visual results are provided and there is no visual comparison with the baseline (R2, R3). This makes it hard to judge whether the method advances the state-of-the-art.
2. **Novelty concerns about multi-scale style encoder and contrastive loss**: R3 argues that the multi-scale style encoder is not novel because a similar idea was proposed in [a] (2D CNN vs. the paper’s 1D CNN—a minor implementation detail). The style contrastive learning loss is also similar to the style learning loss in [b], and the combination of multi-scale features with contrastive learning is not a novel design. This reviewer questions the novelty of the style encoding pipeline. (Note: R4 praises overall novelty; the minority view from R3 should be preserved.)
3. **Clarity and completeness of method description**: R2 and R3 find the method description unclear or needing refinement. Specific points from R3:  
   – Lines 233-237 do not clearly explain how style reference samples (used as context prefixes) guide layout generation.  
   – The modality of style references (online data or offline images) is unspecified.  
   – The number of reference samples (one-shot or few-shot) is not given.  
   R2 notes that the methods section contains complex explanations with intertwined details of layout generator, font synthesizer, and style encoder, making it hard to follow. *In contrast, R4 finds the paper well-organized and clearly written, indicating disagreement among reviewers.*
4. **Limited evaluation of calligraphy style learning**: R3 notes that Section 4.3.2 lacks quantitative experiments on calligraphy styles, raising doubts about whether the Multi-Scale Style Encoder can accurately extract styles from entire text lines. R2 suggests additional experiments on style consistency across diverse text lines.
5. **Effectiveness of layout generator**: R3 observes that in the ‘Conditional’ row of Figure 7, the generated layouts (red boxes) show significant absences at the beginning of the text line, raising concerns about the layout generator’s effectiveness. R4 raises a related assumption: the assumption that character generation is independent given their positions may be too strong—does style only manifest in relative positions and sizes? The reviewer requests discussion on reasonableness and whether it limits style learning, especially for cursive connections.
6. **Limitation with cursive connections**: R1 points out that the independent generation of each character fails to capture inter-character connectivity in many Chinese calligraphy styles, leading to unnatural breaks and discontinuities. This is a significant limitation for styles with strong ligatures. R2 also asks about adaptability to connected handwriting styles.
7. **Complexity and efficiency concerns**: R1 notes that decoupling adds model complexity; independent training of layout generator and diffusion-based font synthesizer leads to a more complex optimization landscape and slower convergence. The sequential two-stage generation may introduce latency compared to a single-stage model.
8. **Missing comparisons and ablation studies**: R3 recommends comparing with style transfer-based approaches (which could be done by replacing character embedding with a CNN content encoder). R2 asks for more details on how layout-glyph separation specifically enhances performance compared to prior models. R4 questions why the two models are not jointly trained end-to-end given the unified probabilistic formulation (Equation 1). R4 also asks about handling mismatched bounding box shapes between layout and character models.
9. **Practical applicability limitations**: R1 notes the paper lacks discussion of real-world application scenarios and practicality beyond synthetic data generation. R3 points out that the layout generator requires real layouts of style references, which may not be directly available in application; the impact of using pseudo-layouts from simple extraction methods is not explored. R2 and R4 query adaptability to non-Chinese scripts or connected handwriting styles.
10. **Minor presentation issues**: R4 notes that Figures 8 and 9 lack clear sub-figure indices and that Figure 7 should explicitly label the proposed method. R4 also requests clarification: Line 285 – what does L represent? Line 230 – is the ground truth the same as the reference input in Figure 3? (These are specific technical clarifications.)
