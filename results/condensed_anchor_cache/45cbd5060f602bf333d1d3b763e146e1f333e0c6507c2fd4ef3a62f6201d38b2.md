## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Bayesian scaling laws for in-context learning (ICL), derived from the assumption that ICL approximates Bayesian posterior calculation. The scaling law relates the number of in-context examples to prediction probability via terms for task priors, learning efficiency, and per-example probabilities. The work validates the scaling law on small transformers trained on synthetic data (GINC) and on real-world LLMs, including instruction-tuned models, and applies it to study safety alignment scenarios such as many-shot jailbreaking. Experiments compare the Bayesian scaling law against baseline power law models, claim superior predictive accuracy, and examine how SFT and DPO affect ICL behavior.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an interesting and well-motivated problem: understanding ICL scaling laws and whether ICL is Bayesian (R1, R2, R4).
- The proposed scaling law is derived from first principles and offers interpretable terms (task prior, learning efficiency, per-example probabilities), which is conceptually valuable even if fit quality varies (R3).
- Experiments cover both controlled synthetic settings (GINC) and real-world LLMs, strengthening ecological validity (R1).
- The analysis of SFT’s effect on the task prior vs. in-distribution likelihood, and the finding that SFT becomes increasingly superficial (prior-only) on larger models, is interesting and practically relevant (R3).
- The demonstration that DPO destroys Bayesian ICL behavior unlike SFT provides a qualitative contrast between fine-tuning methods (R3).
- The paper is well written, clear, and has a strong narrative motivating interpretable scaling laws (R3).
- Reviewer 1 is particularly positive, rating the work 8, and appreciates the theory and comparison to prior work (R1). In contrast, other reviewers (scores 5,6,5) are more critical, especially about theoretical justification and evidential support.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical derivation for the final scaling law (Eq. 3).** The connection between the Bayesian posterior (Eq. 2) and the final functional form (Eq. 3) is unclear and insufficiently justified. The introduction of the ICL efficiency coefficient \(K\) and the weight-tying scheme appears ad hoc and not derived from first principles, making the law seem arbitrary (R3 W5, R4 W1).
- **Unclear notation and mathematical rigor issues.** The expectation in Eq. (2) is ambiguous over which random variables it is taken. In Appendix Eq. (17), the authors appear to assume \(\mathbb{E}[A/B] = \mathbb{E}[A]/\mathbb{E}[B]\), which is not generally valid and needs clarification (R2).
- **Inconsistent application of logarithms and clamping.** For non-Bayesian baselines, the logarithm is taken inside the likelihood with a +1 denominator; for Bayesian scaling laws, the logarithm is taken outside, yielding a negative log-likelihood. The use of `torch.clamp` to constrain log-space parameters to \((-20,20)\) may significantly affect results. These choices are not justified, raising concerns about the validity and comparability of experimental outcomes (R4 W2).
- **Weak empirical support for the claim that the scaling law “exceeds or matches” baselines.** Table 1 shows the best method alternates among Bayesian variants, and the gap over baseline methods is very thin. The weight-tying scheme introduces many degrees of freedom (e.g., low-rank \(P\) choices) without assessing sensitivity. It is unclear whether the DPO fit remains significant with 10× more samples (R3 W1).
- **Missing visual plots for scaling law fits on GINC data.** The reader cannot assess goodness of fit by eye. Adding plots of the scaling law curves (e.g., like Figure 6 for LLMs) would strengthen the claim of better performance (R3 W2, R3 Q7).
- **Model scaling claims confounded by training budget.** The paper makes claims about model depth/width scaling, but it is unclear whether small models were trained for the same number of steps or FLOPs. Larger models train faster (in gradient steps), so small models may be undertrained, confounding scale with optimization. At minimum, equal FLOPs should be reported for fair comparison (R3 W6). Reviewer 1 also asks for insights on the role of model width given the observation that ICL efficiency \(K\) increases with depth (R1).
- **LLM fits do not convincingly support Bayesian ICL.** The goodness of fit in Figure 6, especially for instruct models, is not persuasive. The paper claims support for Bayesian behavior, but the fits appear poor; the evidence rather suggests LLMs behave in a non-Bayesian way. The paper’s argument would be stronger if it acknowledged this limitation (R3 W4).
- **Lack of error analysis for the scaling law.** The parameters \(K, n, M\) can amplify errors exponentially or polynomially, yet no treatment is provided to suppress or control error propagation. This omission makes it difficult to assess reliability and robustness (R4 W3). Relatedly, why was NRMSE chosen over KL divergence from ground truth probabilities? Would KL divergence yield different conclusions? (R3 Q4)
- **Other minor issues:** The prior notation (\(p\) vs. \(\rho\)) is inconsistent; “task” and “distribution” are used interchangeably without clarification; the prior is excluded from \(\mathcal{M}\) in Theorem 1 without explanation; Figure 2b should explicitly label interpolation vs. extrapolation; Figure 1 could be made colorblind-friendly (R3 Q1–Q6). Reviewer 1 suggests extending experiments to non-transformer architectures (e.g., state space models) to test generality (R1).
