## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes JOOCI, a framework to disentangle “content” (linguistic) and “other” (non-linguistic) information in speech representations using two separate encoders trained with different losses. The method is evaluated on a subset of SUPERB tasks and reports improvements over HuBERT and WavLM. The four reviewers are not in agreement: three rated the paper 3 (weak reject) and one rated it 5 (strong accept). The positive reviewer found the paper well‑written and addressing an important challenge, while the other three raised serious concerns about novelty, clarity, baselines, and whether the claimed disentanglement is convincingly demonstrated.

### Strengths
+ **Important problem**: Addressing the need to capture both linguistic and non‑linguistic content in speech representations is an active and valuable research direction (all reviewers).
+ **Sound method**: The proposed dual‑encoder architecture with separate objectives is technically sound (R1, R3, R4).
+ **Strong results on several SUPERB tasks**: JOOCI outperforms HuBERT and WavLM on tasks such as speech recognition and speaker identification (R1, R2, R4). The positive reviewer (R4) specifically notes that the model “performs well on the SUPERB benchmark.”
+ **Evaluation is technically sound** (R3).
+ **Well‑structured paper** (explicitly noted by R4; R1 and R2 find it less clear).
+ **Relevance to the community**: The research community is highly interested in speech representation learning (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The approach is very close to existing works, e.g., Chan et al. (InterSpeech 2022) and Zhao et al. (EMNLP 2023) (R1). The combination of known components (GRL, teacher‑student, masked prediction) does not constitute a sufficiently novel contribution.
- **Flawed claims about SOTA**: The paper claims state‑of‑the‑art on SUPERB but only reports results on 7 out of 10 tasks (R1). On those 7 tasks, JOOCI outperforms baselines on only about half (R2). The chosen baselines (HuBERT, WavLM) are outdated and not current SOTA (R3, R4); missing comparisons to MS‑HuBERT, Data2vec, ContentVec, and SPIN (R4). The “SOTA” claim is therefore not supported.
- **Unfair advantage from RDINO teacher**: The “other” encoder uses a RDINO teacher that was pretrained on an additional 2.5k hours of data, giving JOOCI more pretraining data than HuBERT or WavLM (960 hours). This makes claims of data efficiency questionable (R4). Furthermore, the GRL module is said to prevent the other encoder from specializing in speaker information, but the RDINO teacher is trained to produce speaker embeddings—a contradiction (R4).
- **Disentanglement not convincingly shown**: The mixed results on SUPERB tasks do not demonstrate that the two encoders actually encode content vs. other information (R2). The CCA analysis in Section 4.3 does not prove disentanglement (R2); the word “prove” is too strong (R2). The ablation studies (Sec 4.1) change both DGRL and data augmentation simultaneously, making it impossible to attribute effects (R2). Table 5 has two identical “JOOCI (6-11)” lines, suggesting an error (R2).
- **Poor presentation and unclear writing**: The definition of “content” and “other” is never clearly stated; for example, is prosody always considered “content”? (R2). The name “other encoder” is confusing (R1). The model description is too brief, leaving many details to cited papers (R1, R2). There are contradictory statements: Abstract says losses are summed without hyperparameter tuning, but Sec 2.2 says GRL uses a scaling factor of 1/10 (R1). Many grammatical errors impede readability (R2). Equations have typos (Eq.1 uses index d unused; Eq.3 undefined Student^PN, Teacher^RDINO) (R2).
- **Missing comparisons and ablations**: Table 1 lacks FBANK and competitor citations (R2). MS‑HuBERT, the foundation of JOOCI, is missing from Table 1 (R2, R4). Comparisons to ContentVec, SPIN, and Data2vec are requested (R4). The impact of random vs. MS‑HuBERT initialization is not explored (R4). Table 2 compares JOOCI to adapters, which is an inappropriate comparison (R2). The “Houlsby” and “CHAPTER” adapters are not defined (R2).
- **Insufficient analysis of architecture choices**: The paper does not explore how different architectural details affect the learned representations or downstream performance (R3). The discussion is shallow, limiting the contribution (R3).
- **Experimental issues**: Figure 2 does not clearly show the effect of data augmentation (R2). The CCA similarity measure is not defined, and it is unclear how word labels are encoded (R2). The ablation study claims to separately show DGRL and augmentation effects but changes both simultaneously (R2). Section 4.2’s discussion of “higher‑level” information is vague (R2). The claim that JOOCI outperforms on “the majority” of tasks is misleading (R2). The data augmentation description (“very lightly”) lacks detail (R2).
- **Minor weaknesses**: Figure 1 can be simplified by removing hyperparameters (R3). The discussion of “Data augmentation” in line 52 feels out of place (R3). If possible, the “-” results in Table 3 should be reported to better show encoder specialization (R2).
