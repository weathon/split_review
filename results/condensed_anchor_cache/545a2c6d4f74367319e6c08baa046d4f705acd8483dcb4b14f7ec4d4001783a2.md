## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates textual hallucinations in score-based diffusion models—where individual symbols are correctly generated but assembled nonsensically—attributing the phenomenon to a local generation bias. The authors introduce the Local Dependency Ratio (LDR) to quantify this bias, perform controlled experiments on two toy datasets (Quarter MNIST and Parity Parenthesis), and show that the bias persists across architectures (CNN, MLP, UNet, DiT). They also provide a theoretical analysis for a two-layer MLP learning parity on a hypercube. Reviewers find the perspective novel and the LDR metric useful, but raise concerns about the generalizability of results, the strength of the central claim that hallucinations stem from training dynamics rather than architecture, missing architecture details, potential memorization in toy experiments, the limited scope of theoretical analysis, and the lack of evaluation on strong modern models or practical recommendations.

### Strengths
- **Novel perspective on text hallucination.** The paper offers a fresh, theoretically motivated lens on a core problem in diffusion models, shifting attention from purely empirical descriptions to local dependency analysis.  
- **Introduction of the LDR metric.** The Local Dependency Ratio is a well-designed, interpretable probing tool that quantifies how much the denoising network relies on local vs. global regions. It has the potential to benefit other fields and aid future interpretability research.  
- **Solid evaluation on controlled proxy tasks.** The construction of two suitable toy problems (Quarter MNIST, Parity Parenthesis) and the thorough evaluation across training steps, architectures (UNet, DiT, MLP), and different symbol correlations provide compelling empirical insights.  
- **Rigorous derivation and clear structure.** The paper features a sound theoretical derivation and is logically well-organized, making the argument easy to follow at a high level.  
- **Sound theoretical examination.** The analysis of training dynamics for a two-layer MLP offers a foundational explanation of why local marginal distributions can be learned over global correlations, contributing a principled account of the mechanism.

### Weaknesses
- **Central claim insufficiently supported.** The claim that hallucinations stem from fundamental training dynamics rather than architectural limitations lacks direct evidence:  
  - No proposed training paradigm that demonstrably improves global generation.  
  - Recent strong models (e.g., Stable Diffusion 3 with MMDiT) achieve significant typography improvements through architectural changes (R1), yet the paper does not test them or decouple architectural from dynamic factors with its own experiments—Figure 4 only shows multiple architectures hallucinate, not that architecture is irrelevant (R2).  
- **Evaluation not extended to strong modern models.** The paper only tests toy models on simple datasets. Key open-weight models (SD3, Flux, Playgroundv3, SD3.5, Recraftv3) that have become progressively better at text generation are not evaluated with the LDR metric, leaving unknown whether they use more global structure or rely on strong text conditioning priors (R3). Real-world applications like ImageNet generation are also not considered (R5).  
- **Theoretical analysis limited in scope.**  
  - The proof is restricted to a two-layer MLP learning parity points on a hypercube, with strong assumptions (Assumption 5.1) that may not hold for more complex architectures (UNet, DiT) or real-world data (R1, R4, R5).  
  - It is unclear whether the theory is relevant for the attention-based models actually used in diffusion (R5). The authors should acknowledge this limitation more explicitly (R1).  
- **Potential memorization in toy experiments undermines generalization claims.** The DiT model appears to memorize Quarter MNIST training samples; if it can simply memorize all training pairs, the failure to learn the global relation (e.g., \(s_1 + s_2 = s_3 + s_4\)) may be an artifact of insufficient testing on held-out examples that require extrapolation (R1). The paper does not provide evidence that the model is generalizing rather than memorizing (R1). R4 notes that memorization may be valid output, but the distinction between extrapolated and memorized performance needs clarification.  
- **Missing architecture details and capacity control.** R1 notes that the model architecture details claimed to be in the appendix are missing, especially parameter count and depth, making it difficult to assess whether the network is large enough to capture long-range dependencies. R3 asks whether UNet and DiT are compared with equal parameter counts; if not, performance differences could be due to capacity rather than architecture type.  
- **Insufficient experiments on variety.** The paper only uses two toy datasets under two experimental settings (R2). More validation on diverse datasets (including real-world language data) is needed to demonstrate generalizability (R2, R5). The symbol correlations in natural language are far more complex than simple pairwise dependencies (R4).  
- **Lack of practical recommendations or future work.** Despite identifying the cause of hallucinations, the paper does not propose any method, training strategy, or even partial mitigation to address the local generation bias (R3, R5).  
- **Dataset description error.** For the Parity Parenthesis dataset, the description “both have even numbers” seems incorrect, and the claim "random guess has 50% chance of satisfying parity requirement" is questionable without a precise definition of the parity constraint (e.g., sum or product of tokens) (R1).  
- **Clarity and readability issues.** The theoretical sections are hard to follow for many readers; interleaving more concrete examples (e.g., explicit LDR computations) would help (R3). Also, the terminology “text hallucination” may cause confusion with LLM hallucination; a clarification at the outset is advisable (R5).  
- **Figure and axis inconsistencies.**  
  - In Figure 5 (right), the LDR plot includes timesteps t=121 and t=168 even though the total timestep length is 100 (R4). This appears to be an error.  
  - The x-axis scales in Figures 4 and 5 are not aligned, making it harder for readers to correlate LDR with downstream performance (R3).  
- **Counter-example from Dyck parenthesis experiment (Section A.4.2).** The diffusion model appears to correctly learn valid Dyck sequences, seeming to overcome the local generation bias. The paper should explain why this result does not contradict the main conclusion (R4).  
- **Missing ablation on model size and training data size.** It is unclear whether the observed local generation bias is an inherent property of diffusion models or simply a result of insufficient model capacity or training data size (R4).  
- **No LDR comparison for hallucinated vs. correct samples during pretrained model sampling.** Such an analysis would strengthen the connection between LDR and generation quality (R4).  
- **Applicability to Vision-Language Models (MLLMs).** The paper does not discuss whether the same issue would appear in multimodal models that generate text conditioned on images (R5).  
- **Timestep progression interpretation.** In Figure 5, it is unclear whether the notation \(t \rightarrow 0\) means larger \(t\) corresponds to earlier/more noise. If so, the result (earlier steps focusing more locally) is counter-intuitive to the consensus that early steps generate global image aspects and later steps refine local features (R3). The authors should explain why UNet shows a clear progression from global to local while DiT does not (R3).
