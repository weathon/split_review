## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates the Neural Collapse (NC) phenomenon under the mixup training regime. Using an adapted Unconstrained Features Model (UFM) / Layer-Peeled Model, it characterizes the geometric configuration of last-layer activations for mixup samples. The authors show theoretically and empirically that activations from same-class mixup align with the classifier (simplex ETF), while those from different-class mixup form channels along the decision boundaries. Experiments across datasets and architectures validate these findings.

### Strengths
- The investigation of Neural Collapse in the mixup scenario is novel; this phenomenon has not been previously explored in this context (Reviewer 3).
- The paper provides both theoretical explanation and proof of the observed geometric structures, using a modified unconstrained feature model that is clearly presented and easy to understand (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The analytical depth, particularly the analysis of the UFM’s dependency on mixup, stands out as a significant contribution (Reviewer 2).
- A detailed examination of feature density for λ=0.5 adds a worthwhile dimension to the research (Reviewer 2).
- Extensive and clear visualizations (Figures 1–7) support the conclusions, and the results are corroborated across multiple architectures and datasets (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The paper is well-motivated, clearly written, and the theoretical results are well discussed (Reviewer 4).

*Contrast:* While most reviewers rated the contribution as fair to good (6), one reviewer (rating 5) considered the contribution limited, describing it as merely showing that mixup leads to a distinctive configuration without actionable insights. Another reviewer (rating 6, but with lower confidence) found the observations “not much of a surprise” and the results superficial for a formal paper.

### Weaknesses
- **Practical implications and connection to mixup’s success:** The paper does not adequately connect the proven geometric configuration to mixup’s empirical benefits (generalization, calibration, adversarial robustness). The analysis focuses on *what* configuration emerges rather than *how* or *why* it improves performance. The discussion linking the FashionMNIST test performance to failure to achieve the theoretical structure is not definitive. The study lacks actionable insights for practitioners (e.g., how to set λ or select mixup pairs). *Supporting reviewer questions:* How do the observed phenomena explain mixup’s improvement in generalization and calibration? How is “good generalization” in Section 1.5 justified – is it based on well-defined metrics or intuition? (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).

- **Layout, organization, and presentation:** The paper’s structure is non-conventional and hurts readability. Figures appear before they are referenced in the text; Sections 1.2–1.5 could be placed before the figures on pages 2 and 3. Experimental results in Section 2 feel out of order. Minor: typo “additionally” in Section 5.3 (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).

- **Clarity and justification of visualizations:**
  - The method of projecting activations into 2D is insufficiently explained. It is unclear whether training or test data are used, what “project activations onto the classifier” means, and how the statement “last-layer activations align with the classifier” is justified from the plots. Given that CIFAR10 and FashionMNIST have only 10 classes, direct visualization without projection is possible. Using a pair plot or scatter plot matrix (as in [1]) could provide a more conventional representation and clarify inclusion of all classes versus systematic/random exclusion (Reviewers 2, 3).
  - In Figures 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7, the gradient effects of λ are not visually distinct, making it hard to see variation. Moreover, both same-class and different-class activations at λ=0.5 use the same black color, which may cause misunderstandings despite caption clarity (Reviewer 3).

- **Theoretical limitations and strong assumptions:**
  - The analysis assumes the classifier forms a simplex ETF without providing proof or demonstration that this holds under mixup training – a very strong assumption (Reviewer 3).
  - The unconstrained features model ignores input features, only considering last-layer features and training targets. This can lead to contradictions: for example, a point mixed from two same-class examples could spatially fall into a different class’s region, creating ambiguity about which classifier component it should align with (Reviewer 3).
  - The concept of “amplification” along decision boundaries (Figure 4 caption) lacks a thorough explanation and clear evidence. The suggestion to initiate training with a fixed regular d-simplex ETF (as in [A] and [B]) could help clarify this behavior (Reviewer 2).

- **Insufficient depth of analysis:**
  - The claim that representations exhibit linearity (Section 4.4) is not sufficiently corroborated because only last-layer features are examined; early and middle layers are not investigated. The paper could also be interpreted as suggesting linearity in all layers (Reviewer 3).
  - Some claims lack rigorous justification: e.g., “reaped minimum benefits” (Section 4.2), “potential correlation” (Section 4.2) with no further investigation, and “channel collapse” (Section 4.2) is undefined (Reviewer 3).
  - The behavior shown in Figure 6 (second row) – metrics dropping at the very start, then rising, then dropping again – is not explained or given intuitive insight (Reviewer 3).

- **Specific missing experiments/ablations:**
  - Why are models kept in train mode when plotting? Would results differ? (Reviewer 4).
  - No ablation or investigation of how the results would change if the classifier were fixed to a simplex ETF (Reviewer 2).
