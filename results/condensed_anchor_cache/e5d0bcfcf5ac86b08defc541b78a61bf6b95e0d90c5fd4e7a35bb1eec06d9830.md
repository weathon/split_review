- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 1, 5, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework for generating counterfactual explanations (CFXs) using Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs). It formally defines probabilistic counterfactuals for BNNs, provides a gradient-based algorithm that averages over sampled model predictions, and evaluates on four tabular datasets and MNIST against deterministic MLP and ensemble baselines. The authors claim BNN counterfactuals are more robust, plausible, and less costly. Reviewers 1, 3, 4 gave scores of 5 while Reviewer 2 gave a score of 1, reflecting a deep disagreement about the technical soundness and significance of the work.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a clear motivation and explanation of the problem from the ground up, with well-defined metrics for evaluating counterfactual quality (Reviewer 1).
- The method is presented via an algorithm and the experimental setup is reproducible (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Proposal of probabilistic counterfactuals specifically for BNNs, using the approximate BNN output \(\tilde{B}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum f_w(x)\) and gradient-based optimization (Reviewer 3).
- Novelty: Reviewer 2 noted that this is the first explicit work on counterfactual explanations for BNNs, contributing to novelty. Other reviewers (1, 4) considered the contribution small or limited, but the novelty claim was positively noted by Reviewer 2.
- Multiple tabular datasets are considered along with MNIST, which provides visual qualitative comparisons (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The manuscript is well-structured, well-written, and the experiments are reproducible (Reviewer 4).
- Empirical results indicate that the proposed method performs well across the considered metrics (plausibility, robustness, cost) on low-dimensional datasets (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution**: The method differs from standard counterfactual generation (e.g., Wachter et al.) only by using an empirical average over model predictions rather than a single model prediction. Several reviewers questioned whether this is sufficient novelty, especially since sampling multiple models can also be achieved with ensembles or multiple training seeds (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 2 however considered it novel; this disagreement is noted.
- **Technical soundness and statistical rigor**: Tables 1 and 2 do not report standard deviations or errors, and it is unclear whether experiments were run over multiple random seeds. The results may be from a single seed with a mean over 50 test samples, and the differences between methods could lie within the standard deviation (Reviewer 2). This severely limits the conclusiveness of the findings.
- **Weak qualitative evidence**: Figure 1 is based on one sample and one random seed, making it unconvincing for concluding method superiority (Reviewer 2). Reviewers 4 also noted that the visual counterfactuals (Figures 1 and 2) look very similar and suggested moving one to the appendix.
- **Inconsistent learning objective**: The BNN is trained using linear loss while the MLP baselines use cross-entropy. This is an inherent difference that could bias the comparison; the same objective should be used (Reviewer 2).
- **Significance and scope of explainability**: Reviewer 2 questioned the overall motivation: if BNNs are not better at prediction than MLPs, then the argument for better explainability is weakened. The goal of explainability research is typically to generate explanations for the same learning algorithm, not to compare different algorithms in terms of interpretability.
- **Lack of comparison to relevant prior work**:
  - Prior work on robust Bayesian recourse (arXiv:2206.10833) is not discussed (Reviewer 3).
  - Optimization strategy is similar to generating counterfactuals for ensembles; related work on counterfactuals under argumentative ensembling (arXiv:2312.15097) should be compared, with a clear explanation of any additional mathematical nuance in the BNN setting (Reviewer 3).
  - The paper has close connections to a large body of work on robustness of counterfactuals [1-4] that is not compared. The proposed robustness metric is similar to prior works [2,3] that sample in the neighborhood of a counterfactual. The validity metric shares similarities with robustness under model changes [1-2]. The authors should compare with these baselines (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited baselines and missing ablations**: Only two baselines (MLP and ensemble) are used. Alternative robust CFX mechanisms should be evaluated in the BNN setting to compare plausibility, robustness, and validity. Validity does not seem to decrease for all datasets; this merits further investigation (Reviewer 3).
- **Practical feasibility**: BNNs are storage- and compute-intensive (HMC is used), raising questions about scalability to larger models and datasets and real-world applicability (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing details and unclear claims**:
  - Clarification is needed on the meaning of “cost” in the experiments (lines 415-416) and why BNN counterfactuals have lower cost than ensembles (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation (7): what is \(l\)? Line 192: “has \(n\) units” – notation should follow standard BNN conventions (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation (7) appears incorrect: the constraint should be \(\mathcal{B}(x_c)=1\) (Reviewer 4).
  - Line 58-59: “these limitations stem from the deterministic nature of traditional neural networks, which fail to capture the inherent uncertainty in the data” – which specific limitations are referred to? (Reviewer 1).
  - The choice of prior over model parameters is not discussed or evaluated; its role in producing robust, plausible, less costly counterfactuals is unclear (Reviewer 4).
  - Why is linear loss chosen over cross-entropy for classification? Cross-entropy is more standard and likely better suited (Reviewer 4).
  - The claim (line 71) that the method “consistently” produces counterfactuals closer to the data manifold is contradicted by the result on the spambase dataset where the ensemble method has a better LOF score (Reviewer 4).
  - MNIST is not a proper vision dataset; if claiming vision tasks, use CIFAR or CelebA, otherwise remove the claim (Reviewer 4).
  - Line 481: “we will focus” – the word “will” should be removed (Reviewer 4).
  - Typo in equation (2): \(w_i\) should be used (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient intuition**: The paper should provide intuition on how and why the improvement in plausibility arises from the BNN framework (Reviewer 3).
- **Gradient computation**: In the algorithm, are gradients with respect to \(x\) computed using gradient accumulation? (Reviewer 1).
- **Requirements consolidation**: Section 2 lists multiple requirements (sparsity, proximity, actionable, validity, plausibility). How are these connected and can be consolidated? The final experiments only evaluate three metrics (Reviewer 1).
