## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Fair Influential Sampling (FIS), a method to learn fair classifiers without using sensitive attributes during training. The key idea is to shift the training data distribution by sampling influential examples from an unlabeled pool based on a combined influence score for prediction and fairness. Theoretical analysis provides upper bounds on generalization error and fairness disparity under distribution shift. Experiments are conducted on image, tabular, and language datasets. Reviewers overall found the problem setting realistic and the algorithm technically sound, but raised substantial concerns about insufficient baselines, weak connection between theory and algorithm, missing accuracy evaluation, unclear assumptions, and incomplete discussion of related work. One reviewer (R2) was more positive (score 6) and after the rebuttal raised his score, while the other four gave 5 and remained critical. Confidence varied: R3 had very high confidence (5), R2 moderate (4), and the others low (2).

### Strengths
- The problem of training fair classifiers without access to sensitive attributes is relevant and realistic. The proposed FIS algorithm does not require sensitive attributes during training, which is a desirable property (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Theoretical analysis provides upper bounds for fairness disparity and generalization error under distribution shift (R1, R2, R5). In particular, Theorem 3.2 on the fairness disparity bound is considered mathematically interesting (R5). R5 noted that the idea that better generalization might improve fairness is an important theoretical direction.
- The algorithm is technically sound and straightforward (R3).
- The experimental evaluation covers multiple data types (image, tabular, language), which is a strength (R2, R3). R1 also found the algorithm useful on three datasets.
- The approach of sampling influential examples from an unlabeled dataset based on combined prediction and fairness influences is interesting (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**: The experimental section lacks comparisons with many relevant methods. Missing comparisons include: (a) other fair training methods that do not require sensitive attributes ([5], [6] as cited by R2; [1,2,3,4,5] as cited by R4); (b) in-processing fairness methods such as MinDiff [7] (R4); (c) fair active learning frameworks [6] (R4); (d) a conceptually similar influence-based sampling/reweighing method [1] that also does not access sensitive attributes (R3). Several reviewers (R1, R2, R4, R5) agreed that the baseline set is insufficient.
- **Weak connection between theory and algorithm**: The theoretical analysis serves as high-level motivation but has limited direct connection to the specific FIS algorithm (R3). The bounds are closely related to existing results in domain adaptation, making the theoretical contribution incremental (R3). R2 and R5 also questioned how the theory relates to the practical algorithm and to the well-studied accuracy-fairness tradeoff. R5 noted the paper says “training on datasets with a strategically implemented distribution shift” but does not clarify how this relates to “simple traditional training” or to adversarial training methods (e.g., PGD-based).
- **Missing accuracy evaluation and trade-off analysis**: The paper claims that fairness and accuracy can be improved simultaneously, but the experiments do not report model accuracy numerically (R3). Without accuracy numbers, it is impossible to assess the accuracy-fairness trade-off, which is critical (R2, R3). R4 requested a sensitivity analysis of the labeling budget versus both accuracy and fairness. R2, R3, R4 all urged explicit discussion of how the method addresses the inherent trade-off.
- **Problematic or unclear theoretical assumptions**: Assumption 3.2 is considered too strong, and the assumption (before Lemma 3.1) that the loss is bounded is uncommon and needs justification (R1). The assumption of an auxiliary hold-out validation dataset in Section 4.1.1 is unrealistic because test data distribution is unknown (R1). Definition 3.1 uses symbols P and Q inconsistently with the preliminaries, and it is unclear why the model trained on P is used instead of one trained on Q (R1). Equation (1) seems to treat x as a feature instead of the original data (R1).
- **Lack of hyperparameter and initialization discussion**: The paper does not explain how to select critical hyperparameters: the number of new examples per round (r), the number of rounds (T), and the tolerance epsilon (R1, R4). It also does not discuss how to choose the initial training dataset for warm-start or how r affects results (R1). R4 noted that hyperparameter selection is a known challenge in fair model training.
- **Computation cost concerns**: The algorithm requires pre-calculating loss values to test sample influence, which may be computationally costly (R1). First-order influence estimation should be validated, e.g., by showing actual influence versus estimated influence per sample (R3).
- **Insufficient and poorly organized related work**: The related work section is categorized by pre-processing/post-processing rather than by relevance to the paper’s contributions (R5). It does not engage with the literature on adversarial training and reliable deep learning that also use distribution shifts (R5), nor with recent work on distribution shifts in fairness (R2). The paper over-emphasizes that sensitive attributes are not used during training, but many existing methods also operate without them; the novelty needs to be clarified (R5). R2 and R5 both requested a clearer discussion of how this work differs from and relates to prior methods that train fair models without sensitive attributes.
- **Clarity and exposition issues**: Several parts lack clarity: (a) “influence function” is not clearly defined or distinguished from standard definitions in robust learning or data poisoning (R5); (b) tables have no captions (R5, minor); (c) “That” should be “that” in the paragraph before Definition 3.2 (R1); (d) symbols P and Q are not used consistently in Sections 3 and 4 (R1); (e) the algorithm description could better label the sampling strategy and the influence calculation steps (R1).
