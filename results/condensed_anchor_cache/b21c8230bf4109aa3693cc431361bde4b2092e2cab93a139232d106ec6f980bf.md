## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CASTOR, an EM-based optimization method that simultaneously learns the number of unknown regimes, the regime partition, and the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) for each regime from multivariate time series within an additive noise structural equation model. It uses a score function with a continuous acyclicity constraint (NOTEARS-style), allows both linear and nonlinear relationships via neural networks, and proves identifiability of regimes and DAGs. Experiments on synthetic and real-world data compare against PWGC, VARLiNGaM, and DYNOTEARS.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and practically relevant problem: causal discovery from heterogeneous time series composed of multiple unknown regimes. (Multiple reviewers)
- The framework is general enough to handle both linear and nonlinear causal relationships. (R2, R4)
- Fusing the EM algorithm with SEMs to simultaneously infer regimes, causal graphs, and functional relationships is a meaningful extension of prior work (Rhino, DYNOTEARS); the introduction of multiple regimes provides a new dimension. (R1) *Note: One reviewer (R2) finds novelty lacking compared to existing NOTEARS extensions.*
- The technical derivations using EM optimization to show structure identifiability appear correct (proofs not checked in detail), and the introduction of a regime participation variable is interesting. (R4)
- The proposed method can handle instantaneous (contemporaneous) causal relations. (R4)
- In experiments, CASTOR outperforms VARLiNGaM on each single regime. (R4) *But another reviewer (R1) notes that baselines (VARLiNGaM, DYNOTEARS) are not the strongest.*
- The method includes a regime detection component. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and positioning:** The proposed method is closely related to existing NOTEARS-based approaches (Zheng et al. 2018, 2020; Pamfil et al. 2020) but adds an EM loop. It is unclear why combining EM is the most efficient solution, or why a nonparametric version of NOTEARS (e.g., Zheng et al. 2020) was not considered within DYNOTEARS. (R2) *A minority reviewer (R1) considers the contribution meaningful despite limited originality.*
- **Problem setting significance and realism:** The assumption that time series are generated from a few unknown stationary regimes with sudden transitions is unnatural for many real-world scenarios. Usually nonstationarity changes gradually or continuously. The authors should provide concrete motivating examples. (R4) Additional clarity needed: are the “time partitions” (Definition 2) required to be contiguous blocks? If yes, state clearly; if no, provide motivation. Also, are similarities between regime graphs assumed or constrained? (R4)
- **Handling by existing methods:** Even under the proposed setting, the problem can be solved by existing nonstationary causal discovery methods like CD-NOD (Huang et al. 2020). The paper’s claim that CD-NOD “cannot infer individual causal graphs and necessitates prior knowledge about the number of regimes” is incorrect: CD-NOD Phase III can detect regime changes via kernel visualization, after which any stationary method (e.g., PCMCI) can recover individual graphs. What benefit does CASTOR provide over such a pipeline? (R4)
- **Regime identifiability concerns:** Theorem 1’s claim that regimes are identifiable is skeptical. Consider two regimes with overlapping distributions (e.g., normal mixtures N(0,1) and N(0,2)): it is impossible to assign each sample uniquely to a regime. The proof likely relies on additional assumptions that are not clearly stated or justified. The number of regimes also needs to be chosen, and it is unclear if it is identifiable. (R3)
- **Limited experimental evaluations:**
  * Baselines are weak: only stationary methods (PWGC, VARLiNGaM, DYNOTEARS) are compared. Stronger temporal causal discovery methods such as Rhino are missing. (R1, R2)
  * Nonstationary temporal causal discovery methods (e.g., CD-NOD) are not compared, despite being directly relevant. (R4)
  * Nonlinear settings are not thoroughly explored; more comprehensive benchmarks for nonlinear data generation are needed. (R1)
  * VARLiNGaM is misspecified for non-Gaussian distributions. (R4)
  * The relationship to change-point detection methods is not reviewed or compared. (R3)
  * Need an analysis of computational complexity or at least running times. (R2)
- **Parametric assumptions:** Like many optimization-based methods (e.g., NOTEARS), this method assumes equal-variance Gaussian exogenous noise (standard normal). This assumption is generally untestable and impractical, and when violated performance degrades. The paper does not discuss relaxing the equal-variance assumption. (R4) Furthermore, with only an additive noise model and no further constraints (e.g., equal variance), the exact DAG may not be identifiable, especially with instantaneous relations; what critical factor ensures exact identification? (R4)
- **Presentation quality:** Several reviewers note serious clarity issues. (R1, R2, R4)
  * Unclear ordering of time partitions: should time in \(u_1\) always precede \(u_2\)? (R1)
  * Role of \(\gamma_{t,u}\) is ambiguous: it appears as a continuous variable in Eq. 11, yet the likelihood (above Eq. 8) assumes a discrete regime assignment, creating a mismatch. A variational inference perspective might clarify. (R1)
  * Mixing coefficients \(\pi_{t,u}(\alpha)\) in Eq. 5 are not defined – what is \(\alpha\)? Need a physical meaning from a definition perspective, not just a parameter choice reference (Samé et al. 2011). (R4)
  * Why spell out \(Pa(<t)\) and \(Pa(t)\) separately in expressions like Eq. 2? (R4)
  * The term “heterogeneous time series” is used without rigorous definition; in causal inference, heterogeneity typically refers to individual-level variation, not regime-switching. (R2)
  * The claimed contribution of handling “heterogeneous time series” should not be made without a clear statement. (R2)
  * Section 1 contains numerous formatting flaws (citing and capitalization). (R2)
  * Several typos in the appendix (e.g., Eq. 22 missing equation number, \(p^{(l)}\) undefined, \(\pi\) in Theorem 2 may be different from main text). (R1)
  * The method is categorized as “score-based” but should be specified as “optimization-based” (NOTEARS-type) to avoid confusion with true score-based methods like GES. (R4)
- **Missing ablation and derivation details:**
  * Derivation of Eq. 11 (E-step update) is not shown. (R1)
  * Does the method output a specific DAG or an equivalence class (given instantaneous relations)? (R4)
  * How to choose the number of regimes? (R3)
- **Generalizability:** One reviewer questions whether output can truly be called a causal graph due to the absence of scale invariance in score-based methods. (R2)
