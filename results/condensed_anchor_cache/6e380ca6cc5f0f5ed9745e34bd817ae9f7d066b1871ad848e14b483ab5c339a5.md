## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers find the RadEyeVideo approach—integrating radiologists’ eye-tracking data as video sequences for CXR analysis—novel and promising, with notable performance improvements (up to 25.4% on Impression generation, average 7.9%) using general-domain LVLMs. However, significant weaknesses are raised: limited dataset (MIMIC-Eye, 2,298 images) raising generalizability concerns; high computational overhead from increased tokens; unclear methodological details (video generation, frame sampling, gaze point radius, in-context example selection, Figure 2 ambiguity); reliance on eye-tracking data at inference hindering real-world deployment; lack of a related work section; and insufficient comparisons with task-specific medical LVLMs. One reviewer (Score 6) rates the experiments as comprehensive across multiple tasks and datasets, while the other three (Scores 5) judge the single-dataset evaluation insufficient and point to overfitting risks.

### Strengths
1. **Unique contribution**: RadEyeVideo captures both temporal and spatial dynamics of radiologists’ gaze by converting eye-tracking data into video sequences, a novel use of scan-path information that goes beyond static heatmaps or text prompts (R1, R2, R3, R4).
2. **Significant performance gains**: The method improves Impression generation by 25.4% and yields an average improvement of 7.9% across all tasks, outperforming models specifically trained for CXR report generation on the benchmark dataset (R2, R4).
3. **Thorough evaluation of eye-tracking techniques**: The study provides the first comprehensive comparison of video prompts, static heatmaps, and text-based eye-tracking prompts, establishing a new standard for integrating gaze data (R1, R4). One reviewer (R3) specifically notes that experiments are comprehensive, covering multiple diagnostic tasks across datasets.
4. **Clear presentation**: The paper uses precise and concise language, making the methodology and results easy to follow (R2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited dataset size and generalizability**: The evaluation uses only a single dataset (MIMIC-Eye, 2,298 CXR images), which is small and may not capture real-world variability. This raises concerns about overfitting to dataset-specific characteristics and limits applicability to different imaging protocols, patient demographics, and disease manifestations (R1, R2, R4). In contrast, one reviewer (R3) describes the experiments as comprehensive across multiple datasets, indicating a disagreement.
2. **High computational overhead**: Converting eye-tracking data into a video format substantially increases the number of tokens, leading to greater GPU memory usage and longer inference times. The paper samples 16 frames but does not analyze the computational trade-offs (e.g., increase over image‑only or text‑only prompts) nor explore more efficient alternatives such as non‑uniform sampling, key frame selection, optical flow, or compressed representations (R1, R4).
3. **Methodological clarity issues**:
   - Figure 2 does not clearly distinguish between text descriptions and video inputs, making it ambiguous whether textual prompts are used alongside or instead of video (R1).
   - The process of generating the video sequence from raw eye-tracking data is not explained in sufficient detail (e.g., how eye-movements are sampled, how frames are constructed, how the “duration” parameter controls frame count) (R1, R3, R4).
   - The choice to uniformly sample 16 frames is not justified, and the impact of using more or fewer frames on model performance is not explored (R1, R4).
   - The radius size of gaze points used for heatmap generation is not specified (R3).
   - In-context learning examples are provided but the paper does not discuss what constitutes suitable examples or how example quality influences results (R3).
   - The paper lacks a distinct related work section, missing reference to recent evaluations of Med-LVLMs (e.g., MedVH, MedThink, CARES) (R2). Reference formatting issues are also noted (R2).
4. **Real‑world applicability concerns**:
   - The approach requires synchronized eye-tracking data at inference time, which is resource-intensive to collect, disrupts clinical workflow, and limits scalability. Practical deployment challenges (availability of equipment, fallback mechanisms when eye-tracking is unavailable) are not addressed (R2, R4).
   - Handling multiple radiologists’ individual differences in gaze patterns is not discussed, and there is no consideration of synthetic eye-tracking data generation to broaden applicability (R3, R4).
   - One reviewer (R2) suggests that temporal/spatial information could be used only in training, with inference simulating a zero-shot scenario to ease real-world deployment.
5. **Limited comparisons**: The evaluation mainly compares against a small set of general-domain LVLMs with minimal domain tuning. A wider range of task-specific medical LVLMs should be included for a more comprehensive assessment (R2).
