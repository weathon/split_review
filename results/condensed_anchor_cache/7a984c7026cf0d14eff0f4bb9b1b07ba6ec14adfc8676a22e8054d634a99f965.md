## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Time-equivariant Contrastive Learning (TC) for longitudinal medical image analysis. TC uses an encoder to project two unlabeled scans from different time points of the same patient into a representation space and trains a temporal equivariance module to predict the representation of a later visit from an earlier visit and the time interval between them. An invariance loss is applied to a projection of the representation space to encourage robustness to irrelevant transformations, and a regularization loss term prevents the displacement map from collapsing to zero. The method is evaluated on two longitudinal ophthalmic imaging datasets, outperforming several equivariant contrastive baselines. The approach is clinically significant for capturing irreversible anatomical changes due to disease progression, but is limited to irreversible diseases with scans acquired at discrete intervals.

### Strengths
- The medical application (disease progression in longitudinal images) is compelling and the modification to contrastive objectives is clearly motivated by clinical significance.
- The proposed TC method achieves highly competitive accuracy scores compared to the evaluated baselines on two temporal eye scan datasets, demonstrating superiority over existing equivariant contrastive techniques.
- Figure 1 is very helpful in explaining the method.
- The addition of the regularization loss term to prevent collapse of the displacement map is interesting and its effectiveness is demonstrated in experimental results.
- The temporal equivariance module allows direct prediction of representation transformations, enabling generation of future image representations without requiring corresponding patient scans (beneficial when timely scans are unavailable).
- Constructing contrastive pairs for each patient visit enriches the dataset and promotes smooth training.
- The method addresses the challenge of capturing irreversible anatomical changes (e.g., degenerative diseases) where contrastive learning has not previously been leveraged effectively.
- The potential impact on patient care (timely and personalized interventions) is clearly articulated.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and exposition**: The introduction is muddled, with unnecessary details making it hard to identify key ideas and limitations of existing work. One reviewer (R1) rated presentation as poor, while two others rated it good. English issues and improper use of \citet/\citep are present.
- **Limited scope and strong assumptions**: The method is designed only for irreversible diseases with monotonic non-decreasing progression (displacement change must be >0). This assumption excludes conditions with plateaus, temporary reversals, or stability (e.g., healthy patients with no change). The method’s utility seems confined to this specific scenario, and its broader medical applicability is unclear.
- **Thin comparative analysis**: Only three baselines are tested; performance gains over them are minor. Error bars are missing, so it is unclear whether improvements are statistically significant or robust to random seeds. A deeper analysis of why TC outperforms (or underperforms) existing methods is needed, including discussion of steerable equivariant representation learning (Bhardwaj et al. 2023) and the large body of medical image registration literature.
- **Hyperparameter and loss term details**: 
  - Hyperparameters (especially loss term coefficients) are selected “to bring the magnitude of the loss terms in the same range,” but the rationale is unclear. What happens with original VICReg hyperparameters?  
  - The regularization loss term in Equation 4 includes an unexplained constant “+1”; it is unclear why this prevents collapse to zero and how the term relates to a normal L2 loss on the MLP adjusted by τ.  
  - The structure of ψ (Equation 2) is insufficiently described; it cannot be just a simple MLP given its role in predicting future representations. The exact architecture and design choices need elaboration.  
- **Missing ablations and sensitivity analyses**: 
  - Ablations only remove one loss component at a time; comprehensive sensitivity analysis on loss coefficients is needed.  
  - Additional analysis of how loss terms evolve during training (e.g., monotonic decrease) is expected.  
- **Lack of qualitative results**: The paper does not show predicted future representations or displacement maps, nor does it compare actual vs. predicted images from a patient’s last visit. Such visualizations would strengthen the evaluation.  
- **Data and anomaly robustness**: 
  - No discussion of how the method handles imaging artifacts, motion blur, noise, or variations in acquisition parameters.  
  - The method’s reliance on specialized, hard-to-obtain temporal medical datasets is a bottleneck; experiments on non-medical datasets would support generalizability.  
- **Computational complexity**: No explicit breakdown of parameters, training time, or memory requirements, which is important for clinical deployment.  
- **Unclear definition of “time”**: In scenarios where a healthy individual has multiple identical scans, the time equivariance module may collapse; the paper does not address this.  
- **Other questions with critical implications**: 
  - Would using negative time (predicting previous images from current) enlarge the dataset? Feasibility of inverting the transformation is not discussed.  
  - Can the model handle 3D scans (in addition to 2D) to learn richer representations?  
  - The term “CIn testing” in Section 6 is undefined.  
- **Missing related work discussion**: The paper does not compare or contrast with steerable equivariant representation learning (Bhardwaj et al. 2023) or state-of-the-art medical image registration methods, leaving gaps in justification.
