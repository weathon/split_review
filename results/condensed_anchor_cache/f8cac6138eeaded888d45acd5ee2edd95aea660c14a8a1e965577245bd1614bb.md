## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes PRIME, a method for identifying and describing failure modes in trained image classifiers. Unlike prior work that clusters misclassified images in latent space and then attempts to label clusters, PRIME first tags each image with human-understandable concepts (using a pretrained model, RAM) and then searches over tag combinations to find minimal, low-accuracy subgroups. The authors argue this interpretability-first approach avoids the mismatch between feature-space similarity and semantic similarity that hurts clustering-based methods. Evaluation on multiple datasets includes generalization tests, new metrics (AUROC, etc.) based on CLIP embeddings, and comparisons to DOMINO.

### Strengths

*   The paper addresses an important and timely problem: providing human-understandable descriptions of failure modes in black-box image classifiers, which is critical for trust and safe deployment (Reviewers 2, 3).
*   The approach is novel in that it prioritizes interpretability by starting with tags rather than clustering. This directly tackles a known drawback of prior work – that similarity in representation space need not imply similarity in semantic space (Reviewers 1, 3).
*   PRIME is simple, clear, and reproducible as presented; the criterion of searching for a *minimal* set of tags is intuitive and has not been discussed in the error-discovery literature before (Reviewer 2).
*   The paper includes an instructive analysis of CLIP embedding limitations (Section 5.2), which is timely given the popularity of such embeddings in error discovery (Reviewer 2).
*   Experimental validation covers multiple datasets and several dimensions (generalization to unseen images, generated images, and tag quality), with visual examples that illustrate the benefits of the method (Figures 1, 3, 5) (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
*   The paper is well-organized and easy to follow (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

*   **Heavy reliance on a single pretrained tagger (RAM).** The method is bottlenecked by the tagger: failure modes due to concepts not in `T_c` (especially rare tags that are filtered out) will be missed; tag inaccuracies (false positives/negatives) directly propagate to downstream analysis; the tagger likely has biases and is not comprehensive (e.g., may fail on domain-specific datasets like chest X-rays). Reviewers 1, 2, and 3 all raise this as a major concern. No ablation with other general-purpose taggers or experiments on domain-specific models are provided. Additionally, the paper does not report precision/recall of RAM on any dataset (e.g., by comparing to available attribute labels), which would validate the reliability of the first step (Reviewers 2, 3).
*   **Insufficient detail on hyperparameters and selection method.** Many hyperparameters (`b_i`, `a`, `s`, `l`, tag-confidence threshold) are listed but their selection is only vaguely justified (“we generally use…”). No sensitivity analysis or specific range/selection methods are given (Reviewers 1, 4).
*   **Generalization claims are questionable.** The claim that accuracy drop should “translate” from `D` to `D'` because they come from the same distribution is not a valid generalization argument. Additionally, the experiment on generated images is interesting but fine-tuning on the training data nullifies any claim of generalization. Reviewer 1 says this dismisses all generalization claims. Reviewer 3 notes that the results in Figure 4 could be more rigorous by reporting the correlation between accuracy drops in training and test sets for each subgroup.
*   **Lack of transparency in caption generation and figure selection.** The process for using ChatGPT to turn tag sets into descriptive captions (Section 4.2) is not explained, and no qualitative verification that generated captions match their groups is provided. It is also unclear how images were selected for Figures 1, 3, and 5 (random or chosen), raising the possibility of cherry-picking (Reviewers 1, 2).
*   **Evaluation metrics are themselves suspect.** The proposed metrics (including AUROC) are based on yet another auxiliary model (CLIP), suffering from the same dependency as the tagger. The pipeline (RAM → tags → ChatGPT → caption → CLIP embedding → distance) makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions from these metrics, and some values are not very different from those of DOMINO (Reviewer 1).
*   **Interpretation of tag intersections can be ambiguous and potentially misleading.** For example, the group “fox+white” may not actually reflect foxes with white fur – “white” could tag any image containing that color. Intersections of tags may lead to spurious or unintended interpretations (Reviewer 2). Relatedly, the analysis in Section 5.2 of similarity between representation space and semantic space is flawed: representation space stores fine-grained details that tags may not capture, so a high number of overlapping tags in nearby images is expected, and the analysis does not account for this (Reviewer 1).
*   **Lack of comparison to state-of-the-art methods and insufficient detail on prior work.** The paper mentions other studies (Eyuboglu et al., 2022; Jain et al., 2022) but does not elaborate on their strengths/weaknesses or quantitatively compare PRIME to the latest methods for failure mode detection and description (Reviewer 4).
*   **Missing discussion of limitations.** Several reviewers note that the paper lacks a dedicated limitations section, despite many important limitations (dependence on RAM, hyperparameter tuning, generalizability, etc.) (Reviewers 1, 2).

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* While all reviewers identify similar concerns, Reviewer 3 (score 8) is notably more positive about the approach and evaluation, whereas Reviewer 1 (score 5) is most critical, especially regarding the soundness of claims and evaluation methodology.
