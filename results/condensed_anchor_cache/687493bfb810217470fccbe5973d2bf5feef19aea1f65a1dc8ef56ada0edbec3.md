## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper identifies an empirical phenomenon: removing certain training examples improves unsupervised contrastive learning performance. The authors develop a theoretical framework using a simplified augmentation similarity graph (Figure 3: adjacency matrix entries only 1, α, β, γ) to show that difficult-to-learn samples negatively impact generalization in spectral contrastive learning. They propose solutions: example removal, margin tuning, and temperature scaling to enhance generalization bounds. Empirical validation is provided on small datasets (CIFAR, Tiny ImageNet). The paper is motivated by the observation that, unlike supervised learning, difficult-to-learn examples are detrimental in unsupervised contrastive learning.

### Strengths

- The idea of determining difficulty-to-learn examples is intuitive and interesting (R1).
- A simplified theoretical framework (Figure 3 adjacency matrix with four possible entries) allows tractable analysis with spectral contrastive loss (R2).
- Based on the framework, the paper provides valuable analysis for Margin Tuning and Temperature Scaling, clearly explaining why and how those methods work (R2).
- Novel insights into the impact of difficult-to-learn examples in contrastive learning (R3).
- Theoretical analysis is robust and proposed solutions are straightforward to implement (R3).
- Any advancement in theory for difficult-to-learn examples in contrastive learning is of interest (R4).
- Empirical results show improvement on classification tasks (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Terminology confusion**: The paper focuses on *difficult-to-learn pairs* (different classes close to the boundary) but uses the term "difficult-to-learn examples" without rigorous definition; definitions of difficulty-to-learn or easy-to-learn pairs are not rigorous (R1).
- **Theoretical framework concerns**:
    - In developing Theorems 3.1, 3.2, etc., it is unclear how the feature extractor *f* is trained. If learned using Eq. (1) (HaoChen et al.), the relevance of α, β, γ is unclear (R1).
    - The idealized adjacency matrix *A* (only entries 1, α, β, γ) is used throughout Section 3 & 4; the connection to real-world continuous similarity entries is weak. The only justification (lines 164-166: "difficult-to-learn examples lie around the decision boundary, so they should have higher augmentation similarity to examples from different classes") is ambiguous intuition (R2). There is a lack of intuitive or empirical justification linking the idealized model to practice; suggestions include relaxation (intervals, stochastic) or visualization/quantitative assessment on small-scale datasets (R2).
    - In the proof (Eq. 22, 23, lines 1035-1039), the discretized adjacency matrix from Figure 3d is used to derive specific eigenvalues and eigenvectors for solving ||Ā - FF^T||_F^2; this does not convince for a general similarity matrix, and similar issues affect other theorems and corollaries (R1).
    - The inequality around line 212, “(1-α)+r(γ-β) / (1-α)+nα+nrβ+n_d r(γ-β) > (1-α)/((1-α)+nα+nrβ)”: when does this hold in practice? Is it practical for most datasets? (R2)
    - The condition at line 1019, “when n_d < k ≤ n_d + r + 1,” appears strict; further explanation is needed (R2).
    - The tightness of the derived bounds should be validated through experiments (R4).
    - The inferior performance of hard samples is natural from a similarity graph view (misconnections in neighborhoods) and is likely when class numbers are small or batch size is large. Experiments in Figure 1 should include datasets like ImageNet (many classes) and discuss the impact of batch size (buffer size in MoCo) (R4).
    - Can the analysis be generalized to InfoNCE loss? Paper [1] (ICLR 2024) shows InfoNCE is also related to similarity graph decomposition (R4).
- **Limited scope and practicality**:
    - Theoretical results are limited to spectral contrastive learning; the title should reflect this focus (R3).
    - The paper appears to be a combination of HaoChen et al. and Zhou et al. (R1).
    - Experiments are humble and small-scale; the largest dataset is Tiny ImageNet. Larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet) are strongly encouraged to demonstrate broader applicability and practical impact (R1, R3, R4). The limited scale raises questions about generalizability to more complex real-world scenarios (R3).
- **Experimental methodology issues**:
    - The generating process of γ-Mixed CIFAR-10 is missing; the paper only states "mixing at pixel level" (lines 106-107) – details needed (R2).
    - In Table 1, showing additional statistics (e.g., number of removed datapoints overall and per class) would be beneficial (R2).
    - Line 344-345: what features are used? Is the network pre-trained on some dataset before unsupervised contrastive learning? (R2)
    - In Figures 4 & 5, what are the other hyper-parameters when tuning the plotted parameter? (R2)
    - Is Section A.4 about the combined method? (R2)
- **Heuristic selection**: The method for selecting difficult-to-learn examples is very heuristic and only loosely connected to the theory (R1).
