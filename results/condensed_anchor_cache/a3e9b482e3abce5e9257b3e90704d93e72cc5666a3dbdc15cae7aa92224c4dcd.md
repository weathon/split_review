## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CLIP-Enhance, which improves CLIP zero-shot classification by reinterpreting it as a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) clustering problem on a hypersphere and optimizing the mixture model via self-supervised teacher-student distillation. Reviewers’ scores range from 3 to 5 (multiple 3s, two 5s), reflecting mixed opinions on novelty, evaluation completeness, and fairness of comparison. Some reviewers found the vMF formulation interesting and the ablation convincing, while others raised major concerns about missing baselines, unfair comparison settings, insufficient analysis, and parameter sensitivity.

### Strengths
1. The idea of reformulating CLIP zero-shot classification as a clustering problem on a hypersphere using a vMF mixture model is interesting and provides a new angle (R1, R2, R5, R6).
2. The closed-form approximation of the vMF normalization constant is novel (R2).
3. Ablation results validate the effectiveness of vMF distribution modeling (R2).
4. The self-supervised learning framework (inspired by DINO) is used to improve text–image alignment (R5).
5. Detailed analysis of CLIP’s limited performance due to misalignment and data imbalance (R4).
6. The method performs well on several benchmarks under limited compute/data resources (R3).
7. The knowledge distillation with confident view selection is reasonable, and convergence of vMF clustering can be mathematically substantiated (R3).
8. Training vMF weights on test data yields performance improvements on multiple datasets (R6).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited evaluation and missing baselines**  
   - Missing results for CALIP and TPT on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and MNIST (R1).  
   - Missing results on widely used zero-shot evaluation datasets: Caltech101, Aircraft, Stanford Cars, UCF101 (R1), plus OxfordPets, ImageNet-V2/A/R/Sketch (R3, R4).  
   - Missing comparison with several related works ([1],[2],[3],[4]) and an important work with the same goal (R2).  
   - Ablation studies use only naive TPT as baseline; at minimum CoOp+TPT (which outperforms naive TPT) should be included to support SOTA claims (R3).  
   - Experiments on only 10 datasets are insufficient (R3).  
   - Table 1 contains “N/A” entries for open-source methods like TPT; why were these not reproduced? (R4)

2. **Unfair comparison setting**  
   - CLIP-Enhance adapts to the entire test set (unsupervised fine-tuning on all data), whereas TPT adapts to and evaluates on one test sample at a time (single-sample test-time adaptation). This makes the comparison in Table 1 unfair (R4, R6).  
   - The paper incorrectly describes TPT as “online test-time adaptation”; TPT does not accumulate updates across samples (R6).  
   - The authors should consider a single-sample adaptation setting for a fair comparison (R6).

3. **Inadequate explanation of how the method addresses the claimed challenges**  
   - It is unclear how the vMF mixture model addresses (a) the misalignment between text and image embeddings and (b) the long-tailed distribution of CLIP’s training data. The approach simply tunes the text embedding matrix (W) in a teacher–student setup similar to TPT (R6).  
   - No in-depth analysis of how misalignment affects downstream performance, especially when multiple classes share similar visual features (R1).  
   - The reported improvement in “alignment” (Table 2) is too small; stronger metrics or visualizations of the embedding space and clustering quality are needed (R1).  
   - No explicit component for vision–text alignment appears in Figure 2 and no explicit constraints enforce it; is alignment merely a byproduct of self-supervised training? (R5)  
   - Addressing the long-tail issue by constructing a balanced dataset is impractical because real-world data are often long-tailed and collecting equal samples per class is infeasible (R5).  
   - To support the long-tail claim, ablation on long-tailed downstream tasks is necessary (R2).

4. **Parameter sensitivity and robustness**  
   - The initial concentration parameter κ₀ is sensitive: when κ₀=5000, performance drops drastically on F‑102 (67.3→11.3) and MNIST (64.95→53.1). Further analysis across different κ₀ values and datasets is needed to verify robustness (R1, R6).  
   - The chosen κ₀=3000 may not generalize to datasets with varying CLIP similarity ranges (R6).  
   - How does the learned concentration parameter κ differ across classes? Statistics or analysis should be provided (R2).  
   - What are the performance gains from confident view selection, and why is the top‑10% threshold used? (R2)  
   - An ablation on the component F_d(W) in Eq. 5 is needed (R6).  
   - The condition “when all concentration parameters are equal” (Line 228) is not clarified (R6).  
   - Why not tune existing CLIP blocks or add an additional projector? (R2)

5. **Parameter / computational efficiency claims**  
   - The claim of parameter efficiency compared to previous tuning methods should be supported by reporting FLOPs, number of trainable parameters, or wall‑clock tuning time (R2).

6. **Writing, presentation, and scientific rigor**  
   - The prompt template used is “This is an image of a [CLASS]” instead of CLIP’s default “A photo of a [CLASS]”. This is a basic inaccuracy and raises concerns about attention to detail; all references and derivations should be carefully checked (R3).  
   - The related work section is cluttered and should be organized with headings (R6).  
   - Concepts in Lines 248–252 are confusing (R4).  
   - The method lacks visual demonstrations of results; such figures should be added in the appendix (R3, R4).  
   - The self‑supervised training approach is not new; it is a simplified version of SimCLR/BYOL (R5).

7. **Focus on image encoder only**  
   - The current method mainly modifies the image encoder; consideration should be given to generating better text features from the CLIP text encoder (R5).
