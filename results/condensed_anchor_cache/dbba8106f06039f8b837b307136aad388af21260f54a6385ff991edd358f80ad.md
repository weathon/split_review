## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Unified Semantic Similarity Metric Benchmark (USMB), composed of 5 tasks and 30+ datasets, to evaluate text similarity metrics. It compares pre-existing metrics (cosine similarity, BM25, Levenshtein distance, Rouge score, Jaccard similarity) and finds that cosine similarity scores highest among them. A task-specific ensemble of these metrics yields a 40.3% improvement over cosine similarity. Reviewers broadly note that the problem is relevant and experiments are broad, but raise major concerns about novelty, evaluation setup, theoretical grounding, and generality.

### Strengths
- Tackles an appealing and widely used problem: questioning the de facto use of cosine similarity for text embeddings, which is of broad interest to NLP/ML practitioners (R1).
- The experimental setup with perturbations (e.g., Fig. 1 and 2) is novel and offers valuable insights into metric behavior under controlled distortions (R1).
- Combining multiple similarity metrics is a promising direction, likely because different metrics capture unique aspects of similarity (R2).
- The experiments cover multiple datasets and embedding models, providing a useful overview of individual metric strengths and limitations (e.g., cosine similarity’s poor robustness) (R2).
- Provides a benchmark for semantic text similarity with data and baselines, along with a clear description of background knowledge needed to follow the work (R3).
- Comprehensive comparison of metrics and neural models within the proposed benchmark (R3).
- A good reminder that combining multiple types of similarity (string-based and embedding-based) is likely to outperform cosine alone (R4).

**Note**: One reviewer (R3, rating 6) is substantially more positive than the others (R1: 5, R2: 3, R4: 3), particularly regarding the benchmark as a contribution and the clarity of presentation. The other reviewers find the contributions marginal and the evaluation setup flawed.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and overclaiming**: The empirical takeaway “ensembling improves scores” is trivial (R1). Claiming this as a benchmark proposal is overclaiming, as the work simply reuses existing datasets with some perturbations (R1). It is already well known in retrieval and MT evaluation that combining string-based (sparse) metrics like BM25 with embedding-based metrics gives better results, especially when training is involved (R4). The paper makes inaccurate claims about lack of prior work (lines 41–45, 511–516) and the abstract stating “relatively little research in how to best utilize embeddings” (R4). The benchmark feels like an afterthought, and the title misaligns with the paper’s primary focus on ensembling metrics (R2).

- **No theoretical contribution**: The paper offers no theoretical insights (e.g., building on Zhelezniak et al., NAACL 2019) into why cosine similarity works or fails, which ICLR readers would expect (R1). The chosen similarity aspects lack theoretical grounding and clear motivation (R2).

- **Faulty evaluation setup**:
  - The ensemble is directly trained and tested on the same benchmark (train/test split of the benchmark’s datasets), so it is optimized for those specific data nuances. This inflates its performance compared to static, unoptimized metrics like cosine similarity. To assess real-world utility, the ensemble should be tested on entirely different datasets (R2, R4). The setup may not generalize to new data or tasks (R4).
  - Runtime and computational demands of the ensemble are not compared with individual metrics (R2).
  - Performance variation across different embedding models is ignored; averaging scores across models conceals important interactions (e.g., strong embedding models may benefit less from metric aggregation) (R4).

- **Problematic assumptions in the robustness/sensitivity tasks** (R1, R2, R4):
  - The linearity assumption that similarity decreases proportionally with text length (e.g., adding a needle 100% the length of original text reduces similarity by 50%) is simplistic and not validated across a large number of texts. Summaries can be much shorter yet semantically highly similar, so content relevance matters more than length (R1, R2). The approach does not distinguish between semantically unrelated, redundant, or adversarial added text (R2). The three assumptions (lines 250–256) are ad hoc, especially the third (R4). An alternative would be to test task performance under different perturbations directly (R4).

- **Limitations of the benchmark** (R4):
  - The “human preference alignment” task is limited to comparing machine-generated text with human text, and humans may not have access to the reference, solving a different task. It does not compare automatic scores with human-assigned similarity scores.
  - Robustness data is restricted to summaries alone.

- **Incomplete related work and baselines** (R2):
  - Discussion omits important metrics for neural representation similarity, such as CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019) and generalized shape metrics (Williams et al., 2021). These should be compared. Overall, the experiments lack stronger baselines.

- **Wording and presentation issues** (R2, R4):
  - Wording is sometimes imprecise and difficult to parse (e.g., lines 105–107, 178–180) (R2).
  - Section 2 lacks clear formalization; the problem of comparing two arbitrary texts is not formally defined (e.g., TF-IDF introduces documents but their role is unclear). String-based and embedding-based metrics should be clearly distinguished (R4).
  - There is an anonymous reference error at line 289 (R3).

- **Dependence on monolingual (English) datasets** (R3): The benchmark only uses English. It should define the language(s) used and elaborate on possibilities for multilingual extension. Also, whether OOD evaluation (e.g., domain shift) could be managed by the robustness section is unclear (R3).

- **Training-test metric discrepancy**: The validity of using cosine similarity at test time may depend on which metric (cosine, inner product, L2) was used during training of embedding models, especially in contrastive learning. This is not discussed (R1, question).

- **Lack of detail on the needle perturbation**: The content of the “needle” (irrelevant or adversarial piece of text) is not clearly specified – are there multiple types? (R2, question). How are categorical decisions made when metrics output continuous scores? (R2, question).
