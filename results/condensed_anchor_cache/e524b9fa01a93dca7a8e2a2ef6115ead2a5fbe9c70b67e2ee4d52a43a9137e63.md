## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper applies mechanistic interpretability methods to analyze visual question answering (VQA) in Llava, focusing on color-related tasks. It compares VQA with textual QA (TQA) in Vicuna, finding that VQA shares a similar in-context learning mechanism to TQA, that visual features are interpretable when projected into the embedding space, and that visual instruction tuning enhances Vicuna’s existing abilities. Based on these findings, the authors introduce an interpretability tool that identifies important image patches for predictions, claiming faster and more effective interpretation than existing methods.

### Strengths
- **Relevant and intriguing research problem**: The paper addresses a current, important need for mechanistic interpretability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). One reviewer (R1, rating 5) found this highly intriguing; others agreed the objective is relevant.
- **Novel comparative perspective**: The paper compares attention mechanisms in VQA (Llava) and TQA (Vicuna) for the same color task, offering a fresh viewpoint on how visual and textual modalities might be processed similarly (R1, R2).
- **Detailed mechanistic analysis**: The investigation into attention heads, embeddings, and log-probability dynamics in both Vicuna and Llava is informative and empirical (R3, R4). The comparison of attention patterns across models is a strength (R4).
- **Interpretability tool with computational efficiency**: The tool identifies key image regions and is described as computationally efficient, enabling near‑real‑time interpretations (R3, R4). It provides an intuitive way to locate important patches (R1, R2).
- **Empirical validation on color prediction**: The authors back their claims with experiments on color-based VQA, including an analysis of attention heads before and after visual instruction tuning, demonstrating how Llava enhances Vicuna’s capabilities (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow experimental scope limits generalizability**: All experiments are conducted on color-related questions only; the paper does not test spatial reasoning, object counting, fine-grained recognition, or abstract visual concepts. The conclusions may not extend to other VQA types, and generalizability to other MLLMs (beyond Llava/Vicuna) is not established (R1, R2, R3, R4). Evaluation on real-world (not synthetic) data is also lacking (R4).
- **Limited technical novelty**: The core hypothesis for textual QA is highly similar to that in [1] (Figure 1 of this paper vs. Figure 1 of [1]), and the visual QA analysis methodology largely parallels the textual QA analysis. The paper extends existing interpretability techniques (e.g., log-probability increases) to a specific color task without introducing fundamentally new methods or theoretical insights. Findings are not surprising (R2, R3, R4).
- **Insufficient baselines and quantitative comparison**: The paper does not compare its tool against standard gradient-based attribution methods (e.g., Integrated Gradients, Grad-CAM, SmoothGrad) or perturbation‑based techniques. It also lacks a comparison with Gandelsman et al. (2023) (text-based decomposition of CLIP). No quantitative evaluation against other interpretability methods is provided (R3, R4).
- **Presentation quality issues**: Figure 4 is poorly labeled and difficult to interpret; Figure 1 lacks a legend for its green, blue, and pink colors (R1, R3). The paper incorrectly claims that Figure 3 displays results across 1,024 attention heads, which is inaccurate (R3). There are spelling errors: “Figure 3” in line 368 likely should be “Figure 5” (R1); “serious” in line 134 should be “series” (R4).
- **Lack of rigorous theoretical foundation for the interpretability metric**: No formal justification is given for why log-probability increases should provide superior interpretability compared to attention scores, especially since attention is explicitly designed to capture input-output relationships (R3).
- **Missing verification of a key hypothesis in Section 3.2 (line 225)**: The paper does not provide evidence for a stated hypothesis; this is needed to support the mechanistic claims (R1).
- **Unclear definition of probability function in Equations 6‑7**: It is not specified whether the probability function involves a softmax over the entire vocabulary or a more localized calculation; the mathematical operation and inputs need clarification (R1).
- **No analysis of failure cases or limitations**: The paper does not discuss scenarios where the interpretability tool might produce misleading results, such as complex visual contexts with multiple objects of the same color, or other failure modes (R3). The meaning and scope of “visual hallucination” as addressed by the study is not clearly defined (R2).
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (R1, score 5) was very positive, emphasizing the intriguing research problem and novel perspective. However, the majority of reviewers (R2, R3, R4, scores 3) identified the above weaknesses as substantial, particularly concerning novelty, scope, and evaluation.
