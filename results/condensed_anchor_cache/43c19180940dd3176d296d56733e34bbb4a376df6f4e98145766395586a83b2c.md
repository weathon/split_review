## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Active Multi-task Fine-tuning (AMF), an algorithm for adaptively selecting tasks to demonstrate when fine-tuning a pre-trained generalist robot policy under a limited demonstration budget. AMF selects tasks that yield the largest information gain on the expert policy, using approximations with Gaussian processes (AMF-GP) or neural networks (AMF-NN). The authors provide theoretical convergence guarantees under smoothness assumptions. Experiments in a 2D integrator, FrankaKitchen, MetaWorld, and with the Octo model show that AMF outperforms uniform task selection, especially when pretraining data is skewed. However, reliance on parameter isolation is critical for AMF-NN performance, and without it AMF often matches uniform selection.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a timely and relevant problem: efficiently fine-tuning generalist robot policies, which are becoming increasingly important in robotics. [Reviewer 3]
- The authors provide a compelling justification for why active learning could be useful in robot learning, noting that prior works use more bespoke methods for task distribution decisions. [Reviewer 4]
- The paper includes theoretical performance guarantees: it proves that under Lipschitz continuity and other assumptions, the optimality gap decays as O(info_gain * n^{-1/2}), and that the fine-tuned policy converges to the expert policy in smooth MDPs. This adds credibility to the algorithm. [Reviewers 1, 2, 3]
- AMF demonstrates improvements over uniform sampling, particularly when the pretraining data is biased towards a subset of tasks. This is valuable as real-world pretraining datasets are often unevenly distributed. [Reviewers 1, 3, 4]
- The algorithm is adjusted to be applicable to deep learning-based policies (AMF-NN) and is evaluated in high-dimensional robotic environments (FrankaKitchen, MetaWorld), showing practical applicability. [Reviewers 2, 3, 4]
- AMF was also applied to the large-scale pretrained Octo model, demonstrating its potential beyond small-scale setups. [Reviewers 2, 3]

### Weaknesses
- **Assumptions limit real-world applicability**: The theory assumes a noisy expert policy that is a perturbation of an optimal policy, with well-characterized stationary noise. Human demonstrations, which are common in real-world robot learning, are often suboptimal, inconsistent, and do not satisfy these assumptions. This severely limits the method's applicability to physical robot tasks. [Reviewer 1, confidence 3]
- **Weak baselines and missing comparisons**: The only baseline is uniform task selection. The paper does not compare against simple, natural alternatives such as balancing the task distribution across the entire dataset (including pretraining data), or selecting the task with the fewest demonstrations so far. Such baselines could be strong and would clarify whether AMF provides a benefit beyond naive balancing. [Reviewers 1, 4; Reviewer 2 asks about model merging (arXiv:2310.01362)]
- **Parameter isolation is critical but problematic**: AMF-NN relies heavily on parameter isolation (separate weights per task partition) to achieve improvements. Without parameter isolation, AMF-NN performs similarly to uniform selection (Octo/WidowX in Fig. 15; no explicit ablation for AMF in other domains—only uniform is ablated in Fig. 5/7). This is a significant limitation because parameter isolation effectively transforms the multi-task problem into separate single-task problems, may lead to overfitting on individual tasks, prevents positive transfer across tasks, and increases the total number of parameters. [Reviewers 2, 3, 4; Reviewer 2 explicitly states “performance gain is likely due to this overfitting rather than a superior multi-task learning strategy”]
- **Performance gains are small and diminish quickly**: In many settings, even the friendliest ones with skewed task distributions, the improvement from AMF over uniform is modest and often closes by 10 rounds (e.g., Fig. 4). When the pretraining distribution is already uniform, AMF offers little to no advantage. The added complexity of AMF may not justify these marginal gains. [Reviewers 3, 4]
- **Uncertainty estimation is fragile**: AMF-NN's effectiveness depends on accurate uncertainty quantification via loss-gradient embeddings (Eq. 2). Uncertainty estimation in neural networks remains challenging, and the proposed method may not capture true uncertainty, especially in underrepresented regions. It also does not address overconfident predictions. This can lead to selecting tasks that provide little information gain. [Reviewer 3]
- **Computational cost**: Evaluating the information gain criterion can be computationally expensive, especially with many tasks or long trajectories. The computation of loss gradients for each task and the embedding process is demanding, potentially limiting scalability to larger models and more complex robotic systems. [Reviewer 3]
- **Benchmarks are relatively simple**: FrankaKitchen and MetaWorld have narrow initial state distributions and short task horizons, which do not fully capture real-world complexity. More challenging benchmarks (e.g., RLBench, RoboSuite, ManiSkill, BiGym) with diverse initial states, longer horizons, and complex interactions would be needed for comprehensive evaluation. [Reviewer 3]
- **Octo evaluation is limited**: The Octo experiments only fine-tune the action head on a scenario with relatively uniform pretraining distribution. This does not fully assess AMF's potential with large-scale generalist policies under skewed pretraining. [Reviewer 3]
- **Empirical importance weights may be unreliable**: With a budget of only 20 demonstrations, it is questionable whether the empirical importance weights can accurately approximate high-dimensional trajectory spaces. [Reviewer 4]
- **AMF-GP vs AMF-NN presentation imbalance**: The main paper spends significant space on AMF-GP, which is only used for the simple 2D task, while the more relevant AMF-NN could be described in more detail, including design choices for kernel approximation, batch selection, and parameter isolation. Algorithm 1 could be replaced with a full description of AMF-NN. [Reviewer 2]
- **Questions raised about specific aspects** (where they overlap with criticisms or missing ablations):
  - What is the trajectory count per task at the end of each round? Is AMF doing more than balancing trajectory counts across tasks? [Reviewer 1]
  - Do the simulated robot environments and expert policies satisfy the assumptions in Section 4.1? If not, what are the implications for performance? [Reviewer 1]
  - For AMF-NN (Eq. 2), how is conditional entropy estimated? Is it by comparing samples from the initial pretrained policy? [Reviewer 2]
  - How is the task-space partitioning done? Are different weights used for each task? Does this scale to large task spaces and larger models? [Reviewer 2]
   - Why is parameter isolation ablation only done for uniform? How does AMF perform without parameter isolation in FrankaKitchen and MetaWorld? [Reviewer 2]
  - What tasks are selected by AMF over each round? A plot of selected tasks alongside per-task success rates would be useful. [Reviewer 2]
  - In Figure 4, why does the red curve always drop in the first round across all tasks? [Reviewer 3]
- **Minor issue**: The points in Figure 2 are not well aligned. [Reviewer 3]

Overall, reviews are split: reviewers 2 and 3 are more positive (score 6) citing principled approach and timely problem, while reviewers 1 and 4 are more critical (score 5) pointing to limited baselines and over-reliance on parameter isolation.
