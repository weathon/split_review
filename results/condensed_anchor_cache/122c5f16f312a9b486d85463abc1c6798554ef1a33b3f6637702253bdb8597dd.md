## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper defines an LLM lie as a false statement despite the model being able to produce the correct answer in a standard prompt (inconsistency). It proposes a black-box lie detector that works by asking a fixed set of 48 unrelated yes/no follow-up questions after a suspected lie and feeding the answers into a logistic regression classifier. The detector is trained on examples from GPT-3.5 explicitly prompted to lie and surprisingly generalizes out-of-distribution to other architectures, fine-tuned liars, sycophantic lies, and real-world role-playing scenarios (e.g., sales). The core idea is simple and novel, and the findings are intriguing. However, some reviewers found the definition fuzzy, the evaluation scenarios limited, the method under-ablated, and the paper's organization and formalization lacking.

### Strengths

- **Surprising and impactful generalization.** The lie detector transfers across LLM architectures, fine-tuned models, sycophantic lies, and real-life scenarios, despite being trained only on GPT-3.5 prompted to lie explicitly. This suggests a consistent lie-related behavioral pattern, enabling low-cost, general-purpose black-box detection.
- **Practical black-box design.** No access to model activations or internal states is required. The method only needs a fixed set of yes/no answers from the LLM, making it applicable to models with limited API access.
- **No ground-truth knowledge needed.** Unlike methods relying on assistant LLMs that may be less knowledgeable, the detector does not need to know the true answer to the question under evaluation.
- **Well-controlled for spurious correlations.** Extensive ablations show the detector does not simply pick up on repeated lying behavior or other superficial cues (e.g., ambiguous questions also predict lying).
- **Smart use of unrelated elicitation questions.** The fixed set of 48 questions is explicitly optimized for lie detection, opening follow-up work on behavioral baselines.
- **Detailed appendix.** The appendix provides comprehensive dataset details, experimental setup, lie generation methods, elicitation questions, additional results, prompts, and role-playing scenarios, making the work reproducible.
- **Novel problem definition.** Formalizing lying as inconsistency in knowledge (LLM produces a false statement while being able to produce the correct one) provides a clear operational target for reliability safeguards.

### Weaknesses

- **Fuzzy definition and insufficient formalization (minority reviewer R3).** The definition “outputting false statements despite ‘knowing’ the truth in a demonstrable sense” is considered fuzzy. A model that is non-robust (prompt-sensitive) could be labeled a liar under this definition. A more formal treatment of lying/deception (e.g., from Schneider et al., 2020) is suggested. Also, no pseudocode or algorithmic description of the method is given; the paper jumps straight into experiments.
- **Limited scenario evaluation for lying instructions.** Only direct lie prompts (“Lie when answering the following question”) are tested. Reviewers note that “consistent lying” (e.g., a system prompt like “Lie to the first question and then treat your answer as truthful to avoid detection”) is not evaluated. Different kinds of lies and lying strategies are discussed only superficially.
- **Lack of ablation on the number and type of elicitation questions.** The set of 48 questions (some factual, some ambiguous) is arbitrary. No experiments show whether a smaller subset (e.g., only 2 ambiguous questions) achieves similar performance, or whether the detector saturates with fewer questions. A feature-selection study (lasso) is requested by R4. This also raises concern about robustness and redundancy.
- **Brittle labeling for correctness.** Correctness is defined as whether the LLM’s response contains the ground-truth entity. This fails for cases like “The capital of France is not Paris” (incorrectly labeled correct) and does not handle synonyms or paraphrases that are factually correct but do not contain the exact entity.
- **Lack of descriptive analysis of what the classifier learns.** The mechanism connecting lying behavior to answers on unrelated questions remains mysterious. A deeper, more intuitive explanation of the thought process and underlying inductive biases would help understanding.
- **Missing comparative experiments.** No comparisons against alternative black-box lie detection approaches are provided; the paper would benefit from demonstrating a clear advantage over baselines.
- **Presentation and organization issues.** The paper is difficult to follow: Sections 4 and 5 are scattered (dataset presented in 4.1/4.2, methodology in 4.3/5.2), many details are deferred to the appendix (e.g., finetuning details) causing non-linear reading. Figure captions are inconsistently placed (Figure 4 below, Figure 5 above). Numerical results are given inline rather than in tables, hampering recall. The introduction is very short and includes contribution points that belong in the main text, weakening the overall structure.
