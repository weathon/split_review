## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a multiagent approach for LLM self-improvement finetuning. Multiple agents are initialized from the same base model and assigned distinct roles (generation and critic). Using multi-agent debate, each agent generates its own training data, and agents are independently finetuned on their unique datasets. This process is iterated to enable multiple rounds of self-improvement. The method is evaluated on three math reasoning benchmarks (Arithmetic, GSM, MATH) and shows improvements of approximately 1-15% over baselines. The authors also demonstrate that finetuning multiple agents preserves diversity better than single-agent finetuning.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized and easy to follow (Reviewer 1, 2).
- The proposed approach jointly optimizes generation and critic agents, which enhances reasoning ability and allows multiple rounds of iterative self-improvement; this is a promising direction for autonomous LLM improvement (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).
- Finetuning multiple agents on independent data from multi-agent debate preserves response diversity, which is a critical challenge for LLM finetuning (Reviewer 2, 3).
- Evaluation results across 4 baselines show consistent performance gains on reasoning tasks (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).

### Weaknesses
**Major concerns (all reviewers):**

1. **Dataset scope and difficulty** – All experiments use only math reasoning tasks (Arithmetic, GSM, MATH with only first three difficulty levels). These tasks are not particularly challenging; a single LLM may suffice, making the need for multiple agents questionable. Including graduate-level math or datasets from other fields would strengthen generalization claims (Reviewer 1). Additionally, only 500 training examples are used from GSM and MATH, which does not follow standard evaluation procedures (Reviewer 3).

2. **Incremental performance gains** – The performance improvements on these modest tasks are not substantial, especially given the overhead of multiple large models. The paper should quantify other metrics (e.g., KL divergence from the original LLM distribution) to assess whether multiple agents achieve better results without large parameter changes (Reviewer 1).

3. **Lack of explicit diversity mechanisms** – The paper claims specialization and diversification among agents, but no explicit measures enforce heterogeneity across generation agents; diversity only arises from the number of agents. There is no theoretical guarantee or conceptualization of how specific agents differ or which sub-tasks they capture (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 also notes that Figure 3 suggests diversity gains may come entirely from the initial improvement; the paper does not report initial diversity or how the diversity metric evolves over iterations for each ablation, nor whether the metric is sensitive to response types.

4. **Role specialization and loss functions unclear** – The paper uses generation and critic agents but does not clearly specify the objectives and loss functions optimized for each role (Reviewer 2). The terminology “Single Agent” vs “Multi Agent” is vague (e.g., Sec 2.2 discusses multiple agents under “Fine-tuning Single Agent”) and may confuse readers (Reviewer 3). The title “Multiagent Finetuning” implies a broader scope than the paper’s focus on multi-agent debate for reasoning (Reviewer 3).

5. **Zero-shot generalization claims not convincing** – Section 4.3 evaluates only 100 random samples per held-out benchmark; the rationale for this sample size and selection criteria is not given. Standard errors are missing from Figure 5. Only one cross-dataset direction (MATH→GSM) is reported; other directions (e.g., Arithmetic→GSM) are not shown, and consistency across subsets is not verified (Reviewer 2).

6. **Computational and resource trade-offs not quantified** – The increased computational demands of training and deploying multiple agents are acknowledged but not quantified. Details on training time per iteration, scaling with number of agents, memory requirements, and inference cost are needed. A possible baseline to reduce cost is to use a single LLM with a unique identifier per agent; this comparison is not performed (Reviewer 2, 3).

7. **Limitations discussion is too brief** – The limitations section lacks detailed analysis of bottlenecks, constraints, scalability, potential failure modes, or future research directions (Reviewer 2).

8. **Standard error computation** – How the standard errors in Table 2 are computed (e.g., samples used for standard deviation) is not explained (Reviewer 2).

**Minor concerns:**

- The abstract sentence “A set of language models are initialized…” is lengthy and could be simplified (Reviewer 2).
- Citation formatting issues: `\cite{}` used where `\citep{}` is more appropriate (Reviewer 2).
- Figure 4: multiple dots per method and their ordering are not explained (Reviewer 2).
- Algorithm 1’s “Combination of datasets” (line 25) and the role of the hyperparameter are not clearly defined; the mixture of query-response and feedback data for training the critic agent needs explanation (Reviewer 1).
- The paper does not discuss whether the method can integrate with preference-based approaches like DPO or PPO (Reviewer 1).
- The claim that the approach “promotes diversification within the society of models” (Line 77) is not justified (Reviewer 3).
- How fair comparison with baselines is established is unclear; the method appears to combine multiple components (A+B+C), and it may be unfair to compare an ablated variant (B+C) against B alone without ablating A (Reviewer 1).
- The effect of varying the number of agents (beyond 3–5) on performance and resource consumption is not explored (Reviewer 1, 3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 (score 8) was notably more positive about the significance and clarity of the work, while Reviewers 1 and 3 (both score 6) had stronger reservations about the experimental scope, depth, and clarity.
