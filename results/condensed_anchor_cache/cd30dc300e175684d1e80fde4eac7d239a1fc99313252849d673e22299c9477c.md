## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Derivative Causal Models (DCM) to model causal relations when variables are observed at different scales (e.g., combining discrete events and continuous states). DCM uses bijective transformations (including derivatives and integrals in the specialization ∂CM) to decompose complex relations into simpler linear ones under three constraints. A greedy score-based algorithm (Greedy Derivative Model Search, GDS) is proposed to discover DCMs/∂CMs from data, and results on synthetic data and a real-world gaming scenario are reported. Reviewer opinions range from critical (score 3) to supportive (score 6), with general agreement that the problem is practically meaningful but concerns about clarity, rigor, and experimental depth.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and problem framing**: The paper tackles a practically meaningful and important scenario where variables have different scales; the problem of mixing observations at different scales is pressing in real-world applications, and DCMs constitute an interesting formalization of the problem (R2, R3). Reviewer 3 notes the idea is worth presenting and discussing at the venue.
- **Algorithm**: The Greedy Derivative Model Search (GDS) algorithm is well presented and explained in the appendix, and it is capable of handling transformation constraints while yielding a valid DCM (R1, R4). R1 also notes the code is provided, aiding reproducibility.
- **Theoretical exposition**: The manuscript has an excellent presentation and describes its formal models with rigorous writing, e.g., introducing theoretical constraints C1–C3, Theorem 3.1 on admissibility, and Definition 2 for DCM (R4). R1 and R2 also find the model seems reasonable.

### Weaknesses
- **Definition and clarity of “scale”**: The concept of “scale” is key but only vaguely defined as “the nature of certain variables to exhibit different qualities in observation.” This is either too vague or contrasts with the more intuitive meaning (variance); it remains unclear how to know when a variable is measured at the “right scale” (e.g., for the watering example, by second, hour, day, or week) (R2, R3, R4).  
- **Lack of rigor in key mathematical foundations**: The integration of transformations and causal variables is not rigorously grounded. The authors do not discuss how the framework handles transformations \(f(x)\) where \(f^{-1}\) does not exist (R1). Theorem 3.1 is not an actual proof—it merely restates constraints C1–C3, and it is insufficiently formalized (what is an “evaluation”? “with respect to”?) (R1, R3).  
- **Missing identifiability analysis**: The GDS algorithm is a GES-type method, but no identifiability condition or identification analysis is provided for learning causal structures from data (R2).  
- **Assumptions and constraints**: The method relies on three key constraints (C1, C2, C3) for correctness, but there is no discussion of how common or extreme these constraints are, nor what happens if they are violated. The trade-off between expressiveness and admissibility is not explored (R1, R4). Reviewer 1 notes this may rule out a large portion of practical use cases.  
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation**: The results section is extremely small, limited to a single table comparing to only two methods. The experiments do not convincingly demonstrate the modeling benefits of DCM, the paper’s main contribution. Section 4.2 on game dynamics is brief; longer and more insightful experiments are needed (R1, R4). Reviewer 4 notes the results in Figure 3 are not discussed for insights.  
- **Missing related work and background**: Many relevant works are not cited (e.g., on multiscale causal structures, deliberate undersampling, temporally aggregated time series) (R1). Background on CCM and CMC is insufficient—these are models from a small research area and need more explanation (R2, R4).  
- **Presentation issues**: The introduction should be self-contained but starts by referring to the abstract; abbreviations like SCM are used without definition (R1). Some paragraphs are too long (e.g., page 4) (R2). The methodology for DCM discovery is not sufficiently explained in the main paper, with the algorithm relegated to the appendix (R3). Figures aside from Figure 2 do not add information (R1).  
- **Compact representation claim**: Section 3.2’s reduction in node count does not actually compact the graph or operations—it only affects drawings, similar to drawing an edge \(W \to S\) without latent intermediate variables in the classical SCM (R3).  
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms**:  
  - How common are the assumptions? What if they are violated? (R1)  
  - How does the method compare to other published works? (R1)  
  - For the watering example, why would a lagged causal model be more convoluted, and why is the claim of independence between watering event and flower size valid? One would expect \(W_t\) and \(F_{t+\varepsilon}\) to be marginally dependent given appropriate lag (R3).  
  - When composing functions \(\phi_i^{(l)}\circ X_i\), are these implicitly structural equations? The set \(\mathbf{F}\) is defined but never used (R3).  
  - How is the semantic grouping performed in the game dynamics experiment? Is the method applied to tabular data or directly on visual frames? (R3)  
  - What are the challenges of GDS implementation (complexity, magic numbers like repeating backward-forward 5 times, time to convergence)? (R4)  
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: While Reviewer 3 is more positive about the problem and formalization, Reviewer 1 is highly critical of the theoretical grounding and evaluation; Reviewer 4 acknowledges strong theoretical presentation but finds experiments unconvincing. The definition of “scale” and the proof of Theorem 3.1 are criticized by multiple reviewers as unclear or insufficient.
