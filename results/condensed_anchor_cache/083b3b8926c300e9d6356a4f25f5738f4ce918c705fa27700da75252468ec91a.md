## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a systematic framework based on k-NN label clusterability to detect and fix label errors in safety/toxicity datasets used for training harmless language models. Applied to 11 datasets from Jigsaw Civil Comments, Anthropic Harmless & Red Team, and PKU BeaverTails & SafeRLHF, it finds an average of 6.16% label errors. Cleaning these datasets improves data credibility and downstream classification performance (e.g., F1 gains on Civil Comments). A human study confirms that ~70% of algorithm-detected labels are indeed mislabeled, with a reported 90% cost reduction versus manual cleaning.

### Strengths
*   The problem of label quality in safety data is important, trendy, and underappreciated; the paper directly addresses a critical need in training harmless models.
*   The method is simple and intuitive, offering a straightforward approach to detecting mislabeled examples via nearest-neighbor agreement in embedding space.
*   Results are convincing and impactful: cleaning data consistently improves performance even on the original (uncleaned) raw data, which is a strong and practical result.
*   The framework reduces human effort for cleaning and is generalizable beyond toxicity detection to other noisy-dataset tasks.
*   A human study (on Civil Comments) confirms the algorithm’s effectiveness, and the overall analysis of existing dataset errors is interesting and valuable.

### Weaknesses
*   **No external validation for credibility metric.** The improvement in “data credibility” is measured using the paper’s own formulation, not an independent standard, so this is a sanity check rather than evidence of real-world effectiveness.
*   **Heavy reliance on embedding quality and potential bias.** The method assumes embeddings (from BERT/GPT-2) reliably capture safety-relevant features. However, these models are themselves trained on potentially biased data; it is unclear whether the embeddings truly reflect safety, and they may simply reproduce training-set biases. One reviewer (score 5) considered this a fundamental design concern.
*   **Presentation issues.** Descriptions of key components are unclear or missing: definition of “Data Credibility” and the role/interpretation of singular values in the transition matrix; the intuition behind “consensus vectors” and why optimizing them estimates the transition matrix; details on which embeddings are used (e.g., sparse vs. dense) and whether similarity is computed on full text or per turn; thresholds for detecting mislabeled samples, and whether parameters are constant across datasets or tuned per dataset. One reviewer (score 5) rated presentation as “1 (poor)” and found the method straightforward but the exposition “overly convoluted,” with many definitions not actually used.
*   **Limited and opaque human evaluation.** The controlled human study is restricted to a single dataset (Civil Comments), limiting generalizability. The paper does not report the number of annotators per example, nor inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen’s / Fleiss’ Kappa), making it difficult to assess the reliability of the “ground truth” labels—especially critical for ambiguous harmfulness/toxicity judgments.
*   **Metrics for RQ1 and RQ2 are confusing.** The sampling design precludes measuring recall or precision directly. RQ2 appears to compute “recall” from a sample where the algorithm already predicted mislabeling, which is methodologically backwards.
*   **Artificial use of Anthropic Harmless dataset.** That dataset contains pairwise preferences, but the paper treats them as pointwise labels, which may create artificially noisy labels. A more natural target would be detecting incorrect pairwise rankings.
*   **Concerns about novelty and generalizability.** The method is not novel; similar k-NN based label cleaning exists. Testing is limited to older models (BERT, GPT‑2). For a more powerful classifier (e.g., GPT‑3.5), the embedding space may be less capable than the classifier’s own reasoning, potentially causing the approach to enforce “simpler heuristics” instead of catching nuanced errors. Testing on newer models would strengthen impact.
*   **Unclear handling of dialogue structure.** In dialogue datasets (e.g., Anthropic), a label of “harmful” may refer only to the user question rather than the full context. Table 2 examples suggest this ambiguity is not addressed.
*   **Unexplained performance drop on “obscene” category.** Table 8 shows GPT‑2 and BERT perform worse on the “obscene” category after cleaning; no explanation is provided.

**Note:** One reviewer (score 5, confidence 3) was notably more critical than the others (scores 6), rating presentation as “1 (poor)” and contribution as “2 (fair).” This reviewer emphasized that the method is straightforward but the presentation is convoluted, raised fundamental concerns about embedding reliability, and found definitional details unexplained.
