## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses two vulnerabilities in vertical federated learning (VFL) when passive parties unexpectedly quit in the deployment phase: severe performance degradation and intellectual property (IP) leakage of the active party's labels. The proposed PlugVFL framework introduces Party-wise Dropout, which omits the passive party's representations with probability \(p\) during training to maintain accuracy after dropout, and DIMIP, which minimizes mutual information between passive party representations and labels to protect label IP. Evaluation on CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet18 in a two-party setting shows that Party-wise Dropout improves accuracy after party dropout by more than 8% and DIMIP prevents passive parties from fine-tuning a classifier that outperforms random guessing, while keeping VFL accuracy drop under 2%. The paper claims to be the first to study IP protection in the VFL deployment phase.

### Strengths
- The problem is meaningful and clearly relevant to VFL deployment (R1, R4).
- First work to identify that a passive party dropping out causes significant performance degradation in VFL (R2).
- Highlights a novel IP leakage risk: passive parties can exploit their feature extractors for model completion after dropping out (R2).
- The alternative training design (Party-wise Dropout) is intuitive and effectively addresses the overhead of computing mutual information objectives (R2).
- Demonstrates effectiveness on CIFAR image classification tasks, with clear evidence that the proposed mitigations significantly improve robustness and reduce IP leakage (R2, R4).
- The defense via mutual information minimization is well-formulated as an adversarial training algorithm that minimizes a variational upper bound, achieving >8% accuracy improvement after party exit and preventing label leakage (R3).
- Claims to be the first to provide IP protection specifically in the deployment phase of VFL (R3).
- The robustness solution (Party-wise Dropout) is simple yet effective, and the evaluation adequately covers parameter selection and comparisons to related works (R4).
- The paper is well-organized and easy to read (R2; note that R1 rated presentation as poor – see weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental setup and generalizability**: Experiments are restricted to a two-party VFL setting with CIFAR-10/100 datasets (R2, R3, R4). The paper claims “without loss of generality” but does not demonstrate scalability to three or more parties, which would require significantly increased computation (e.g., the objective splits into four equations in a three-party scenario) (R2). The impact of one party dropping in a larger (e.g., five-party) system is not studied, making it unclear whether the performance drop remains critical (R4). Only a single experiment is conducted (two parties, CIFAR) – complexity in larger, more heterogeneous tasks is unverified (R3).
- **IP leakage issue is not clearly tied to party dropout**: Multiple reviewers note that the IP leakage attack (active model completion, AMC) is based on training-time behavior and could be carried out regardless of whether the passive party drops out. The paper conflates two unrelated problems – performance degradation after quitting and IP leakage – without articulating how the attack vector specifically follows from dropping out (R2, R4). R4 states this is a major flaw requiring major revision; R2 asks for clarification on how the passive party retains necessary components after leaving. The same attack is possible if the passive party continues participating, yet the paper frames DIMIP as a defense against deployment-phase IP leakage (R4).
- **Comparison to naive alternatives is insufficient**: The paper dismisses fine-tuning the head model after a passive party quits as “time-consuming and impractical,” but does not measure how many iterations would be needed to recover accuracy (R4). R3 asks for an empirical comparison to such a naive fine-tuning approach, as it might outperform the proposed method. R2 suggests a possible simpler alternative: setting the missing party’s representation to a mean vector instead of zero (R2 question). No comparison to simple multi-head training is given with statistical significance; Figure 3 shows only minimal advantage of Party-wise Dropout over a multi-head approach (R2).
- **Unclear figures and missing hyperparameter details**: Fig. 3 and 6 plot test accuracy before vs. after party 2 quitting, and Fig. 4 and 5 plot test accuracy vs. attack accuracy. It is unclear whether all figures share the same hyperparameters, what the lines connecting points mean, and why these specific relationships are plotted (R1). The hyperparameter \(p\) (dropout probability during training) is debatable: the paper says a small \(p\) improves robustness, but guidelines for choosing \(p\) are lacking, and the evaluation only goes up to \(p=0.5\) in Table 3 – what happens for high dropout probabilities? (R2, R4). R3 asks: if \(p=0\), does accuracy after party 2 quit still hold?
- **Questionable performance relative to independent training**: Table 3 shows that PlugVFL can yield worse accuracy than simply training the active party independently (without any passive party). This undermines the rationale for the proposed method – does it make sense if it performs worse than a trivial baseline? (R1).
- **Weak connection between IP leakage and label information**: The paper assumes IP leakage is due to label information and uses mutual information regularization accordingly. However, a counterexample is that a feature extractor can be learned without any label information (unsupervised learning), so the attack may not rely on label leakage at all. The justification for why mutual information is the appropriate defense is not well established (R2). R3 similarly questions whether the definition of “label” as IP is limited to certain label types (e.g., numeric matching) and whether the framework generalizes beyond text labels.
- **Inaccurate assumptions and inaccuracies**: The paper asserts that “the service provider cannot afford to shut down the service while fine-tuning,” but in practice a backup model could replace the running one without service disruption (R2). Figure 1 is unclear about what information the active party provides to the passive party to enable the IP leakage attack (R4).
- **Presentation quality disagreement**: One reviewer (R1) rates presentation as poor (1), while others (R2, R4) rate it as good. The unclear figures (noted above) may contribute to this disagreement.
