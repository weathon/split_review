## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes RewardMATH, a benchmark for evaluating reward models in mathematical reasoning. It argues that the math subset of RewardBench is flawed because: (i) it relies on single pairwise comparisons; (ii) chosen and rejected completions have different numbers of steps (spurious feature); (iii) it inherits incorrect annotations from PRM800K; (iv) it uses product for PRM evaluation which biases shorter responses. RewardMATH addresses these by: using MATH500 (derived from MATH) with corrected annotations; employing one-to-many comparisons (one correct vs. 10 responses including multiple incorrect); ensuring matched step counts; using mean instead of product for PRMs. Experiments show that RewardMATH scores correlate strongly (r²=0.6–0.8) with Best-of-N (BoN) downstream performance on MATH, Gaokao, etc., whereas RewardBench shows near-zero correlation (r²=0–0.1). The paper also includes a synthetic overoptimization setup (gold RM = Internlm2-7B-reward) and a proxy RM trained on MetaMATH, and shows that RewardMATH detects overoptimization trends better than RewardBench.

### Strengths
- **Impactful topic**: Improving reward model evaluation for reasoning tasks is highly relevant and could be impactful if done correctly.
- **Clear problem identification**: The paper identifies specific flaws in RewardBench (annotation errors, step-count spurious correlation, single-comparison limitation) and makes explicit fixes.
- **Strong empirical evidence of improved correlation**: Figure 3 shows RewardMATH yields r²=0.6–0.8 between benchmark scores and BoN downstream performance, while RewardBench gives r²=0–0.1. Figure 4 further demonstrates stronger correlation with policy optimization outcomes.
- **Diverse reward model evaluation**: Evaluated models include generative LLM judges (GPT-4, Llama), classifier-based RMs, and process reward models (PRMs).
- **Overoptimization analysis**: The synthetic gold/proxy RM setup (Figure 6) examines reward overoptimization, showing that RewardMATH better predicts overoptimization than RewardBench.
- **Thorough experimental protocols**: Paper provides detailed hyperparameters and implementation details, aiding reproducibility.
- **Compelling motivation and critique**: The case against RewardBench is persuasive, backed by analysis of step-count imbalances and annotation quality.

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental methodological contribution**: Replacing pairwise comparisons with one-to-many (k-way) comparisons is a straightforward extension. Correlation between k-way comparison accuracy and BoN sampling is expected and does not yield deep insights into reward model behavior. The change alone may not be sufficient for a top conference publication (R4, R2).
- **Unclear definition of robustness**: The term “robustness” is used throughout but never precisely defined. It is implicitly equated with correlation to BoN downstream performance, creating a circular argument because the benchmark methodology (scoring highest among 10 responses) closely mirrors the BoN evaluation (picking the best of N). A clear framework for what makes a reward model robust is missing (R4, R5).
- **Lack of PPO/DPO validation**: The paper relies almost exclusively on Best-of-N sampling to verify the benchmark. PPO (or DPO) is the primary method practitioners use for reward-based fine-tuning. Figure 5 includes a synthetic PPO experiment, but the number of checkpoints and whether the trends follow Gao et al.’s findings are unclear. Without solid PPO results, it is uncertain whether high RewardMATH scores generalize to PPO training. The correlation with BoN may be inflated because both use the same scoring paradigm (R2, R4). A reviewer also asks whether DPO suffers from reward model overfitting and what advantage reward models have over DPO for reasoning (R1).
- **Benchmark biases from GPT-4 curation**: All correct solutions in RewardMATH are generated by GPT-4 (line 206: prompt with 4 exemplars per subject). This introduces a bias toward GPT-family models. Indeed, GPT-series LLM judges score significantly higher than others (line 286), which may be a consequence of this curation bias. Similarly, incorrect solutions are created by GPT-4-0125-preview inserting errors into other models’ answers (line 805). Such a pipeline risks favoring GPT-family evaluation. The authors should use diverse LLMs to generate correct solutions and validate that no systematic bias exists (R3).
- **Insufficient justification of gold RM**: The gold RM in the synthetic overoptimization setup is Internlm2-7b-reward (line 402), selected because it performs well on both RewardBench and RewardMATH. This is circular because RewardMATH is the benchmark under study, and RewardBench is itself criticized for unreliable annotations. The paper does not provide independent validation (e.g., human judgment alignment) that Internlm2-7b-reward reliably approximates human preferences (R3).
- **Narrow scope and missing domains**: The benchmark only covers mathematical reasoning. It would be more impactful to show the framework (one-to-many, diverse LLM outputs) applies to coding, summarization, safety, or open-ended generation without ground truth. Any initial experiments would be helpful (R2, R4). Additionally, the comparison is only with RewardBench, a holistic benchmark; it should be clarified whether RewardBench was the best prior math-specific eval set. The lack of cases evaluating varying quality of correct solutions (e.g., missing reasoning steps) is a limitation (R5).
- **Missing ablations and analyses**:
    - No ablation on the step-count spurious correlation. A reviewer requests explicit tests: does a reward-hacking model assign lower reward if the accepted response is made longer? Does it assign higher reward if the rejected response is shorter? (R1)
    - The choice of 10 responses (9 incorrect, 1 correct) is not justified. How does varying k and the number of correct/incorrect answers affect correlations and overoptimization detection? (R4)
    - For one-to-many, if a Llama model is used as the base policy, does using non-Llama rejected responses matter due to distribution mismatch? Figure 4 should compare one-to-many against one-to-one where the rejected responses are from Llama (R1).
    - Figure 6 lacks statistical measures (e.g., Kendall’s tau) for agreement between RewardMATH/RewardBench and the oracle reward. (R2)
    - The proxy RM trained on synthetic data (13% on RewardMATH, 69% on RewardBench) is far from optimal; could using better proxy RMs change the overoptimization trends? The increase from 12.68% to 13.51% over KL steps is not convincing (R2).
    - In Figure 6, the gold reward does not drop even at the maximum KL distance. Would larger BoN N cause a drop, and would RewardMATH still successfully identify robust models? (R2)
- **Dataset and experimental clarity issues**:
    - MATH500 is not cited or explained. Is it prior work or a contribution? Does it fix the PRM800K annotation problems? Could fixing RewardBench’s annotations alone change the comparison? (R1, R3, R5)
    - Lack of evaluations on gsm8k and MATH (the most common reasoning eval tasks) beyond what is already in the paper. (R1)
    - The paper does not specify whether the prompts used to evaluate LLM judges on RewardMATH are the same as those on RewardBench. Different prompting strategies would undermine fair comparison. (R3)
    - No validation that the incorrect solutions are actually incorrect (e.g., human checks or automatic verification). (R1, R3)
    - Section 5 is hard to follow and contains clarity issues. (R3)
- **Scope of overoptimization analysis**: The synthetic data experiment uses MetaMATH and only 80K out of 155K augmented samples. The motivation and selection criteria are not explained. (R3) Additionally, the assumption that KL divergence adequately captures policy optimization degree is not justified; other aspects of policy change may be missed. (R3)
- **Limited breadth of evidence**: Tables 1 and 2 simply show that RewardBench and RewardMATH rank models differently. It is not clearly demonstrated that high RewardBench scores fail to guarantee robustness—the missing link is a concrete definition of robustness (R5). Benchmark samples (500) may not cover diverse reasoning tasks. (R5)
