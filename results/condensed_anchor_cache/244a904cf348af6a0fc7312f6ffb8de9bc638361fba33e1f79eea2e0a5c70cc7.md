## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper addresses the grokking phenomenon – delayed generalization after near-perfect overfitting. It proposes to accelerate generalization by amplifying the low-frequency components of the gradient trajectory via two simple algorithms: an exponential moving average (EMA) and a windowed simple moving average (MA) of gradients. These operations are interpreted as low-pass filters in the signal processing sense. Experiments on modular multiplication, MNIST, QM9, and IMDB datasets show reduced generalization delay, sometimes by more than 50×. The paper also discusses connections between momentum and filtering.

### Strengths

- **Clear main idea and interesting domain-spanning approach** (R1, R4). Connecting optimization to classical signal processing (low-pass filters) is attractive and provides a fresh perspective (R3, R4).
- **Extensive numerical simulations** across diverse datasets (image, language, graph) and architectures (MLPs, Transformers) (R1, R2, R3). The algorithms are validated in multiple settings (R2).
- **Simple and easy-to-implement algorithms** requiring only a few lines of code (R4). This practical simplicity is valuable (R1, R4).
- **Demonstrates reduction of generalization delay** via simple momentum/averaging modifications – a potentially useful observation not previously focused on (R2).
- **Provides clear intuition** for why the modifications work (low-pass filtering of gradient oscillations) (R2, R3). The paper is generally easy to read (R2).
- **Discussion section offers additional insights** about the role of weight decay and the impossibility of removing all fast components (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of rigorous theoretical analysis** (R2, R4). The central claim that delayed generalization is caused by high-frequency gradient oscillations (line 94) is asserted without evidence or citation (R2). No mathematical derivation explains why amplifying low-frequency components **accelerates** generalization under grokking (R4). The theoretical appendix (Appendix A) only proves equivalences and is not referenced in the main text, so it does not increase confidence (R2).
- **Proposed EMA algorithm is essentially standard momentum** (R1, R2, R3). The paper does **not compare** with conventional momentum-based optimizers (e.g., Heavy Ball, Adam’s momentum) (R1, R3). Without such a baseline, it is unclear whether the observed speedup comes from the proposed filtering or simply from added momentum (R1, R3). R2 explicitly asks: “Can we view standard Heavy Ball as low-pass filtered GD? Does Heavy Ball eliminate/reduce grokking?” R1 suggests an ablation study **without Adam** (e.g., plain SGD) to isolate the effect. R3 notes that MA yields only a slight improvement over EMA (50× vs 44×) and that the absence of a conventional momentum comparator leaves the reader unconvinced.
- **Experiments on real-world data are not fully convincing** (R2). For MNIST, a three-layer MLP achieves only ~90% accuracy (vs. typical 98%), and this low ceiling is not discussed (R2). For QM9 and IMDB, grokking is not pronounced and the improvement appears to be a standard benefit of momentum/averaging (R2). R3 adds that grokking can often be avoided by proper hyperparameter tuning (citing Liu et al., 2022a), yet the paper presents speedup as its primary goal and minimal code change as a key advantage (R3). R4 doubts effectiveness on large-scale modern networks, noting that common MA/EMA tricks do not beat SGDm/AdamW in general.
- **Insufficient hyperparameter baseline comparison** (R3). The paper does not investigate the effects of varying learning rate, which is known to significantly influence grokking (Liu et al., 2022a). The baseline case without the proposed algorithms uses a fixed setup; no sweep of optimizer hyperparameters is provided. R3 questions why the primary goal is to accelerate grokking when the literature suggests it can be mitigated by simple hyperparameter choices.
- **Missing ablation studies** (R1, R4). The paper should test without the Adam optimizer (e.g., plain SGD) (R1). To verify the low-pass hypothesis, an experiment with **high-pass filtering** of gradients (which should slow grokking) is recommended (R4). R4 also suggests visualizing the dynamics of high- vs. low-frequency parameter components.
- **Insufficient background on grokking phenomenology** (R2). The paper does not extensively discuss previous theoretical studies or alternative explanations (e.g., Kumar et al., 2024), nor does it relate its filtering idea to existing accounts of why grokking occurs (R2).
- **Minor writing and presentation issues** (R1, R3, R4): convolution notation in eq. 3 is non‑standard (R1). “Avg(Q)” in Algorithm 2 is not defined until Section 4 (R1). Line 188 typo: mentions window size as hyperparameter of EMA instead of momentum α (R3). Labels in Appendix C are mixed up (R4). These can be fixed but reduce clarity.
- **Practical significance questioned** (R2). Grokking is a relatively rare and artificial phenomenon; algorithms aimed solely at eliminating it may not be broadly important (R2).

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers (R1, R4) gave scores of 5 and praised the idea and experiments, while the other two (R2, R3) gave scores of 3 and highlighted major weaknesses in theoretical grounding, experimental rigor, and comparison to existing methods. The merged review reflects both the positive aspects and the substantial concerns raised by the more critical reviewers.
