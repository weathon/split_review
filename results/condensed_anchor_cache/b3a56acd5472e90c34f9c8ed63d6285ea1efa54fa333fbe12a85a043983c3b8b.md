## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents an open-source modular package for benchmarking energy-forecasting time-series models. It collects 11 public load and renewable energy datasets (including a new renewable energy dataset with station- and region-level generation plus meteorological data) and integrates 21 forecasting methods (ML and DL) under 11 evaluation metrics. The framework supports feature engineering for load (temperature/calendar variables), custom loss functions linked to power dispatch costs (simulated on IEEE 30‑bus test system), and point/probabilistic forecasting. The authors aim to address domain‑specific needs (external factors, cost‑sensitive losses, data scale). The package also handles missing data and preprocessing. Reviewer scores span 3–6, with one reviewer (score 6) finding the application important and the framework comprehensive, while three others (scores 5, 3, 3) raise significant concerns about clarity, dataset choice, missing baselines, reproducibility, and overclaimed novelty.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely application** for energy forecasting, addressing real needs in the power industry. (R1: high impact)
- **Comprehensive framework** that handles feature engineering, missing data, and common preprocessing pipelines in a modular manner. (R1, R4)
- **Customizable**: supports integration of new models and custom loss functions (e.g., linking error to dispatch costs). (R1, R2)
- **Multiple evaluation metrics and public datasets** (load, wind, PV) are integrated, enabling benchmarking across diverse scenarios. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Provision of a new renewable energy dataset** with both station‑level and region‑level generation and corresponding meteorological factors. (R2, R3)
- **Combination of various techniques** (pre‑/post‑processing, feature engineering, point and probabilistic forecasting models) in one package. (R3)
- **Framework can be customized** to include more models and loss functions, designed for flexibility. (R1)

### Weaknesses
#### Clarity, presentation, and reproducibility
- **Overall clarity is poor**; paper flow and writing need significant improvement. Multiple reviewers note missing details, lack of proofreading, and citation formatting errors (e.g., missing spaces, incorrect \citet/\citep usage). (R2, R3)
- **Experiment settings are insufficiently described**: it is unclear whether tasks are univariate-to-univariate, multivariate-to-univariate, or multivariate-to-multivariate. Figure 3 and Table 3 lack this specification, hurting reproducibility. (R3: E2)
- **Figures and tables lack explanations**: e.g., what does “MQ[model_name]” stand for in Figure 3? What do “$123\,456\,789$” mean in Table 4’s first column? (R3: E3, E4)
- **Missing training/validation split details**: how are train and test datasets selected? For building‑level vs. aggregated data, are each building trained separately? No forecast vs. ground‑truth plots are shown, making it hard to judge model behavior. (R1: Q6, Q7)
- **Reproducibility is uncertain**: the Git repository does not contain all described datasets (e.g., is `load_with_weather.pkl` the REF dataset?). (R3: D5)
- **Insufficient description of the UCI dataset**: it is referred to only as “UCI database” without clearly naming the Electricity Load Diagrams (ELD). (R3: D1)

#### Dataset concerns
- **Datasets are relatively small and lack modern‑scale complexity**: they do not capture bus‑level load, wind‑farm‑level forecasts, or large‑scale systems like the ARPA‑E PERFORM dataset from NREL. (R4)
- **Missing critical well‑known datasets**: the London smart meter dataset, NREL Solar energy data, and the UCI ELD dataset (most famous in TSF) are not included or benchmarked. (R3: Goal, D2)
- **REF dataset stations/regions are not geographically identified**, which limits feature engineering based on location. (R3: D4)
- **Spatial aggregation of REF lacks external variables** (e.g., min/max/std of weather indicators) even though providing such features is a claimed advantage. (R3: D3)
- **ELD and ELF datasets contain only energy data without weather data**, contradicting the stated goal of providing datasets with both energy and weather. (R3: Goal)

#### Feature engineering limitations
- **Feature engineering is heavily centered on temperature and calendar variables**; other external factors (economic indicators, pandemics, etc.) are not explored. Generalization across datasets and energy types is insufficiently justified. (R2, R3)
- **How engineered features are integrated into transformer‑based models (Informer, etc.) is unclear**. If inputs are treated as channels, this effectively limits tasks to univariate‑to‑univariate forecasting, which is a significant usability drawback. (R3: F2)
- **Feature engineering for renewables (REF) based on calendar info (workdays) may not be beneficial**; the package only offers the specific combination described in Section 3.2.1, limiting user flexibility. (R3: F1)
- **No results are shown for the UCI ELD dataset, even with only calendar‑based feature engineering**, which would demonstrate the feature engineering’s applicability. (R3: D2)

#### Model and baseline limitations
- **Missing important state‑of‑the‑art time‑series baselines**: e.g., iTransformer, Pathformer. The transformer models included are outdated. (R3: Limitations)
- **No comparison with physical/hybrid models** that are well‑established in the industry (e.g., NREL’s System Advisor Model, Solcast). The PERFORM dataset has SAM predictions with outstanding accuracy for PV power. (R4)
- **Code repository lacks clear OOP structure**: a base model class is not found, and it is not clear if new models can inherit common training logic. A JSON config file for hyperparameters would improve usability. (R1: Q2, Q4)

#### Custom loss function and task alignment
- **The custom loss function is derived from a simplified IEEE 30‑bus test system**, which may not capture the complexity and variability of real‑world power networks, limiting generalizability. (R2)
- **Effectiveness of the loss function is only demonstrated via simulation, not on real tasks** within the provided datasets. (R3: Goal)
- **No comparison to alternative uncertainty‑aware losses** (e.g., negative log‑likelihood (NLL) for probabilistic prediction). Why should the proposed loss be preferred over models that provide uncertainty distributions? (R3: Questions)
- **The link between forecasting error and dispatch cost is claimed but not tested on actual power system data** – only simulation evidence is provided.

#### Probabilistic forecasting weaknesses
- **Quantile regression does not capture correlation between errors across time series**, which is essential for multivariate stochastic optimization in power dispatch. (R4)
- **Evaluation metrics for probabilistic forecasts are limited**: only Pinball Loss and CRPS are used; important multivariate metrics like the energy score and variogram score are missing. (R4)
- **Pinball losses reported are extremely high**, raising suspicion about the validity of the testing protocol. (R1: Q6)

#### External factors and weather forecasting realism
- **The framework assumes perfect forecasts of future weather** (e.g., “we will input … meteorological factors of the next 24 hours”). This is highly unrealistic; noise should be added to account for real‑world forecast errors. (R3: Q1)
- **The claim that external factors for renewables have not been extensively studied is inaccurate** – weather‑informed and physics‑informed models are already widely adopted in industry. (R4)
- **Availability of weather forecasts** is not addressed: does the model require predicting external factors as well, or are they assumed given? If given, their reliability is not considered in benchmarking. (R3: Q1)

#### Other specific concerns
- **No analysis of building‑level vs. aggregated performance** is provided, even though the package supports both. (R1: Q8)
- **The statement “ensuring minimum data information distortion” is not justified** – no evidence is given. (R3: Q2)
- **No multivariate‑to‑multivariate demonstration** (e.g., multiple renewable types under similar climate) even though the authors note its potential benefit. (R3: E1)
- **Whitespace and English issues** throughout (e.g., missing spaces before citations, “To our knowledge…” should be “To the best of our knowledge”). (R3)
- **$L(\epsilon)$ is introduced before it is defined** (missing equation reference). (R3: Revision)
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 6) is highly positive about the framework’s flexibility and impact, while three others (scores 5, 3, 3) find the contribution overstated, the evaluation incomplete, and the novelty limited compared to existing industrial practice. The positive reviewer also had several reproducibility and design concerns (e.g., pinball loss issues, missing OOP structure).
