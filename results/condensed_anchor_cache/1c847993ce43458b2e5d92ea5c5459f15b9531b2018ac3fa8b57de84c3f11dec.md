## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces EconAI, an LLM-enhanced agent-based simulation framework for modeling economic activities (macroeconomic trends). It aims to incorporate human-like preferences and cognitive processes—self-learning, self-reflection, and self-updating of a “knowledge brain”—into LLM agents to improve realism. Experiments compare EconAI to prior frameworks (notably EconAgent), show alignment with the Phillips Curve, and simulate a COVID-19 shock. One reviewer (score 5) found the approach novel and effective, while three reviewers (score 3 each) expressed concerns about novelty, clarity, implementation details, and evaluation rigor.

### Strengths
- The framework attempts to model individual agents’ cognitive processes in learning and decision-making; the idea of incorporating a “knowledge brain” from historical data is novel and useful for generating realistic, fine-grained agent behavior (Reviewer 1).
- The framework is sound for learning from observations, reflecting on insights, and updating experiences (Reviewer 2).
- The paper attempts to model both macroeconomic and microeconomic perspectives, examining how the environment interacts with agents—a practical research direction (Reviewer 2).
- The design concept of including self-reflection and self-updating knowledge establishes a foundation for agents to assess their trajectories and adapt over time, aligning with human-like decision-making (Reviewer 3).
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow; it includes micro and macro analysis, an ablation study, and interesting experimental findings such as the impact of COVID-19 on the simulated economy (Reviewers 1 and 4).
- The paper addresses a timely topic and presents an intriguing application of LLM agents to simulate complex economic behaviors, with potential to become a powerful tool for economic research (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The framework for learning, reflection, and self-updating may lack novelty, as it builds on well-established techniques such as ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) and reflection (Shinn et al., 2024), and similar agent-environment interfaces have been surveyed (e.g., Guo et al. “Large language model based multi-agents”, arXiv 2024). The main contribution beyond previous work (EconAgent) appears to be incorporating preferences via agent profiles and enhanced prompting, which limits technical innovation (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Insufficient implementation details**: The paper is light on reproducibility-critical details—number of agents, hyperparameters, prompt templates, LLM parameters, and input data are not specified. No code is attached. The process for constructing the knowledge brain (line 041, Section 4.1) is unclear: the authors mention using GPT-4 for preliminary construction followed by “fine-tuning with manual refinement” but do not explain what data or procedures this entails. It is not clarified how the “economic plan” is concretely defined—whether it is a simple modifiable prompt or a parametric structure with iterative updates, and how it is updated (via parameter adjustments, environment feedback, or internal criteria) (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Missing micro-level quantitative analysis**: The micro-level analysis is shallow, relying on qualitative LLM responses. No quantitative measures are provided to show what knowledge or preferences different agents learn. Comparisons against other models like EconAgent are needed at the micro level, and the paper lacks a detailed qualitative or quantitative comparison to EconAgent despite both frameworks being evaluated on similar tasks; the reported results appear very similar (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Authenticity and evaluation of agent preferences**: The paper argues that existing LLM agents are “rationality-driven” and that EconAI addresses this by incorporating human-like preferences. However, it is not demonstrated that the LLM itself possesses intentions or that the simulated preferences reflect real human preferences. Two key aspects are overlooked: (1) how individual-level preferences are generated (e.g., risk aversion heterogeneity) versus whether “preference” is simply rules learned from experience, and (2) verification of the consistency and authenticity of agent decisions (e.g., analysis of whether decisions match human economic behavior). The claim that “most LLMs are rationality-driven” lacks support from literature (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Missing comparisons, statistics, and sensitivity analyses**: Several experiments lack statistical significance testing. In Table 2, it is unclear whether performance differences are significant. In Figure 5, the points are close and no statistical evidence is provided for the observed outperformance. No sensitivity analysis is reported to show how parameters affect simulation results. The paper does not compare EconAI’s cost to other LLM baselines like EconAgent, nor does it explain why GPT-4 is used for the knowledge brain while GPT-3.5 is used for simulation (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: Several concepts are introduced without concrete examples (e.g., what constitutes “an economic plan”). The notation is inconsistent: the action space is denoted as “A” (lines 238–239) and later as “ε” (lines 259–260). No flow chart covers all design elements; a flowchart would improve readability. The paper lacks example scenario prompts and model responses in the appendix. Reference to equation 25 (lines 356–357) does not exist. The citation format is incorrect (should use \cite or \citep). The discussion of agent-based modeling (ABM) and neural networks in the introduction is unclear and lacks logical connections (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Inadequate evaluation of framework limitations**: The paper fails to discuss the limitations of the EconAI model itself. In the ablation study (Section 5.4), the framework’s “too steady” behavior is labeled unfavorably without objective criteria. In Section 5.3, it is unclear whether observed market strategies relate to enhanced simulation or simply reflect patterns already present in the LLM’s training corpus. The paper does not explain how economic variables (inflation, unemployment, saving rate, profit margin) are aggregated and measured from the agent interactions (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Missing justification and incomplete answers**: The question of how the agent’s preferences interact with environmental information in Figure 2 is not addressed. The relevance of marketing strategy to economic simulation analysis is not justified. The paper does not provide an end-to-end example of how the framework’s workflow aggregates economic variables (Reviewers 2, 3).
