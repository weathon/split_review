## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper challenges Feldman and Zhang (2020)’s claim that memorization is necessary for generalization. It identifies three methodological issues in the original study – lack of model convergence, data leakage due to near‑duplicate train/test points, and sub‑population shift when removing memorized examples – and proposes fixes (weight decay, deduplication, preserving sub‑population points). After correcting these, the reported accuracy loss from removing memorized points drops from 2.54% to 0.54%, leading the authors to argue that memorization is not necessary for generalization. Reviewers overall found the topic interesting but the contribution limited; one reviewer (score 6) considered it good science that rectifies past work, while three reviewers (score 3) raised substantial concerns about the analysis, experimental fairness, and interpretation.

### Strengths
- Good science that clearly identifies and corrects methodological errors in a influential prior work (Reviewer 1).
- Well‑structured with clear figures that enhance understanding of the issues (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Interesting observation that exposes the fragility of the original findings; results on model convergence and data leakage are sensible and challenge a widespread belief (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Visualizations of memorized and sub‑population shifts are informative (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Minimal contribution beyond pointing out flaws:** The paper provides no new algorithmic contribution (e.g., better memorization measures) and largely stems from prior methodology. One reviewer felt it is only just above the bar for a conference paper, while another found the study inadequately explored, suggesting a workshop or reproducibility track would be more appropriate (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Sub‑population shift analysis undermines the original hypothesis:** By putting examples from small sub‑populations back into the training set, the paper defeats the central claim of Feldman and Zhang – that near‑singleton sub‑populations require memorization to perform well. This criticism is seen as unfounded by one reviewer (Reviewer 2). Another reviewer (Reviewer 3) notes that both mislabeled and sub‑population points are part of the long‑tail; the original argument aligns with the model memorizing rare points, so the sub‑population shift does not necessarily contradict the prior work.
- **Failure to replicate the original paper’s key analysis:** The original paper showed varied memorization thresholds and accuracy drops across multiple datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR‑100, MNIST). This paper only analyzes one dataset (CIFAR‑10) at a single threshold, lacking a replication of the critical Figure 2 from Feldman and Zhang. This limits the robustness and generality of the findings (Reviewer 2; also noted by Reviewers 1 and 4).
- **Unfair comparison due to different training set sizes:** The post‑pruning training set retains 516 additional points (from sub‑population preservation) compared to the original removal, and the analysis uses the memorized points identified *in the original study* rather than those memorized in the new setup. This makes the reported accuracy loss reduction (0.54% vs 2.54%) potentially misleading; one reviewer questioned whether adding 516 random points to the original removal would produce a similar drop (Reviewers 2, 4). Also, the paper should check what the *new* models memorize after modifications (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of model convergence is a misleading term:** “Lack of convergence” may imply gradient descent did not converge, but it refers to early stopping before test‑set saturation. Changing the optimization objective or regularization is natural and makes findings hard to compare with the original setup (Reviewer 4). Additionally, one reviewer questioned why models should be trained to maximum test‑set accuracy when the test set is not available during training (Reviewer 3).
- **Data leakage handling is already acknowledged in prior work:** The original paper explicitly noted near‑duplicates in test images (Figure 3). The current paper’s presentation of this issue is not sufficiently explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Repetition and presentation issues:** The paper repeats information across Introduction, Background, Related Work, and Discussion. Table 1 (symbols) and much of the detailed discussion of the original work can be moved to the appendix. Implementation code was not provided (Reviewer 3). One reviewer rated presentation as poor (Reviewer 3), while others rated it good/excellent (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Questions raised about definitions and assumptions:** What constitutes a sub‑population? Does the analysis assume duplicate points are only found in memorized points? Which points impact the same test point in Section 5.1? The paper defers defining memorization to future work without strong justification (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Lack of theoretical justification:** The original work has a theoretical foundation; the current paper’s observations are empirical and do not identify a flaw in the theory. Further theoretical grounding is needed to fully contradict the prior claim (Reviewer 3).
- **Math/numerical checks requested:** One reviewer questioned whether the reported numbers (e.g., 1.78% halved from 2.54%? 1.25 vs 0.54 ratio?) are correct (Reviewer 4).
