## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper attempts to prove that well-trained deep neural networks (DNNs) encode only a small number of sparse interactions between input variables, which can be interpreted as symbolic primitive inference patterns. The proof relies on three assumptions (high-order derivatives zero, monotonic expected output on masked inputs, bounded confidence degradation on masked samples) and uses Harsanyi dividends to define interactions. Empirical tests on several DNNs are provided to support each assumption.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow; plots are illustrative and helpful (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The idea of proving that DNNs learn a small number of symbolic concepts is novel and grounded (Reviewer 2). The proof (Theorems 2 onward) is novel and significant (Reviewer 4).
- The paper is well placed in the relevant literature and tackles an important problem (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The authors provide a detailed justification of each assumption, including restating them in less formal language and presenting empirical evidence (Reviewer 1: “does a really nice job justifying their assumptions and explaining in detail where they come from”, Section 3.2). Reviewers 1 and 3 found the assumptions generally reasonable and supported by experiments.
- The proof is worked through clearly; a reviewer without prior background could follow it (Reviewer 3).
- While two reviewers (2, 4) raised concerns about the soundness of assumptions and empirical verification, two other reviewers (1, 3) were more positive about the contribution (ratings 8 vs 6).

### Weaknesses
- **Scope of the claim (“AI models”)**: The title and abstract claim the results apply to “AI models”, but the proof and all empirical evidence (Figure 3, 4, 5, Table 1) only involve DNNs. No evidence is provided for other model classes such as Random Forests, k-NN, or Gradient Boosting Machines (Reviewer 4). It is also unclear whether the Harsanyi interaction framework can be applied to non-subdividable inputs like real-valued time series or reinforcement learning state spaces (Reviewer 1, question).

- **Choosing input variables / granularity**: The paper does not provide a clear methodology for how to select the set of input variables \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) for image data (e.g., should they be pixels, patches, human-annotated segments?). The dog image example uses “image regions” but there is no discussion of how large those regions should be; it is unclear whether the sparse interaction property holds if regions are too small (single pixel) or too large (half the image). This choice directly affects the number and interpretability of identified interactions (Reviewers 1, 3, 4; also Reviewer 2 asks: for MNIST, is each \(x_i\) a pixel?). Relying on pre-defined human-annotated segments may not be generalizable or scalable to arbitrary data modalities (Reviewer 1). The paper does not sufficiently address how the level of abstraction impacts the result (Reviewer 3).

- **Soundness of Assumption 2 (monotonic expected output on masked inputs)**:
  * Assumption 2 is not universally applicable; even Figure 2 shows a counterexample where three unmasked regions (row 2) give a much smaller output than two unmasked regions (row 1). The authors mention noisy regions, but how can one know a priori which regions are salient? (Reviewer 2).
  * The monotonicity assumption seems to presuppose a well-trained model and well-partitioned input data; it is unclear how to determine a priori whether a given dataset allows for meaningful masking and smooth inference (Reviewer 1).
  * The empirical verification of monotonicity is weak: error bars are missing for Figure 4, Figure 5(a), and the top row of Table 1. In Table 1 and Table 2 the error bars are so large that they overlap, suggesting the results are not statistically significant and the claim is not empirically verified (Reviewer 4). More random seeds are needed to reduce variance (Reviewer 4).

- **Soundness of Assumption 3 (bounded confidence degradation)**:
  * The justification for the upper bound on inference confidence is based on empirical observation, not theoretical necessity. It is not explained why this should be a general property of AI models, nor what happens when the bound does not hold. This weakens the overall argument (Reviewer 1).
  * No bound on the constant \(p\) is derived; \(p\) can be arbitrarily large. If so, the term \(n^{p+\delta}\) could dominate the combinatorial factor and invalidate the claim of sparsity in Theorem 2. The paper needs a more rigorous analysis of when \(p\) is bounded and how this affects the sparsity result (Reviewer 2).
  * Relatedly, the implications of Theorems 2 and 3 are unclear: the coefficients \(\lambda^{(k)}\) in Eq. (4) and Eq. (7) can be large, and the bound \(n^{p+\delta} \ll \binom{n}{k}\) is not justified when \(p\) is unconstrained. How exactly does a constant-\(p\) condition guarantee sparsity? (Reviewer 2).

- **Practicality and implications**:
  * Even if sparse interactions exist relative to the total possible set, for high-dimensional inputs (e.g., images) the absolute number of salient interactions could still be very large, potentially hindering interpretability. The paper does not discuss how to choose the right level of abstraction to ensure the identified sparse interactions are both meaningful and computationally manageable (Reviewer 3).
  * The paper does not explain how to validate the three conditions prior to training; they can only be checked empirically after training. This limits the prescriptive or predictive utility of the framework (Reviewer 2).
  * Knowing that DNNs encode a few key concepts may be interesting, but the paper stops short of discussing how this insight could benefit practitioners (e.g., for model simplification, debugging, or understanding behavior) (Reviewer 2).

- **Missing experimental details**: Reviewer 2 asks for more details on Figure 3: whether models were trained from scratch or pre-trained, size of training/testing data, how subsets were selected for MNIST (are \(x_i\) pixels?).
