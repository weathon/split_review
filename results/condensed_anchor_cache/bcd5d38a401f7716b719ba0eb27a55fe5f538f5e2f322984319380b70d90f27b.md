## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper addresses domain adaptation under label shift, where source and target label distributions differ but class-conditional feature distributions are identical. The authors propose a Class Probability Matching (CPM) framework that matches class probabilities directly, improving computational efficiency over feature probability matching (which used costly GAN-based estimation). Within CPM they introduce CPMCN (Class Probability Matching with Calibrated Networks) for target-domain classification, supported by a theoretical generalization bound. Experiments show CPMCN outperforms existing matching-based and EM-based methods.

### Strengths

- The paper is well‑written and easy to follow.
- The class probability matching perspective is a novel angle compared to existing feature probability matching, and the framework is simple yet effective.
- The theoretical analysis is thorough: a generalization bound is proved for CPMCN, explaining the benefits of calibrated networks.
- Experimental results demonstrate superior accuracy over baselines and confirm the computational complexity advantage (reducing complexity from O(n_p³) to O(n_q K²)).
- The paper compares, analyzes, and improves on existing frameworks, and the improvements are well founded.

### Weaknesses

1. **Computational efficiency claim unverified empirically.**  
   The paper states a complexity improvement from O(n_p³) to O(n_q K²) but provides no runtime experiments. Empirical comparisons are needed to show at what target domain size the method becomes prohibitive and how it compares with other matching approaches (R1, R2, R4).

2. **Missing comparisons with related domains.**  
   - Methods similar to the class‑probability‑ratio estimation have been extensively explored in long‑tailed learning (e.g., logit adjustment [1]); the paper should discuss and compare with that literature (R1).  
   - Label shift can be reframed as an imbalanced semi‑supervised learning problem (labeled set and unlabeled set having different class priors). Several strong works exist [1–4] that the paper should reference and compare against (R3).  
   - **Minority reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer considered the problem less significant because it can be seen as a specific instance of imbalanced semi‑supervised learning, while others found the class probability matching perspective novel.

3. **Limited novelty of “calibrated networks.”**  
   The use of calibration is not new; prior work [2] already demonstrated that calibration improves performance. The paper uses calibrated networks off‑the‑shelf rather than designing a calibration mechanism specifically for label shift (R1, R2).

4. **Theoretical analysis lacks intuition and optimization details.**  
   - The paper does not explain why Assumption 5.1 is needed or what it means; proof sketches would help readability (R4).  
   - No analysis is given for the optimization problem (11): can BFGS reliably find the global optimum? How would an approximate solution affect the generalization bound? (R4).  
   - Optimizing in ℝ^K (Eq. 11) and then normalizing may introduce numerical instability; constraining to the simplex directly could be more efficient and stable (R3).

5. **Omitted experimental details.**  
   - Figure 1 does not show curves for other matching approaches (R4).  
   - The MNIST tweak‑one‑shift results (at ρ=0.02) contain many identical accuracy values with identical standard deviations, raising concerns about reproducibility or rounding (R1).  
   - It is unclear whether the network is fixed or fine‑tuned on the target domain (R3).

6. **Missing discussion of minibatch training.**  
   The current method does not support minibatch training; the paper does not discuss how the algorithm could be adapted for that setting (R2).

7. **Minor typo:** On page 13, line 4, there is an unexpected bracket: “p(k))” (R3).
