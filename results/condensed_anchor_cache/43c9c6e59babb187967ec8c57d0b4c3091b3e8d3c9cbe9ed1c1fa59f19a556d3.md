## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses “context option bias” in multiple-choice question (MCQ) tasks: when in-context demonstrations are biased (e.g., all answers are “A”), LLMs are predisposed to predict that same option. To mitigate this, the authors propose CoLo. First, by comparing attention outputs from ordinary vs. biased demonstration inputs, they localize attention heads that are sensitive to context option bias via sequential intervention. Then, during inference, they apply an attention-scaling method (custom temperature) to those heads. Experiments on MMLU, CEVAL, and other datasets show improved MCQ accuracy and recall standard deviation. The method is also shown to transfer across tasks and to be robust to different numbers of choices and demonstrations. The paper focuses on models up to 7B/8B parameters.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and important focus** on a previously overlooked bias – context option bias in in-context learning for MCQ – and a well-motivated design for CoLo. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
2. **Simple and effective approach**: using a custom temperature per attention head for scaling is straightforward yet yields improvements. (Reviewer 1)
3. **Extensive empirical evidence** showing improvements in MCQ accuracy and recall standard deviation across several datasets, including zero-shot and few-shot settings and cross-dataset evaluation (e.g., MMLU → CEVAL). (Reviewers 1, 4)
4. **Transferability across tasks and robustness** to input style, dataset, and the particular biased option suggests that identified heads are feature of the model/pretraining rather than domain-specific. (Reviewer 1)
5. **The problem is practically important** and the paper is clearly written and easy to understand. (Reviewer 3; also noted by Reviewer 4 regarding clarity of motivation)

### Weaknesses
1. **Practical motivation and real-world applicability**:
   - The need for addressing context option bias in MCQ tasks should be better motivated, given that in-context options can be shuffled freely and the bias may be less pronounced in practice. (Reviewer 1)
   - The evaluation relies on **artificially biased prompts** (e.g., all answers “A”); it is unclear whether reducing bias in these extreme scenarios translates to improvements in more realistic, subtle settings. (Reviewer 2)
   - The method requires **both biased and ordinary versions of in-context examples**; this may not be feasible in open-ended tasks where the bias is not predefined. (Reviewer 3)

2. **Experimental rigor and reporting**:
   - **Standard deviations are not reported** for any key results, making it impossible to assess statistical significance – especially problematic given the small (1–3%) improvements and small differences in some experiments. (Reviewer 2) *Note: Reviewer 4 considered the experiments “extensive,” but this omission is a common criticism from Reviewer 2.*
   - Performance of Llama-3 and Gemma differs notably from official reported numbers, without explanation. (Reviewer 1)
   - The **baseline could be strengthened**: previous work used different temperatures for different domains; the paper does not search over temperatures for the baseline and report the best-performing one. (Reviewer 1)

3. **Scalability and larger models**:
   - Experiments are limited to 7B/8B-level models; it is unclear how CoLo scales to larger models (30B/70B) or MoE architectures. Specific GPU hardware and hours for various-sized models are not reported. (Reviewer 4)
   - A related concern: the impact of model size on susceptibility to context option bias and on the method’s effectiveness is not analyzed. (Reviewer 1)
   - Only older model families (e.g., LLaMA-2, Gemma, Qwen) are tested; recent models like LLaMA 3.2, Mistral, Qwen 2 are omitted, so generalizability to modern architectures is unknown. (Reviewer 2)

4. **Theoretical grounding and interpretability**:
   - The paper provides **no theoretical explanation** for why only certain attention heads contribute to context option bias or why scaling those heads mitigates the bias effectively. This hinders reproducibility and understanding, and raises the question of whether the effect is genuine debiasing or an artifact. (Reviewer 2)

5. **Methodological narrowness and complexity**:
   - The approach is confined to **attention scaling**; other intervention methods (e.g., layer-based adjustments, targeted fine-tuning) are not explored or compared. No justification is given for choosing scaling over alternatives. (Reviewer 2)
   - Identifying bias-sensitive heads requires **multiple rounds of sampling**, adding computational overhead beyond the PriDe baseline. Potential optimizations or alternative approaches to reduce this cost are not discussed. (Reviewer 4)
   - The process for selecting and intervening on attention heads may be **convoluted to readers** unfamiliar with attention-head analysis; additional clarifications or illustrative examples would improve accessibility. (Reviewer 4)

6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and selection**:
   - The method involves hyperparameters: scaling factor \(T\) and head selection counts \(K_l\), \(K_h\) (top layers and heads). Their influence on performance is not systematically analyzed. The paper does not explain how \(K_l\) and \(K_h\) were chosen, nor provide guidelines for tuning them in new settings. (Reviewers 1, 2)
   - Sensitivity to the scaling factor \(T\) is under-explored; performance appears to depend heavily on its value (see Table 1), yet no ablation or grid search over \(T\) is presented. (Reviewer 2)

7. **Limited scope of tasks and domains**:
   - Experiments focus exclusively on fixed MCQ tasks; the method is not evaluated on more **open-ended question answering** where in-context bias could also occur, and which are more representative of real-world use. (Reviewer 3)
   - The **cross-domain evaluation** is limited to a few datasets (e.g., CEVAL, CHID, RACE); a broader assessment across diverse domains is needed to confirm generalizability, especially given the lack of standard deviations on the reported small changes. (Reviewer 2)

8. **Clarity and presentation issues**:
   - Some reviewers found the paper **clearly written** (Reviewer 3), while others noted **readability problems**: long/repetitive sentences (e.g., “To better observe the LLMs’ behavior…”), ambiguous pronoun references (“This inspires us…”), redundancy (“excavate the reasons”), and grammar errors (e.g., “degrade” should be “degrades”). (Reviewer 2)
   - Equation 2 should read “\(y = \text{argmax}\)”. (Reviewer 1)
   - “Confidence” in Section 4.5 needs clarification. (Reviewer 1)
   - Caption of Figure 5 is not grammatically sound. (Reviewer 1)
   - Caption under Figure 8 is misleading; it is unclear how to read the “frequency of the localized field” from picture (c). (Reviewer 2)

9. **Minor missing experiments and questions**:
   - No study on the **minimum number of demonstrations** needed for successful bias detection; how does performance scale with more examples? (Reviewer 1)
   - No experiment with a **custom prompt message** that explicitly warns LLMs about unbiased options to see if it steers attention heads. (Reviewer 1)
   - The paper should discuss whether the method could work **without needing both biased and unbiased versions** of examples, especially for open-ended tasks. (Reviewer 3)
