## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates feature splitting in Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) for LLMs, using a first‑letter identification task on Gemma‑2B to study monosemanticity and interpretability. The authors identify a phenomenon called “feature absorption,” where a latent that generally fires for a property (e.g., “starts with L”) fails on specific tokens because other latents “absorb” those tokens. They analyze how SAE width and sparsity affect this, concluding that varying these hyperparameters does not eliminate absorption.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: how SAE features can become less interpretable via absorption (R1).
- Investigating the effect of width and sparsity on training performance is helpful for future SAE training (R1).
- Written in an easy‑to‑understand way (R1).
- Provides useful insights and points out research directions at a time when understanding LLM techniques is crucial (R2).
- Describes an interesting controlled setting (first‑letter identification) for studying feature disentanglement in natural language (R3).
- Discovers a new phenomenon, “feature absorption,” that may pose problems for desired future applications of SAEs (R3).
- The controlled setting allows thorough analysis of failure cases for absorption and feature splitting; the analysis is good and informative (R3).
- Clear problem statement and good structure; the “S”/“_short” example builds intuition effectively (R3).
- Explores a natural task well; establishes that first‑letter information is linearly available (LR probe with high F1) and that SAE latents align with the probe direction (Figure 4b, Figure 13) (R4).
- Provides a novel perspective on the documented issue of feature splitting and good theoretical justification for why absorption occurs and why it poses challenges (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generality:** Observations and conclusions are based solely on Gemma‑2B and a single first‑letter task. It is unclear how general the phenomenon is. The authors should explore models of different sizes/architectures and tasks (e.g., sentiment analysis, QA) (R2, R4). *Note: After rebuttal, the authors added results with different SAE architectures and three model types, but the original weakness still stands (R2).*
- **Insufficient demonstration of absorption mechanism:** The paper claims four steps (high‑precision latent, subset where latent fails, separate token‑aligned latents absorbing those tokens, latent becomes good after subset removal). Steps 1 and 2 are shown well, but steps 3 and 4 are only shown for a single example in Section 4.2. Step 3 could be addressed by confirming that the latent with the largest negative‑magnitude ablation effect (Section 4.3) is a token‑classifying latent (R4).
- **Flawed metric (lines 148–157):** Averaging logits over non‑target letters interacts poorly with softmax. Example: logits g[A]=10, g[B]=25, others=0 → metric gives the same value as if all non‑target logits were 0, even though the model would predict B. A max or softmax‑based metric would be more convincing (R4).
- **Confusing figure and presentation disagreements:** Figure 1 is confusing and lacks sufficient description in the main text (R2). One reviewer finds the overall presentation messy, lacking narrative flow, and in need of restructuring (R2). Another reviewer finds the structure good (R3) – this disagreement should be noted.
- **Insufficient method elaboration:** More details needed on SAE training, loss functions, feature extraction and analysis. The pseudo‑code (Algorithm 1) should be accompanied by step‑by‑step explanation, mathematical formulations, and rationale (R2).
- **Unexplored commonalities and prediction:** It would be useful to explore commonalities between different absorption instances or further verify the claim that absorption is a logical consequence of sparsity loss (R3). Also, are there rules to predict when absorption occurs? (R3, as a question).
- **Lack of practical interpretability examples:** The paper could analyze the first letters of all tokens associated with specific latent activations to reveal patterns that enhance interpretability (R1).
- **Other variations of absorption:** Only one form is presented; it is unclear whether other variations exist in different contexts that could impact interpretability differently (R1).
- **Undefined concept:** “Approximately token‑aligned” (lines 69–71) is not defined or measured (R4, Q1).
- **F1 conversion unclear:** How are SAE activations converted to binary for F1 (Figure 2, lines 169–172)? Is a non‑zero threshold used and how is it chosen? (R4, Q2).
- **Implausible absorption rate:** Figure 7b shows a mean absorption rate of ~35%; for a vocabulary of ~256k tokens that implies ~90k tokens absorbed into 65k latents. How is this possible? (R4, Q3).
- **Cosine similarity range:** In Figure 13, why is the high cosine similarity in the 0.4–0.6 range, not closer to 1? This suggests the feature is learned mixed with an orthogonal component (R4, Q4).
- **Encoder vs. decoder directions:** Why do Figures 4 and 13 use decoder directions rather than encoder directions for cosine similarity? (R4, Q5).
- **Algorithm 1 output ambiguous:** Does it return one ablation effect per token per latent, or are these averaged? (R4, Q6).
- **Minor errors / missing citations:** (R4, Other comments)
  - Lines 94–107 should reference (Lieberum et al., 2024).
  - Line 100 should use a generic activation function (JumpReLU is used, not ReLU).
  - Lines 118 and 147: “include the SAE error term” is ambiguous; needs citation to (Marks et al., 2024) or a hyperlink to the glossary.
  - Line 205: citation to (Gao et al., 2024) for sparse probing likely should be (Gurnee et al., 2023).
  - Lines 291–292: “highlighted in green” should be “yellow”.
  - Line 404: the coverage measure of (Karvonen et al., 2024) is similar to the method in Figure 2 and should be referenced.
  - Line 429: “on the decoder *or* another SEE” – “or” should be “of”.
  - Citation (Huben et al., 2024) should follow the order of authors in the paper, i.e., (Cunningham et al., 2024).
