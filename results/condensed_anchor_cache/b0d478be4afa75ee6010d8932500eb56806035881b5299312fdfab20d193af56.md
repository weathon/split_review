## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents VideoGPT+, a video-language model that integrates an image encoder (for spatial details) and a video encoder (for temporal context) via segment-wise sampling and adaptive pooling. The authors also introduce a semi-automatically annotated instruction-tuning dataset (VCG+112K) and a new benchmark (VCGBench-Diverse) covering 18 video categories with 4,354 QA pairs. Reviewer ratings ranged from 3 to 5; one reviewer (R4) considered the dual-encoder design innovative and the dataset/benchmark valuable, while R1, R2, R3, R5 rated the paper 3 and raised significant concerns about novelty, missing comparisons, entangled contributions, and unclear benchmark uniqueness.

### Strengths
- The dual-encoder combination (high-resolution image encoder + low-resolution sparse video encoder) is a reasonable design choice that reduces training cost and inference latency compared to full high-resolution dense video input, and it achieves strong empirical performance on benchmarks such as VCGBench, MVBench, and VideoMME (R1, R2, R3, R5). One reviewer (R4) highlighted this design as innovative for spatiotemporal understanding.
- The introduction of VCG+112K, a 112K-sample video instruction-tuning dataset generated via a semi-automatic pipeline (PySceneDetect, LLaVA-v1.6, GPT-4, GPT-3.5), is a potentially valuable resource for the community once refined and released (R1, R3, R4).
- The proposed VCGBench-Diverse benchmark (877 videos, 4,354 QA pairs across 18 categories) provides a resource for evaluating video models across diverse domains; it was noted as a valuable contribution (R1, R2, R4).
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the architecture and rationale (R2, R5). The experimental results show robust capabilities on several video QA tasks (R1, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and technical depth.** Several reviewers found the dual-encoder design not novel, noting that combining multiple visual encoders has been widely explored in both traditional video understanding (e.g., Two-Stream, SlowFast, R3D) and recent video LMMs (e.g., BRAVE, Eagle, SPHINX, Grounded-VideoLLM). The paper lacks discussion and comparison to these works (R3, R5). One reviewer considered the contribution trivial, offering no new theoretical insights (R2). The three contributions (model, dataset, benchmark) are somewhat independent, making the paper’s focus ambiguous and the key research question unclear (R1, R3, R5).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines.** Comparisons with several strong recent models are absent, including LongVA, InternVL, Kangaroo (R2); and LLaVA-NeXT-Video, LLaVA-OneVision, PLLaVA, SlowFast-LLaVA, Tarsier, MiniGPT4-Video, ShareGPT4Video, VideoLLaMA 2 (R3). No alternative fusion designs for combining image and video features are compared (R5), and the different training data recipes used across models make the claimed improvements less convincing (R3).
- **Inadequate ablations and entangled contributions.** Ablation studies are performed on a selectively chosen and inconsistent subset of benchmarks (R3). Experiments entangle the model contribution (dual encoders) and the data contribution (VCG+112K), so performance gains cannot be attributed to architecture alone (R5). Missing ablations include: comparing the dual-encoder design to an image-only model with an equivalent number of tokens (R2, R4); ablating the interaction between encoders (R4); using a fixed data recipe from prior work (e.g., VideoLLaVA) to isolate the dual-encoder effect (R3, R5); and evaluating the training data by training a base model (e.g., Video-ChatGPT) on VCG+112K to show data effectiveness (R5). Ablation details for “without segment-wise sampling” and “without adaptive token pooling” are insufficient (e.g., number of frames used) (R3). The table in the VCG+112K ablation is confusing: a cross mark on better-performing row suggests VCG+112K was not used (R3).
- **Data design and quality concerns.** The source of videos for VCG+112K (HDVILA) is a pretraining dataset, potentially causing leakage when used in inference (R1). The pipeline’s novelty is not clearly established, and no direct quality evaluation of the generated data is provided (R5). Training data size is not controlled in the ablation, so differences could be due to size rather than quality (R5). The video source for VCG+112K is not clearly stated (R5). Questions also arise about annotator instructions and why existing training data is not used (R1). The claimed “semi-automatic annotation pipeline” novelty is unsupported (R5).
- **Benchmark limitations and unclear uniqueness.** VCGBench-Diverse is small (877 videos) and uses only open-ended QA, which several reviewers argued is less robust than multiple-choice QA and likely problematic when evaluated by LLM-as-a-judge (R3). Its uniqueness relative to existing benchmarks (e.g., VCGBench, MVBench, VideoMME, TempCompass, CVRR-ES, Vinoground, VideoVista, etc.) is not demonstrated, and the claim of increased diversity lacks quantitative comparison of domain coverage (R3, R5). The use of HDVILA for inference is also a concern (R1).
- **Presentation and clarity issues.** Tables on pages 9 and 10 lack captions (R1). Latency and training/inference costs are not discussed (R3). The terms “local/global features” are misleading; “static/dynamic” or “image/clip features” would be more appropriate (R5). The introduction should scope the paper to video-language understanding, not general video understanding (R5). Figure 1 caption contains a typo (“permors better”) (R3). The paper tries to cover too much, making contributions ambiguous (R1). Implementation details for ablations are insufficient (R3).
- **Theoretical depth.** The paper lacks theoretical derivations to justify the design choices (R4).
- **Missing details.** The frame rates for image and video encoders, total number of frames per encoder, number of videos in VCG+112K, whether ActivityNet is excluded from training for zero-shot QA, and clarification on combined training dataset splits are not provided (R1, R2, R3).
