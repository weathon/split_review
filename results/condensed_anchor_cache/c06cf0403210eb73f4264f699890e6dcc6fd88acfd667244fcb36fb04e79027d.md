## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Alternating Tuning and Merging (ATM), which replaces the standard one-shot merge of separately fine-tuned models with an iterative process that alternates between task-specific fine-tuning and merging (via task arithmetic or TIES). The authors motivate this by linking task vectors to multi-task gradients, arguing that merging after a single epoch approximates gradient descent. They evaluate ATM on ViT-B-16 and BERT models on vision and NLP tasks, reporting up to 20% accuracy gains over single-merge baselines.

### Strengths

- The proposed method (ATM) is simple and shows significantly higher performance over one-shot model merging baselines (e.g., Task Arithmetic, TIES) that do not alternate tuning and merging. This demonstrates the value of intermittent merging rather than fully training models separately before merging.  
- The initial theoretical insight linking task vectors to single-task gradient updates after the first gradient descent step, and the connection to multi-task gradients, is considered novel by some reviewers.  
- The paper provides a comprehensive ablation study, investigating the effect of merging frequency, epoch budget, and data availability (e.g., valFT ATM with validation set only).  
- ATM has a GPU memory advantage compared to gradient-balancing multi-task learning approaches, because fine-tuning for each task can be performed independently.  
- The paper addresses the emerging and relevant area of model merging for reducing fine-tuning costs.

### Weaknesses

- **Misalignment with model-merging goals.** Several reviewers note that ATM requires access to all task data *a priori* and merges models during training, which loses the modularity that motivates model merging (combining independently trained checkpoints from separate sources). Adding a new task with ATM requires full retraining on all tasks, unlike one-shot merging methods. The baseline most relevant to this setting is standard multi-task learning (MTL), which is not included as a baseline. ATM is closer to joint fine-tuning or federated learning than to model merging, and it should be compared with MTL (native joint training) to justify its benefits.  
- **Missing MTL baseline and misconfigured baselines.** No standard MTL result is reported; reviewers argue that MTL (joint fine-tuning) would likely perform at least as well as ATM, and its omission undermines the claimed improvements. Moreover, the baselines (Task Arithmetic, TIES) are used with suboptimal hyperparameters: mean aggregation of task vectors with a scaling factor of 1, which is not the recommended setup (e.g., Task Arithmetic typically tunes the scaling factor). This makes comparisons unfair and overstates gains. State-of-the-art merging methods such as AdaMerging are cited but not compared directly (AdaMerging is less resource-intensive than ATM).  
- **Experimental scope is limited.** Experiments are restricted to small/outdated models (ViT-B-16, BERT). Standard larger architectures (ViT-L, T5, LLaMA) are not evaluated, likely due to ATM’s high computational overhead when scaling to many tasks.  
- **Presentation issues.** Figures are low quality, extend beyond page margins, are truncated, and some captions are missing (e.g., Figure 3). Table 2 appears before Table 1, breaking logical flow. Notation is inconsistent (θ₀, θ_base, θ_init used interchangeably). Results are shown with qualitative radar charts rather than numerical tables, making it difficult to compare accuracies. Single-task accuracies after normal fine-tuning and normalized accuracies are not reported.  
- **Theoretical analysis lacks soundness and testing.** The claim that task vectors equal multi-task gradients for one epoch is not tested with gradient descent (GD); experiments use SGD, which invalidates the exact equivalence. Testing with GD (via gradient accumulation) would be straightforward but is not done. Merging after a single epoch is not shown to be optimal (the model at that point is still near zero-shot). The insight that “the greatest contribution is given by the first epoch” is not novel—it is a known property of fine-tuning adaptation. The increased orthogonality comparison is unfair because the authors compare task vectors from the last ATM iteration (which already contain joint features) against task vectors from standard full fine-tuning (task-specific only).  
- **Inappropriate cost comparison.** Section 6.6 compares only the *merging stage* computational/storage costs of ATM against baselines, ignoring the substantial training costs (which are unavoidable for ATM). For competing methods, training is done once per task; for ATM, training is done repeatedly in an alternating cycle.  
- **Overstated results.** Given suboptimal baselines and missing comparisons, the claimed improvements “up to 19% in vision and 20% in NLP” are considered far-fetched by some reviewers.  
- **Lack of proper citations.** The paper overlooks the multi-task learning literature and does not cite relevant work on gradient conflict interference (e.g., gradient surgery) and task interference analysis, which are closely related. The similarity to heterogeneous federated averaging (local SGD) is not acknowledged or discussed.  
- **Data requirements.** The method requires the original training data (or at least validation data) for alternating fine-tuning, whereas many baselines are data-free and training-free. This makes the application scenarios fundamentally different. ATM should instead be presented as a new training paradigm that uses merging, not as a model merging method.  
- **Specific reviewer questions that overlap with weaknesses:** Why is pure MTL (no task-specific updates) not compared? The baseline in Table 1 likely corresponds to epoch 10; is 10 epochs necessary when the method could stop earlier? Does the performance decline of one-shot merging in Figure 6 indicate over-training of individual models? The valFT ATM result with only the validation set is noteworthy but lacks details. Theorem 3.1 equivalence holds only for 1 epoch; reporting MTL results would help validate the theory.
