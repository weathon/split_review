- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents IRIS, an iterative framework that combines web document retrieval, LLM-based value extraction, and causal discovery algorithms to construct causal graphs from a set of initial variables. It tests the approach on five epidemiology-based variable sets, comparing output graphs to those built by human experts. All four reviewers acknowledge the novelty of using LLMs and text corpora for causal discovery and appreciate the quantitative evaluation effort. However, there is strong consensus that the evaluation focuses on replicating expert judgments rather than true causal effects, that the data extraction pipeline produces data incompatible with standard causal discovery assumptions, that the handling of cyclic and temporal models is underspecified, and that the experimental reporting is inconsistent or incomplete. One reviewer (R1, score 1, confidence 4) is significantly more negative, questioning the fundamental validity of the approach, while the other three (score 3) raise overlapping concerns about applicability, assumptions, and interpretation.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and interesting challenge: using LLMs and large text corpora to assist in constructing causal graphical models (R1, R3).
- The iterative design (IRIS) could lower computational complexity by stopping when a sufficiently stable solution is found (R4).
- Automated data collection and missing variable proposal are useful for bootstrapping causal discovery and addressing causal insufficiency (R4).
- The experimental evaluation is extensive: it uses real-world variable sets from epidemiology, recruits three experts to create gold-standard graphs, tests each hybrid component’s contribution, and includes multiple datasets (R2, R3).
- The hybrid algorithm (IRIS) is shown to outperform previous LLM-based competitors (R2). One reviewer notes that the results are limited but understandable given the cost of creating gold-standard graphs (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation validity** – The “ground truth” graphs are constructed by human experts, so the evaluation only checks whether IRIS replicates expert causal judgments that are already embedded in LLM training data. This avoids the core goal of causal inference: estimating interventional effects that are independent of expert opinion. One reviewer (R1, score 1, confidence 4) strongly argues that this design eliminates any possible mismatch between the formal model and actual causal effects, and that IRIS simply reinforces, rather than checks, human judgments.
- **Unclear mechanism** – The paper shows *that* IRIS improves accuracy but does not explain *why*. Neither controlled experiments nor a detailed mechanism are provided, making the results unconvincing (R1, R2). R1 further states that it seems unlikely sufficiently accurate quantitative data can be extracted from large text corpora to improve causal graph construction.
- **Lack of semantics for cyclic and temporal models** – The authors construct cyclic graphs and claim to discard the acyclicity assumption, but they provide no formal semantics for cycles, no inference rules, and no discussion of challenges such as consistent timescales among feedback loops (R1). Relatedly, it is unclear whether time is modeled implicitly or whether the framework handles the difference between static and dynamic causal models (R4).
- **Causal sufficiency claim** – The paper claims that the missing variable component relaxes the causal sufficiency assumption. In reality, it only makes the assumption more likely to hold if the added variables are true latent confounders; no evidence is given that the added variables typically correspond to confounders. In the provided examples, very few of the added variables are confounders (R1, R2).
- **Missing variable assumptions** – No evidence is provided that the discovered variables satisfy necessary assumptions of causal graphical models, such as modularity, autonomy, or independence of causal mechanism (R1).
- **Data extraction challenges unaddressed** – Automatic extraction of structured data from text can introduce variation in unit/variable definitions, heterogeneity that creates new confounding, and selection bias (e.g., conditioning on a collider). These threats are not mentioned or addressed (R1).
- **Value extraction procedure is problematic and poorly explained** – The prompt asks whether the existence of a variable can be inferred from a document, which forces binary output. Yet the method is applied to datasets where variables have three values, and it is unclear how precision/recall are computed for multiclass variables (R2). The AppleGastrome dataset (LLM-generated apple reviews) is used as a test case, but such documents are fundamentally different from the kind of documents one would expect in epidemiology (R2). Two reviewers (R2, R3) question whether numeric data can be reliably extracted: R1 gives the concrete example that documents about “smoking and cancer and pollution” would rarely contain instance-level numeric data (e.g., a person’s exact smoking rate, pollution level, and outcome).
- **Structured data incompatible with causal discovery algorithms** – The value extraction produces a binary document-by-variable matrix (True/False indicating whether a variable’s existence can be inferred from a document). Causal discovery algorithms (PC, GES, NOTEARS) are designed for continuous or multi-valued observational data where each row is an i.i.d. unit. Forcing variables to binary discards information, documents may be dependent (e.g., one cites another), and selection bias from keyword-based retrieval is ignored. No justification is given that discovery algorithms can be meaningfully applied to such a table (R2, R3). R4 notes that the “statistical causal discovery” in this paper actually uses term-document frequency, which has nothing to do with the cited literature.
- **Assumptions of causal discovery methods unmet** – The discovery methods rely on assumptions such as acyclicity and causal sufficiency, which are not met by the extracted data, and the paper provides no justification that the results are causal (R3). The relationships identified are co-occurrence, not causation (R3, R4).
- **Integration of new variables is naive** – When a new variable is added with background knowledge (e.g., A → X → B), it is not clear whether edge A → B should be removed or retained, nor whether existing orientations (e.g., A → B ← C) should be re-evaluated in light of the new edge (R2).
- **Inconsistent experimental reporting** – Tables 3-7 report different subsets of IRIS model variations across datasets (e.g., some include IRIS (Llama)-PC + VCR, others IRIS-GES + VCR, etc.). This selective reporting is unsound (R3). With 10 IRIS variations and 3 comparison methods, concluding that IRIS outperforms based on one best variation per dataset introduces a multiple-comparison bias (R3). R2 also notes that GES is claimed to have the highest F1/NHD ratio, but no standalone GES test is shown in Section 6.
- **LLM training data contamination** – LLMs are trained on a vast corpus that likely includes the very papers from which the evaluation datasets are derived (e.g., epidemiology literature). No conclusions about generalization or validity can be drawn (R4). One reviewer (R4) asks: How can we test a model that has been trained on the train set?
- **Notation and presentation issues** – The paper uses arrow directions inconsistently (“ → ” vs “ ← ”) and leaves several symbols undefined (bold X, Ĝ, NHD) (R4). Citations are given without parentheses, making it hard to distinguish them from text (R1).
- **Document retrieval quality** – Queries are based on iterative keyword reduction; there is no assurance that retrieved documents are relevant, fake, or come from reputable academic repositories (R4). One reviewer (R4) asks: How can we assure that the retrieved documents are specific to our context?
- **Acyclicity as a limitation** – It is unclear why acyclicity is framed as a limitation. Feedback loops require explicit modeling of time; the paper does not address whether time is being modeled or how (R4). R1 also raised this as part of the missing temporal semantics.
- **Assumption that variables are categorical** – The method implicitly assumes variables are categorical; no justification is given for this assumption (R4).
