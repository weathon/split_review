## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces NeuroLM, a multi-task foundation model that bridges EEG signals and Large Language Models (LLMs) by treating EEG as a foreign language. A text-aligned neural tokenizer (via vector-quantized temporal-frequency prediction) encodes EEG into discrete tokens, which are fed into an LLM (GPT-2) for multi-channel autoregressive pre-training. Multi-task instruction tuning enables unified handling of diverse EEG tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. The largest variant (NeuroLM-XL, 1.7B parameters) is pre-trained on ~25,000 hours of EEG data and evaluated on six downstream tasks (abnormal detection, event type classification, emotion recognition, sleep stage classification, cognitive workload detection, slowing event classification). The model demonstrates competitive but not always state-of-the-art performance across tasks.

### Strengths
- Innovative first multi-task foundation model for EEG, using instruction tuning to unify several tasks in a single model without per-task fine-tuning.
- Large-scale pre-training (25,000+ hours) and evaluation on six diverse EEG datasets, showing broad applicability.
- Treating EEG as a “foreign language” and leveraging LLMs is a novel direction for EEG signal processing.
- Detailed ablation studies examining EEG-text embedding alignment, multi-channel autoregressive pre-training, and robustness to instruction variations (R1 notes this; R4 questions its depth—see Weaknesses).
- Clear descriptions of model architecture, training procedures, and experimental setups, aiding reproducibility (R1; contrast with R3’s reproducibility concerns below).

### Weaknesses
- **Performance gaps vs. task-specific models**: NeuroLM underperforms specialized models like LaBraM on most tasks, with gaps that are “not just marginal” for some (R2). R1 suggests emphasizing the generalization benefits of unified instruction tuning, but the paper does not demonstrate whether multi-task learning improves adaptability to unseen tasks or enables zero-shot/few-shot learning.
- **Fluctuation across tasks and lack of analysis**: Performance varies widely across datasets without explanation (R2). For example, on TUAB, large-scale pretraining yields only minimal improvement (R1). The paper does not analyze factors such as dataset size, task complexity, or EEG signal characteristics that might explain variability. Also missing: discussion of robustness to noisy/artifact-laden EEG data (R2).
- **No exploration of fine-tuning for SOTA**: The paper presents zero-shot/instruction-tuned results but does not examine task-specific fine-tuning of NeuroLM. It is unclear how much improvement could be gained on each task or whether multi-task capabilities would be retained (R3).
- **Limited LLM architecture testing**: Only GPT-2 is used; insights into scalability to more advanced LLMs (e.g., larger models, different architectures) are absent (R3). This raises questions about the approach’s generalizability and adaptability.
- **EEG-text alignment concerns (R4, R1, R3)**: 
  - Effectiveness of adversarial alignment is questioned: EEG and text have no inherent connection (especially since EEG signals are not language-evoked), and a single gradient reversal layer may only distinguish domains rather than align embeddings (R4).
  - Source and generation method of text embeddings are not explained (R4).
  - No t-SNE visualizations or quantitative ablation demonstrate the alignment’s impact on downstream performance (R4, R1). R3 notes lack of ablation for adversarial training specifically.
- **Theoretical foundation of multi-channel autoregression**: The explanation of causal dependency modeling between EEG channels is high-level (analogy to VAE) and lacks detailed mathematical formulations or justifications (R4, R1 on stop gradient operator).
- **Instruction design impact**: One reviewer (R4) finds no analysis of how variations in prompt formulations affect downstream performance; another (R1) claims robustness to instruction variations is ablated. This disagreement signals insufficient clarity or depth in the analysis.
- **Data curriculum / input order**: No exploration of how sequencing data (e.g., simpler tasks first) affects multi-task learning outcomes (R4).
- **Complexity and reproducibility**: The multi-stage pipeline (adversarial training, pre-training, instruction tuning) and hyperparameters are insufficiently documented for easy reproduction (R3). R1’s positive assessment of clarity does not fully mitigate these concerns.
- **Minor presentation issue**: Figure 1 colormap makes NeuroLM and LaBraM hard to distinguish (R1).
