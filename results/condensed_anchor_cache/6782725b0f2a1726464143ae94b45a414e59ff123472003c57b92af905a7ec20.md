## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces KARA (Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Autoencoder), an autoencoder with learnable activation functions based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem. The learnable activations use B‑spline basis functions via the Cox‑de‑Boor formula. The encoder and decoder include Learnable Activation (LA) and Shift‑Invariant Learnable Activation (SILA) layers. Experiments on MNIST and Fashion‑MNIST compare reconstruction accuracy against linear, convolutional, and LA encoders/decoders. The paper targets high‑dimensional data processing.

### Strengths
- The paper is well‑written, easy to follow, and logically organized; mathematical notation is clear (R1, R3, R4). The “verbose” notation in Section 3.3 makes the strategy immediately understandable (R4).
- Integrating the Kolmogorov‑Arnold representation theorem with autoencoders is interesting and underexplored (R1).
- The proposed method for generating learnable activations (B‑splines) is sensible (R3).
- The experiments, while limited, are valid and demonstrate potential for learnable activations in data compression (R2, R3).
- There is substantial reviewer disagreement: two reviewers rated the paper 3 (indicate some value), while two rated it 1 (strong rejection), reflecting a split opinion on quality (contrast: positive minority).

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow scope and insufficient evaluation**: The paper evaluates only reconstruction accuracy on MNIST and Fashion‑MNIST, which does not justify the title “Enhancing High‑dimensional Data Processing.” Experiments on more complex datasets (e.g., CIFAR‑10, CIFAR‑100, ImageNet, SVHN) and comparisons to state‑of‑the‑art methods are needed (R2, R4).
- **Missing analysis of learned activation functions**: The paper does not examine the properties of the learned functions. It is unclear whether they differ significantly, converge to similar shapes, or generalize beyond dimensionality reduction (R1, R3). Suggested analyses: visualize activation functions, measure their diversity and distribution across features, and investigate whether the problem or network structure shapes them (R3).
- **Novelty and contribution are questionable**: Sections 3.1, 3.2, and 3.4 recap known advancements from the original KAN paper; Section 3.3 describes convolutional KANs (arXiv:2406.13155) without citation (R4). Combining two KANs into an autoencoder is a straightforward application; a substantial model contribution or a thorough theoretical/empirical analysis is required for a top venue (R2, R4).
- **Missing comparisons and ablation experiments**:
  - No comparison with architectures that have the same number of learnable parameters (R1).
  - No evaluation of parameter count, training speed, or computational cost (FLOPs, time complexity) (R2, R4).
  - The SILA layer is said to have fewer parameters than LA, but this is not exploited or analyzed; details on kernel sizes, number of combined kernels, and number of layers are absent (R4).
  - The paper does not test whether good results are due to trainability or simply mixing different fixed activation functions; additional experiments with mixed fixed activations are needed (R3).
  - Table 2 omits the SILA‑SILA configuration; why is SILA not needed in the decoder? (R3)
  - The significance of results given the cost of training activation functions is not analyzed; a classification layer to test latent space quality is suggested (R3).
- **Missing experimental details**: No detailed experimental setup (e.g., layer counts, kernel sizes) is provided in the paper or appendix (R2, R4). No code is released (R4).
- **Insufficient related work**: Does not cite prior KAN‑based works such as U‑KAN, KAT, or KAN Autoencoders (arXiv:2410.02077) (R4).
- **Limited generality**: The paper does not discuss applicability to other tasks (e.g., NLP) (R2).
