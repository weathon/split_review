## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a method for learning transferable sub-goals in hierarchical RL by training an ensemble of classifiers (using Diversity-By-Disagreement Training, D-BAT) that each represent a unique hypothesis about which features of a sub-goal generalize to new contexts. The agent selects among these hypotheses based on which yields higher external reward. Experiments are conducted in MiniGrid DoorKey and Montezuma’s Revenge. One reviewer (Rating 6) considered the paper a strong, novel contribution and praised the concept of hypotheses and the integration of D-BAT, while three reviewers (Rating 3) identified significant flaws in method clarity, experimental design, positioning, and lack of comparison to existing HRL and representation learning work.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses the important and necessary problem of learning transferable skills in hierarchical RL. The motivation for robust skill generalization is clear.
- The concept of maintaining multiple diverse hypotheses (via an ensemble of classifiers) about which features of a sub-goal will generalize is novel and highlights a typically overlooked aspect of representation learning in RL.
- The use of D‑BAT, a non-standard classification method, to learn state classifiers for encoding sub-goals is an interesting application.
- The paper is generally well‑written, organized logically, and the Results section explicitly lists and attempts to answer several specific questions.
- Multiple pieces of empirical evidence are provided (accuracy of classifiers, policy transfer in MiniGrid, Montezuma’s Revenge) to demonstrate that the ensemble approach can improve generalization over a single CNN classifier.
- The method builds upon and intersects with multiple areas: sub‑goal transfer (generalizing forward from a single option instance), unsupervised representation learning, and the RL hypothesis that the most transferable hypothesis will maximize external reward.
- One reviewer (Rating 6) praised the quality of the empirical investigations and the validation of each algorithmic component.

### Weaknesses
- **Overgeneralizing claims about HRL / lack of theoretical grounding**  
  The paper makes imprecise and overgeneralizing statements about the options framework (e.g., equating termination conditions with sub‑goal sets, claiming all HRL methods condition on the entire state without supporting evidence). Using D‑BAT to claim generalizability for RL sub‑goals is not justified theoretically; the implicit assumption that robust classification implies transferable RL skills is not proven.
- **Algorithm and method description is critically unclear**  
  It is not obvious what the full algorithm is: what are the inputs to D‑BAT, how are option policies trained from the classifier outputs (e.g., sparse reward? manually initialized states?), what is the reward function for skills, and how does the hierarchy work? No pseudocode, mathematical formulation of the “hypothesis”, or theorem is provided. The paper relies heavily on D‑BAT without adequate formalism and does not explain why D‑BAT is chosen over other robust hypothesis methods (no ablation comparing D‑BAT to alternatives, only to a CNN).
- **Sub‑goal discovery is not automated**  
  The images used to train D‑BAT are hand‑picked, and there is no mechanism for autonomous sub‑goal discovery. This severely limits scalability; the paper does not address how sub‑goals would be identified in general settings.
- **Dependence on pre‑existing option policies**  
  The method requires training preliminary options to generate rich enough data for the ensemble classifiers. It is unclear where these options come from (e.g., in MiniGrid, three hand‑designed options were used). If those options already solve the task, the benefit of learning classifiers on top of them is questionable. The paper does not justify why this extra step is beneficial or how to guarantee rich data generation.
- **Missing comparisons**  
  - No comparison with any other hierarchical RL method (e.g., DCEO, deep Laplacian options, meta‑learning shared hierarchies).  
  - Baselines in Montezuma’s Revenge are only CNN classifiers, not other skill‑discovery or transfer methods.  
  - Baselines in MiniGrid are DQN and PPO, which are deep RL algorithms, not state‑of‑the‑art HRL or exploration methods (e.g., RND or DCEO might be more appropriate). The poor performance of DQN/PPO (failing to succeed in 1.5M steps) raises concerns about hyperparameter selection and exploration; this makes the comparison invalid without further investigation.  
  - No comparison with reward‑agnostic option discovery, contrastive behavioral similarity embeddings, or invariant representation learning methods (e.g., Agarwal et al. 2021, Barreto et al. 2019, Eysenbach et al. 2019, Frans et al. 2018, Gomez et al. 2022, Klissarov et al. 2023, Touati & Ollivier 2023, Zhang et al. 2021). The paper ignores these relevant works and thus does not support the claim that existing HRL methods fail due to poor state generalization.
- **Experimental design issues**  
  - Only three random seeds per curve are used, which is insufficient to assess variance, especially in deterministic environments.  
  - The accuracy calculation in Section 4.1 is not clearly explained; it is unclear what the ensembles are being compared against. The claim that performance on one‑ladder instances is most important is not justified given the low accuracy compared to the two‑ladder case.  
  - The training process for MiniGrid (Section 4.3) is unclear: how data from different seeds is mixed and used to train the ensemble is not described, raising concerns about validity of the aggregated results.  
  - Downstream performance is only evaluated in one task; the paper lacks evidence of transfer under different reward functions or dynamics.  
  - In Montezuma’s Revenge, the agent is manually placed at the top of stairs; the method does not handle arbitrary initial states, limiting real‑world applicability.  
  - The interpretation of Figure 5 is disputed: one reviewer argues the dots show the agent only learned to go down a few steps, not successfully transfer the notion of going down long ladders.
- **Minor issues**  
  - Line 320 should reference Figure 4, not Figure 5.  
  - “Manhattan” is misspelled multiple times.  
  - P.6 line 1: “need to be” instead of “need be”.  
  - Figure 6 uses standard error instead of standard deviation without justification.
