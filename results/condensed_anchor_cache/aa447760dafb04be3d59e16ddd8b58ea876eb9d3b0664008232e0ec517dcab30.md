## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies stochastic multi-armed bandit algorithms robust to adversarial attacks where the attacker, after observing the learner's action and the realized reward, can alter the reward. Two settings are considered: known attack budget C (upper bound on cumulative perturbation) and unknown C. For known C, the paper provides gap-dependent regret O((K/Δ) log T + KC) and gap-independent bounds Õ(√(KTC)) and Õ(√(KT) + KC). For unknown C, it gives additive bounds Õ(√(KT) + KC²) and multiplicative bounds Õ(KC√T). Lower bounds show tightness for the known C case and separate the attack model from the corruption model. Experiments validate the algorithms against attack strategies from Jun et al. (2018).

### Strengths
- The paper explores an under-studied area of stochastic bandits under adversarial attacks, obtaining novel results and improving prior work on the corruption model (Reviewer 1, high confidence).
- Theoretical bounds are tight (up to log terms) with full proofs; the known‑C upper bounds match lower bounds (Reviewers 1,2).
- Clear separation between attack and corruption models is presented, highlighting the increased difficulty of attacks due to the adversary’s ability to observe the action and reward (Reviewers 1,4).
- The paper examines both additive and multiplicative bounds, providing a comparison that clarifies which approach is better depending on C (Reviewer 2).
- Figures 1 and, especially, Figure 2 illustrate attack‑based multiplicative and additive bounds in a well‑structured way (Reviewer 2).
- For the successive elimination algorithm SE‑WR (from Lykouris et al. 2018), a tighter regret bound is shown: a better concentration analysis gives an O(KC) term instead of a gap‑dependent term (Reviewer 4).
- The paper extends SE‑WR to the unknown‑budget setting with additive guarantees and provides analysis for all proposed algorithms (Reviewer 4).
- Experimental evidence is provided against the attack strategies of Jun et al. (2018) and compared with multiple MAB defense strategies from the literature (Reviewers 1,4).
- The paper is well‑written and clearly describes its relationship to and improvement over prior work (Reviewers 1,3).
- Note: One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 8) rated the paper significantly higher than the others (scores 6), finding the contributions more novel and important.

### Weaknesses
- **Algorithmic novelty is limited.** The core algorithms (phased elimination, CORRAL, successive elimination) are adaptations of existing techniques; no fundamentally new proof techniques or insights are introduced (Reviewers 2 (minor), 3, 4). Reviewer 3 stresses that the modifications are straightforward and the analysis is not involved.
- **Literature review and credit are incomplete.** The terminology shift to “attacks” should credit the original work: “Corruption‑Tolerant Gaussian Process Bandit Optimization” (Bogunovic et al., 2020). The reference list is brief and disorganized; missing citations include “Stochastic Linear Bandits Robust to Adversarial Attacks” and “A Robust Phased Elimination Algorithm for Corruption‑Tolerant Gaussian Process Bandits.” Furthermore, Bogunovic et al. (2020) only covers the GP setting, not linear bandits (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical applicability of the attack model is insufficiently discussed.** The strong adversarial assumption (full knowledge of action and reward realization) may not hold in real‑world settings like recommendation systems or online auctions; the paper should elaborate on the plausibility of this model and provide concrete applications (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear whether gap‑dependent results can be obtained for the unknown horizon setting.** The paper does not clarify this (Reviewer 4). (This is also posed as a question: see questions below.)
- **Lower bound claim is not entirely new.** The Ω(KC) lower bound was already established in “Stochastic Linear Bandits Robust to Adversarial Attacks” (Appendix C.3) with similar proof (Reviewer 2).
- **Experimental results lack confidence bars and show poor performance in low‑corruption settings.** Under no corruptions with known budget, the STOP algorithms perform worse than other methods; this behavior needs discussion (Reviewer 4).
- **Writing is dense and focused on presenting many results; more interpretation would help.** The paper could be better formatted with more discussion around the implications of the results (Reviewer 4).
- **Proofs are relegated to the appendix** (Reviewer 1).
- **Several typos and inconsistencies:** Line 091: “To address with MAB with” → “To address the MAB with”; Line 103: “Show that which type” → “Show which type”; Line 185: “Lipchitz” → “Lipschitz” (Reviewer 1). In the proof of Lemma 14, constants change from 36 to 64 in N_k; Line 798 should read “triggered” instead of “trigger”; Lines 804‑807 contain δ with subscripts (Reviewer 4).
- **Venue suitability:** It is not clear that this work is a strong fit for ICLR; the authors might consider an alternative venue (Reviewer 2, minor).

**Questions that overlap with criticisms / missing experiments / ablations** (included as weaknesses above):
1. Is the uniqueness of the best arm assumption necessary? What happens if it is not fulfilled? (Reviewer 1)
2. Could the authors provide concrete applications addressed by their model? (Reviewer 1)
3. How do the lower bounds change or are implied from the corruption setting to the attack setting in the case of unknown horizons? (Reviewer 4)
4. Can gap‑dependent results be obtained for the unknown corruption case under the considered algorithms? (Reviewer 4)
5. Why do the algorithms perform worse in low corruption settings in the experiments? (Reviewer 4)
