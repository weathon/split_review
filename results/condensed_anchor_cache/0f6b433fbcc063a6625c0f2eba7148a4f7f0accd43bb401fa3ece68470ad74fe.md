## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an Adaptive Clustering (AC) router for Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, viewing routing as a feature-weighted clustering optimization problem. It derives closed-form optimal feature weights that, in a transformed space, promote tight, well-separated clusters for token-expert matching. The method requires no extra learnable parameters, introduces negligible computational overhead, and is integrable into existing MoE backbones. Experiments on large-scale language modeling (WikiText-103) and image classification (ImageNet) with backbones like Switch Transformer, GLaM, and Swin Transformers show faster convergence, improved robustness to data corruption, and overall performance gains.

### Strengths

1. **Novel theoretical perspective**: Views MoE routing through the lens of feature-weighted clustering optimization, deriving optimal feature weights that improve cluster separability. This framing is unique and well-motivated. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)

2. **Solid theoretical framework**: Rigorous proofs support faster convergence and improved robustness. The appendix provides clear, extensive material on the proofs and derivations. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)

3. **No additional parameters or computation**: The AC router requires no extra learnable parameters and introduces negligible computational overhead, making it easily integrable into any existing MoE architecture. (Reviewers 1, 2)

4. **Extensive evaluation**: Experiments on two complementary domains (language modeling on WikiText-103, image classification on ImageNet) using diverse backbones (Switch Transformer, GLaM, Swin Transformers) under both clean and corrupted data settings. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)

5. **Clear writing and motivation**: The paper is clearly written, well motivated, and the experiments are well executed. The appendix contains abundant complementary material. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses

*Note: Reviewers’ scores are split (6, 8, 8, 6). The following weaknesses aggregate concerns from all reviewers.*

1. **Reliance on prior layer assignments and consistent embedding sizes**: The AC router uses expert assignments from the previous layer to compute the adaptive transformation, assuming a consistent feature space across adjacent layers. This limits applicability to architectures with changing embedding dimensions (e.g., some CNNs, transformers with varying layer widths). It also propagates errors if early layer assignments are poor (early layers often learn basic features that lack semantically distinct clusters); no mechanism accounts for uncertainty in these assignments. (Reviewers 1, 2)  
   *Related questions*: How to handle noisy or not well-defined prior assignments? Is there a way to initialize for early layers or when prior assignments are unreliable? How to adapt for architectures with changing embedding dimensions? (Reviewer 2)

2. **Assumption of well-structured, identifiable clusters**: The method assumes input data naturally clusters in the feature space and that clusters are separable. It may struggle with overlapping, highly indistinct, dynamically evolving, or non-Gaussian clusters, and performance may degrade on datasets without clear clustering patterns or where the feature space does not align with cluster structure. (Reviewers 1, 2)  
   *Related questions*: How does the router handle overlapping or dynamically evolving clusters? How does it perform when clusters are non-Gaussian or not well-separated? (Reviewers 1, 2)

3. **Necessity and simplicity of the framework**: While theoretical proofs show the method is sufficient, it is unclear whether simpler operations (e.g., avoiding a full sort for top-k selection) could achieve similar performance. A more systematic comparison with related competitor work would better assess the practical impact. (Reviewer 3)

4. **Lack of quantitative assessment of structural differences**: The paper provides anecdotal evidence (Fig 1) and aggregated load balancing analysis (sec 4.3) but does not give a quantitative distribution of how often the AC router changes top-k selections compared to competing methods. Given that end-to-end performance improvements are slim (few percentage points), this intermediate assessment would help measure the impact of changed routing decisions. (Reviewer 3)

5. **Sensitivity to the choice of dispersion measure**: The paper uses mean absolute deviation (MAD) but does not explore the impact of other measures (variance, interquartile range, median absolute deviation). It is unclear whether the proofs and practical performance rely on this specific hyperparameter; clarification on whether the proof holds for families of dispersion measures would strengthen the paper. (Reviewers 2, 3)  
   *Related question*: How sensitive is the method to the choice of dispersion measure? (Reviewer 2)

6. **Limited evaluation on dynamic or non‑stationary data**: The experiments do not fully explore the router’s behavior under data distribution shifts over time, leaving questions about real-world robustness. (Reviewer 1)

7. **MoE‑specific focus and terminology**: The paper targets MoE experts exclusively, and some notation/terms are specific to the MoE domain, potentially limiting accessibility. (Reviewer 4)
