## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MMMT-IF, a multi‑turn multimodal instruction following benchmark that extends the MMDU dataset by inserting global formatting instructions between questions in image‑based QA dialogues. Instructions are verifiable via code execution. The authors propose the PIF metric (fraction of instructions correctly followed per sample) and PIF‑N‑K (robustness across repeated responses). Evaluations on Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT‑4o, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet show PIF dropping from 0.81 at turn 1 to 0.64 at turn 20; PIF‑4‑4 achieves only 11% for GPT‑4o and Gemini. Appending all instructions at the end improves PIF by 22.3 points, indicating retrieval difficulty. Human correlation is 0.60.

### Strengths
- **Programmatically verifiable metrics** – PIF and PIF‑N‑K provide an objective, automated evaluation of instruction following, reducing human bias (R1, R2, R3). Human correlation of 0.60 supports validity (R2). The paper also analyses self‑assessment bias in LLM judges (R2).
- **Challenging, real‑world‑relevant benchmark** – Extends existing datasets to long‑form multimodal dialogue (up to 20 turns, ~20k tokens) requiring retrieval of instructions dispersed across the context (R2, R3). R3 particularly noted its relevance for real‑world interactions where instructions are scattered.
- **Reveals concrete model shortcomings** – Demonstrates significant performance drops with increasing instruction count (0.81 → 0.64) and that the primary difficulty is instruction retrieval, not just following (R1, R2, R3). The 22.3‑point improvement when instructions are appended provides direct evidence of this retrieval challenge (R1). Provides insight for long‑context reasoning research (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and clarity** – MMMT‑IF is mostly an augmentation of the existing MMDU dataset, and the paper lacks deeper insightful findings beyond the observed performance drop (R1). The differences from MMDU are not clearly explained in the main text, and a full example of the dataset extension is missing from the main paper (R1, R2). More detailed statistics and a concrete example of how instructions are translated into executable code are needed; the concept of “instruction” should be defined more clearly (R2).
- **Insufficient model coverage** – Evaluation is limited to proprietary models (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet). Open‑source models such as LLaMA, Qwen2‑VL, and InternVL‑2 are not tested (R1, R2). This restricts analysis (e.g., attention‑weight studies) that could explain failure modes (R2). Only “needles in a haystack” and sampling robustness analyses are provided; a more in‑depth investigation of why models fail on specific cases is needed (R2).
- **Missing analyses and unanswered questions** – No analysis of model size effect on long‑term instruction following (R1). Questions remain about how PIF scales with larger datasets/models, whether the benchmark can be adapted to single‑modality tasks, how instruction complexity impacts performance, what training adjustments are needed, and how architectural features (memory, context window size) influence performance (R3).
- **Human evaluation details lacking** – The correlation metric (Pearson/Spearman) is not specified, and inter‑annotator agreement is not reported (R2).
- **Potential usability and overfitting concerns** – R3 (score 6, more positive than R1/R2) notes that the benchmark’s high difficulty may limit immediate usability for current models and real‑world applications, and that the computational demands of evaluation could hinder researchers with limited resources. Additionally, the need to follow complex instructions might encourage overfitting on the benchmark rather than generalizable improvements (R3).
