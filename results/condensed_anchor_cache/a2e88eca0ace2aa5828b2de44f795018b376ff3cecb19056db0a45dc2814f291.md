## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Guided Strategy Discovery (GSD), a diversity formulation using a learned task-relevance measure to prioritize behaviors that explore modeled latent factors. GSD is validated on three continuous control benchmarks (interpolation/extrapolation) and a real-to-sim table tennis task. The paper is well-structured, clear, and uses illustrative figures. However, several reviewers note limitations in evaluation scope, comparisons, and task complexity.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and well-structured (R1, R4). The proposed method is formulated clearly and scoped within relevant literature (R1, R4).
- The 2D PointMaze toy task convincingly demonstrates that prior formulations (InfoGAIL, Lipschitz constraints) fail to generate new task-accomplishing behaviors (R3).
- The motivation for task-relevant diversity is insightful and novel (R3, R4).
- The empirical evaluation is robust across multiple domains (robot control, driving, manipulation) and shows consistent improvement over baselines, especially in behavior generalization (R3, R4).
- Metrics like interpolation and extrapolation errors are well-chosen to highlight effectiveness for diverse and novel task variations (R4).
- The use of real-world human data for evaluation (table tennis) is praiseworthy and enhances applicability (R2, R3).
- Figures and visualizations are clear and help capture the contribution (R1, R2, R4). Minority reviewer (R3, score 8) is particularly positive about the method’s effectiveness and motivation.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited to one-dimensional latent factors.** Experiments only cover 1-d latent factors (position, velocity, ball height), restricting assessment of scalability to higher-dimensional or correlated latent factors common in real-world tasks like robot manipulation (R1, R3, R4). It is unclear how GSD would handle pushing tasks from D3IL (R3) or multiple degrees of freedom (R3, R4).
- **Narrow comparison set.** The method is only evaluated within the InfoGAIL framework; comparisons to other adversarial imitation learning (AIL) methods (e.g., those in Orsini et al., Jena et al.) and to non-adversarial approaches (e.g., behavior cloning with VAEs, normalizing flows, diffusion-based methods) are missing (R2, R3). This limits understanding of GSD’s relative performance and general applicability (R2).
- **Unclear exploration limitations and demo sensitivity.** The paper does not discuss whether the exploration strategy is bounded by the framework and how that affects final performance (R1). It also does not analyze how much the representation is limited by the demonstration distribution or whether it suffers from problems similar to behavior cloning (e.g., sensitivity to demonstration quality) (R1).
- **Assumptions in diversity formulation may not generalize.** The linear constraint (Eq. 1) assumes continuously varying task relevance in state-action space, which may fail for tasks with sparse, non-adjacent high-energy regions (R4). The energy function (discriminator) assumes high occupancy correlates with task success, which may not hold for non-expert demonstrations or partially observable environments (R4). The choice of task-relevance measure and its regularization effects are insufficiently discussed (R1). The derivation and modeling of the energy function \( f(s,a) \) are unclear (R3).
- **Writing and presentation issues.** Figure 5 (interpolation/extrapolation) is difficult to read (R1). The term "preference" is used inconsistently with its common meaning in RLHF, causing confusion (R2). Figure 10 caption is integrated into text, making it hard to read (R2). Algorithm 1 (Lines 270-287) is dense and the notation in Line 274 (commas after terms like “policy, \(\pi\)”) is confusing (R2). Some technical sections on the diversity objective could be simplified for broader audience (R4).
- **Missing benchmarks and resources.** The tasks do not align with established benchmarks for diverse behaviors (e.g., Jia et al. 2024) (R2). Code and the real-world table tennis dataset are not promised for release, hindering reproducibility (R2, R3).
- **Open questions about method design and alternatives.** GSD builds on InfoGAIL but does not explore other AIL design choices (discriminator architecture, optimization, regularization) and how they might affect diversity (R2). It is unclear whether the diversity objectives could be used in general behavior cloning (R3). The need for GSD given existing mutual-information and latent-structure methods (Li et al. 2023, ASE, FLD) is not convincingly motivated (R4). For HalfCheetah, the notion of “diverse behaviors” (speeds) and relevance to task performance is vague (R2). PPOBC is used in DriveLaneshift and FetchPickPlace; it is unclear how the method would differ if using pure PPO without demonstrations (R2).
