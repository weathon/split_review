## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Tra-MoE, a trajectory prediction model using sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) with a top-1 gating strategy, and an adaptive policy conditioning technique that learns 2D mask representations aligned with image observations. The approach builds on prior work ATM and aims to scale trajectory prediction by leveraging out-of-domain data. Experiments are conducted on the LIBERO benchmark (130 tasks, 10–20 demos per task) and a real robot. Tra-MoE outperforms a dense baseline scaled to the same parameter count, but the reported gains are modest. Reviewers note that the work is straightforward and worth trying, but raise significant concerns about novelty, the definition of out-of-domain data, the indirect evaluation metric (success rate), missing trajectory-prediction-level comparisons, and the clarity of presentation.

### Strengths
- Sparse MoE (top-1 gating) enables parameter specialization while keeping FLOPs per token constant, offering a computationally efficient way to incorporate more data into trajectory prediction.
- The adaptive policy conditioning using learnable 2D mask representations aligned with image observations is an effective way to enhance policy guidance (reviewers call it innovative and superior to prior conditioning methods).
- The paper provides an empirical analysis of MoE training techniques (router z-loss, load-balancing loss, noise addition) specific to trajectory models, offering practical insights for the robotics community.
- Validation on a low-cost physical robot platform strengthens the practical applicability.
- Tests on the established LIBERO benchmark (130 tasks) and real-world scenarios are included, along with ablation studies covering multiple design choices.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and incremental contribution** – The core ideas (point-trajectory prediction for policy conditioning) were introduced by ATM and related works. The two modifications – MoE blocks and a new conditioning mechanism – are considered incremental. One reviewer states that the work is “essentially combining ATM with MoE,” and another notes that scaling via increased data is common knowledge. The conditioning mechanism appears nearly identical to that in RT-Trajectory; the paper does not sufficiently clarify the differences.
- **Out-of-domain (OOD) data is not truly OOD** – The claimed OOD data (LIBERO-90) shares the same robot morphology, controller, camera pose, lighting, and physical parameters as the in-domain data. The experiments therefore only demonstrate that MoE can consume more similar-domain data, not cross-embodiment or cross-morphology OOD data as explored in prior work (e.g., human videos, different robots).
- **Indirect evaluation and marginal gains** – The main metric (success rate) is an indirect measure of trajectory model performance; the correlation between trajectory quality and policy success is not quantitatively validated. The improvement from adding MoE + OOD data is only 3.8% (Table 1(f)), raising questions about actual benefit. Additionally, adding MoE without extra data causes a performance drop, suggesting the MoE may simply prevent overfitting rather than enable effective scaling.
- **Missing direct trajectory prediction comparisons** – The paper does not compare Tra-MoE’s trajectory prediction quality (e.g., accuracy, multi-modality) against existing models such as ATM (transformer-based) or Track2Act (diffusion-transformer). Such comparisons are needed to isolate the trajectory model’s contribution.
- **Limited model scale and relevance of MoE** – The trajectory model has only 23.6M parameters, whereas sparsely-gated MoE was originally designed for billion-parameter models. The main bottleneck for point-trajectory methods is not model scaling. When the dense baseline is scaled further (in width/depth), it may eventually match MoE performance – the paper provides only one data point on this.
- **Data usage and baselines** – LIBERO provides 50 demonstrations per task, but the paper uses only 10 or 20; no analysis across varying demonstration counts is provided. No comparison with image-based behavior cloning (BC) is given; recent works (e.g., BAKU) achieve >80% success on LIBERO with image-based policies, making it unclear whether trajectory guidance is beneficial.
- **Loss function and trajectory representation** – The use of MSE loss for trajectory prediction is questionable given the multi-modal nature of future trajectories; generative models may be more appropriate. The paper lacks a clear definition of trajectory representation at the outset.
- **Clarity issues** – Figure 1 lacks y-axis scale annotation. Figure 3(b) has a confusing arrow layout that does not clearly explain information flow. The explanation of the 2D mask modality (format, structure) is insufficient. The term “optimization conflicts” in the introduction is undefined. The paper does not explicitly state that Table 1 reports success rates.
- **Ablation scope** – The number of experts is only varied from 1 to 4, whereas modern MoE architectures (e.g., Mixtral 8×7B) use 8 or more. A broader exploration of expert count is needed to understand scaling trade-offs.
