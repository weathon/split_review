## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies nonlinear Bayesian inverse problems in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Leveraging a recently developed framework for score-based generative models in Hilbert spaces, it learns an infinite-dimensional score, uses it as a prior in a function-space Langevin-type MCMC algorithm, and provides non-asymptotic stationary convergence guarantees (Theorem 1). The analysis shows that controlling the score approximation error is essential and that a suitable preconditioner must be selected—an aspect unique to the infinite-dimensional setting. The bound reveals an interplay between the preconditioner and the score approximation error.

### Strengths

- Provides a rigorous convergence analysis for a Langevin-type MCMC algorithm with score-based priors in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, extending previous finite-dimensional results (Sun et al., 2024). This fills a gap in the literature.
- Finite-sample quantitative error bounds with interpretable components are given; the bounds are dimension-free.
- The analysis reveals a novel interplay between the preconditioner and the score approximation error, unique to the infinite-dimensional setting.
- The theoretical framework is general and can be easily extended to algorithmic modifications (e.g., different discretizations).
- The problem of establishing theoretical foundations for Hilbert space MCMC with score-based priors is meaningful, and the presentation is clear.

### Weaknesses

- The results do not appear particularly surprising and seem to naturally generalize what is known from the finite-dimensional case (R1). The technical novelty relative to Sun et al. (2024) is not clearly stated; the extra challenges of the infinite-dimensional setting and novel aspects of the proof should be explicitly discussed (R4).
- More justification is needed for why the infinite-dimensional regime is important: in practice parametrizations are finite-dimensional, and projecting onto a sufficiently large finite-dimensional subspace may suffice. The paper could focus on why infinite-dimensional results imply dimension-free results in finite dimensions (R1).
- No discussion of how to implement the proposed algorithm (Equation (6)) in practice is given. In a proper Hilbert space, direct implementation without further approximations (e.g., basis expansion or finite-dimensional representation) is not obvious. The analysis of (6) is only half the story; the impact of converting it into an implementable algorithm on convergence properties should be addressed (R2).
- The structure of the proof makes it difficult to gain insight without a line-by-line reading; the supplementary material lacks linking text and is heavy on algebra. Explaining why the lemmas are needed and what they establish would help the casual reader (R2).
- There are no examples illustrating settings where the algorithm could be used or verifying the assumptions. Even for a theoretical paper, examples of problems of interest and, ideally, a comparison to methods without rigorous validation would strengthen the contribution (R2, R3). Numerical illustrations are expected for a top conference paper (R3).
- The bound in Theorem 1 is complicated; an intuitive explanation of each term is needed. The effect of the choice of α (especially near zero or extremely large) and the influence of τ,γ on the bound (and whether an optimal choice exists) should be discussed (R4).
- Assumption 3 (score network Lipschitz continuous with constant L_τ) is problematic because for deep neural networks the Lipschitz constant can increase exponentially with depth. The necessity of this assumption should be justified (R4).
- Ambiguity exists regarding whether the score is learned for the prior or the posterior (line 069) (R1). The phrase “the prior µ0 that we want to learn from data” (line 248) seems at odds with Bayesian inference; clarification is needed (e.g., empirical Bayes) and it raises questions about how confident one can be about the prior form when using score-based learning (R2).
- It is unclear whether Assumption 4 is a reasonable factorization or imposes a strong constraint on the target distribution (R1). The role of this assumption relative to prior work should be clarified.
- Minor typos: “PCM-RED” (line 219) should be corrected; “distribution with respect to the Lebesgue measure” (line 243) should likely read “density” (R2).
- Several open questions remain: What notion of derivative (Fréchet?) is used in the definition of Fisher information? (R1). How does “keeping an explicit likelihood” (line 121) work? (R1). What is g the negative log-likelihood of? (line 223). In what sense does ∇log p_σ converge to ∇log p? (line 224). Is (3) described as “a parallel MCMC algorithm of RED” meant in a computational sense? (line 232).
