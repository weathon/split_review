## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes LMV-IQL, an EM-based extension of inverse Q-learning (IQL) to handle multiple latent intentions (discrete reward functions) in animal behavior modeling. Two variants are considered: Bernoulli (LV-IQL) and Markov (LMV-IQL) dynamics for intention transitions. The method is tested on a simulated gridworld (2 intentions) and on mouse data from a two-armed bandit task (with up to 3 latent states). Results show recovery of ground-truth rewards in simulation and interpretable intention-specific reward functions in the mouse data.

### Strengths
- **Timely and well-motivated extension of IRL to multiple intentions** – addresses a recognized gap in discrete-time multi-intention IRL, particularly after the continuous-time work of Ashwood et al. (NeurIPS 2022) and the large-scale discrete-state analysis of Ashwood et al. (Nat Neurosci 2022). The need for a discrete version has been anticipated in the field (Reviewer 4).
- **Novel combination of IQL with EM** – holds promise for outperforming previous multi-goal IRL methods by leveraging IQL’s ability to handle nonlinear reward functions and a model-free setting (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 1 notes this as a definite strength).
- **Well-written and structured** – definitions, theorems, and algorithmic steps are clear; the paper is easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Application to real neuroscience data** – demonstrates practical applicability on mouse behavior, which is appreciated (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 calls it an intriguing generalization of IRL to neuroscience).
- **Model foundations are sound** – uses reasonable choices like the Baum-Welch algorithm for intention inference (Reviewer 4). After rebuttal, Reviewer 4 highlighted that the new priors enabling abrupt changes of goal maps were more consistent with mouse data than DIRL, making the model important (minority positive view).
- **Simulated and animal data analyses are a plus** – provide both controlled recovery and real-world demonstration (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient discussion of related multi-goal IRL work** – the paper initially did not reference any prior multi-goal IRL methods (e.g., [1–6]). Many of these (especially [4–6]) also use an EM approach, raising questions about true novelty. After rebuttal, the added discussion still does not fully distinguish the approach from prior work like Nguyen et al. (2015); the method is conceptually very close, and the main difference is swapping the backbone IRL algorithm to IAVI/IQL. The authors’ claims of novelty are considered overstated (Reviewer 2; echoed by Reviewer 1, 3). Reviewer 4 also notes high similarity to Ashwood et al. (NeurIPS 2022) and says data analyses mainly serve as proof of principle.
- **Missing comparisons on experimental data** – only IAVI and IQL are compared in simulation; no state-of-the-art methods (e.g., Ashwood et al., Ebitz et al., or flexible models from Pillow/Pitkow groups) are compared on the mouse dataset. LV-IQL performed identically to LMV-IQL in simulation, so the additional complexity of LMV-IQL is not justified without a more thorough comparison on real data (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3 also notes absence of comparison to Ashwood or similar models).
- **Simplistic model assumptions** – latent states are restricted to multinomial or Markov dynamics, but the most biologically plausible assumption would allow transitions to depend on reward/satiety/recent history (Reviewer 3). The paper also claims “animals alternate between multiple intentions under the Markov property” is common, but this is not how behaviors were characterized before Ashwood et al. (Reviewer 4).
- **Key metric (EVD) not defined** – the primary evaluation metric is cited but never explained, making it difficult to assess significance or compare with other studies (Reviewer 1).
- **Weak definitions of comparison methods** – statements like “IAVI was further extended to IQL” lack citations and implementation details; it is unclear how model-free adaptation was done and how it compares to existing model-free IRL (Reviewer 1).
- **Figures need improvements** – Figure 1, 2 lack color scalebars; Figure 3 legend unclear (red/blue squares?), cross/dot overlap hard to follow; Figure 4C colors uncorrelated with legend (Reviewer 1, 4). Figure 3 overlap of crosses and dots should use an alternative representation (Reviewer 4).
- **Scalability limited to small number of intentions** – only 2–3 intentions are tested; no analysis of how the method extends beyond that, and bottlenecks (IAVI regression size vs. EM complexity) are not discussed (Reviewer 1, 3).
- **Several technical concerns and questions**:
  - Figure 4A: why does the training LL drop? Overfitting is unlikely to explain it (Reviewer 4).
  - Model selection: using BIC instead of pure NLL to choose K would be more principled; the chosen 3 states may be inflated—the third intent in Figure 5C appears spurious (unstable, immediately reverses to first intent) (Reviewer 4).
  - Page 5 Eq. 10: what does ΔZ mean? (Reviewer 4).
  - Page 6 Fig. 3: why punish reward types irrelevant to intentions instead of setting them to zero? (Reviewer 4).
  - Can recent reward history be incorporated into transition structure? Is any EM-compatible latent variable model feasible? (Reviewer 3).
- **Overclaimed literature statements** – “IRL adoption in neuroscience is relatively limited” ignores up-and-coming work from Pillow and Pitkow groups; exploration-exploitation is not “comparatively understudied” given large literature on intrinsic motivation and Bayesian optimality (e.g., Pisupati et al. 2021, Yu & Cohen 2008) (Reviewer 4).
- **Reviewer disagreement on novelty** – Reviewers 1, 2, 3 find the novelty insufficient and the contribution fair (2/3). Reviewer 4, after rebuttal, finds the model important for enabling abrupt goal-map changes and outperforming DIRL, and increased score (to 6), but still notes high similarity to prior work and calls the contributions fair (2). The majority view is that novelty is limited and comparisons are missing.
