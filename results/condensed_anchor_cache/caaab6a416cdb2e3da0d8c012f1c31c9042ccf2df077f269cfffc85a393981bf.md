## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Diff-Prompt, a three-stage diffusion-driven prompt generator for fine-tuning pre-trained multimodal models on complex, fine-grained tasks. Stage 1 trains a Mask-VAE to compress mask information into latent space. Stage 2 uses an improved Diffusion Transformer (DiT) to generate prompts in that latent space under mask supervision. Stage 3 aligns the denoising process with the pre-trained model’s semantic space and uses the generated prompts for fine-tuning. Experiments are conducted on referring expression comprehension (RefCOCO, Flickr30k) using GLIP, and results show improvements over several parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (up to 8.87 in R@1, 14.05 in R@5). Code is released.

### Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated approach.** The idea of using a diffusion model to generate rich, fine-grained prompts is innovative and departs from conventional prompt learning methods (Reviewers 2, 4). The motivation is clear and engaging (Reviewer 2).
- **Strong performance on the evaluated task.** Diff-Prompt outperforms other efficient fine-tuning methods on multiple metrics for referring expression comprehension, with notable recall improvements (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5). The experimental results validate effectiveness on a complex fine-grained multimodal task (Reviewer 3).
- **Novel mask-supervision design.** Training the prompt generator via mask supervision to control fine edge information is a creative idea (Reviewer 1). The use of Mask-VAE and diffusion in latent space enables rich prompt representations (Reviewer 3).
- **Clear presentation and code release.** The paper is well organized, with clear illustrations (Reviewers 2, 5). The validation on visual grounding is comprehensive (Reviewer 2). Code is available to support reproducibility (Reviewer 2).
- **Useful statistical comparisons.** The comparative experiments include multiple models and provide statistical results, offering guidance for other researchers (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited generalizability evaluation.** Testing is only on GLIP and only on referring expression comprehension. It is unclear how the method would perform with other backbones or on other complex tasks (e.g., referring expression segmentation RES, panoptic narrative grounding PNG, novel-class generalization, domain shifts). If it only works with this model and task, the contribution is very limited, and the title should be made more specific (Reviewers 2, 4). One reviewer asks for improvement plans to mitigate poor generalization (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparisons with important baselines.** The number of baselines is imbalanced between the two types of efficient tuning methods; more adapter-method comparisons are needed (Reviewer 1). The paper does not compare with recent prompt learning works for generalization, such as PromptKD, Self-regulating Prompts, Align Your Prompts, Consistency-guided Prompt Learning, Concept-Guided Prompt Learning, and Conceptual Codebook Learning (Reviewer 4). Why were these methods not considered?
- **Inference speed and parameter cost.** Using diffusion slows inference speed by about twofold, a significant cost that could be optimized (Reviewer 2). Diff-Prompt has slightly more learnable parameters (5.5M), requiring more memory and longer inference time (Reviewer 3). A reviewer also asks about strategies to address high computational complexity and image input size limitations (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear or insufficient mechanistic analysis.** The core contribution of using diffusion for prompt generation is not deeply analyzed or justified (Reviewer 2). It is unclear how Diff-Prompt differs from independently training a conditional mask generator and then interacting with the multimodal encoder (Reviewer 1). The advantage of using generated prompts over learnable prompts is not explained clearly (Reviewer 5). Further exploration of why and how diffusion helps would provide deeper insights (Reviewer 2).
- **Claim of fine-grained control vs. actual results.** The authors claim to generate rich, finely controllable prompts, but the masks generated during the diffusion process do not exhibit fine-grained control; the method may not actually adapt well to high-granularity tasks, contradicting the stated problem (Reviewer 1).
- **Modality-balance and architecture concerns.** The vision and language adaptors share the same architecture; guidance on how to exploit their respective cues to ensure consistent fine-tuning and avoid modality bias is missing (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Minimal improvement on certain metrics.** On the UB metric in Tables 1 and 2, the improvement over the much simpler MaPLe method is minimal (Reviewer 2).
- **Clarity issues in method description.** Several details are unclear: the definition of \(E_i\) in “Deep Prompting” (Reviewer 5); the mechanism used to obtain the \(D\) latent features of the generated prompt (Reviewer 5); and how the method generates a position to accurately locate the target object described in the expression in the REC task (Reviewer 5).

*Note:* One reviewer (score 3) is more negative, emphasizing that the contribution beyond mask generation is insufficient, that mask controllability is not achieved, and that the evaluation is narrow. Other reviewers (scores 6, 6, 8, 6) are generally positive about the novelty and results but share concerns about generalizability, missing comparisons, and computational cost.
