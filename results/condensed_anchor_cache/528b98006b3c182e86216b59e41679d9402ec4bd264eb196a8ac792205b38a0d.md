## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes F-ML-IRL, a federated inverse reinforcement learning algorithm that combines local maximum-likelihood IRL updates (following Zeng 2022) with global model averaging. The authors provide a convergence analysis and evaluate the algorithm on MuJoCo robotic control tasks. Reviewers generally find the topic interesting but raise major concerns about limited novelty, insufficient engagement with existing distributed IRL literature, problematic claims, experimental flaws, and poor presentation.

### Strengths
- The federated learning framework for IRL is interesting and practically relevant (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The algorithm is intuitive, practical, and comes with a rigorous convergence guarantee (R1, R2).
- The paper provides both theoretical and empirical validation (R4).
- (Minority view, R2: The paper is clearly written and easy to follow; contrast with R4, who rates presentation as 1, and R1, who notes issues.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty / incremental contribution.** Several reviewers note that the algorithm is a simple combination of ML-IRL (Zeng 2022) and federated averaging: local updates (Alg. 1 lines 7–12) are identical to ML-IRL, and the global update (lines 17–19) is standard parameter averaging. The problem formulation (4) is almost the same as ML-IRL except the upper-level problem is distributed. The authors do not identify or solve any unique technical challenge that does not already appear in ML-IRL, federated learning, or distributed bilevel optimization (R2, R3, R4). R3 specifically asks the authors to outline the unique challenges. R4 calls the contribution “fairly poor” and “incremental”.

2. **Incorrect or questionable claims.**
   - R1: The claim (lines 282–283) that aggregating Q‑values reduces to aggregating model parameters when Q is represented by a neural network incorrectly implies a neural network is linear. The common federated learning practice is to aggregate gradients, not weights. The paper provides no justification for direct parameter averaging.
   - R1: The same claim holds for the reward function. Unless a linear function approximation is assumed (which is not the paper’s focus), the theoretical analysis appears disconnected from the practical implementation.
   - R2: The introduction states that “IRL using decentralized clients and distributed data is an open problem” (line 49). This is incorrect. Several existing works address this: e.g., *Federated Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Smart ICUs with Differential Privacy*; *Federated Imitation Learning* (multiple papers); *Distributed Inverse Constrained Reinforcement Learning for Multi-agent Systems*; *Distributed Inverse Optimal Control*; *Distributed Estimation and Inverse Reinforcement Learning for Multi-agent Systems* (R2, R3). The paper does not discuss or compare with these.
   - R3: The related work claims that existing FL methods cannot be directly applied because ML-IRL is bilevel (lines 159–160). However, there are existing federated bilevel optimization works (e.g., FedNest, Communication-Efficient Federated Bilevel Optimization, Achieving Linear Speedup in Non-IID Federated Bilevel Learning) that should be considered.

3. **Insufficient related work and missing comparisons.**
   - R2, R3: The paper lacks discussion and experimental comparison with existing distributed IRL algorithms (e.g., those listed above).
   - R4: The related works section is poorly written and contains factual errors. For example, line 108 claims that “Apprenticeship learning via inverse reinforcement learning” uses the recovered reward to derive a policy, but that paper does not recover a reward.
   - R4: Lines 144–145 have no citation; the function *f* in Eq. (3) is undefined.

4. **Experimental issues.**
   - R1: In more than one case, a centralized agent given *medium*-level demonstrations learns the best policy. This is counterintuitive and requires analysis.
   - R1: The table reports only mean performance; given large variance, standard deviations should be shown.
   - R2: Centralized baselines (e.g., ML-IRL) should be trained on the *same total number of trajectories* as the federated setting (e.g., if 3 clients each have 100 trajectories, centralized ML-IRL should be trained on 300 trajectories). The paper does not clarify whether this was done.
   - R2: The algorithm communicates local Q‑functions and the global policy. This prevents application to continuous state/action spaces, and communication overhead is high for large discrete spaces.
   - R4: Why does Eq. (4) take an unweighted average over clients instead of weighting by the number of trajectories per client? This needs justification.

5. **Presentation and notation problems.**
   - R4: The paper is written imprecisely. The background and preliminaries are confusing, lack definitions, and have many introduced symbols without explanation. Many typos exist (e.g., line 045 “FL” not defined; line 117 “an MDP” instead of “a MDP”; line 285 “inequation”; incorrect citation style using \citet instead of \citep; and many others).
   - R2: Several mathematical typos hinder verification of theoretical results:
     - In the soft value function (5), “θⁱ_{m,k+1}” should be “θⁱ_{m,k}”.
     - In (9), the step size α should be time-varying.
     - In Theorem 1, the notations \bar{π}ⁱ_{(m,T)} and \bar{θ}ⁱ_{(m,T)} are never defined.
     - Notations in (18) and those in Step 1 on page 8 do not match.
     - In Steps 1–3 on page 8, the notations for local soft value functions are inconsistent.
   - R1: The proof of Theorem 1 (Lemmas 1 and 2 are from prior work) appears identical to the ML-IRL analysis up to Eq. (51). The authors should clarify the key analytical differences contributed by the federated setting.

6. **Motivation and justification gaps.**
   - R3: The claim that demonstration data are often distributed across decentralized clients (line 41) needs concrete applications or scenarios.
   - R1: Why cannot standard federated learning frameworks work? The proposed method also simply aggregates objectives (Q‑function and reward function).

7. **Disagreement on presentation quality.** While R2 finds the paper clearly written, R4 rates presentation as 1 (very poor) and R1 notes issues (soundness score 1). This contrast should be acknowledged.
