## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a risk-sensitive stochastic differential equation (SDE) framework to improve diffusion model robustness on noisy non-image data (tabular, time series). Each sample is paired with a risk vector indicating data quality. The risk-sensitive SDE minimizes noise-induced instability by adjusting the forward diffusion process; Gaussian noise can be fully mitigated, and analytical forms are given for Gaussian and Cauchy noise. Experiments show the method outperforms baselines in handling noisy samples. Concept and theory are praised, but empirical realism and scope are questioned.

### Strengths
- **Novel and principled problem setting:** The risk vector concept is interesting and the risk-sensitive SDE is a principled, theoretically grounded modification to standard diffusion algorithms. (R1, R2, R4)  
- **Strong theoretical foundation:** Analytical solutions are provided for both Gaussian and non-Gaussian noise, with sufficient/necessary conditions for perturbation stability. (R1, R2, R3)  
- **Method intrinsically outperforms simple risk-conditional baselines:** The framework handles noise better than simply conditioning the score on the risk vector, as shown in low-dimensional experiments. (R3)  
- **Simple, illustrative experiments:** Low-dimensional results effectively demonstrate stability intervals, robustness to class imbalance, and the flexibility of “minimal instability”. (R3)  
- **Experimental validation on tabular and time-series datasets:** Effectiveness is shown even with mis-specified or non-Gaussian noise, outperforming prior baselines. (R1, R2)  
- **Minor yet elegant modification:** The changes to the diffusion algorithms are small but well justified by theory; toy examples are persuasive. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited to non-image data (tabular, time series):** The paper does not evaluate generalizability to noisy image data, which is common in practice. (R1; echoed by R2, R4)  
- **Heavy reliance on accurate risk vector estimation:** The method requires extensive prior noise information; inaccurate estimation can lead to inappropriate adjustments and reduced effectiveness. (R1; echoed by R2)  
- **No comparison with learned denoising (no explicit noise model):** Alternative approaches that directly denoise the data (e.g., via a learned denoising function) are not explored or compared, limiting practical applicability. (R2)  
- **Limited noise types:** Analytical solutions are given only for Gaussian and Cauchy noise; whether this covers most real-world noise distributions is not justified, and experiments do not confirm that real datasets actually exhibit Gaussian-like noise. (R2; R3 also notes missing conditions for non-Gaussian tractability)  
- **Mathematical clarity and precision need improvement:** The definition of the risk vector \(r\) and the noise family \(P_{\epsilon}\) is ambiguous; the notation shifts confusingly between \(P_{\epsilon}\) and \(P_r\). Theorem 3.1 lacks intuitive explanation (e.g., what does the stability interval look like, why \(f(r,t)\) does not depend on \(r\), behavior at \(t=0\) and boundaries). (R3)  
- **Experimental setup appears tailored to highlight strengths, raising fairness concerns:**  
  - In tabular experiments, the imputed data is described as “very noisy”, so the method may effectively ignore those samples (small stability interval) rather than truly handling noise. A proper baseline would train only on clean samples. (R3)  
  - In time-series experiments, the interpolation method and evaluation procedure are insufficiently detailed (e.g., kernel used in Gaussian process, specific portion of dataset for generative performance measurement). (R3)  
- **Empirical results are overly artificial:** Current experiments are closer to synthetic data than to real-world problems where risk is inherent and not artificially constructed. They do not convincingly demonstrate practical utility in realistic corruption scenarios (e.g., vision, video, finance). (R4; echoed by R1, R3)  
- **Missing discussion of limitations and future work:** The paper would benefit from explicitly acknowledging these limitations and suggesting directions for improvement. (R1)

**Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 8) finds the approach elegant and the toy examples persuasive, viewing the minor algorithmic changes as well-justified. Another reviewer (score 5) is more critical of mathematical rigor and experimental fairness, questioning whether performance gains stem from ignoring rather than handling noisy samples. The majority (scores 6, 6, 5) share concerns about reliance on risk vector accuracy, limited data domains, and artificial experiments.
