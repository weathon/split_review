## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper tests debate as a scalable oversight method by training language models to debate via self-play on a long-context reading comprehension task (QuALITY). Using a GPT-4-Turbo judge evaluating transcripts from Llama3-8B-Instruct debaters trained with a novel DPO-based method, the authors find that models optimized to win debates improve judge accuracy. In contrast, non-adversarial “consultancy” models trained to persuade without an opponent show no such positive correlation. The paper argues that debate training encourages stronger, more informative arguments, promising for supervising tasks that are difficult to evaluate directly.

Reviewers are split: two rate the paper 6 (accept-range) and two rate it 3 (weak reject). The higher-rated reviews highlight the novelty and interesting insights; the lower-rated ones cite limited evaluation, similarity to prior work, and insufficient rigor.

### Strengths

- **Novel idea and approach**: Exploring debate-model training to enhance evaluator accuracy via self-play is novel and interesting. The paper introduces a DPO-based training algorithm specifically for training debaters, leveraging asymmetric information (debater has full context, judge does not). (from R1, R2, R3)
- **Rigorous experimental design**: The study uses a carefully constructed asymmetric information setup and compares debate with multiple consultancy baselines (single, ensembled, double) to isolate the effect of adversarial interaction. The findings—positive correlation between debate win rate and judge accuracy, absent in consultancy—are intriguing and potentially useful for scalable oversight / super-alignment research. (from R2, R3, R4)
- **Comprehensive analysis**: The paper provides detailed analyses connecting debate model performance and judge accuracy across both adversarial and non-adversarial settings, offering insights for future multi-agent debate systems. (from R2)
- **Grounding in prior work**: Builds on prior work (Michael et al., Kenton et al. 2024) to show that trained debaters outperform trained consultants. (from R1)

### Weaknesses

- **Limited task and dataset**: Experiments are conducted only on the QuALITY dataset (hard subset). This raises concerns about generalizability to other complex reasoning domains; broader tasks are needed. Moreover, the narrow evaluation makes it hard to compare contributions with prior work (e.g., Kenton et al. 2024) which uses a broader set of evaluations. (from R1, R2, R4)
- **Restricted debate protocol**: The debate is limited to a two-turn setting, restricting depth of argumentation and refutation. Real-world debates often require extended exchanges to expose subtle flaws. This simplified structure may overestimate debate’s effectiveness in fostering accurate judgments. (from R4)
- **Only one judge and one debater/consultant model pair**: The judge is always GPT-4-Turbo and the debate/consultancy models are always Llama3-8B-Instruct. The results would be stronger if tested with smaller judge models (e.g., Llama3-8B itself) and across varied architectures and sizes. (from R2, R4)
- **Insufficient evidence that explicit refutation matters**: The paper states that “explicit refutation does not yet seem to play a role in judge decision-making” (line 473), but this claim is not substantiated with direct experimental evidence. This undermines a key proposed benefit of debate as an oversight mechanism. (from R3, R4)
- **Unclear or insufficiently detailed methodology**:
  - The use of “self-play” for evaluating judge accuracy is ambiguous; judge accuracy should be evaluated by its ability to correctly discern correct/incorrect outputs, not by self-play. (from R3)
  - The method for calculating consultant win rate is unclear: if a consultant defends an incorrect answer, when is that considered a “win”? Similarly, for a correct answer, what counts as a “loss”? (from R3)
  - Insufficient details on judge training: What instances are in the training dataset? What is the input/output format? What evidence supports the claim (line 215) that judge decisions “were no longer clustered at the boundaries” after fine-tuning? (from R3)
  - Several operational details are missing or vague: (i) In the round-robin tournament for the debate setting, are both sides identical debate-trained checkpoints, or is one side a baseline (e.g., without debate training)? (from R2) (ii) In Figure 3 (right), what does the “judge Brier score” represent? (from R3) (iii) In line 264, how exactly is a “win” or “loss” response determined when constructing the DPO training datasets? (from R3) (iv) In line 313, what does “confidence” refer to in the reward function? Was GPT-4 Turbo trained to output a confidence level? (from R3) (v) In line 318, what do the two win rates in parentheses represent? (from R3) (vi) In line 362, why are there three pairs per round and two rounds per question? (from R3)
- **Similarity to prior work and unclear differentiation**: Many findings seem similar to those in Kenton et al. (2024). The paper does not clearly distinguish its contributions either in the main text or the related work section. (from R1)
- **Lack of fine-grained evaluation**: The paper does not assess aspects like factuality—e.g., whether debate training yields more factual transcripts and whether the judge improves at detecting factual discrepancies. (from R2)
- **Poor presentation**: The abstract and introduction are dense and hard to read (R1). Figures 1, 2, and 5 are not referenced in the text (R4). Writing quality is low (presentation scores: R1=1, R4=1). (from R1, R4)
