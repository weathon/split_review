## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes WASA, a watermarking framework for source attribution and data provenance in LLMs. It uses imperceptible Unicode characters as watermarks to embed source information. The framework is evaluated on GPT-2 with datasets like Clean-ArXivCorpus and BookSum. Reviewers find the problem important and the approach novel, but raise concerns about evaluation methodology, watermark robustness, scalability, experimental diversity, and presentation clarity. One reviewer is more critical of the threat model and ease of watermark removal.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and timely problem (source attribution / data provenance for LLM-generated data). [R2, R4]
- Clearly lists desired properties for a watermarking framework (accurate attribution, robustness, performance preservation, scalability, transferability, adaptability). The paper argues how WASA satisfies each, making it easy to follow. [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- The use of imperceptible Unicode characters as watermarks is novel and inspiring. [R3]
- Provides a well-scoped experimental setup (training GPT-2 on ArXiv, BookSum). [R2]
- Thorough assessment of watermark robustness against various attacks. [R2]
- Extensive experiments overall. [R1]

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation methodology may be flawed:** The main experiment uses sentences *selected for watermarking* (after removing the watermark) as prompts for source attribution. This is too close to evaluating on the training set and may significantly skew results. A held-out set of sentences not selected for watermarking should be used. [R2] Even if the prompt is from a particular provider’s training data, the model might complete it using a different source (e.g., prompting with an ArXiv-style query could still lead to a Wikipedia-based generation). [R2]
- **Watermark can be easily removed:** The watermark consists of invisible Unicode characters. A straightforward defense is to strip all invisible characters, rendering the watermark entirely ineffective. [R1]
- **Questionable threat model and tokenizer manipulation:** Inserting these rare invisible characters may require increasing the vocabulary size and modifying the tokenizer of the target LLM. This is not clearly explained, and the threat model is only informally introduced in the introduction. [R1]
- **Scalability concerns:** Source attribution accuracy decreases as the number of data providers increases. More optimization and analysis on scalability with many providers is needed. [R4] (R4 mentions this; R2’s minor concern about requiring training intervention also relates to scalability across models.)
- **Limited experimental diversity:** Experiments focus on academic papers and books. Including more diverse text types (social media, news articles, informal text) would demonstrate broader applicability. [R4]
- **Missing ablation studies on watermark behavior:** At what positions in the generated text do watermarks appear? Can watermarks be easily removed by randomly masking words or sentences? [R3]
- **Lack of comparison with standard watermark detection metrics:** Recommend using p-value scores, which are common in watermarking literature, rather than just raw accuracy. [R3]
- **Impact on generated text quality not fully shown:** While the paper claims preservation of quality, no qualitative examples or detailed analysis demonstrate that watermark insertion does not degrade naturalness or coherence. [R4]
- **Concern about generality:** The framework requires specific intervention during training; it cannot be applied to already trained LLMs, limiting applicability. [R2] (R2 minor; also related to R3’s question about SFT vs second-stage pre-training.)
- **How to handle combined data sources:** The evaluation assumes each sample comes from a single provider. In practice, generated text may combine content from two providers (e.g., books and Wikipedia). How would the framework determine the source then? [R3]
- **Uncertainty about unseen watermark generation:** The paper states that the model almost never generates incorrect unseen watermarks, but it is unclear how often this happens for texts not in the training set or for training texts not selected for watermarking. [R2]
- **Presentation clarity needs improvement:** The methodology section blends background with the proposed method; the threat model is introduced informally in the introduction. The paper is somewhat difficult to follow; statements could be simplified, and experiments could be better presented with examples. [R1, R3] (R1 specifically notes the structure makes it hard to grasp the central idea; R3 says it is somewhat difficult to follow and suggests simpler statements/examples.)
- **One reviewer (R1) is particularly critical** of the watermark’s effectiveness and the threat model, expressing low confidence (2) in understanding, while other reviewers are more positive about the novelty (R3: “genuinely impressed”). This disagreement underscores the need for stronger justification and robustness analysis. [R1 vs R3]
