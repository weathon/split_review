## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MQuant, a post-training quantization framework for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). It addresses three identified challenges: slow inference due to long visual token sequences, distributional differences between visual and textual tokens, and performance degradation from visual outlier clipping. MQuant introduces Modality-specific Quantization (MSQ) and Attention-Invariant Flexible Switching (AIFS) for per-tensor static quantization, a unified LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm transformation to enable Hadamard rotation on the vision encoder, and Rotation Magnitude Suppression (RMS) to mitigate rotation-induced outliers. Experiments on five mainstream MLLMs show MQuant maintains ~98% floating-point accuracy under W4A8 and outperforms LLM quantization baselines. Reviewers agree the problem is valuable and experiments are extensive, but raise concerns about novelty (many components are borrowed from LLM quantization literature), redundancy in presentation, and insufficient handling of variable-length and multi-turn inference scenarios. One reviewer (rating 5) is more critical than the others (rating 6).

### Strengths
1. **Valuable and timely topic:** Quantization for MLLMs is an important and underexplored problem. The motivation is clear and the paper identifies concrete challenges (visual token latency, modality distribution differences, outlier clipping).  
2. **Well-presented and easy to follow:** The paper includes informative figures and tables; the step-by-step problem-solving approach is intuitive. The modality-specific quantization and LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm transformation are well-motivated by distributional differences and architectural design.  
3. **Extensive experimental validation:** Experiments cover five mainstream MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen-VL) on various multimodal tasks. Comparisons to popular LLM quantization baselines are provided. Ablation studies demonstrate the contribution of each component. The method maintains around 98% of floating-point accuracy under W4A8.  
4. **Overall performance:** Achieves superior accuracy and inference speed under certain settings compared to LLM quantization baselines, per-tensor static, and per-token dynamic baselines.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and technical contribution:** Most techniques are borrowed from LLM quantization literature with only trivial modifications.  
   - MSQ and AIFS are simple adaptations of per-token dynamic quantization to the MLLM setting; they could serve as baselines rather than contributions.  
   - LayerNorm-to-RMSNorm transformation and Hadamard rotation are directly taken from SliceGPT and related work (Ashkboos et al., 2024a,b). The paper does not discuss the difference from SliceGPT’s solution or emphasize novel adaptations.  
   - The paper reads more like a technical report; actual novel contributions should be highlighted more clearly.  
2. **AIFS scheme – positional embedding concern:** When AIFS reorders visual and textual tokens to allow static quantization, the positional embeddings must be re-ordered accordingly to maintain correct positional information. The paper does not clarify whether this adjustment is made. Without it, attention operates on incorrect positional information, potentially causing significant deviation from the original computation.  
3. **Incomplete experimental settings (reviewer 2, rating 5):**  
   - **Variable-length text in a batch:** How does AIFS handle batches with varying text token counts? The attention masks need adjustment; padding and masking handling are not explained.  
   - **Interleaved image-text with multiple turns:** Experiments only test “text-image-text” with 15 textual tokens. Real-world scenarios with more complex interleaved turns are not evaluated, so robustness is unclear.  
   - **Multi-image inference:** Single or multiple turns with multiple images are not tested. Further evidence is needed to show applicability in such settings.  
   - **Batch sizes for latency:** The paper should report the batch size used when measuring inference latency.  
4. **Marginal improvement of MSQ and AIFS:** In Table 4, MSQ and MSQ+AIFS show only marginal improvement over the per-tensor static baseline. The overhead and justification for these components are unclear; why not simply use token-specific quantization?  
5. **Speed comparison vs. per-token dynamic quantization:** Although AIFS is designed to reduce latency with larger image resolutions, the reported speedup does not show a clear advantage over the per-token dynamic baseline as resolution scales.  
6. **Presentation quality issues:**  
   - **Redundancy:** The introduction repeats contribution points; Section 4.1 over-explains intuitive operations (MSQ/AIFS), while Sections 4.2-4.3 spend too many words explaining others’ prior work. The paper could be condensed by 30-40%.  
   - **Missing overall pipeline figure:** Algorithm 1 is in the Appendix; a main figure showing the full quantization pipeline with novel parts highlighted would improve readability.  
   - **Unclear/inconsistent definitions:** Abbreviations like GEDD and W4A8 should be defined at first use. In line 321, “easier to quantize” is vague; in line 287, “outliers” (extremely low or high values?) needs clarification.  
   - **Minor errors:** Typo “whthin” (line 304); grammar in line 305; Figure 1 legend font too small; line 85-87 has two “slightly” and unclear meaning; Table 3/4 arrow directions are counter-intuitive (use down arrow for “lower is better”); Table 5 appears to have “MDQ” instead of “MSQ”.  
7. **Missing latency breakdown:** Table 5 could benefit from latency comparisons among the proposed quantization methods themselves (e.g., MSQ vs. full MQuant) to separate contributions.  
8. **Equation 6 question:** Should the denominator of scale factor \(s\) be \(2^b - 1\) (range \(0\) to \(2^b-1\)) rather than \(2^b\)? The paper should verify.
