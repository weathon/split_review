## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes UniTabE, a universal pretraining protocol for tabular data. The model represents each table element via a TabUnit that embeds data type, column name, and cell value, then refines the representation with a Transformer encoder and uses an LSTM decoder. It is pretrained on a self-collected dataset of ~13 billion samples from Kaggle, using cell/column masking and siamese triplet loss. Fine-tuning is done via free-form prompts. The method is evaluated on 12 Kaggle tasks and 7 open datasets for classification and regression, and compared against baselines including XGBoost and TransTab. Results show that UniTabE often achieves superior AUC/R², though the gains are sometimes small and the performance of the pretrained+fine-tuned model is very similar to the scratch-trained version. The paper also provides ablation studies and robustness tests. Reviewers note the large-scale pretraining effort, the sensible design of TabUnit, and the thorough baseline coverage in part of the evaluation. However, concerns are raised about the narrow evaluation scope given the diversity of training data, the disproportionate computational cost for incremental gains, the lack of some comparisons (AutoML, TUTA, consistent baselines across all experiments), and insufficient discussion of data ethics and generalization to complex table structures.

### Strengths

- The chosen topic is highly relevant to the venue, as tabular ML is important but often overlooked in foundation model research.
- The TabUnit approach – learning separate embeddings for data type, column name, and value – is sensible and distinct from existing methods.
- The authors collected a large pretraining dataset of ~13 billion samples from Kaggle; open-sourcing it would be valuable.
- The evaluation on open datasets includes over 10 recent baselines (e.g., XGBoost, TransTab, FT-Transformer), and the baselines are well-chosen.
- The paper provides multiple ablation studies (e.g., omitting architectural components, robustness to missing data) confirming that improvements come from the expected method components.
- The method often achieves better predictive performance (AUC/R²) than competitors on fine-tuned, zero-shot, and missing-data tasks.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with sufficient detail for reproduction.
- Methodological contributions are clearly indicated and well-justified; the undertaking of creating a foundational model for tables is novel and potentially impactful.
- The results are revealing: despite exposure to 13B samples, the performance uplift after fine-tuning is only slight, highlighting the inherent difficulty and heterogeneity of tabular data. This sets a performance benchmark for BERT-inspired tabular methods and may spur research into new modeling paradigms.

### Weaknesses

- **Evaluation scope vs. training diversity:** The training data covers 303 diverse domains via Kaggle, but the downstream evaluation uses relatively few datasets (12 Kaggle tasks + 7 public). This narrow scope may not sufficiently test the model’s versatility and adaptability. The authors should justify why these datasets are representative or expand the evaluation.
- **Disproportionate gain vs. cost:** The incremental performance improvement of UniTabE over baselines is often small, and the gap between a scratch-trained UniTabE and the pretrained+fine-tuned version is remarkably similar. Given the massive pretraining overhead (13B samples), the computational cost appears disproportionate to the benefit, raising questions about the efficiency of the elaborate pretraining process.
- **Incomplete and inconsistent baseline comparisons:**
  - In Tables 4 and 5, only a subset of baselines is used (e.g., only TransTab-LSTM, and a ‘UniTabE scratch’ variant). The rationale for selecting certain baselines for each experiment is not motivated, making it hard to fully judge relative performance.
  - Regression results for XGBoost (and other regressors) are not reported on the open datasets, while they are reported for classification; this asymmetry limits the comparison.
  - The comparison lacks a representative AutoML system, which would help show the boundaries of the approach.
  - The method TUTA, which also learns refined embeddings, is omitted from the benchmarks.
- **Novelty concerns:** While some parts are novel (TabUnit decomposition), the overall processing technique draws heavily from prior work such as TransTab and FT-Transformer. The supervised pretraining strategies (multi-cell masking, contrastive learning) bear significant resemblance to methodologies described in earlier research.
- **Missing discussion of data ethics and licensing:** The use of ~13B Kaggle samples raises issues of copyright, licensing, and data biases. The paper does not address these ethical and legal aspects, nor does it discuss what the use of this model as a foundation model for tables would entail given the specific pretraining data.
- **Generalization to complex table structures:** The paper tests generalization to tables with more columns, but does not address hierarchical column structures (e.g., grouped columns) or multi-row/block layouts. It is unclear how UniTabE would process such real-world tables.
- **Lack of detail on baselines and variants:**
  - The three UniTabE variants in Table 3 (“scratch”, “+XGBoost”, “fine-tune”) are not clearly defined in the text, leaving ambiguity about how they differ.
  - The training procedure for Llama2 13B on tabular predictions is not explained, making the comparison difficult to interpret.
  - Commentary on Table 3 is unclear: it refers to “a slight performance enhancement” even though the two versions appear to perform roughly on par.
- **Robustness results:** The ablation on robustness to missing data is interesting but only reported for UniTabE; it would be more insightful to see whether the same advantage holds for other competitors.
- **Presentation issues:** Figure 3a is unreadably small; a typo “Expect for” should be “Except for”.
- **Missing analysis:** The paper does not identify the most challenging datasets tested or discuss what other advantages (beyond predictive performance) the method offers (e.g., whether robustness is systematically better than competitors).
