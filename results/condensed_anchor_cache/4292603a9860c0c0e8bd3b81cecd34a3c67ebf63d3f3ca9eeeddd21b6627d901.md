## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers evaluated the paper (scores 3, 3, 3, 5). One reviewer (score 5) found InjectAR to be a strong method that introduces discrete vision conditions into pre-trained autoregressive models, achieving superior performance with minimal training and flexible object placement. The other three reviewers (score 3) had significant concerns about motivation, novelty, insufficient experimental validation, and unclear design choices. All reviewers agreed that the paper is generally clear and well-structured, but the majority found the contributions limited and the evaluation lacking.

### Strengths
- Introduces a new method (InjectAR) to integrate discrete vision tokens into pre-trained autoregressive image generation models, enabling multimodal conditional generation.
- Proposes a hierarchical attention mechanism for balanced multimodal control with low-cost fine-tuning.
- The heuristic of separating objects from background (using BLIP for text, VQ+mask for visual) is intuitively useful for object customization tasks.
- The paper is well-organized and easy to follow; some experiments and implementation details are provided.
- One reviewer (score 5) highlighted that InjectAR achieves high prompt fidelity and flexible control over object placement, matching or surpassing state-of-the-art diffusion models while requiring minimal additional training.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited motivation and novelty**: Many existing models (e.g., ControlNet) already support both image and text conditioning. Using BLIP to generate prompts and standard classifier-free guidance is common and lacks novelty. The proposed hierarchical attention mechanism consists of two standard attention mechanisms with a weighting scheme that is not sufficiently justified.
- **Insufficient experimental validation**:  
  - Missing standard evaluation metrics such as MSCOCO FID (only CLIP-T and DINO-I reported).  
  - Incomplete comparison: Table 1 does not include up-to-date methods; no comparison with ControlNet or other recent image-conditioned generation approaches.  
  - Visual quality appears compromised in Figure 4 (e.g., subject consistency, image appeal); showing base model outputs would clarify degradation.  
  - CLIP-I similarity is lower than other methods, but the reason is not explained.  
- **Unclear design choices and lack of ablation**:  
  - The necessity of extracting textual conditions (via BLIP) is questionable given the strong visual pipeline (VQ model + object mask + MLP); an ablation with only visual conditioning is missing.  
  - The hierarchical attention mechanism is insufficiently explained: how the weight \(Hier\) is derived (“from label-specific descriptions” is vague), and \(Hier\) is not shown in Figure 3.  
  - The design choice to weight only visual attention (not textual) and to use “textual features of objects” as the target for \(Hier\) (rather than visual patches after masking) lacks clear motivation.  
  - Ablation studies on condition extraction (e.g., using a pre-trained VAE instead of VQ) and on the attention mechanism (e.g., alternative weighting strategies) are missing.  
- **Reliance on BLIP**: BLIP is an older captioning model; more advanced alternatives exist, and the authors do not justify this choice or assess its impact on generation quality.  
- **Limited scope of experiments**: Only single-object images are tested; handling multiple objects is unclear. The model’s performance on more complex scenes or multiple objects is not demonstrated.  
- **Presentation issues**: Empty line in Line 211, confusing section 3.2, the text “Suppl” in Line 361, and suboptimal layout of Figures 5 and 6 hinder readability.
