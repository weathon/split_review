## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Symbolic Autoencoding (ΣAE) with Straight-Through Gradient Estimators, a latent variable model that uses a discrete sequence bottleneck to map between two symbolic systems (X and Z). Training combines unsupervised autoencoding objectives with weakly-supervised learning from parallel data. Three quantization mechanisms are tested: softmax-argmax, Gumbel-softmax-argmax, and VQ-VAE quantisation. A straight-through gradient approximation for EOS masking is introduced. Experiments on four sequence transduction tasks (SCAN, PCFG SET, CFQ, COGS) show improvements over fine-tuned T5 and GPT‑3.5 in-context learning baselines, particularly in low‑resource settings.

### Strengths
- The framework enables learning from both parallel and non-parallel data, which is a common limitation of current seq2seq models that rely on large parallel corpora (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The approach is original in treating symbolic system pairs as latent spaces of each other and as an end-to-end extension of back-translation (Reviewer 4). The idea of mapping into an emergent language is interesting and could have applications beyond symbolic-symbolic transduction (Reviewer 3).
- The straight-through gradient estimation of EOS masking is novel to the reviewers’ knowledge, though it is not quantitatively evaluated (Reviewer 3). The soft‑masking approximation for the halting mechanism is also interesting (Reviewer 4).
- The validation is thorough: three different discrete bottleneck implementations are compared across four tasks with detailed analyses, and the method shows particularly strong performance in low‑resource scenarios (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 notes strong empirical results against multiple baselines and the paper tackles a key problem (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Related work omissions**: The paper misses several earlier studies on discrete sequence bottleneck models, including Miao & Blunsom (2016), Evtimova et al. (2017), and Graves (2016) (Reviewer 1), as well as Zhao et al. (2018), Kaiser et al. (2018), and Sanchez et al. (2023) (Reviewer 4). The claimed differences (e.g., reparameterized gradient vs. log‑derivative trick) are relatively minor.
- **Interpretability and structure claimed but unsubstantiated**: The paper promises interpretability and structure from the emergent language but provides no definition of interpretability and no evidence that it is achieved (Reviewer 1).
- **Experimental shortcomings**:
  - **Reliance on synthetic datasets** (SCAN, PCFG SET, CFQ, COGS) limits applicability to real‑world language; no natural language translation or low‑resource semantic parsing is tested (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Reviewer 2 asks how the framework handles complex, real‑world language data with high semantic variability.
  - **Unconvincing results**: In Figure 4, the supervised baseline performs on par with ΣAE in 2 of 4 datasets at the second smallest supervision ratio and in 3 of 4 at the third supervision ratio. No training‑time comparison is provided, making it unclear what advantage leveraging non‑parallel data offers (Reviewer 4).
  - **Insufficient baselines**: No comparison with back‑translation, the simplest method that uses both parallel and non‑parallel data (Reviewer 4). The ICL baseline is underspecified (no discussion of number/quality of examples); Chain‑of‑thought prompting (used in semantic parsing, e.g., Tai et al. 2023) is not considered (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 asks why back‑translation was not used.
  - **No quantitative evaluation of hidden sequence collapse**, which the paper claims to address with its EOS gradient estimation (Reviewer 3).
  - **Gumbel‑Softmax noise** reduces sentence‑level accuracy consistency, impacting reliability in token‑precise tasks (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 asks for clarification on the practical impact of this noise on downstream applications.
  - **Insufficient discussion of quantization method performance**: For example, VQ DB achieves 0.00 sentence reconstruction accuracy on CFQ (Table 1) yet obtains the best results on the weakly‑supervised benchmark (Figure 2), especially on CFQ (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 notes that the supervised baseline’s results change across Figures 3–5, raising the question of whether it also uses different discrete bottlenecks and why a Gumbel DB would be used in a supervised setting.
  - Reviewer 4 asks how important the size of the non‑parallel dataset is and whether it was varied; it is not studied.
- **Presentation and notation issues**:
  - The paper is difficult to follow: the introduction does not clearly explain the unsupervised autoencoding task; Figure 1 is cryptic; Section 3.1 (methodology) is placed at the end of page 8 in the experiments section (Reviewer 3). The long discussion of well‑known optimization methods (REINFORCE, annealing, reparameterization) could be moved to the appendix (Reviewer 4).
  - Notation is confusing: probability notation for distributions and densities is inconsistent (Eq. 117 vs. 121); event‑probability notation changes within the same equation (Eq. 18); in Eq. 360–361, $\mathbb{P}$ and $p_\theta$ are interchanged; class probabilities are labeled with both $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{s}$ in the same equations (Reviewer 4). Several symbols are used without definition (e.g., $\mathbf{s}$ in Line 238, $O_k$ in Line 360) and there are typos (Reviewer 3).
  - Redundancy: Section 2.1.1 discusses methods not used (REINFORCE, annealing) and could be condensed; Eq. 13 redefines a mask already defined (Reviewer 3).
  - Lack of references for related autoencoder frameworks with discrete latent sequences (Reviewer 4).
- **Contrast in overall assessment**: Reviewer 1 finds no strengths in the manuscript, stating that most ideas have been studied extensively and that the contributions are minimal (scores: 1/1/1). In contrast, Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 see merits but also identify significant weaknesses, with Reviewers 1 and 4 giving a rating of 3 and Reviewer 2 a rating of 6.
