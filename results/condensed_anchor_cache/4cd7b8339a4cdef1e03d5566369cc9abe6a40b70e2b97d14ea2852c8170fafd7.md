## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a non-uniform layerwise pruning method for LLMs called Outlier Weighed Layerwise sparsity (OWL). It assigns layerwise sparsity ratios proportional to the outlier ratio (large-magnitude features) in each layer, motivated by an analysis of outlier distribution across layers. Experiments on LLaMA and OPT show large perplexity gains over SparseGPT, Wanda, and uniform baselines at high sparsity (e.g., ~70%), and improved zero-shot accuracy. However, several reviewers raise concerns about the clarity of the analysis, the practical relevance of such high sparsity, the exclusive focus on unstructured sparsity, and the lack of differentiation among layer types within a Transformer block.

### Strengths
- **Well-written and organized** (Reviewer 1). Most figures are visually appealing (Reviewer 2).
- **Concise yet informative related work section**, with well-selected baselines for layerwise sparsity (Reviewer 2).
- **Addresses an open challenge**: achieving >50% sparsity without major degradation is an important problem (Reviewer 3).
- **Leverages known quantization observation**: outlier feature distribution in LLMs is strongly non-uniform; applying this insight to pruning is novel (Reviewer 3). OWL emphasizes layers with higher outlier prevalence, enabling more nuanced coordination between sparsity and outlier presence (Reviewers 3, 4, 5).
- **Thorough analysis of outlier distribution** across layers in dense LLMs and how pruning methods (magnitude, SparseGPT, Wanda) affect outlier retention. The finding that pruning effectiveness is strongly correlated with preserving outliers is interesting (Reviewer 5).
- **OWL is computationally lightweight** and can be easily applied on top of existing LLM pruning methods (Reviewer 5).
- **Strong empirical results at high sparsity**: for example, 61.22 perplexity reduction over Wanda and 6.80 over SparseGPT at 70% sparsity (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5). Consistently improved zero-shot accuracy across nearly all settings (Reviewer 3). OWL outperforms baselines significantly at high sparsity levels (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity of key concepts** (Reviewer 1): The definition of \(D_i\) (used in LOD) is confusing. How can the ratio exceed 1 as shown in Figure 1 left? The rationale for choosing \(M=5\) or \(7\) for LOD calculation is not explained theoretically (Reviewer 1).
- **Logical flaws in "Empirical Studies I, II, III"** (Reviewer 1):
  - *Study 1*: The paper observes that LOD is non-uniform in dense Llamas, then concludes that “uniform pruning inevitably disrupts the outlier structure.” This is not logically justified; it assumes, without proof, that the ratio of a weight to its layerwise average is an informative pruning criterion.
  - *Study 2*: Claiming that higher LOD for non-zero weights means “preserving more outliers” is circular: outliers after pruning may not be the same as before, and pruning changes the average used in LOD.
  - *Study 3*: No clear logical conclusion that motivates OWL. Prior work (e.g., SparseGPT) avoided non-uniform sparsity for practical reasons (e.g., n:m constraints), not because uniform was inherently better. The paper’s framing that uniform pruning is an “unexplained preference” is misleading and exaggerates the contribution (Reviewer 1).
- **Practical relevance of evaluated sparsity levels** (Reviewer 1, also echoed by Reviewer 3): The paper focuses on very high sparsity (e.g., 70%) where perplexities of all pruned models remain extremely high (e.g., >20 for LLaMA-7B). Such models are unlikely to be practically useful. A more meaningful comparison would be at the *critical sparsity* where performance remains close to the dense model. At lower sparsities (Table 5), uniform pruning works comparably or better than OWL (Reviewer 1).
- **Uniform 50% sparsity is more practical** on hardware supporting 2:4 structured sparsity than non-uniform sparsity at the same global rate (Reviewer 1). The paper does not address this.
- **Exclusive focus on unstructured sparsity** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5): The method is only evaluated for unstructured pruning, which has limited hardware support. No results for N:M sparsity, structured pruning (e.g., attention heads, FFN layers), or practical inference speedup on hardware. This omission is a major limitation (Reviewers 2, 3, 4, 5). Reviewer 5 suggests OWL could be applied to structured sparsity by choosing whether to prune a layer entirely or with 2:4, but this is not explored.
- **Lack of differentiation among layer types within a Transformer block** (Reviewers 4, 5): The analysis only treats the entire block as a unit. Understanding which specific layers (query, key, value, FFN) should be pruned more would be valuable. OWL could potentially be applied per FC layer, but this is not discussed (Reviewer 5).
- **Omission of other performance aspects** (Reviewer 2): Pruning is increasingly lagging behind quantization under similar storage constraints (citing https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02973). The industry prefers quantization and low-rank compression due to hardware support. Extending OWL’s ideas to mixed-precision quantization or adaptive rank selection could be more impactful.
- **Minor issues** (Reviewer 1):
  - Comparison with ERK is puzzling because LLMs (Llama) have no convolutional layers; ERK was designed for ConvNets.
  - The claim that global thresholding (citing Frankle & Carbin, 2019) is “expensive and inefficacy” is unsubstantiated; no comparison of sorting cost vs. saliency score computation is provided. Moreover, the citation is incorrect: see Figure 1, last row of that paper (it does not use a global threshold as described).
  - Typos: “outlier-weighted” vs. “outlier-weighed”; “inefficacy” should be replaced.
- **Terminology concern** (Reviewer 1): Referring to larger weights as "outliers" may be misleading, since outliers typically refer to extreme values in a distribution including both large and small values.
- **Scalability and practical utility** (Reviewer 3): OWL’s effectiveness is more pronounced for smaller models (7B, 13B) and diminishes at 30B and 65B. Even for smaller models, the “improved” perplexities after pruning are still poor (e.g., >20), limiting real-world deployment.
- **Lack of results on other architectures** (Reviewer 5): It would be beneficial to see OWL applied to ConvNets or Vision Transformers to understand the general role of outliers in pruning.

**Minority disagreement**: Reviewer 1 is consistently more critical, finding the motivation flawed and the high-sparsity evaluations “useless,” while Reviewers 3 and 5 consider the work novel and practically promising at high sparsity. This contrast is noted.
