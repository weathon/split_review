## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a framework to measure language model knowledge capacity in bits per parameter, focusing on factual knowledge represented as tuples (e.g., (USA, capital, Washington D.C.)). Using controlled synthetic datasets, the authors establish that sufficiently trained language models can store approximately 2 bits of factual knowledge per parameter, even when quantized to int8. A 7B model thus holds roughly 14B bits of knowledge. Achieving this capacity requires each knowledge piece to be revisited about 1000 times. Quantization to int4 reduces capacity to 0.7 bits/parameter. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) reduces capacity 1.3× despite using only 8.8% of total parameters during inference. Noisy data significantly reduces capacity, but prepending a domain-special token to useful knowledge mitigates this; the model autonomously identifies high-quality data. Extended training, specific architectures (e.g., GPT-2 with rotary embeddings), higher data quality, and domain annotations boost capacity. The paper also reveals specific architectural choices in Llama and Mistral (e.g., weight tying, MLP layers) that may slightly limit performance.

### Strengths
- **Originality and novelty**: The framework measuring knowledge capacity in bits per parameter is a novel, information-theoretic contribution to scaling law analysis, adding a quantitative dimension beyond loss or benchmarks. One reviewer (score 10) considers it the first to propose a scaling law for knowledge capacity.
- **Comprehensive experiments**: The paper includes a thorough, exhaustive set of controlled experiments covering model size, training duration, architecture (GPT-2, Llama, Mistral families), quantization (int8, int4), sparsity (MoE), and data signal-to-noise ratio. All 12 results are systematically evaluated. One reviewer (score 8) describes the experiments as very thorough and exhaustive.
- **Practical recommendations**: The study provides actionable insights for LLM practitioners, such as domain tagging for pretraining data, and explanations for why quantized models and MoE perform well despite reduced precision or sparsity. One reviewer notes that the experimental framework is reproducible and could be extended to other knowledge structures.
- **Solid methodology**: Controlled synthetic datasets allow isolation of specific variables, and the use of a bit complexity lower bound gives a principled estimation. One reviewer (score 10) rates the soundness as 4, calling the experiments solid and comprehensive.

### Weaknesses
- **Generalization to real-world data**: The heavy reliance on synthetic data with uniformly distributed tuples raises concerns about applicability to real-world, diverse, and long-tailed knowledge distributions. Multiple reviewers question whether the 2-bit/parameter scaling law holds on real-world datasets (e.g., Wikipedia) where information is often implicit, context-dependent, and not uniformly distributed. One reviewer (score 5) notes that the tuple-based representation in synthetic data may miss real-world nuances. Another (score 6) points out the uniform assumption in both data generation and lower bound proofs, and that real-world pretraining data follows a power-law distribution—this limitation is not addressed.
- **Unclear meaning and interpretation of stored bits**: It is not fully explained what it means for an LLM to store N bits of knowledge—whether this knowledge is ready for extraction or merely memorized in a fixed form. Appendix plots on memorization vs. extraction accuracy show similar linear-then-plateau trends, but these are not well connected to the bit information results. One reviewer (score 6) requests a clearer explanation of the information-theoretic definition and its practical implications.
- **Incomplete scaling law and unaddressed plateaus**: The main scaling law only describes the linear increasing part; the long plateaus in stored information are not explained. It is unclear whether the maximum information reached equals the bit complexity upper bound, what the specific maximum value is, and when it is achieved, especially given the N-specific nature of the plateaus. The paper describes influencing factors separately but does not provide a unified empirical law summarizing all results. One reviewer (score 6) asks whether such a combined law exists.
- **Derivation of the bit complexity lower bound**: The derivation feels ad hoc to some reviewers, particularly Lemma 1.6 and equation I.1. The tightness of the lower bound is not discussed, and an intuitive explanation with a simple example is missing. One reviewer (score 6) found several steps unnatural.
- **Limited architectural diversity**: The study focuses on decoder-only models (GPT-2, Llama, Mistral families) and does not explore other architectures such as encoder-only or sequence-to-sequence models, or less similar architectures like S4. One reviewer (score 5) argues that this limits the generalizability of the scaling law. Another (score 8) asks why only these families were selected.
- **Incomplete exploration of quantization during training**: The paper does not investigate whether quantization-aware training or other techniques during training could preserve knowledge capacity when quantizing to int4. One reviewer (score 5) notes this as a missed opportunity for practical impact in resource-constrained settings. Another reviewer (score 8) mentions only the finding that int4 reduces capacity, without addressing potential mitigations.
- **Readability and formatting**: Multiple reviewers complain about the paper's presentation. Specific issues: lack of a conclusion section; figures that are too small, unclear, or with unreadable fonts (especially Figures 1 and 3; Result 8 is said to be invisible); very dense text and a long appendix that makes it hard to absorb all important details. One reviewer (score 6) says writing can be improved; another (score 5) rates presentation as 2. One reviewer (score 8) also notes graphs are not explained appropriately.
- **Missing details**: The paper does not mention computational requirements for the experiments. One reviewer (score 8) asks for explicit suggestions to researchers about pretraining and usage, given that data quality and exposure times seem to matter more than architecture differences. Another reviewer (score 5) notes that the study does not explore whether encoder-only or decoder-only models differ, and the scope of conclusions is limited by the single architecture focus.
