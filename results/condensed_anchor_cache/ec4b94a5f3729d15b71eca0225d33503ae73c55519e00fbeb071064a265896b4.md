## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes elastic time steps in RL, where the policy outputs both an action and its duration (control frequency). The authors present SEAC (Soft Elastic Actor-Critic) based on SAC, with theoretical convergence results and experiments in a 3D racing game simulation and a Newtonian kinematics world. The idea is seen as interesting and relevant to real-world RL, especially robotics, and the presentation is generally clear and well-organized. However, multiple reviewers raise concerns about novelty, depth of literature comparison, clarity of method details, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- **Problem relevance and motivation**: The idea of dynamically adjusting control frequency is important for real-world efficient RL (robotics, autonomous driving) and is a natural extension of the fixed-time-step MDP (R2, R3). R4 finds the precise idea novel and intuitive, and encourages community investigation.
- **Presentation quality**: The paper is well organized, the figures and schemes are vivid and helpful, and the limitations of the approach are clearly stated (R1, R2). R3 notes the work is concisely summarized.
- **Theoretical foundation**: Convergence and stability of SEAC are claimed at a theoretical level (from abstract; R2 appreciates this).
- **Positive outlier**: Reviewer 2 (score 6) rates the contribution higher than others, highlighting clear statement of the contribution and relevance to real-world control.

### Weaknesses
- **Literature review and novelty**: The related work discussion is thin. Several reviewers note that variable‑rate decision‑making is already studied in the option framework (SMDPs), action repetitions, and control theory (adaptive time steps) (R1, R3, R4). The paper does not clearly differentiate from these existing approaches, nor does it provide a detailed comparison or ablation against them (R1: “can be seen as a special instance of traditional RL”; R3: connections to options are unclear and lack proper definitions; R4: “key phrase is ‘adaptive time step’ in control theory”). R2 also finds the comparison with time‑sensitive RL tasks poor and lacks discussion of trade‑offs.
- **Method clarity and correctness**: Several parts are imprecise or under‑specified. R4 criticizes the use of MPC *and* PID together (MPC typically subsumes PID) and the lack of clarity on how they interact, suggesting the work is immature. The reward function (Definition 1) is not clearly a multi‑objective optimization—fixed weighting reduces to a single objective, obscuring trade‑offs (R4). It is also unclear why rewards are only accrued at action changes, which could enable reward hacking; the incentive to minimize the elastic step length \(n\) is not explained (R4). R3 finds the reward definition in Definition 1 motivation unclear. R1 and R3 ask for a precise definition of the action space (Cartesian product of action and time?). The state dimension is claimed as six but nine are counted (R4). The phrase “additional input and output information that is not available within existing RL environments” is questioned because simulators run at small fixed intervals and action durations can simply repeat (R3).
- **Experimental rigor and evaluation**: 
  - Only simulated environments are used; a proof of concept on a real‑world task would strengthen the contribution (R2).
  - There are no error bars or statistical confidence intervals; the shaded areas in plots (Figs. 5,6) are not clearly explained (R4). This makes it hard to assess reliability.
  - Baseline selection: the 5.0 Hz fixed case is shown, but the elastic case uses 1–100 Hz; the paper should also compare against a fixed 100 Hz baseline (R3).
  - Figures 5 and 6 are ambiguous: it is unclear whether the right panels are simply zoomed‑in views, and the legend for lighter‑colored traces is missing (R2, R3). The vertical axis labels in Fig. 5 are wrong (R4). Figure 7 only shows two time steps and lacks context (scenario, corresponding actions) to judge whether changes are meaningful (R2, R3, R4). Figure 8 would be more readable with inverted axes (evaluation metric on y‑axis) and the claimed “overall reward” is not visually represented (R2).
  - The experiments are not complex enough to demonstrate practical advantages; the simple Newtonian world is basic, and the 3D racing game is still a simulation (R3: “experiments are not for sufficiently complex tasks”).
- **Minor issues**: typos (“but but” in §3.2, “epochs” instead of “configurations” in Fig. 7, syntax issues (R2, R4). The brain‑like icon in Fig. 1 is confusing (R2). The text flow in §3.1 jumps between blocks without aligning with Fig. 3; the action duration \(At\) is not explicitly shown in Fig. 3 (R2). The meaning of “p” in Eq. (2) and the impact value of execution (target movement distance) need elaboration (R2). The paragraph after Definition 1 could better highlight SEAC differences from SAC (R2).
