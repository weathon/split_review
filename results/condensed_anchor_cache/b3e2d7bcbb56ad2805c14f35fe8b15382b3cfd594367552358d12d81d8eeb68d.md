## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a test-time self-alignment method for offline safe RL using a transformer-based world model and Lyapunov conditioning, aiming to deploy an offline-trained policy without retraining. It modifies a Decision Transformer to include a VAE for next-state prediction, enabling imagined trajectory rollouts at inference. Trajectories are evaluated via a Lyapunov stability condition, and the least-violating trajectory is used as a prompt to the DT for action selection. The method is tested on Safety Gym and MuJoCo environments, compared to offline safe RL baselines.

### Strengths
- The motivation is strong: training policies entirely offline with deployment safety guarantees would significantly advance real-world RL applicability (Reviewer 1).
- The novelty is good: a new method combining Decision Transformer with Lyapunov conditioning (Reviewer 1).
- The idea of prompting a world model using self-generated trajectories is interesting and promising (Reviewer 2).
- Experimental results show the idea improves test-time performance in some tasks (Reviewer 2).
- Showing that a model-based RL with transformer architecture can be described as model-based hierarchical RL enables combining hierarchical RL and in-context learning for self-alignment (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed self-alignment framework aims to make the agent safe by self-instructing with the Lyapunov condition (Reviewer 3).
- One reviewer found the paper largely well-organized and clear in its presentation (Reviewer 3; contrast: other reviewers had presentation concerns, see Weaknesses).
- The authors compare against many baseline methods on several different tasks (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- Writing needs polishing: grammatical errors, very long paragraphs that combine multiple ideas, incorrect citing format (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Several acronyms and variables are undefined: Eq. 2’s \(B^c\); CDT referenced many times in main text but only cited in the appendix, with no description in the main text; Definition 3.1 lacks a citation (Reviewer 1).
- Training procedure for the DT+VAE model is not provided. It is unclear how the VAE is trained in conjunction with the Decision Transformer, what loss function is used for the VAE decoder, how this loss combines with the DT loss, and what the specific VAE architecture is (Reviewer 1).
- The text on lines 245–256 mentions “sampling policies,” but Algorithm 1 appears to generate trajectories; it is not clear how these relate to specific policies, how policies are parameterized or sampled from a distribution, nor how imagined trajectories connect to policy sampling (Reviewer 1).
- Connection between the theoretical results in Section 4 and Algorithm 1 is missing. The derivation of the control invariant set and the threshold \(D\) does not seem to be directly used in the algorithm; \(D\) becomes a tunable hyperparameter but its choice is not discussed, and it does not appear in Algorithm 1 (Reviewer 1).
- The maximization in equation (8) is unclear. It seems to correspond to the two loops in Algorithm 1, but it is not stated whether \(U\) and \(V\) are maximized jointly or sequentially, nor how the maximization is performed in practice (Reviewer 1).
- The parameters \(\theta\) and \(\psi\) are described as high- and low-level policy parameters, but there is only one transformer model; how these parameters are separated within that single architecture is not defined (Reviewer 1).
- Rigor of the theoretical results is insufficient:
  - Theorem 4.1 claims equivalence between finding a trajectory from a Lyapunov stable controller and solving a specific inference problem. The proof in D2 ends with “Then, the maximizing the above equation implies that the trajectory get close to the Lyapunov condition,” which does not establish equivalence, and the logical steps are unclear (Reviewer 1).
  - Theorem 4.3’s proof in the appendix is only two lines and begins with an equation needing greater explanation; the proof lacks necessary detail to be convincing (Reviewer 1).
- Experimental results lack statistical analysis. No confidence intervals are reported, experiments were run with only three seeds, and the tables are hard to read. Many improvements over baselines appear small, and without confidence intervals it is impossible to tell if they are statistically significant (Reviewer 1).
- In Figure 3, the unsafe regions do not appear to align well with the hazards; the unsafe regions should at least cover the hazards, but this is not the case in many instances, raising questions about the method’s ability to identify unsafe regions (Reviewer 1).
- The connection to Safe RL is not substantive. The method primarily constrains the policy to the data distribution, which is common in offline RL, rather than explicitly addressing safety constraints. It does not use cost information during training, unlike the offline safe RL baselines it is compared against. Comparing to within-distribution constraint methods like CQL would be more appropriate (Reviewer 1).
- The Lyapunov condition is not the main contribution; a large part of the content is used to describe it, which misleads readers about the novelty (Reviewer 2).
- The connection between the proposed Self-Alignment and the original Self-Alignment used in LLMs is unclear (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments are conducted only on relatively easy safe RL benchmarks (Safety Gymnasium, MuJoCo). These do not fully capture real-world complexity and uncertainty; no testing on autonomous driving simulations, dynamic obstacle avoidance in robotic systems, or high-variability logistics tasks (Reviewers 2, 3).
- No information is provided on computational cost: training time, inference time, memory usage, or how these scale with environment complexity or task dimensionality (Reviewer 3).
- No discussion of how SAS adapts to environments with unexpected changes or previously unseen safety hazards; performance under distribution shifts or novel hazards is not demonstrated (Reviewer 3).
- Questions from reviewers that overlap with missing details or comparisons:
  - How is reward conditioning handled in the Decision Transformer in this method? (Reviewer 1)
  - How does SAS generalize compared to non-Lyapunov methods? (Reviewer 3)
  - Could specific metrics on computational costs (training time, inference time, memory usage) and their scaling with complexity be provided? (Reviewer 3)
  - How does SAS perform in scenarios with distribution shifts or novel hazards not present in the training data? (Reviewer 3)
