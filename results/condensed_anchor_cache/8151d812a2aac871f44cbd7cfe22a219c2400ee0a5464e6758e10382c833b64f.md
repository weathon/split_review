## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper empirically investigates self-supervised learning (SSL) of simplicial representations using the Tree-Wasserstein distance (TWD) as a similarity measure within a SimCLR framework. Several probability models (softmax, simplicial embedding, ArcFace with DCT) and TWD variants (total-variation, ClusterTree) are compared on STL10, CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and SVHN. A Jeffrey divergence regularisation is proposed to stabilise the non-differentiable TWD. The main finding is that some combinations of TWD and simplicial models can outperform cosine-similarity-based SSL, but performance degrades on datasets with many classes.

### Strengths
- The paper is fairly well written, clearly explaining the background of SSL, optimal transport, TWD, the TV–TWD link, and the simplicial models (softmax, SEM, ArcFace). The rational for JD regularisation (non-differentiability) is clear.
- The proposed RTWD (robust TWD) and the equivalence to total variation—yielding a tree-structure-independent metric—is an interesting theoretical result apart from the SSL application.
- Originality: combining TWD with simplicial embeddings in a self-supervised setting is novel.
- The new loss function based on TWD is easy to compute, facilitating incorporation into existing simplicial SSL frameworks.
- Empirical evaluation on four image datasets with a 50-NN classifier demonstrates that several TWD+model combinations can obtain enhanced classification scores, showing the utility of the approach.

### Weaknesses
- The motivation for learning simplicial representations (instead of standard vector representations) is not well justified.
- Only the average of three trials is reported; standard deviations are missing, making it hard to judge statistical significance. This was noted by multiple reviewers.
- Ablation study is insufficient: hyper-parameters τ, λ, L, V, batch size, learning rate, and temperature are fixed; their impact on performance is not explored.
- Only ResNet18 backbone and only SimCLR are used. Experiments with other backbones (e.g., ResNet50, as in Lavoie et al.) and other SSL methods (e.g., BYOL) are needed to show robustness and wide applicability.
- The comparison in Table 1 is unfair: cosine similarity with simplicial models does not use JD regularisation, while the TWD methods do. Without JD, TWD appears comparable or worse than cosine, so the source of improvement (TWD vs. JD) is unclear.
- There is no theoretical or qualitative explanation why TWD is beneficial in SSL. The claimed advantages of Wasserstein distances (handling different supports, identifying matches) are not exploited because the similarity is measured between categorical distributions with the same support.
- The method’s performance degrades on CIFAR100 (large number of classes), strongly limiting its applicability; the paper does not adequately discuss why this happens.
- The k‑NN classifier is fixed at 50 for all datasets without justification; linear probing should also be reported for a fair comparison with prior work (e.g., Lavoie et al., Caron et al.).
- There are reference confusions: Lavoie et al. should be cited as ICLR 2023; ArcFace is originally a loss function with an additive angular penalty, not a layer, so its use as a layer needs clarification.
- Several notation issues and typos remain (e.g., confusion between U(a,a') and U(μ,μ'); ambiguous use of N; non-square I in Section 3.3; edge-weight N in Section 3.3).
- Specific questions that overlap with missing analysis: (a) How is the ClusterTree built for each dataset? (b) What are the state-of-the-art SSL results on these datasets? (c) Why choose a k‑NN classifier instead of a linear probe? (d) What is the advantage/disadvantage of TWD over other Wasserstein distances? (e) How does changing the similarity function affect transferability? (f) Could increasing the learning rate replace the JD regularisation? (g) How exactly does the method fail on many-class datasets given that SSL does not use labels?
