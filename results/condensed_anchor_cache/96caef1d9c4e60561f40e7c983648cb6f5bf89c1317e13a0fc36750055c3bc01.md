## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the exposure bias in diffusion models—the distribution mismatch between training and sampling trajectories caused by prediction error accumulation. It models the sampling distribution analytically, attributes the bias to increased variance at each step, and proposes a metric for exposure bias. To mitigate it, the authors introduce Epsilon Scaling, a training-free method that scales down the predicted noise during sampling by matching the empirical $\ell_2$ norm ratio between training and sampling predictions. Experiments on ADM, DDIM, EDM, LDM, DiT, and PFGM++ show improved FID, particularly in the mid-NFE (20–100) regime.

### Strengths
* Provides a clear didactic explanation of exposure bias, including a derivation showing how increased variance at sampling causes cumulative error.
* First training-free calibration method for exposure bias; no re-training or fine-tuning is needed—only a scaling factor applied during sampling.
* Simple and easy to implement; the scaling factor is determined from dataset statistics.
* Works across a wide variety of diffusion frameworks (DDIM, ADM, EDM, LDM, DiT, PFGM++) and settings (unconditional/conditional, deterministic/stochastic).
* Improves FID scores, especially in the mid-NFE regime; for example, ADM-ES achieves 2.17 FID on CIFAR-10 (100-step unconditional).
* Insightfully connects exposure bias to single-step prediction error and analytically expresses the error.
* Extensive experimental validation with solid coverage of baselines and architectures.
* One reviewer (score 8) strongly emphasizes that the empirical demonstration of correcting exposure bias without retraining is a valuable contribution.

### Weaknesses
* The core assumption that $\hat{x}_\theta^t$ follows a Gaussian centered at the true $x_0$ is questionable: $\hat{x}_\theta^t$ is often a degraded version of $x_0$, and the error distribution after multiple iterative steps may not remain Gaussian. Further theoretical grounding and empirical validation of this averaging are needed.
* The method is not tuning-free: it requires hyperparameter search for the linear schedule $\lambda_t = kt + b$, often to the 4th or 5th decimal (e.g., $k=0.00022, b=1.00291$). Optimal values depend on dataset and number of sampling steps $T$, making the method less practical.
* Sensitivity analysis for hyperparameters is limited: only $b$ is varied; $k$ is not explored over a wide range, and the analysis does not cover large strides or small strides.
* Hyperparameter tuning is computationally intensive: generating 10k samples for FID evaluation takes minutes (small NFE) to hours (large NFE), and tuning for each dataset and step count requires extensive GPU compute. The paper does not specify the compute used for tuning.
* The method is not completely simulation-free: computing the scaling factor $\lambda_t$ requires simulating the sampling trajectory to obtain dataset statistics.
* Only constant or linear $\lambda_t$ schedules are explored. Ablation studies with non-linear or fully-searched $\lambda_t$ over all timesteps are missing. A fully-searched schedule could potentially outperform the simplistic linear approach.
* Comparison with Analytic-DPM is insufficient. Analytic-DPM can achieve good low-NFE performance (NFE=10) with proper variance clipping in the high-SNR regime. The paper should compare with and without clipping, and clarify whether the proposed method inherently avoids or also requires such clipping.
* Several prior works that observed the same norm discrepancy are not discussed as baselines: (1) EDM Section 4 / Fig. 13 (neural network removes too much noise; they use inflated noise $S_\text{noise}$). (2) PFGM Section 4.2 / PFGM++ Section 5 / Fig. 4b (exposure bias linked to strong norm-$t$ correlation). These should be compared.
* The discrepancy in prediction norms (Fig. 2) is not further investigated. A simple experiment using the true score up to some time $t$ during sampling could test whether the bias mainly occurs at large $t$ due to norm-$t$ correlation.
* The method is not tested on PFGM++, which claims robustness to exposure bias. Testing this would help verify whether the bias stems from the strong norm-$t$ correlation inherent in standard diffusion models.
* No exploration of how different noise schedules affect the method’s effectiveness.
* For the DDIM solver, FID improvements are marginal even at reasonable timesteps ($\sim$50).
* The translation from the norm ratio $\Delta N(t) = \|\epsilon^s\|/\|\epsilon^t\|$ to a scaling factor on $\epsilon(x_t,t)$ during sampling is not principled. A toy example with $p(x)=\delta(x)$ suggests the relationship is not direct; the resulting scaled trajectory depends on $\lambda_t/t$ rather than the integral of $\lambda_t$. The derivation needs rethinking.
* The necessity of matching the forward and sampling processes warrants discussion: reducing exposure bias may force generated images to be more similar to training images, potentially reducing sample diversity. The trade-off between bias reduction and diversity is not addressed.
* The paper underreports some EDM baselines: for CIFAR-10 at NFE=35, EDM achieves FID=1.97 (VP) and 1.98 (VE), not 2.05 as implied. This is misleading.
* Formulations and notations in Section 3.1 and 3.2 are redundant and poorly organized, making it difficult to follow the core arguments. The paper should be more concise and focused.
* The exposure bias metric $\delta_t$ is introduced only in the appendix; it should be presented in the main text.
* Missing ablations for very low NFE (<20) and very high NFE (>100); recommended studies include fully-searched $\lambda_t$ for low NFE.
* The paper should also compare with Heun solvers under suboptimal $k,b$ selection to show robustness.
* The expected noise bias should be shown (not just scaling), to verify that scaling alone (without translation) is sufficient.
* In Figures 5, 9b, 10b, the $\|\epsilon\|_2$ of ADM-ES becomes smaller than training values towards the end of sampling (timesteps 18–21). The reason should be explained.
* Qualitative results (Figures 11–15) should include the number of sampling steps.
