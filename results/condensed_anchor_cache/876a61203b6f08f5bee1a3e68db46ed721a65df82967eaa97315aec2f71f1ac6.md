## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using supervised contrastive losses (specifically the Bradley–Terry loss) to recover the latent fitness function in global epistasis models for protein fitness prediction. The authors argue that these losses are invariant to the monotonic nonlinearity (g) that corrupts the sparse latent function, and they provide theoretical reasoning via a fitness‑epistasis uncertainty principle. Experiments on simulated N‑K landscapes and the FLIP benchmark (GB1, AAV, thermostability splits) show that training with Bradley‑Terry (BT) loss yields higher Spearman correlation with the true latent function than training with mean squared error (MSE). Reviewers found the idea innovative and well‑motivated, but raised concerns about limited empirical scope, missing baselines, evaluation metric choice, noise handling, and the strength of the theoretical claims.

### Strengths
- **Innovative application of supervised contrastive learning** for fitness prediction in protein engineering (R1, R2).
- **Strong theoretical foundation**, especially the “epistatic domain” formalism (Section 2.1) which the reviewers called a useful tool for reasoning about sparsity (R2, R4).
- **Empirical validation** on synthetic data and the FLIP benchmark shows clear improvement of BT loss over MSE loss (R1, R2, R3).
- **Well‑designed simulation experiments** that illustrate the advantage of contrastive loss in recovering the latent fitness model under global epistasis (R3).
- **Well‑motivated and easy‑to‑implement** modeling idea (R4, updated assessment).
- Performance gains on standard benchmarks are convincing (R4).
- The paper increases attention to the concept of global epistasis, which is often overlooked (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited empirical scope**: evaluation only on the FLIP benchmark; no testing on other protein fitness datasets (e.g., from the DeepSequence paper) (R2, R3). Synthetic experiments focus heavily on the high‑data regime; analysis of bias‑variance tradeoff and how performance scales with data amount is missing (R4, after‑rebuttal).
- **Missing direct baseline**: a simple quantile transformation of the target variable followed by MSE training would isolate the benefit of the Bradley–Terry loss more fairly (R1).
- **Uncertainty about regimes of effectiveness**: the paper does not systematically vary interaction order (K) or nonlinearity intensity (α) to show exactly when BT loss helps and when it might not (R1, R3). The conditions under which ranking‑based losses underperform MSE are not discussed (R3).
- **Statistical test details absent**: the test used to declare significance in Figure 3 is not specified; for some splits (e.g., samples and 7‑vs‑rest in AAV) performance is nearly identical yet a significant p‑value is reported (R3). The claim that BT improvement is *due to* global epistasis corruption is not directly proven (R3).
- **Figure 1 – scale/coefficient normalization unclear**: coefficients in the epistatic domain are not explained; the scale of predicted \(\hat{f}\) does not match the true \(f\) (R3).
- **Metric choice (Spearman correlation) questioned**: Since BT loss can be seen as a differentiable relaxation of negative Spearman, improvement in Spearman is unsurprising and not a strong argument (R4). Spearman correlation is argued to be a poor surrogate for protein engineering usefulness because it ignores asymmetric precision‑recall for high‑fitness variants and its optimal value is unclear given assay noise (R4). *(This reviewer’s view contrasts with other reviewers who found the results convincing.)*
- **Noise handling unrealistic**: the exposition assumes noiseless observations; real‑world experimental noise is often heteroskedastic (e.g., Poisson noise from sequencing counts). The pairwise‑comparison nature of BT loss may be easily flipped when noise is large relative to fitness differences (R4).
- **Identifiability issues not addressed**: the global epistasis model’s identifiability is not discussed; the latent function \(f\) is only identifiable up to a monotonic transformation, and it is unclear whether changing the loss affects identifiability (R4).
- **Assumption of monotonic g() may be violated**: in many biological settings (e.g., toxic gene expression, enzyme activity harming host cells) the nonlinearity \(g\) is not monotonic (R4).
- **“Fitness‑epistasis uncertainty principle” (Section 3.2)**: interesting background from compressed sensing, but its conclusions are too informal to be practically useful (R4).
- **Terminology clarity**: “corrupting data” suggests data corruption rather than the increased complexity due to nonlinear transformations; using “supervised” before “contrastive learning” would avoid confusion with unsupervised methods (R1).
- **Limited methodological novelty for ICLR audience**: using learning‑to‑rank losses is standard in many other domains; the primary contribution is a re‑framing rather than a new technique (R4, minority opinion; R2 and R3 considered the contribution good and the application novel).
