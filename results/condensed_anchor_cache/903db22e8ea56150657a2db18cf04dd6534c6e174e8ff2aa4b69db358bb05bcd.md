## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes LintSeq, a synthetic data generation algorithm that refactors existing programs into sequences of static-error-free code edits (insertion-only diffs). Models are finetuned on instruction + program-diff-sequence tuples. Evaluation on HumanEval and MBPP shows improved inference-time scaling (pass@k) for small-to-medium LLMs; e.g., on HumanEval pass@50, small LMs finetuned on LintSeq sequences outperform instruction-tuned baselines by +20% (±3%) and become competitive with GPT-4. A 150M-param model finetuned on edits is claimed to match larger models. Reviewers find the direction interesting and the combination of program analysis with LLM data generation smart, but raise serious concerns about the naturalness of synthetic edits, limited evaluation scope, misleading presentation (pass@1 vs pass@50 comparisons), insufficient clarity of the algorithm, overstatement of tiny-LM results, and lack of generalizability to other languages/benchmarks.

### Strengths

- The paper explores an interesting and important research direction (R1, R3, R4) and the idea of constructing fine-tuning datasets that mimic human developers by synthesizing edit sequences is novel (R1, R4).
- The overall idea of processifying code generation – decomposing code generation into syntax-preserving insertions – makes sense and provides more diverse context than left-to-right generation (R3).
- LintSeq is a smart combination of program analysis (linter) and LLM training (R4). It is unbounded by the number of human demonstrations and can be scaled using any static analysis tool (R2).
- The results demonstrate substantial improvements over instruction-tuned models at high sample counts, and the ablation shows that the linter-based filtering step is essential (R2).
- The authors are transparent that LintSeq may sacrifice inference-time compute for gains in program synthesis diversity (R2).
- LintSeq could inspire new approaches for code-editor assistants and incremental generation of natural language responses (R2).
- The pretraining experiment (TinyCodeLM) provides a better understanding of how the technique behaves at smaller scales (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Naturalness and realism of synthetic edits**: LintSeq ensures each intermediate state is free of static errors, but does not guarantee that the edit sequence reflects a human-like reasoning process (R1). Edits are insertion-only; deletions and modifications (e.g., replacing `pass` with real implementation) are not considered, limiting diversity and realism (R1, R3). The random trajectories may often be meaningless (R3).
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only HumanEval(+) and MBPP(+) are used (R1, R3, R4). These are simple, function-level benchmarks; the paper does not test on more complex or practical tasks such as DS-1000, APPS, LiveCodeBench (R1), SWE-Bench (R3), BigCodeBench, or code editing benchmarks like CanItEdit (R4). The generalizability to real-world coding scenarios or repository-level code is unclear.
- **Misleading presentation and unfair comparisons**: Figure 1 compares other models’ pass@1 with LintSeq’s pass@50, which is highly misleading (R2). Pass@1 for LintSeq models can be equal to or worse than instruction-tuned baselines; the paper does not thoroughly discuss this tradeoff (R2, R4). Only in appendix Tables 10 and 11 are pass@1/5/10 results clearly shown (R2). Additionally, the training comparison between LintSeqInstruct and Instruct may not be fair if LintSeq sequences are longer, providing more training tokens despite equal optimizer steps (R1). The emphasis on large pass@k over practical pass@1 is a weakness (R4).
- **Clarity and terminology issues**: The LintSeq algorithm description is insufficiently clear; reviewers request pseudocode or a more detailed natural-language explanation (R2). Terminology is imprecise: “static program verifier” and “verifies correctness” overstate the role of a linter (R3); it should refer to preserving syntactical validity/well-formedness (R3). “Coverage” is ambiguous (code coverage vs. pass rate) (R3). “small LLMs” is a contradiction (R2).
- **Insufficient discussion of related work**: Only two recent papers on edit-based fine-tuning are cited. Earlier work such as the “code sketch” paper (filling grammar holes) should be discussed and compared (R3). Papers like https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.13337 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.11006 also relate (R2). The claim that “open-source edit data with good coverage … is nonexistent” is debatable given datasets like EditPackFT (R3).
- **Tiny LM claims overstated**: The 150M param model underperforms CodeT5+ 220M at pass@10 on HumanEval and underperforms Codex 300M at pass@1 (R2). The statement that it “matches or outperforms code models with twice as many parameters” is misleading given these counterexamples (R2). The rationale for pretraining TinyCodeLM from scratch instead of reusing existing small language models is not justified (R3).
- **Generalizability to other languages and scalability**: LintSeq relies on a Python-specific linter (`pylint`); it is unclear how easily the approach transfers to other languages with different linting rules or paradigms (R4). The paper lacks discussion of how `pylint`’s specific rules influence the generated edits (R4). Dependencies on other files in a repository are not handled (R4). Computational resource requirements for synthesizing edits are not reported; cost-friendliness for pretraining is not addressed (R1).
- **Missing ablations and comparisons**: The paper should compare LintSeq with other leading LLMs for code (e.g., Llama3, CodeLlama, DeepSeekCoder, GPT-3.5) at equal pass@1/5/10/50 (R2). It is important to test whether the increased diversity of LintSeq can be matched by simply increasing sampling temperature in instruction-tuned models (R2). Wall clock time or estimated FLOPs comparisons between LintSeq and instruction-tuned models would aid transparency (R2). The effect of using min-p sampling (instead of top-k/top-p) could be explored (R3). It is unclear why Chain-of-Thought data was disabled (R3).
- **Overemphasis on large pass@k**: While pass@50 shows benefits, pass@1 is more practically relevant and not consistently improved (R4). The intuition behind pass@1 gains (if any) is not explained (R3).
