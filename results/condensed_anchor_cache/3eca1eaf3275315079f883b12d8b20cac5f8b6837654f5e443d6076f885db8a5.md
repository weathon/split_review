## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes eleganTE, an open-source framework for training and evaluating reinforcement learning (RL) routing algorithms using the ns-3 network simulator. Traffic engineering is cast as a Swarm Markov Decision Process (SwarMDP), with node-level policies that could generalize across topologies. The framework includes configurable topologies (BA, ER, WS) and traffic generation (gravity model with perturbations). Experiments compare MLP and GNN agents to OSPF/EIGRP. Reviewer 3 (score 6) finds the policies match or outperform shortest-path routing in some cases; Reviewers 1,2,4,5 (scores 3) are largely negative, citing weak RL performance, insufficient novelty, missing details, and limited evaluation.

### Strengths
- The SwarMDP formulation (Section 4) offers a way to train node-level policies that can generalize across network topologies (R1, R3, R5).
- eleganTE is built on top of the ns-3 discrete-event simulator, enabling faithful, repeatable experiments that capture interference, protocol interplay, and delays (R1, R2, R5).
- The framework supports a variety of random topologies (BA, ER, WS) and realistic traffic patterns (gravity model with perturbations) (R2).
- Extensive ablation studies are provided in Appendix D, covering policy architecture, reward function variants, and actor design (R2).
- The work tackles a hard, practically important problem at the intersection of networking and ML, and contributes a step toward automating network operations (R3, R4).
- The GNN architecture has the potential to generalize to unseen topologies, including unexpected topology changes (R5).
- The three custom ns-3 modules (topology monitoring, traffic generation, routing protocol simulation) provide a more realistic environment than purely abstract graph simulators (R5).
- The paper defines the requirements that routing optimization techniques should fulfill for effective traffic engineering in practice (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **RL performance against standard protocols is not convincing.** Reviewer 2 (confidence 5) strongly argues that OSPF and EIGRP generally outperform MLP and GNN by large margins. In the predef4s topology (Figure 4), MLP shows slightly lower packet delay but a visibly higher drop ratio, cancelling the benefit. In random topologies (Figure 6), GNN exhibits particularly high max delay and drop ratio. No single setting shows RL clearly beating both metrics. Reviewer 1 also notes high variance in GNN results, indicating instability issues. Reviewer 3 (the minority more positive reviewer, score 6) claims the policies “match or outperform” shortest-path routing, but the overall evidence across reviewers is that RL rarely, if ever, offers a clear advantage. This casts doubt on the necessity of RL-based routing optimization for TE (R2, R1, R5).
- **Lack of detail in the formulation of state, action, and reward.**
    - Action space definition is unclear: whether it is discrete next-hop choices or continuous weights, how heterogeneous neighbor counts are handled, and how this scales computationally (R1, R4, R5).
    - State representation is incompletely described: what metrics (queue lengths, link utilization, loss rates) and how they are aggregated and normalized into the graph input for the RL agents (R1, R4). It is unclear if traffic demand observations are included and how they would be measured (R4).
    - Reward scalarization is ad-hoc: the constants in Appendix B.4 are not justified, and the trade-off between delay and drop ratio may be better addressed by multi-objective RL (R2, R4). The ablation on reward functions (Appendix D.2) is performed on a single topology (predef5), insufficient to draw general conclusions (R2).
- **SwarMDP assumptions and applicability.**
    - Swarm MDP assumes homogeneous collaborating agents, which may not hold in small networks (3–10 nodes) where nodes have very different roles (e.g., high-degree vs. peripheral nodes) (R2).
    - The advantages of SwarMDP over other RL formulations are not explained, and its justification for TE is weak (R5).
    - The framework does not currently support changing topologies within an episode or policy adaptation to such changes (R1, R3).
    - Training is centralized; the framework lacks a decentralized training and execution paradigm necessary for truly distributed TE (R3).
- **Limited evaluation scope.**
    - Experiments are restricted to small networks (up to 50 nodes). Scalability to 500+ nodes is not demonstrated (R1, R2).
    - No real-world network topologies are used (R2, R3).
    - Only two TE objectives are considered (packet delay and drop ratio). Common objectives such as maximum link utilization (MLU) and throughput are missing (R5).
    - Traffic is assumed to be fixed for periods during which routing optimization is performed; dynamic traffic patterns beyond gravity-model perturbations are not explored (R4, R1 question about “complex benchmark traffic patterns”).
- **Missing comparisons with state-of-the-art RL-based TE methods.** Only OSPF and EIGRP are used as baselines. No comparison with recent RL-based TE works, nor with MPLS (a traditional TE method that can outperform OSPF) (R5). This makes it difficult to assess the relative contribution of eleganTE.
- **Lack of novelty and insights.** The combination of RL with ns-3 for TE is widely explored in existing works. The three custom ns-3 modules are described only as environment components, offering no direct contribution to the TE solution itself (R5). The TE requirements listed in Section 3 are also widely known (R5). The paper reads as a use case of existing RL toolkits with small modifications (R4).
- **Training stability and hyperparameter sensitivity.** GNN shows high variance in some experiments (R1). Sensitivity to hyperparameters, initialization, and training dynamics is not analyzed (R1). It is unclear whether the scalarized reward and other hyperparameters are robust across a wide range of network topologies (R2).
- **Presentation deficits.** The paper “fails to show how RL-based routing optimization makes sense in the first place” (R2), and its description of the multi-agent RL portion is “poorly described” (R4). The motivation for the MDP formulation and time-slicing method is not explained over existing TE formulations (R5).
