- Scores: 6, 3, 6, 6, 6, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a back-propagation-free (BP-free) training framework for physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) on photonic hardware, targeting real-time, energy-efficient edge training. The approach combines: (1) a sparse-grid Stein derivative estimator to avoid BP in loss evaluation, (2) a dimension-reduced zeroth-order (ZO) optimization via tensor-train (TT) decomposition to improve scalability and convergence, and (3) two photonic accelerator designs (TONN-1 and TONN-2) using photonic tensor cores. The authors validate on low- and high-dimensional PDE benchmarks (Black-Scholes, 20-dim HJB), showing faster convergence and reduced chip area compared to prior BP-free ONN methods, including hardware nonidealities (quantization, noise). Scores range from 3 to 8 (two 3s? Actually 3,6,6,6,6,8; one 3, one 8). The reviewer with score 3 is significantly more negative, questioning novelty, claims, baselines, and model size; the reviewer with score 8 is positive but requests minor additions. Overall, the method is seen as relevant and combining algorithmic + hardware design, but weak on experimental scope, convergence/ timing details, and specific implementation clarifications.

### Strengths

1. **Novel combination of BP-free training for PINNs on photonic chips**: The two-level approach – sparse-grid Stein estimator to avoid BP in loss evaluation, plus tensor-compressed ZO variance reduction – reduces memory footprint and improves convergence compared to prior BP-free ONN training methods (FLOPS, L2ight). (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 6)

2. **Complete evaluation from algorithm to chip, including hardware nonidealities**: The paper considers realistic constraints such as quantization, noise, and resolution limits in photonic devices. (Reviewers 1, 2)

3. **Reduced MZI count and chip footprint**: TT decomposition and the proposed accelerator designs (TONN-1, TONN-2) significantly cut the number of required Mach-Zehnder interferometers and the total chip area, enabling scalability to larger architectures. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)

4. **Two accelerator design options**: TONN-1 (full on-chip) and TONN-2 (time-multiplexed single tensor core) allow trade-offs between footprint and latency, with detailed area and latency calculations in the appendix. (Reviewers 4, 5)

5. **Clear and well-organized presentation**: The paper is concisely written with clear explanations of each contribution. (Reviewers 5, 6)

6. **Faster convergence and better error than prior ZO-based ONN training**: Reported improvements on PDE benchmarks. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited and toy-problem experimental scope**: The method is tested only on a 3-layer MLP (toy model) and two PDE benchmarks (Black-Scholes, 20-dim HJB). Several reviewers request additional PDEs (Navier-Stokes, Darcy flow, Burgers, heat spread) and finer 2D/3D meshes to demonstrate generality. The model is not optimized for PDE tasks and is likely overparameterized (Table 2 shows compression improves accuracy on 20-dim HJB, implying redundancy). (Reviewers 2, 4, 5, 6)

2. **Missing convergence and timing details**: The paper lacks per-epoch time, total convergence time, number of epochs to convergence, and clear convergence criteria. Figure 5 does not define when convergence is reached. This makes it hard to assess practical efficiency. (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 3 notes synchronization overhead not analyzed)

3. **Baseline implementation concerns**: The implementation of ZO-based ONN baselines (FLOPS, L2ight) is not fully detailed. It is unclear how these methods are trained and what photonic tensor core size is assumed. In Table 3, L2ight has far fewer parameters than the proposed method, mixing the effect of model size with training algorithm. (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 1 also notes different parameter sizes)

4. **Claims of scalability not fully supported**:  
   - The paper claims a photonic accelerator would not build a huge N×N matrix directly (Sec.4), but prior ONNs already blockify large matrices into smaller sub-blocks, making this claim inaccurate. (Reviewer 2)  
   - The claim that results “can be easily extended to solve image and speech problems” is too strong given only simulation on a 20-dim task (much smaller than MNIST) and no convergence or cost analysis. (Reviewer 3)

5. **Missing hardware implementation details**:  
   - No analysis of the achievable effective bit resolution rate given device SNR. (Reviewer 3)  
   - The interconnection between the Digital Control System and TONN is not discussed (e.g., photodiodes, additional noise). (Reviewer 3)  
   - Synchronization overhead between digital control and TONN (~500 ns/epoch) is mentioned but not analyzed. Tensor compression increases calls to the digital system, potentially harming scalability. (Reviewer 3)  
   - Hardware difficulty for TONN’s complicated signal crossings is not addressed. (Reviewer 1)

6. **Lack of analysis on TT decomposition**: No experiments on different TT-ranks, their impact on model expressivity vs. trainability, or the trade-off with dense NNs. The rank selection process is not explained (Section 3.2.1). (Reviewers 1, 6)

7. **Minority concerns about novelty and comparative performance**: One reviewer (score 3) states that the main methods (tensor-train decomposition, sparse-grid estimators) are not new and are borrowed from other domains without significant modification. Additionally, ZO performance is 5× worse than first-order (FO) on the harder 20-dim HJB task (Table 2), weakening the paper’s claims. (Reviewer 2)

8. **Focus on PINNs appears unnecessary**: The proposed techniques are general to lightweight neural networks; the specific relevance to PINNs (extra derivative computation) is not clearly distinguished. (Reviewer 5; also Reviewer 6 asks how contributions relate to PINN itself.)

9. **Presentation issues**: Figures are too small and lack sufficient explanation; environment details (code, chip info) are missing, hindering reproducibility. (Reviewer 4)
