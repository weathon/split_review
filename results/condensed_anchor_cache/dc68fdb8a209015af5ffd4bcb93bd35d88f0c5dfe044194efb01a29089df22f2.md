## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes translating garbled adversarial prompts (e.g., from GCG) into coherent, human-readable adversarial prompts via an interpretation LLM followed by a translation LLM, thereby improving jailbreak attack success rates against safety-aligned LLMs. On HarmBench, the method achieves an average 81.8% ASR against seven closed-source commercial LLMs (including GPT and Claude-3 series) with at most 10 queries. On AdvBench, it exceeds 90% ASR against Llama-2-Chat models. The method requires no manual prompt design, hyper-parameter tuning, additional model training, or informative feedback from victim models.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and motivates the approach in a lucid manner.
- The study of making confusing adversarial suffixes semantically meaningful is interesting and novel.
- The proposed translation of garbled prompts into coherent natural language is both simple and novel.
- The method is demonstrated across different datasets (HarmBench, AdvBench) and various LLMs.
- Experiments are comprehensive, evaluating on state-of-the-art closed-source and open-source LLMs.
- The attack success rate improvement is significant, especially on closed-source models.
- Table 1 clearly explains the intuition behind the method; the approach is efficient and transferable.
- The method combines the advantages of optimization-based (GCG) and natural-language jailbreak approaches, avoiding expensive manual design, hyperparameter tuning, additional model training, or victim model feedback.
- Prior optimization-based jailbreaks produced gibberish that was not robust under perplexity filtering; this method overcomes that limitation.

### Weaknesses
- **Over-claiming of fully automatic framework (Reviewer 1 minority critical view):** The paper claims no access to the victim model and no model gradients, but the method uses GCG to generate the adversarial suffix on a white-box surrogate (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), which requires gradient access. This overstates the contribution.
- **Missing evaluation details (Reviewer 1):** The system prompt settings for evaluated models are not given. Prior work (GCG, AutoDAN) shows system prompts significantly affect jailbreak difficulty, so this omission hinders reproducibility.
- **No code provided (Reviewer 1):** Without code, the effectiveness cannot be independently assessed. A reviewer tested a translated adversarial prompt against GPT-4o and received a refusal ("I'm sorry, I can't assist with that.").
- **HarmBench evaluation pipeline (Reviewer 1):** HarmBench uses a fine-tuned Llama-2-13B-chat to compute ASR; the paper does not explicitly state following that exact pipeline. Following it would improve comparability.
- **Lack of qualitative examples (Reviewers 1 and 4):** No examples of adversarial prompts are shown. Reviewers requested examples, especially for Llama-2-Chat, to visually inspect prompt quality.
- **Limited technical novelty (Reviewer 1 minority view):** The technical contribution is essentially prompt engineering, not a new algorithmic or theoretical advance.
- **No theoretical guarantee (Reviewer 2):** There is no theoretical justification that the translation always improves attack power; the work is purely empirical.
- **Missing standard deviations (Reviewer 2):** Reporting standard deviations across runs would validate the significance of the reported ASR results.
- **Limited testing on cutting-edge models (Reviewer 3):** The method was not tested on models like O1 that employ extended reasoning paths before answering. Such models may require more sophisticated prompts to manipulate internal reasoning.
- **No system-level defense testing (Reviewer 3):** The paper does not evaluate against system-level defenses like Purple Llama that classify and filter prompts. This limits assessment of real-world robustness.
- **Unjustified assumption about GCG readability (Reviewer 4):** The method assumes GCG-generated adversarial suffixes are always somewhat readable, but this assumption is not clearly justified or validated.
- **Lack of explanation for outperforming GCG (Reviewer 4):** It is counterintuitive that translated prompts outperform GCG, since GCG directly minimizes the adversarial loss on the surrogate model. The paper does not provide a clear explanation for this discrepancy.
- **Missing comparison of adversarial loss (Reviewer 4 question):** A side-by-side comparison of the adversarial loss between GCG-optimized prompts and the translated prompts would help clarify why translated prompts achieve higher ASR despite likely having higher loss.
- **Missing analysis of generator/translator model interplay (Reviewer 3 questions):** The paper does not quantify how differences in architecture or training data between the generator model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and the translation LLM affect attack success. It also does not explore whether there is an optimal complexity match between these two models.
- **Generalizability across LLM types (Reviewer 3 question):** The paper does not validate whether the attack generalizes across different LLM architectures, sizes, and training datasets.
