## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes to learn approximate symmetries in unconstrained models by augmenting the loss with an equivariance penalty. Reviewers found the paper well-written and motivated, but the novelty is limited: the method is very close to data augmentation, and many prior works on approximate/soft equivariance are neither cited nor compared. Experimental evaluations use classic models rather than state-of-the-art, and key hyperparameters (β, α) lack formal grounding. While one reviewer (R3, score 6) was slightly more positive, highlighting the multi-domain experiments and the limitation section, all reviewers raised major concerns about theoretical foundations, missing baselines, insufficient justification of the sampling strategy, and unclear advantages over strictly equivariant architectures. Suggestions include broader comparisons (e.g., QM9, ModelNet40), fixed-architecture ablation, and explicit discussion of group size and data efficiency.

### Strengths
- (R1) The paper is well-written and presents core ideas clearly and accessibly.
- (R1) Using a “landscape” to describe benefits of unconstrained models is novel and insightful.
- (R2) The augmented loss function is generalizable across various architectures.
- (R2) The augmented loss function requires relatively few samples to work effectively, making it computationally efficient.
- (R3) The paper is well-motivated and clearly written (particularly background and methods).
- (R3) The limitation section discusses an important limitation of the interplay between optimization paths and loss landscape.
- (R3) Experiments are conducted in different domains and examine several essential aspects, giving insights into how levels of equivariance affect task performance.
- (R4) Relaxing equivariance is a valuable research direction that can break through constraints on generalization or expressive power caused by strictly equivariant operations.

### Weaknesses
**Novelty, Background, and Comparisons**
- (R1) The equivariant loss is introduced without strong theoretical foundation. Its distinction from data augmentation is unclear: it essentially computes loss on a larger augmented dataset by sampling transformed data. The method appears already well-known within the community, limiting novelty.
- (R1) Experiments compare only classic models, not state-of-the-art, raising concerns about practical applicability.
- (R2) The paper lacks adequate background and comparative analysis against existing works on approximate equivariance (e.g., [1], [2] for average measure). It does not benchmark against other approximate equivariance methods (e.g., [1]), theoretically grounded symmetry breaking ([3], [4]), or simpler strategies like SE3Transformer with MLPs.
- (R2) The loss landscape difference may stem from architectural differences rather than the loss; a fixed-architecture comparison is recommended.
- (R3) Missing many relevant works: learning equivariance from data [1,2], approximate/soft equivariance [3,4,5,6], equivariance as constrained optimization [7,8], unconstrained models [9,10,11,12], and Sec 3.2 of [13]. Authors should highlight differences.
- (R4) The idea of group transformations for data augmentation is native, but for tasks like point clouds or molecules, sampling over all angles would expand the dataset hundreds of times and still struggle with fine-grained rotation equivariance. Validation on common 3D datasets (QM9, ModelNet40) is needed.
- (R4) The introduction addresses general equivariance, but the impact differs per group; permutation and translation equivariance are already covered by simple operations, making the method inapplicable. The scope of tasks should be specified.
- (R4) Lacks citations on relaxing equivariance [1][2][3][4]. Moreover, strictly equivariant operations can limit expressive power [5][6], but the authors’ emphasis on lower computational complexity is incorrect: torchmd is strictly equivariant yet has low complexity.

**Hyperparameters and Control of Equivariance**
- (R3) β and α: no formal definition of the “level” of equivariance (is it just intuition tied to the loss, i.e., higher β/α means more equivariant?). The optimal β is not learned; results show it can be identified from test data, which is not ideal. How does one know how much equivariance the task requires?
- (R2) Section 6.2: “Best performance is observed at an intermediate level of equivariance” is confusing because the paper modifies the loss, not the architecture. This needs further explanation; otherwise the conclusion is simply not to use strict equivariant architectures for non-equivariant tasks.

**Methodology and Evaluation Details**
- (R2) Sampling procedure: if one random group element per sample per training step is used, this should be stated explicitly in Section 3.2 where M is introduced. Justification that one element per sample per epoch is sufficient is missing.
- (R2) How is the equivariance error measured in Section 6? How many samples are used?
- (R2) MD17 has two targets (energy and forces); clarify which is used (likely force regression) and how results are generated.
- (R3) How does the algorithm work if group G is unknown? How can it approximate equivariance if G is very large and training duration insufficient?
- (R3) α=0, β=1 equals data augmentation, which does not guarantee exact equivariance. Can the algorithm guarantee exact equivariance?
- (R3) Which metric (Eq.9 vs Eq.10) is better? How does Eq.9 work when f(x) is non-scalar?
- (R3) Motion Capture: if symmetry constraints are known, why not select models equivariant to the required SE(3) subgroup or consider symmetry breaking [14,15]? What group G did the algorithm use? If it is a subgroup, the comparison is unfair.
- (R4) Figure 1 loss surface: unclear how it was created and how it demonstrates advantages of unconstrained models.
- (R4) Data efficiency: equivariant models are inherently data efficient, but the proposed algorithm does not appear to be.
- (R3) Loss landscape plots depend on the selected directions; inferring from only two random directions is insufficient. Although the limitation section touches on this, more discussion is needed.

**Presentation Issues**
- (R3, R4) Spelling errors: L156 “requiring equivariant into” should be “requiring equivariance in”; L396 “it is” should be “its”.
- (R4) Equations in lines 218, 224, 227 lack numbering.
- (R4) Line 215 defines G as finite, which is problematic for integrals over infinite groups (most groups mentioned are infinite).
- (R4) Reference formatting is incomplete; e.g., “Equivariant Graph Hierarchy-Based Neural Networks” was accepted at NeurIPS 2022, not arxiv.
- (R4) Appendix B is incomplete; several titles are clustered without explanatory text.
