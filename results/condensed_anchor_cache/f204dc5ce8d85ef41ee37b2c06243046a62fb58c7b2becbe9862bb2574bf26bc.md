## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes k-Class-Conditional Conformal Prediction (k-CCP), a method that modifies Class-Conditional Conformal Prediction (CCP, also known as Mondrian Conformal Prediction) by restricting candidate labels to the top‑k classes based on classifier scores. The aim is to achieve class‑conditional coverage guarantees while producing smaller prediction sets than standard CCP, especially for imbalanced data. The authors provide theoretical analysis showing that marginal conformal prediction (MCP) can arbitrarily under‑ or over‑cover minority classes, prove that k‑CCP provides class‑conditional coverage, and claim it yields smaller prediction sets than CCP. Experiments on several benchmark datasets compare k‑CCP against MCP and CCP.

### Strengths
- The problem of imbalanced data under conformal prediction is important and practically relevant (e.g., medical diagnosis). The paper addresses the lack of efficiency guarantees for imbalanced settings and gives an explicit procedure to modify the significance level, making the method relatively easy to implement.
- The intuition of using only top‑k classes to remove spurious labels is nice and intuitive. The experimental setup (code provided, multiple datasets, different imbalance scenarios) is generally well done.
- The investigation into over‑/under‑coverage of MCP helps motivate the need for class‑conditional methods. The analysis for class‑conditional coverage is thorough, with theoretical proofs and empirical support.
- Three discrete contributions are presented: a theorem showing MCP is insufficient for class‑conditional coverage, the k‑CCP algorithm, and a theoretical analysis of k‑CCP.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and modest contribution**. The failure of MCP for class‑conditional coverage has been established in prior work (Lei 2014; Sadinle et al. 2019; Ding et al. “Class‑Conditional Conformal Prediction with Many Classes”, NeurIPS 2023). The core modification of restricting CCP to top‑k classes appears to be a heuristic extension; the paper does not adequately justify why this is a significant advance over CCP with a more refined nonconformity score.
- **Heavy reliance on classifier top‑k accuracy**. The method’s efficiency (smaller set size) depends on the assumption that the classifier has good top‑k accuracy. This undermines the black‑box nature of conformal prediction, and in imbalanced settings the top‑k accuracy may be skewed toward majority classes, making the assumption unreliable. Some reviewers viewed this as a significant limitation; others found it a reasonable trade‑off.
- **Strong and under‑explained assumption in Theorem 2** (equation (10) / σ_y condition). The theorem essentially assumes the method is better (via the sigma factors) rather than proving it; its condition lacks clear practical motivation, is difficult to verify, and does not come with guidance on when it holds. The theoretical contribution of Theorem 2 is therefore questionable.
- **Hyperparameter g and computational cost**. The choice of k depends on hyperparameter g, yet the paper provides no ablation study on g, no discussion of its impact on coverage and set size, and no guidance for selection. Tuning g per dataset is expensive (a linear search over the calibration set multiple times), and a fairness issue arises if baselines are not similarly tuned. There is no theoretical guarantee that the chosen k(^) always satisfies target coverage.
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons**. Only one CCP baseline is used. Missing comparisons with more recent methods such as RAPS (Angelopoulos et al. 2020), the approach of Ding et al. (2023), “Classification with Valid and Adaptive Coverage”, and other conditional coverage techniques. The main claimed advantage (smaller prediction set size) is not demonstrated against these more advanced baselines.
- **Problematic evaluation and metrics**. The Under‑Coverage Ratio (UCR) definition is incorrect because conformal coverage guarantees are in expectation over calibration and test sets, yet UCR treats the calibration set as fixed. Moreover, the experiments force UCR to be the same for k‑CCP and CCP, which artificially constrains the comparison and does not show the true trade‑off between coverage and set size.
- **Missing analysis of per‑class prediction set sizes**. The paper reports only average prediction set size across classes, but understanding sizes for majority vs. minority classes is crucial. An overall average and a per‑class breakdown would be informative.
- **Presentation and readability issues**. The paper contains numerous typos and grammatical errors (“pupulation” level, “K” for number of classes, missing articles, etc.). The notation is confusing in places (e.g., ε used for both top‑k error and a general constant; r_f(X,Y) sometimes refers to a dataset, sometimes to a single point). The abstract is too long with unnecessary in‑depth description. Algorithms reference theorems and equations rather than being self‑contained. Minor errors: Eq. (6) mixes variables (left side uses y, right side uses c); Lemma 1 proof misses a factor n in the Chernoff bound.
