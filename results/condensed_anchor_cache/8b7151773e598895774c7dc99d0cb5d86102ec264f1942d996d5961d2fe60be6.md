## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes the Perceptual Group Tokenizer (PGT), a visual backbone that replaces self-attention with iterative grouping operations and is trained self-supervised. Reviewers generally find the core idea interesting and the connections to perceptual grouping well-motivated, and note promising properties like adaptive inference and interpretable attention maps. However, there is significant disagreement: some reviewers (R1, R4, R5) praise the novelty, while others (R3, R5) question technical novelty and empirical benefits. Serious concerns are raised about insufficient performance relative to strong baselines (DINO, ViT), missing comparisons with recent token-grouping methods (ToMe, CoCs, DINOSAUR, CAST) and 2023 SOTA, lack of scaling experiments (only ViT-B level), and absence of computational cost / inference time metrics. Claims about adaptive computation and interpretability are considered not fully supported. One reviewer (R5, confidence 5) is highly critical of experimental comprehensiveness.

### Strengths

- **Novel concept of grouping without self-attention.**  
  (R1, R2, R4, R5) The idea of using only grouping operations (no self-attention) is innovative, theoretically sound, and offers a fresh perspective. R4 calls the connection between grouping and self-attention an important and original contribution.  
  **Minority view – limited novelty:** R3 argues PGT is essentially a combination of ViT and slot attention, and notes many prior token-grouping works (ToMe, CoCs, DINOSAUR). R5 similarly asks how iterative grouping differs from cross-attention.

- **Adaptive computation without re-training.**  
  (R1, R5) The model can vary the number of group tokens at inference, with accuracy increasing as tokens increase (Table 1: 79.3→79.7 from 256 to 768 tokens). This is considered a useful property for adjustable speed-accuracy trade-offs.  
  **Counterpoint:** R3 notes ToMe also claims adaptive computation without retraining. R4 and R2 call for more rigorous demonstration (e.g., how groups change with input complexity).

- **Interpretability / visualization of grouping.**  
  (R1, R4, R5) Attention maps show more iterations yield clearer grouping and different grouping heads learn disjoint visual representations. R1 calls the visualization “very interesting.” R4 says the model produces highly interpretable representations. R5 considers interpretability relatively good.  
  **Counterpoint:** R3 states other grouping methods (ToMe Fig. 4, CAST) offer similar advantages. R4 and R2 request concrete comparison with other SOTA models and alignment with human vision expectations.

- **Well-written and motivated.**  
  (R4, R3) The paper clearly motivates the problem and contextualizes the significance of perceptual grouping. Prior work on feature detection is well-discussed (though recent token-grouping work is missing according to R3).

- **Thorough analysis of grouping hyperparameters.**  
  (R4) The analyses of number and size of grouping are insightful and demonstrate thorough evaluation.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient empirical performance and missing strong baselines.**  
  (R2, R3, R4, R5) PGT does not outperform existing strong methods. R2: Table 1 shows it cannot beat baselines. R3: performance is not better than DINO. R4: PGT performs very similarly to ViT, undercutting its practical advantage. R5 calls the results “weak” and notes the segmentation margin over ViT-B is small. Missing comparisons with 2023 SOTA self-supervised methods (R2, R3). R1 notes the segmentation comparison uses a different architecture and suggests a fairer linear-probe comparison with ViT-B.

- **Limited scaling experiments.**  
  (R1, R5) Only a ViT-B level model (70–80M params) is reported. R1: the paper should explore PGT-Large or larger to see if the grouping mechanism scales. R5: more model sizes (PGT-Ti, PGT-S, PGT-B) with corresponding baselines are needed.

- **Missing computational cost / efficiency comparisons.**  
  (R1, R5) FLOPs, peak memory, and inference speed (vs. ViT-B) are not reported. R1 wants a detailed analysis; R5 calls GFLOPs/MACs and inference time essential for fair efficiency evaluation. R1 also asks for a graph of number of tokens vs. accuracy vs. inference speed for fewer than 256 and more than 768 tokens.

- **Unclear technical novelty – strong prior work on token grouping.**  
  (R3, R5) R3 claims PGT is essentially slot attention or token merging (ToMe, CoCs, DINOSAUR) applied to ViT. R5 asks whether the iterative grouping is just multiple cross-attention steps, and if so, what is the advantage. This view is disputed by other reviewers who find the work novel.

- **Claims not fully supported (adaptive computation, interpretability).**  
  (R2, R4) R2: adaptive computation lacks rigorous demonstration (e.g., how group count adapts to input complexity and its effect on cost). Interpretability lacks concrete examples comparing learned groups to semantically meaningful regions or to ViT attention maps. R4: the adaptive computation section (4.3) is too brief; the interpretability results (Section 4.5, Fig. 6) need better contextualization against other SOTA models and human vision.

- **Missing supervised learning experiments.**  
  (R5) Since a new architecture is proposed, both supervised and self-supervised performance should be presented.

- **Insufficient details on grouping operation mechanics.**  
  (R2, R5) R2: implicit operations like MLP are not clearly linked to the term “grouping” – how do the weights determine which features are grouped? R5: it is unclear whether group tokens in each block generate independently or carry information across blocks; the iterative process resembles cross-attention and needs explicit differentiation.

- **Missing ablations on patch size.**  
  (R1) Comparisons with ViT at the same resolution (e.g., patch 16×16, patch 8×8) are missing.

- **No fine-tuning comparison with MAE.**  
  (R1) Higher linear probing accuracy does not guarantee better representations; fine-tuning results with MAE-ViT are needed.
