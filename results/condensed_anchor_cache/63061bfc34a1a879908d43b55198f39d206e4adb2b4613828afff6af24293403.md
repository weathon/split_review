## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces OpenMeta, a multi-task benchmark for metagenomic function prediction that integrates diverse datasets (ranging from 1,000 to 213,000 sequences) and evaluates over 20 models including classic ML, pretrained genomic models, and LLMs. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of uniform benchmarking in this domain but raise serious concerns about dataset selection criteria, biological rigor, novelty, presentation clarity, and missing experiments. One reviewer (score 1) finds the datasets merely piled up and the conclusions trivial; two reviewers (scores 5) see value in the broad overview and overarching conclusions; the fourth (score 3) notes good structure but insufficient interpretability and validation.

### Strengths
- Uniform benchmarking of computational methods is important for a healthy field, especially domain-specific benchmarks because different models may suit different downstream tasks. (R2)
- The paper includes a large variety of datasets and methods, providing a broad overview of metagenomic function prediction. (R3)
- It reaches overarching conclusions about how the field could move forward, such as the need to better incorporate hierarchical data. (R3)
- The benchmark is up-to-date with recent developments (e.g., pretrained LLMs like DNABERT2, FGBERT). (R3)
- The paper provides a thorough comparison of models, including both ML models and specialized genomic models. (R4)
- The writing is well-structured and detailed. (R4)
- The evaluation metrics chosen are reasonable (same as those used in Ref 13). (R2)
- Highlights that pretrained/foundation models are increasingly used in metagenomics but extensive benchmarks are still lacking. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset selection and biological rigor**:
  * The benchmark datasets appear to be a mere pile-up of third-party datasets; the bioscience logic for selection is unclear. Categories ("small scale", "large scale", "fine grained", "hierarchical") are not mutually exclusive, and it makes no sense why antibiotic resistance is "fine grained" while enzyme function is not. (R1)
  * The "large-scale" datasets are actually small for metagenomics; much larger standardized databases exist (e.g., NCBI virus database: 3.4M complete genomes; HumanMetagenomeDB, TerrestrialMetagenomeDB, MarineMetagenomeDB, MetaGeneBank). (R1)
  * The two disease-microbe datasets (Table 5) are each extracted from a single research paper; existing curated databases like Disbiome (10k experimental results to identify 1k microorganisms) are more rigorous. (R1)
  * The choice of negative data is crucial but appears ignored; it should be described in detail and better included in the benchmark (see Bib 2020, DOI: 10.1093/bib/bbz159). (R3)
  * No real-world validation or case study is provided. (R4)
- **Overstated novelty**:
  * The claim “To our knowledge, there is no benchmark in the field of metagenomics” is incorrect; existing community-run benchmarks like CAMI have >1000 citations, and many function prediction datasets exist. (R3)
  * Overall novelty is low; the most impactful benchmark papers include a new model. The authors discuss the need for new methods but stop short of developing them. (R2)
  * The paper recognizes the need for a unified model handling sequence and hierarchical data but offers no concrete progress or solution. (R4)
- **Biological inaccuracies**:
  * Phylogenetics is commonly a poor proxy of taxonomy (see Nature 2020, DOI: 10.1038/s41587-020-0501-8). Metaphlan is a marker-gene-driven approach, not a classic example of sequence alignment vs. reference. (R3)
  * The paper is surprisingly simplifying and often openly wrong in its understanding of metagenomics. (R3)
- **Presentation and formatting**:
  * "Benchmark" is not defined; the text convolving (1) an evaluation methodology, (2) the paper where it was first proposed, (3) its implementation, and (4) its application to a specific model. (R2)
  * Many tables of numbers are hard to parse; using plots or bold/color would help. (R2)
  * Frequent lack of articles and other grammatical mistakes make the manuscript unnecessarily hard to follow. (R2, R3)
  * More evaluation metrics should be included for fair comparisons. (R4)
- **Interpretability and bias**:
  * Models like FGBERT and DNABERT2 lack interpretability for biologists. (R4)
  * FGBERT may be biased on other tasks/datasets; the authors should discuss its limitations. (R4)
- **Missing explanations / unanswered questions** (overlap with criticisms):
  * Why were these specific tools and datasets selected? Can the authors provide a criteria-driven approach? (R3)
  * How easily is OpenMeta extendable to novel developments? (R3)
  * How were negative groups defined for learning (e.g., for pathogenic potential)? (R3)
  * Can one model be recommended above the rest for metagenomics tasks? This is not explicitly stated. (R2)
  * What are the major technical challenges or limitations of OpenMeta, and how do the authors plan to overcome them? (R4)
  * Give a few examples of real-world applications when applying this benchmark. (R4)
