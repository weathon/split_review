## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper theoretically and empirically shows that autoencoders for object-centric representations can achieve compositional generalization under two key structural constraints: (1) a decoder that is additive (each pixel is the sum of slot-wise function outputs) and (2) an encoder that is trained with a compositional consistency loss on slot-shuffled representations. The theory builds on identifiability and assumes each pixel depends on only one object (compositionality) and objects cannot be decomposed (irreducibility). Experiments on two-object synthetic images support the theory, including an ablation on slot attention normalization (softmax vs. sigmoid). The work extends prior results (notably [1]) by also enforcing encoder generalization.

### Strengths
- Formalizes compositional generalization as an identifiability problem, giving theoretical guarantees for in-distribution identifiability and out-of-distribution generalization via an additive decoder. (All reviewers acknowledge the significance, though one rated contribution as fair.)
- Introduces compositional consistency regularization to enforce encoder generalization—a novel step beyond prior work that focused only on decoder generalization. (Appreciated by R2, R3.)
- The joint encoder-decoder framework is elegant and ties theoretical, algorithmic, and experimental components together. (R3; limitations are adequately stated.)
- Paper is well-written and easy to follow. (R2, R3 explicit; R1 rated presentation good.)
- The ablation on softmax vs. sigmoid in slot attention is interesting and directly relevant to additive decoder design. (R2.)
- Theoretical results are sound and connect well to related work in object-centric learning and identifiability. (R2, R3; R1 rated soundness fair but acknowledged theoretical contributions.)

### Weaknesses
- The assumptions of compositionality (each pixel depends on one object) and irreducibility (objects cannot be decomposed) are restrictive. They are violated by overlapping, occluded, or non-rigid objects common in natural images. (R1 strong concern; R2 agrees assumptions should be relaxed.)
- The additive decoder cannot model object interactions such as occlusion, reflection, or relational effects. This is a major limitation for real-world scenes. (R1; R3 asks about allowing low-expressivity non-linear combinations instead of strict additivity.)
- The consistency regularization requires sampling implausible (shuffled) slot combinations, leading to training on unrealistic scenes. A principled sampling strategy is lacking. (R1; R3 indirectly via questions about scaling and non-synthetic data.)
- Experiments are limited to two-object synthetic images with 16-dimensional slots. No results are shown for multi-object settings, non-synthetic data, or more complex scenes. (All reviewers; R2 and R3 explicitly ask for results in more complex environments and real data.)
- A contemporary work [1] covers most theoretical aspects except the encoder generalization, making the novelty incremental in the view of one reviewer. (R2.)
- Scalability concerns: the consistency loss requires additional forward passes through encoder and decoder, which could be prohibitive for large-scale datasets or high-resolution images. (R1.)
- Unaddressed questions that overlap with missing analysis/ablations:
  - How does the consistency loss scale with the number of slots? (R3)
  - What is the impact of the slot-supported region width (blue band in Figure 2) on empirical effectiveness? (R3)
  - Can the framework generalize to non-image domains or handle occlusions under weaker assumptions? (R2, R3)
- While two reviewers rated contribution as good and soundness as good/excellent, one reviewer rated contribution as fair and soundness as fair, citing the restrictive assumptions and limited experiments as significant concerns that reduce practical relevance.
