## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes StructRepDiff, a diffusion-based generative model for periodic crystal structures that operates on a symmetry-invariant representation space rather than direct Cartesian coordinates. The representation concatenates composition, lattice parameters, and Embedded Atom Density (EAD) features. Atom positions are reconstructed from generated EAD representations via gradient-based optimization. The approach aims to avoid known issues with periodic boundaries and translational/rotational invariances. Evaluations on benchmark crystal datasets report reconstruction metrics and generation quality (validity, coverage, uniqueness).

### Strengths

- **Originality and motivation**: Several reviewers note the novelty of generating materials in a symmetry‑invariant representation space and reconstructing coordinates from EAD via gradient search (R1, R4). The problem of periodic material generation is important and relatively under‑addressed (R4). The idea of dealing with symmetries by learning in a controllable deterministic invariant space is described as “nice” (R2) and “straightforward and powerful” (R3). The study of real crystalline materials is highly praised (R5).

- **Clarity and exposition**: The paper is generally well‑written, clear, and easy to follow (R1, R2, R5). Figure 1 is especially effective in conveying the overall pipeline (R5). The main ideas are outlined clearly (R2). A comprehensive description of model algorithms and experimental methods is provided (R3).

- **Implementation and experimental results**: The implementation appears well done (R2). The model achieves good performance on benchmark datasets in terms of reconstruction and random generation (R1). The proof of invariance to rotation, translation, and periodic transformations is given in the appendix (R1).

- **Potential significance**: The model could be useful for real‑world novel material design (R1). The approach addresses a genuine need in periodic structure generation (R3).

**Disagreement on novelty**: R1 highlights several novel ideas, while R2 remarks the idea is “nice (albeit not novel)” and R4 notes considerable reliance on prior work.

### Weaknesses

- **Evaluation insufficient for materials discovery**: The core goal of materials discovery – finding structures that are novel, thermodynamically stable, and have interesting properties – is addressed only for novelty, and only partially (R2). No DFT calculations are performed to assess stability; the word “stable” does not even appear (R2). R4 also states that DFT evaluations are necessary and that results based solely on surrogate models cannot be clearly judged.

- **Missing representation‑based baseline**: The paper compares only with direct‑generation methods, not with other representation‑based generative models (R3). Without a baseline using the same representation but a different generative model, it is impossible to isolate the contribution of the representation versus the diffusion process (R3).

- **Ambiguities and missing details in the reconstruction procedure** (R1):  
  – The formula of the loss function \(L\) used for coordinate reconstruction (Section 4.3) is not given; it is unclear whether it operates in fractional or Cartesian coordinates.  
  – Key hyperparameters (gradient descent algorithm, step size, max_initializations, max_hops, max_iterations) are not reported.  
  – The final loss values (mean and range) over all generated materials are not reported.  
  – It is only in the Results section that one learns the reconstruction is done via back‑propagation through an analytical function (R4).  
  – The method for determining the number of atoms \(n\) from \(C_{comp}\) is unclear, because \(C_{comp}\) is normalized by \(n\); materials with different \(n\) (e.g., only carbon atoms) could share the same \(C_{comp}\) (R1).

- **Lattice representation lacks invariance and suffers from discontinuity** (R5):  
  – The lattice description (three cell edges and three angles) is not invariant – infinitely many bases generate the same lattice (e.g., (1,0), (n,1) for any integer n).  
  – Standard crystallographic reductions (Niggli, etc.) are discontinuous under perturbation, as proved in the literature (Widdowson et al. MATCH 2022, Theorem 13).  
  – The cutoff radius in the EAD representation makes the invariant incomplete because perturbing atoms can scale a unit cell larger than any fixed radius (R5, question).  
  – R5 argues that stronger, fully invariant, Lipschitz‑continuous, and polynomial‑time computable representations exist (isosets, Pointwise Distance Distributions) and that the proposed invariants do not meet practical requirements.

- **Permutation invariance handling is impractical** (R5): The paper claims permuting atoms gives an updated representation, but a periodic crystal contains infinitely many points; even permuting the \(m\) atoms in a minimal cell leads to an exponential blow‑up (\(m!\)) of representations.

- **PCA analysis raises concerns** (R3): The PCA distribution analysis in Figure 5 is performed on training data rather than test data, casting doubt on its validity and the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the generated PCA distribution appears more diverse than the original, which is left unexplained (R3, question).

- **Lack of statistical rigor** (R3): Claims of “significant increase” and “significantly better” are not supported by statistical tests.

- **Property optimization not addressed** (R1): The paper does not discuss how to perform property optimization with StructRepDiff, nor the impact of the non‑invertible mapping between representation and coordinates on such optimization.

- **Inadequate comparison and evaluation scope** (R4): The technical contribution is not deep enough; combining existing methods without deeper insights or surprisingly superior results is insufficient. The evaluation should focus more on the representation, especially permutation invariance.

- **Missing references and exposition issues** (R2, R5): A discussion of Uhrin 2021 (“Through the eyes of a descriptor”) is recommended, as it describes similar core ideas (R2). The paper does not cite classical crystallography results (Niggli 1927, Lawton 1965, Parthe 1987) that are directly relevant to the invariance and discontinuity problems (R5). A rigorous problem statement is missing (R5).

- **Presentation defects**:  
  – Table 1: “Dataset” column should be removed; the “P” under “RMSE” uses inconsistent italic font; “Cond‑DFC‑VAE” is mistakenly written as “Cond‑DFC” multiple times (R1).  
  – Figure 1 terminology (Cell, Comp, Structure) does not match the descriptions in Section 4.1 (R3).  
  – The caption of Figure 1 does not reference Section 4.1 (R3).  
  – The reference to “algo. 2” on page 5 should be “algo. 1” (R3).  
  – Hyperparameter details are in Appendix 3.4.4 but not cited in the main text (R3).  
  – An inappropriate self‑citation (“following from our earlier work (Fung et al., 2022)”) violates anonymity (R1).  
  – Source code is not provided in the supplementary material (R3).  
  – It is unclear if the concatenated representation is the paper’s own contribution; an explicit list of contributions is missing (R4).

- **Questions raised as criticisms / missing experiments**:  
  – How many terms appear in the external sum of formula (2) in Appendix A.2? (R5)  
  – Why does the RMSE not improve much compared to Match Rate? (R3)  
  – Are the evaluation metrics (RMSE) metric‑axiom compliant? Does distance zero guarantee equivalence? (R5)  
  – How many hidden parameters and CPU hours were used? (R5)  
  – Are the competing methods in Table 1 fairly compared – do they have a dedicated reconstruction component like the one from Fung et al. used here? Was validity filtering applied to all methods or only some? (R4)
