## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents Malenia, a zero-shot framework for 3D medical lesion segmentation built on a MaskFormer backbone. The core idea is to decompose radiological reports into eight structured visual attributes (using GPT-4 and human annotators), then align multi-scale mask representations with those attribute embeddings via a multi-positive contrastive loss. A Cross-Modal Knowledge Injection (CMKI) module fuses visual and textual features. Evaluation on three datasets (public MSD, KiTS23, and a private set) across 12 lesion categories shows improved performance over previous zero-shot segmentation methods.

### Strengths
- The idea of decomposing free-text radiological reports into categorized attributes for fine‑grained vision‑language alignment is intuitive and reduces noise/ambiguity from unstructured reports (R1).
- The paper describes key components and implementations in good detail, and presents thorough ablation studies verifying each component’s effectiveness (R1).
- The proposed approach demonstrates clear performance improvements over previous zero-shot medical image segmentation methods (R1, R4).
- The topic of zero‑shot lesion segmentation is valuable for clinical open‑set settings where diverse unseen anomalies are common (R2).
- Both qualitative and quantitative results are provided, strengthening the credibility of the findings (R2).
- The paper is well‑organized and easy to follow (R2).
- Malenia bridges the gap between mask representations and textual disease attributes for zero‑shot segmentation, with comprehensive experiments across multiple datasets and lesion types (R3).
- The multi‑scale mask‑attribute alignment is a novel extension of zero‑shot segmentation to 3D medical images (R4).
- The CMKI module is a strong contribution that enriches segmentation predictions by combining visual and textual embeddings in a complementary way (R4).
- The multi‑positive contrastive loss (MP‑NCE) allows the model to learn from shared visual attributes across lesion types, aiding generalization to unseen lesions (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty / similarity to prior work:** The overall framework closely resembles ZePT (Jiang et al., 2024) in using a MaskFormer backbone and interacting visual/textual features from detailed lesion descriptions. The key take-home message may be limited to the benefit of explicitly decomposing textual descriptions into categorized attributes for learning and inference (R1). The authors should clarify differences from prior works such as LViT (Li et al., 2023) and the cross-modal conditioned reconstruction method (Huang et al., 2024) (R3).
- **Practicality of attribute extraction:** Using GPT‑4 for attribute construction from radiological reports raises privacy and legal concerns in clinical practice. The authors are encouraged to discuss whether switching to open‑source LLMs (possibly without human annotators) would yield similar gains (R1, R2, R4). The process is not automated and requires expert radiologist involvement for the eight visual attributes, limiting scalability to large datasets (R4).
- **Inference details missing:** The Zero‑Shot Inference section lacks detail on how the model handles test CT images containing multiple unseen tumor types (e.g., kidney and gallbladder tumors in the same image). The phrase “we obtain the class information for each predicted lesion mask by referencing a clinical knowledge table” is ambiguous – the structure, content, and use of that table for disambiguation need clarification. It is also unclear whether the model predicts all lesions simultaneously or sequentially, and how overlapping or adjacent lesions of different types are handled (R2).
- **Unfair comparisons (Tables 1/2):** The proposed method uses additional ATTRIBUTE DESCRIPTIONS annotations not available to fully‑supervised baselines (TransUNet, nnUNet, Swin UNETR). This gives an unfair advantage. The paper must clarify how attribute descriptions are encoded and fused (simple concatenation or more complex mechanism), and include an ablation comparing the method with and without attribute descriptions to quantify their contribution (R2).
- **Low baseline performance (Table 2):** The reported fully‑supervised performance of baselines is unusually low – e.g., nnUNet liver tumor Dice 61.33 vs. originally reported 76, and lung tumor 54.44 vs. 74. This discrepancy raises reproducibility concerns; the experimental setup (preprocessing, hyperparameters, training protocols) for reproducing baselines must be detailed and compared with original paper results (R2).
- **Incomplete vision‑language comparisons (Table 7):** It is unclear how vision‑language pretraining methods were reproduced, as they require diagnostic reports that are not available in public datasets (MSD, KiTS23). The pretraining datasets and procedures for compared models should be specified. Additionally, the absence of CT‑CLIP, a widely used medical vision‑language model, weakens the evaluation (R2).
- **Limited generalizability and evaluation:** The model’s effectiveness is demonstrated only for 12 lesion categories; the ability to generalize to a wider range of unseen lesions in clinical practice remains uncertain (R4). The paper does not discuss applicability to other imaging modalities (e.g., X‑ray) (R3).
- **Performance on complex attributes:** As noted in Appendix D, the model performs less well on attributes requiring complex visual semantic understanding, such as “Surface Characteristics” and “Specific Features.” This indicates a limitation in capturing subtle visual cues, which can be crucial for accurate lesion segmentation (R4).
- **Handling of ambiguous/borderline cases:** There is no discussion of how the framework handles cases where lesion boundaries are not well‑defined or lesions exhibit highly atypical appearances (R3).
- **No thorough discussion of limitations or failures:** The paper does not present any failure analysis or a dedicated limitations section (R3).
- **Missing details and ablations:** More information is needed on the computation of MP‑NCE loss (definition of positive/negative pairs in the multi‑attribute context) (R4). An ablation study on hyperparameters (e.g., number of mask tokens N=16, number of attributes R=8) is missing to assess sensitivity and optimal settings (R4). The computational efficiency of the framework (training and inference time) is not addressed (R3).
