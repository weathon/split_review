## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces an approach to automatically formulate mathematical optimization models from natural language descriptions. The authors define the concept of autoformulation and identify three core challenges: defining the hypothesis space, searching it efficiently, and evaluating correctness. Their method uses Large Language Models (LLMs) within a Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) framework, where LLMs serve as both hypothesis generators and correctness evaluators. A pruning technique based on Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers removes trivially equivalent formulations. Experiments on benchmarks (NL4OPT, IndustryOR) for linear and mixed-integer programming show improved performance over baselines. The paper is well-written and provides a clear motivation, but several reviewers have concerns about the definition’s scope, alignment with the actual method, novelty relative to existing LLM+MCTS works, and practical usefulness for experts.

### Strengths
- The approach leverages LLMs for both hypothesis generation and evaluation, a novel and promising aspect that enables verification of partial problem formulations. (R1, R2)
- The MCTS integration efficiently navigates the large hypothesis space, and the SMT-based pruning technique saves computational resources by focusing on unique, meaningful formulations. (R1, R2)
- Strong experimental results demonstrate significant accuracy and efficiency gains compared to non-MCTS baselines. (R2, R3)
- Clear and compelling motivation with well-articulated method explanation; the writing is easy to follow. (R3, R4)
- Specific adaptations of MCTS to the optimization scenario, such as merging trivially equivalent formulations and including solver signals (optimality) in the reward, are valuable improvements. (R4)
- Sufficient experimental details and extensive comparisons are provided. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- The definition of “autoformulation” is too broad: it includes both mathematical and computational formulation, plus evaluation metrics (optimality gap, computational efficiency) that are solver-dependent and not addressed in the paper. This distracts from the main contribution, which focuses on mathematical formulation. (R1, R4) *Question from R1: justify why optimality gap and computational efficiency, which depend on the solver, are included in evaluation.*
- The formal definition involves optimizing parameters \( \phi \) and \( \psi \) (probabilistic models for mathematical and computational formulation), but the proposed method does not optimize any parameters (GPT-4 is used without fine-tuning). The probabilistic model \( p_\psi \) seems unnecessary because the computational representation step can be handled by a deterministic parser or commercial packages (line 241, 194). (R4) *Question from R4: why have a probabilistic model with complex joint optimization?*
- The relationship of the Type I–III problem classification to the rest of the paper is unclear; these categories do not seem to drive the method or evaluation. (R1) *Question from R1: what is the relationship of Types I–III with the rest of the paper?*
- The same LLM is used for both hypothesis generation and verification, introducing a high correlation that could limit objectivity and diversity of evaluations. (R2)
- The method lacks a clear explanation of how the LLM is guided to explore the hypothesis space with sufficient diversity. Figure 4 suggests pruning is effective, but it also raises the question whether the LLM fails to generate diverse partial formulations. (R2, R3) *Question from R2: how is exploration of the hypothesis space ensured?*
- More insight into partial problem formulation at intermediate steps is needed; improved verification of partial models could enhance accuracy and reduce runtime for complex problems. (R2) *Question from R2: In Section 5.2, how does the DFS expand the tree? Does it expand the child with highest prior score first or a random model?* *Question from R2: how should Figure 5 be interpreted? Section 5.4 seems not directly related.*
- The work may not be practically useful for OR practitioners, as they still need to do formulation themselves; the primary benefit appears to be providing examples for students. (R3) *Question from R3: how will the system be utilized by domain scientists?*
- The approach oversimplifies real‑world complexities by focusing on automated translation from text descriptions. The crucial step of translating real-world nuances into mathematical structure (decision variables, constraints) is abstracted away, risking models that are not robust or practically useful. (R3) (Minority reviewer is more negative on practicality.)
- There are already many works combining LLMs with MCTS (e.g., arXiv:2309.17179, 2402.03289, 2409.09584, 2406.07394, 2402.08147), raising the bar for novelty. This paper’s pipeline is largely standard, applied only at inference, and aside from the specific adaptations (merging, solver rewards), it lacks sufficiently unique features to stand out. (R4)
- The method is limited to (reformulated) convex problems (types I and II). (R4)
- Minor issue: the font size in the figures is too small. (R2)
- *Suggestion from R4 (not a weakness but included context): It may be worth fine-tuning the model with MCTS‑based self‑enhancement in the future; also highlight the relation and difference with other LLM+MCTS works and align the problem definition with the methods.*
