## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the “reversion to OCS” hypothesis: As inputs become increasingly OOD, neural network predictions tend to a constant value—the optimal constant solution (OCS) that minimizes average training loss without input. This is supported by extensive empirical evidence across 8 datasets (including CIFAR10-C, ImageNet-R/S), multiple architectures (ResNet, VGG, DistilBERT), and loss functions (cross-entropy, MSE, Gaussian NLL). The authors show that OOD inputs produce smaller feature norms, causing output to be dominated by biases, which aligns with the OCS. Theoretical analysis is given for deep homogeneous ReLU networks (Thm 4.1, Prop 4.2). An application to risk-sensitive selective classification using MSE loss is demonstrated on 4 datasets (Fig 6, 7). Limitations on adversarial perturbations and impulse noise are acknowledged (Appendix A, Fig 8, 9).

### Strengths
- Novel and interesting finding that challenges the common belief that neural network OOD predictions are unpredictable and overconfident.
- Paper is well-written, clear, and careful in presentation and experimental design.
- Comprehensive evidence: empirical (Fig 3, 4) and theoretical (Thm 4.1, Prop 4.2) validation of the main claims.
- The appendix includes honest discussion of failure cases (adversarial examples, impulse noise), which strengthens the work.
- The OCS hypothesis connects existing OOD detection ideas (feature norm reduction, confidence drop) under a unified framework.
- The selective classification application provides a practical use case, and the approach is compared to a standard (CE) classifier and an oracle.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of baseline comparisons (R2, R4):** The selective classification algorithm is not compared to existing OOD detection or abstention methods (e.g., threshold-based confidence, MSP baselines). The paper does not explain how OCS-based abstention differs from simple confidence thresholding, and whether it offers advantages beyond standard approaches.
- **Generalizability concerns (R2, R3, R4):** The hypothesis fails for certain distribution shifts (adversarial perturbations, impulse noise in Appendix A). The paper does not provide a formal characterization of which shifts are supported. Questions remain about whether the OCS holds under low-dimensional or unmodified inputs (R4), and whether observations extend beyond feedforward CNNs/transformers (e.g., graph neural networks, multi-path networks; R3).
- **Role of normalization layers (R3):** The core argument relies on feature norms decreasing toward zero as OOD increases, but batch/layer normalization layers introduce learned affine transformations that could counteract this tendency. The paper does not sufficiently address how normalization impacts the hypothesis, leaving a significant gap in the explanation.
- **Insufficient distinction from prior work (R4):** The observation that OOD data have smaller feature norms is already known (e.g., Sun et al. 2022, Tack et al. 2020). The paper should more clearly articulate what the OCS hypothesis adds beyond this known phenomenon—specifically, whether it provides a predictive model or only an explanatory framework.
- **Missing analyses and unanswered questions (R1, R2, R3, R4):**
  - R1: Whether the behavior of \(W_i \phi_i(x)\) (Section 4.1) persists on realistic datasets like ImageNet.
  - R2: Why early-layer feature norms (Fig 4, CIFAR10) increase with noise instead of decreasing; whether the reward improvement in Fig 7 is due to abstention or MSE loss advantage, and if accuracy (not reward) also improves.
  - R3: Whether the feature norms also decrease for in-distribution data, and details about the role of learning algorithm, data augmentation, and architecture choices.
  - R4: Whether the OCS hypothesis represents an “average” behavior, and why OfficeHome shows lower abstention for the oracle compared to other datasets (Fig 6).
- **Training details and experimental opacity (R2, R3):** Some aspects (e.g., construction of the \((x,a,r)\) dataset in Section 5.1, impact of normalization, learning algorithm, data augmentation) are not clearly explained, which weakens the confidence in the empirical results.
- **Minor presentation issue (R4):** Typo on page 1 (“Therefore, the our hypothesis”).

*Note: Reviewer 1 did not identify any significant weakness; other reviewers were more critical of generalizability, evaluation completeness, and clarity of contributions.*
