## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MindSearch, an LLM-based multi-agent framework (WebPlanner + WebSearcher) for complex multi-step information-seeking. WebPlanner decomposes queries into atomic sub-questions as graph nodes and progressively extends the graph; WebSearcher performs hierarchical retrieval per sub-question. Experiments with GPT-4o and InternLM2.5-7B show improved response quality over baselines (closed-book, ReACT) and proprietary systems (ChatGPT-Web, Perplexity.Pro) in both closed-set (HotpotQA, MusiqueQA) and open-set QA. The system can search and integrate >300 web pages in 3 minutes.

### Strengths
- The problem is interesting and important; the multi-agent approach is novel, especially the use of a dynamic DAG for query decomposition and retrieval (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- Writing and framework are clear, easy to follow, and visually well-organized (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- Considerably better output responses than proprietary AI-search engines (Perplexity Pro, ChatGPT-Web) and better than closed-book and ReACT baselines on multi-hop QA (Reviewer 1).
- Extensive analysis and evaluation: comparisons across prompting strategies (graph vs. JSON vs. code), inference cost analysis, and experiments with both closed-source (GPT-4o) and open-source (InternLM2.5-7B) LLMs (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Demonstrated efficiency: can process >300 web pages in 3 minutes (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**: Several relevant methods are not cited or compared: Self-Ask with search, Searchain, AssistantBench, SeePlanAct, SeeAct, ChatKBQA, AutoReAct, and other RAG baselines from AssistantBench (Reviewers 2, 4). This limits the evaluation of MindSearch’s relative performance.
- **Unsupported claims**: The claim that MindSearch “performs in 3 minutes tasks worth 3 hours of human effort” has no supporting human study or time measurement evidence (Reviewer 2). This was flagged as a significant overstatement.
- **Attribution/citation quality not evaluated**: The paper only evaluates final response quality, not the quality of citations or attributions (e.g., using ALCE benchmarks), despite proprietary systems providing such citations (Reviewer 1).
- **Insufficient technical details**:
    - The design and automatic construction of the DAG (graph nodes, dynamic extension) is not clearly explained (Reviewer 4).
    - How WebSearcher selects the most valuable pages from all retrieved web content, and how it limits content to stay within LLM context length, lacks detail (Reviewers 3, 4).
    - The answer generation process (“LLM generates a response based on search results”) lacks specifics on input design and prompt construction (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing analysis and ablations**:
    - No analysis of the dynamic graph: does the number of hops match question depth? How often is an incomplete graph created? No cost analysis on number of search queries vs. ReACT (Reviewer 1).
    - No ablation on whether query decomposition is always necessary or what happens if the WebPlanner and code-style interaction are removed (Reviewer 2).
    - No qualitative analysis of failure scenarios: what happens when an intermediate response is wrong? Could cascading errors occur? Is there any mechanism for WebPlanner to detect mistakes from WebSearcher feedback? (Reviewer 2, 4).
- **Limited backbone verification**: Only two LLM backbones were tested (GPT-4o and InternLM2.5-7B). It is unclear whether MindSearch generalizes to most LLMs (Reviewer 4). Additional experiments with more open-source and closed-source LLMs are needed.
- **Open-set evaluation details lacking**: The five expert evaluators are not described (e.g., bias, prior exposure to MindSearch outputs). No example responses are provided to intuitively show differences between MindSearch, ChatGPT-Web, and Perplexity.ai (Reviewer 3).
- **Token consumption not reported**: No information on token usage per query is given, which would help the community plan budgets (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity issues**: Line 315 states “MindSearch does not yield better performance in terms of facticity,” yet Figure 4 shows factuality is preferred 70% of the time – the contradiction is confusing (Reviewer 1). Figure 5 (example) should include English translations (Reviewers 1, 3).
