## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a Wasserstein distortion measure that unifies pixel-level fidelity and perceptual realism by varying a pooling parameter sigma. It computes Wasserstein distances between pooled feature distributions (e.g., VGG-19 features) over regions of varying size. As sigma → 0 the measure reduces to pure fidelity; as sigma → ∞ it reduces to pure realism. Experiments include texture synthesis, foveated texture synthesis (sigma varying with eccentricity or saliency), and natural image reproduction with saliency maps.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and clearly structured (R1, R2).
- The objective of using HVS-inspired pooling to unify fidelity and realism is novel and well-motivated (R1, R3, R4).
- The Wasserstein formulation is sound and practical, enabling smooth interpolation between fidelity and realism (R1, R3).
- Theoretical claims are supported by proofs and illustrated with numerical experiments (R1, R2).
- Texture synthesis results are efficient and interpretable, though less realistic than recent diffusion-based methods (R1).
- The use of nonuniform weights over pixels is a distinguishing feature over prior works that use equal weights (R3).
- The method is grounded in theories of the human visual system (R3).
- Code and proofs are provided for reproducibility (R1).

### Weaknesses
- The paper is only a proof-of-concept; image encoding is not demonstrated and the method may fill in unrealistic details, similar to single-image super-resolution approaches (R1).
- Reliance on local statistics may limit global structure, leading to inconsistencies in larger scenes (R1).
- The foveal approach degrades quality rapidly outside high-saliency regions (Fig. 4), not merely a resolution drop; consistency is important. R1 asks whether iterating to refine unrealistic portions could improve this.
- Use of VGG-19 features introduces potential bias and may not generalize to other content or tasks (R1).
- Wasserstein distortion is very close to Freeman et al. (2012); the main innovation is that sigma can be freely fixed instead of being constrained by eccentricity (R2, confidence 5). The theoretical results are trivial: large sigma recovers standard texture synthesis (Portilla–Simoncelli, Gatys), already empirically evaluated by Vacher et al. (NeurIPS 2020), and small sigma recovers exact reconstruction (R2).
- The mathematics in Section 2 are overly complicated; in effect the method reduces to adjusting the mean and standard deviation of local features to an exemplar, assuming Gaussianity (R2).
- The title is misleading: “fidelity and realism” here correspond to pixel distance vs. perceptual distance (R2).
- The contribution over prior work is questioned: nonuniform weights versus equal weights may not be significant; simpler modifications (e.g., adapting [Ref1] for spatial control) could achieve a similar fidelity–realism trade-off. Superiority over other methods is not demonstrated (R3).
- Missing comparison of computational requirements (R3).
- Missing comparison with other methods on standard test sets (R3).
- Lacks deep theoretical analysis: mathematical properties (behavior under transformations, relationship to other metrics) are not examined, and the specific choice of pooling PMF is not rigorously justified (R4). The approach is more engineering than theory; may be more suitable for CVPR (R4).
- There is substantial disagreement on novelty: R1 considers the objective novel and the contribution good (score 5, contribution 3), while R2 (contribution 1), R3 (contribution 2), and R4 (contribution 2) find the contribution limited. R2’s post-response soundness increased to 3 but still insufficient for acceptance due to the amount of work needed.
