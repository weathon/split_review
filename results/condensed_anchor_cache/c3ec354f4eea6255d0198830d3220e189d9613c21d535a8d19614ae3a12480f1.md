## Merged Review  

### Summary  
The paper proposes GenP, a graphical modeling approach for label-noise learning that infers the latent generative process of noisy data via causal factors, aiming to avoid explicit similarity assumptions on noise transitions. Reviewers generally consider the motivation and graphical model construction reasonable. However, they raise substantial concerns about novelty relative to prior generative methods (Yao et al. 2021; Bae et al. 2022; Garg et al. 2023), insufficient experimental validation and analysis, high computational cost of generating input instances, and questionable assumptions. Reviewer 3 is more positive about the experimental results, while Reviewer 4 is strongly critical of the fundamental assumptions and necessity of the approach, rating the contribution low.

### Strengths  
- The motivation to avoid explicit similarity assumptions on noise transitions and instead learn the latent generative process is well‑founded (R1, R4).  
- The construction of a causal graph and the development of the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) within a learnable graphical model effectively align with the research goal (R1, R2).  
- The insight that instances have similar noise transitions only when their underlying causal factors for noise are similar is reasonable (R3).  
- The proposed method (GenP) is flexible in its structure (R4).  
- Reviewer 3 notes that experimental results under various settings indicate effectiveness, though other reviewers dispute the sufficiency of these experiments (see Weaknesses).  

### Weaknesses  
- **Novelty and relation to prior work**:  
  - The idea of modeling the generative process of label noise via causal relations has been previously proposed in Yao et al. 2021 (CausalNL), Bae et al. 2022 (ICML), and Garg et al. 2023. The paper does not adequately clarify how its modeling assumptions, identifiability analysis, and causal structures differ from these works (R1, R3).  
  - Requiring input instance generation distinguishes it from Bae et al. 2022, which does not generate inputs, raising questions about necessity (R2).  
  - BLTM (a baseline) trains an instance‑dependent transition matrix without assuming similarity, contradicting the claim that previous methods rely on similarity assumptions (R4).  
  - Missing discussion of sample‑selection methods and how the “small‑loss trick” is integrated (R1).  

- **Insufficient experimental validation and analysis**:  
  - Statistical significance is lacking: results on CIFAR‑10 and CIFAR‑10N may not be significant, and the performance gain on Clothing1M is marginal (R1). Standard deviations are not reported for Clothing1M (R2).  
  - Experiments focus mainly on low‑resolution datasets (MNIST, CIFAR‑10); higher‑resolution real‑world datasets (e.g., WebVision, ANIMAL‑10N, Mini‑Imagenet) are not tested, leaving scalability in question (R1, R2).  
  - Only test accuracy is reported. Missing: error of the inferred transition matrix, stability of the learned latent space, sensitivity analysis for hyperparameters (λ_ELBO, λ_M), ablation studies (e.g., only L_semi loss vs. full loss, end‑to‑end vs. alternative learning, number of latent variables) (R1, R4).  
  - No time/memory comparison with baselines is provided to assess the computational overhead of generating images (R2, R4). This is critical because training a DGM to generate images adds significant burden, and the improvement over DivideMix is marginal (R2). R4 questions why generating images is necessary for classification.  

- **Assumptions and theoretical concerns**:  
  - The assumption that the generative process of causal factors Z is linear appears unreasonable and requires stronger justification (R3).  
  - Eq. (1) is confusing: Z_i is both an element of Z and computed from Z, implying an implicit constraint (R3).  
  - Since Y is latent, there is no guarantee that the estimated Y is true. The method relies on a distilled dataset as a proxy for clean labels, introducing another assumption (that distilled labels are clean) and potential bias affecting generalizability (R4). R4 further questions why the estimated clean labels from distillation are not sufficient without additional processing.  
  - R4 requests empirical evidence for the claim that “noise transitions can be inferred for a special group of instances” and questions why a graph structure is necessary or whether it could be replaced.  

- **Presentation and clarity issues**:  
  - Figure 2: the difference between blue and black arrows should be explained; more details on the generative process in Section 3 are needed (R3).  
  - The term “distillation” is used inaccurately—standard distillation transfers knowledge from a complex model to a simpler one, which is not evident here (R2).  
  - Noise type names “Worst”, “Aggregate”, “Random 1”, “Random 2”, “Random 3” are not standard and require clear definitions (R2).  
  - Overlap between the first paragraph of Section 3 and “Intuition about Inferring Latent Generative Process” (R1). Missing citation for the “small‑loss trick” (R1). Missing numbering in the Baselines section (R1).  

- **Additional requested comparisons/analyses from reviewer questions**:  
  - Specify how the use of a DGM framework with causal graphs differs in motivation and application from CausalNL (R2).  
  - Could the methodology benefit from pre‑trained DGMs to alleviate computational demands? (R2).  
  - What is the meaning of N_i? Provide illustration using Figure 1 (R3).
