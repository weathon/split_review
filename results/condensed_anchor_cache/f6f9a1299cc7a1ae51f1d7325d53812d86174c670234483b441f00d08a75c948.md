## Merged Review

### Summary
The submission introduces Q-TAPE (called LLM4QPE in the paper), a large-language-model-style unsupervised pretraining and supervised finetuning paradigm for quantum property estimation. It performs task-agnostic unsupervised pretraining on diverse quantum systems under different physical conditions, then finetunes for downstream tasks. Experiments on classifying quantum phases in the Rydberg atom model and predicting two-body correlation functions in the anisotropic Heisenberg model show strong performance, especially with limited data (e.g., at L=31 with smallest experimental data, >15% improvement using the pretrained model). All four reviewers rate the paper 8, with high confidence (two at 5, two at 4). They find the approach novel and promising for reducing experimental data requirements.

### Strengths
- Novel paradigm shift: unsupervised pretraining combined with downstream finetuning for quantum physics, inspired by LLM success, rather than relying solely on supervised learning (R1, R2, R4).
- Demonstrates versatility across multiple task types (phase classification and correlation prediction) and system sizes up to 31 qubits (R1, R3, R4).
- Strong empirical results: pretrained model significantly reduces the amount of experimental data needed; e.g., >15% improvement at L=31 with smallest data in Fig. 3 (R3).
- Potential to initiate a fruitful line of research for building powerful pretrained models in quantum many-body physics (R3).
- Excellent presentation, with emphasis on experimental details and open-sourcing code (R1, R4).
- The work aims at building quantum datasets conceptually analogous to the corpus used to train LLMs (R2).

### Weaknesses
- Scaling behavior of the required number of measurements per training example with qubit count is not empirically investigated; exponential scaling could limit practical utility for larger systems (R1).
- Storage method and memory cost for the training dataset (measurement results and operators) are not discussed; for larger qubit counts, memory may become a bottleneck. The authors should clarify if advanced methods address this (R1).
- Missing references: the paper does not cite several related works, including: (i) Refs [1] and [2] from Reviewer 1 on deep neural networks handling multiple tasks and the role of datasets in quantum system learning; (ii) Yang et al. (2022) on pretraining hybrid LLM for quantum data; (iii) Qi et al. (2023) on theoretical understanding of pretraining LLM for quantum machine learning (R1, R2).
- The paper only considers two downstream tasks (phase classification and correlation prediction). More tasks (e.g., predicting entanglement entropy) would strengthen the work (R3).
- The work is purely empirical; a more theoretical understanding or visualization of what universal features the pretrained model learns in quantum physics would be valuable (R3). It remains unclear why pretraining is useful in quantum physics and what “universal” features exist (R3).
- Out‑of‑distribution quantum data is not discussed. For OOD data, the model may not perform well, and quantum‑aware optimization algorithms may be needed (R2). The authors should clarify whether OOD data exists in the datasets and how to handle it (R2).
- The pretraining phase (regeneration of measurement results from incomplete measurements) follows a similar logic to a previous work (Ref. [1] in Reviewer 4) – this work should be cited and differentiated (R4).
- The finetuning phase shares similarities with another framework (Ref. [2] in Reviewer 4); while Table 1 shows Q‑TAPE outperforming NN‑Classical Shadow even without pretraining, the authors should provide a deeper analysis or intuitive explanation for the distinctions and novelty (R4).
- The paper could include an analysis of prediction MSE for correlation functions with respect to different distances between quantum sites (R4).
- There is a typo in the Limitations paragraph (Section 5): “…for expereiments.Though” (R3).
- Some reviewers question whether classical neural architectures are appropriate for quantum data, suggesting that quantum machine learning models (QNNs, QGNNs) might be more suitable. The authors should justify their choice and discuss why the hybrid quantum‑classical architecture is interesting despite simpler classical‑to‑quantum encoding alternatives (R2).
