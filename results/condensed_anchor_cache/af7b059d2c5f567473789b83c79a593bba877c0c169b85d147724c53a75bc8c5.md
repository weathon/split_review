## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper develops a theoretical framework for transfer learning in high-dimensional regression, focusing on deep linear networks. It argues that conventional distribution-based similarity metrics (e.g., ϕ-divergences, integral probability metrics) are insufficient to predict transfer success and instead advocates a feature-centric viewpoint centered on the overlap between the pretrained model's feature space and the target task. The authors analyze both linear transfer (training only the readout layer) and full fine-tuning, deriving phase diagrams that quantify when transfer outperforms training from scratch, particularly under limited target data. They validate their predictions on a two-layer nonlinear ReLU network. The paper presents novel theoretical results (Theorems 3.7, 3.8, 3.9) that are non-trivial even in the linear case because they operate in the feature-learning “rich” regime.

### Strengths
- The feature-centric viewpoint appears novel and addresses a key gap in the theory of transfer learning for foundation models.
- The main theoretical results (Theorems 3.7, 3.8, 3.9) are, to the reviewer's knowledge, novel and relevant to developing a theory of transfer learning in neural networks; they are non-trivial to obtain due to the non-convex optimization in the rich regime.
- The paper is overall well-written and easy to follow; the storyline is logically fluent and results are nicely tied into existing literature.
- The topic is of high importance – understanding when and why transfer learning works is crucial for deep learning.
- The model captures both linear transfer and full fine-tuning, and the theoretical predictions are empirically validated on a two-layer ReLU network.
- The problem setup (assumptions, phase diagrams) is clearly and concisely presented.
- One reviewer (Reviewer 4) found no major technical flaws, though another reviewer (Reviewer 2) had significant concerns about clarity and setup.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clarity and flow in core argument (Theorem 2.2):** One reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 3) found the explanation of Theorem 2.2 unclear and could not follow the argument. The inferred claim – that even if source and target distributions are identical but features differ, transfer will fail – seemed obvious to the reviewer (one can always find a poor-performing function in the same subspace). The reviewer noted that no similarity constraint is imposed between f and g, only that they exist in the same subspace, which makes the counterexample trivial. This reviewer rated the soundness and presentation low (2/2) and the contribution low (2).
- **Problem setup inconsistency (Assumption 2.1):** Reviewer 2 pointed out an inconsistency: the paper initially says x and y are jointly distributed with y real-valued, but in Assumption 2.1 states that y is a function of x with statistical properties derived from f(x)+ε. If y is a function, it cannot have statistical properties; the simultaneous statement y = f(x)+ε and y(x) = f(x)+ε is problematic. The randomness of x becomes unclear, and the analysis appears limited to (possibly univariate) Gaussian data, which is the most basic case.
- **Reliance on linear networks and Gaussian assumptions:** Multiple reviewers noted this as a major limitation. The paper focuses on deep linear networks (which are still linear functions regardless of depth) and assumes normally distributed inputs with a shared covariance structure. The insights may not capture the nonlinear feature learning crucial in deep networks, and the theoretical conclusions may not generalize to real-world scenarios with domain shifts and nonlinear relationships. One reviewer (Reviewer 5) specifically questioned how the phase diagrams would change with varying feature dimensions.
- **Empirical validation is minimal:** The only experiment is a small two-layer nonlinear network. This is insufficient to demonstrate that the theory translates to deep state-of-the-art models (Reviewer 1). The paper would benefit from more extensive experiments, e.g., on nonlinear architectures beyond a single two-layer network.
- **No practical design insights:** The theory does not offer guidance on why certain pretraining methods (e.g., contrastive learning, self-supervised learning) yield more transferable representations, nor does it suggest how to improve pre-training (e.g., via regularizers that maximize feature overlap using a small amount of target data). This limits practical impact (Reviewer 1).
- **Misleading portrayal of dataset-based metrics and double descent:** Reviewer 3 argued that the paper’s criticism of distribution-based metrics is misleading. The “anomalous positive transfer” shown is entirely due to the double descent phenomenon (more pre-training data helps avoid double descent), not to inherent feature transfer. The proposed metric (comparing fine-tuned vs. trained-from-scratch) also suffers from this flaw: it would show positive transfer even when source and target distributions are identical, simply due to double descent. Comparing to a trained-from-scratch model is not sufficient to evaluate transferability. The paper should discuss this limitation and whether the conclusions would hold if double descent were removed.
- **Incomplete results on regularization (Section 3.8):** Reviewer 3 noted that Section 3.8 does not show how the transferability measure \(\mathcal{T}\) changes under regularization (weight decay). The picture in Figure 1(b) would likely change, as regularization mitigates double descent and may make dataset-based metrics correlate better with transferability. The paper should elaborate on this and discuss the implications for the feature-centric view.
- **Missing details and minor issues:** The following were noted: symbol \(M\) is undefined (line 161); the Dudley metric is not introduced; Assumption (22) about initialization is not explained in the main text; Figure C requires better formatting (Reviewer 3). Theorems 3.4 and 3.5 are re-proved from prior work (Yun et al.) but the motivation for re-proving and prominently highlighting them is unclear (Reviewer 5).
- **Unanswered questions about practical scenarios:** The core argument against dataset-based metrics lacks clear empirical connection to real-world transfer learning. One reviewer asked for an experiment showing that source and target distributions can be “far apart” (e.g., in KL or Wasserstein) yet still yield positive transfer. Another reviewer asked whether the framework can handle gradient descent / SGD noise, classification settings (logistic regression), and varying dimensionality.
- **Minor notation/interpretation questions:** Reviewer 5 asked whether the same noise variable \(\epsilon\) in Eqs. (1) and (2) implies shared noise between source and target for a given input; the interpretation of \(\Theta_i\) in Eq. (6); meaning of \(\leq_L\) in line 174.
