## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AutoBench-V, an automated framework for evaluating Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) by using an LVLM (GPT-4o) to generate image descriptions, questions, and answers, and FLUX as a text-to-image model to produce corresponding images. The benchmark covers five evaluation dimensions with varying difficulty levels. Seven LVLMs are evaluated. Reviewers acknowledge novelty and effort but raise major concerns about presentation quality, dataset quality, experimental methodology, and overclaims. One reviewer is extremely positive (score 6), one moderately positive (score 5), one negative (score 3), and one extremely negative (score 1), indicating strong disagreement.

### Strengths
- The idea of automated benchmarking to reduce human cost in dataset construction is excellent (R1).  
- The generation procedure is interesting and generated samples look reasonable (R2).  
- The authors have shown tremendous efforts to build the comprehensiveness of AutoBench-V’s design; the manuscript provides a fluent experience when interpreting the pipeline (R3).  
- The study has novelty in automatic evaluation of multimodal LLMs (R4).  
- The paper is elegantly written, and the automatic evaluation process in the methodology section is clearly presented (R4).  
- Multiple evaluation categories are provided (R1).  
- The models are evaluated in detail (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and citation quality** (R2): The very first sentence of the introduction contains errors: “Attention is all you need” (2017) is cited as a 2023 paper; LLaVA is cited as an LLM though it is a vision model; the formulation suggests LLM works from 2023 pave the way for NLP works from 2020. In the related work, a 2015 paper is cited as preceding the COCO dataset (2014). ImageNet is cited as a “benchmark for assessing LVLMs” though it is an image classification dataset, and is cited as an arxiv paper instead of the proper IJCV venue. Citations in the references are all lowercased, likely due to incorrect bibtex usage. English quality is low (e.g., lines 85–87, 136–137 are unclear). Typos like “fine-frained” (line 215). These issues make the paper difficult to read and suggest citations were not thoroughly examined. (R2 considers this a KO criterium.)

- **Method description is insufficient** (R2): The “guidelines” chapter is not understandable (e.g., “This guideline acts as a guideline…”). Details missing: how is the exact procedure? The descriptions are more prosaic than scientific. The prompt in Figure 23 is not referenced in the chapter. For image description with difficulty grading, even after reading Appendix C.1 the prompts used are unclear and there is no discussion of how this avoids common VLM weaknesses (hallucinations, ignoring parts of text when generating images). The semantic graph part describes what function \(f\) could be but not what it actually is.

- **Benchmark quality is questionable** (R2): In Figure 198, a VQA question is ambiguous: the answer “the stem appears to be pointing upwards” is reasonable but does not match the ground truth. In Figure 19, the ground truth is simply wrong (“the child is not wearing a red clothing”). These examples indicate the generated benchmark may be too low quality to provide meaningful results. (R2 requests random samples of the dataset.)

- **Data quality control and “unnecessary visual content”** (R1): The paper should include more discussion on controlling the quality of generated data (avoiding incoherent or low-quality images/questions). Does AutoBench-V incorporate strategies to address the “unnecessary visual content” issue as discussed in [MMStar] and [NaturalBench] (where questions can be answered correctly without the image)? (R1)

- **Self-enhancement leakage / being player and referee** (R3): Since all prompts and test cases are generated using GPT-4o, and GPT-4o is also one of the evaluated models, aren’t the baseline performances in Table 2 by GPT-4o technically obtained by testing on the training set? Even if prevention actions were taken on the image description text encoder factor (Sec. 4.2), the ad hoc-generated visual examples and guidelines are not fully deprived of the prior brought by GPT-4o. Thus GPT-4o’s top performances (and to a lesser extent GPT-4o-mini) are unconvincing. (R3 suggests amending this by employing multiple Examiners isolated from the candidate model, as in Ye et al., 2024, “Justice or prejudice? quantifying biases in llm-as-a-judge”.)

- **Separation of defects from T2I vs. VLM** (R2, R3): The defects observed could originate either from the text-to-image model not properly generating the image or from the VLM not answering correctly despite a correct image. How are these two cases separated? (R2) Also, what is the suboptimal performance of other T2I models like Stable Diffusion? (R3) Model size could be an important factor.

- **Flexibility overclaimed** (R4): The paper claims “they lack the flexibility…” (line 053) but for charts, diagrams (ChartQA, ScienceQA), it is difficult to draw these using T2I; the ability to handle such inputs is needed. All constructed tasks seem to be natural images, so the claim of greater flexibility than benchmarks like MMT-Bench is not fully justified.

- **Questions lack visual information** (R4): All questions are generated through LLM without leveraging visual information. Examples like “what is the object in the upper left corner of the picture” or “what is the red square object” are absent, which are critical in multimodal evaluation.

- **Limited models evaluated** (R4): Only seven LVLMs are evaluated, with only one open-source model. Compared to other benchmarks (e.g., MME evaluates 20+ models), this affects the claim of “Extensive experiments” (line 123).

- **Cost reduction overclaimed without comparison** (R4): The contribution mentions greatly reduced manual cost, but no manual comparison results between the automatically constructed benchmark and manually collected benchmarks (MME, MMBench, MEGA-Bench) in terms of quality, diversity, etc., are provided. Such a comparison would better support this contribution.

- **Benchmark too simple** (R4): GPT-4o achieves 75% accuracy on the “hard” type and ~90% on “easy” type, which is too high compared to other benchmarks (e.g., GPT-4o only 54% on MEGA-Bench). This suggests the difficulty levels may not be well calibrated.

- **Hierarchy seems random** (R2): The hierarchical structure of evaluation aspects appears arbitrary: “emotional and psychological” appears under “semantic understanding” while “emotional cues” appears under “atmospheric understanding”; this hints at problems with the automatic generation of the hierarchy using GPT-4, which could have been done manually for only a few aspects.
