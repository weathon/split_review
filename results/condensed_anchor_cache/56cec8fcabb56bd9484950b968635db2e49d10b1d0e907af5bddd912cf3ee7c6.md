## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes COLA (COunterfactuals with Limited Actions), a post-hoc refinement method to minimize feature changes in counterfactual explanations (CE) while preserving validity. COLA first generates a candidate counterfactual \(r\) via any CE algorithm, then uses optimal transport (OT) to recover the joint distribution \(\text{Prob}(x,r)\) between the factual \(x\) and the candidate \(r\). This joint distribution is used to compute a new Shapley-based feature attribution (p-SHAP) which scores feature importance by incorporating both the factual and counterfactual distributions. Finally, COLA edits the factual \(x\) by retaining only a minimal set of highly attributed features from \(r\). The method is demonstrated across 4 datasets, 12 classifiers, and 5 CE variants, showing sparser valid counterfactuals. A counterintuitive finding is that exact alignment between factual and candidate counterfactual can be suboptimal.

### Strengths

- Addresses a practically important problem: making counterfactual explanations actionable by minimizing unnecessary feature changes, with connections to real-world AI regulation demands. (R1, R2, R4)
- Provides a detailed theoretical framework with clear mathematical exposition, including a generic p-SHAP that nests other Shapley formulations (RB-SHAP, B-SHAP, CF-SHAP) and explicit theorems linking OT and Lipschitz continuity to counterfactual validity (Theorem 4.1) and causal behavior of p-SHAP (Theorem 4.2). (R1, R3)
- Method is model-agnostic and algorithm-agnostic; it works with any CE generation method and any differentiable or black-box model. (R1)
- Clear algorithm (COLA) with derived computational complexity, aiding scalability understanding. (R1)
- Empirical evaluation is robust and diverse: tested across 4 datasets, 12 classifier models, 5 CE variants, and 4 divergence functions (MSE, MAE, Hamming, cross-entropy). Results show COLA achieves sparser counterfactuals than its own variants with minimal or zero misalignment. (R1, R2, R4)
- Code is shared, making the approach accessible and transparent. (R4)
- The counterintuitive finding that exact alignment (using the CE generation mechanism to define the pairing) can be misleading is interesting and novel. (R4)
- The paper identifies and solves a problem not previously reported in the literature. (R2)

### Weaknesses

- **Optimality and connection between components (pressing – R1, R3):**  
  COLA’s final step does not explicitly minimize an objective function; it is unclear why the chosen \(z\) is optimal or nearer optimal for Equation 1. The link between p-SHAP feature importance scores and minimization of feature changes is not clearly established. (R1)  
  One reviewer (R3) considers the experiments too weak to support claims of near-optimal performance; others (R1, R2, R4) find the empirical support sufficient, but the theoretical gap remains open.

- **Experimental evaluation (pressing – R3; moderate disagreement):**  
  - COLA requires a separate CE generator to produce many candidate counterfactuals for many datapoints to compute the joint distribution. This dependency and its cost are not included in the complexity analysis.  
  - Baseline comparisons are insufficient: only 5 variants of COLA itself are compared; established CE baselines (DiCE, KNN, etc.) mentioned in earlier sections are not evaluated. Furthermore, those baselines are outdated – newer methods like “Amortized Generation of Sequential Algorithmic Recourses” exist.  
  - In Result 1, sparsity for datapoints that do not achieve the desired target class is reported (third column of Table 3), but invalid counterfactuals are not useful; this metric is irrelevant.  
  - The paper implicitly assumes sparsity is the most important metric, but the community recognizes multiple metrics (validity, proximity, manifold distance, causal constraints, generation time). A comprehensive comparison across all these metrics with a wide set of baselines is needed.  
  *Other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) found the experiments robust and diverse; R1 noted 4 datasets, 12 classifiers, etc. as a strength, and R2 called them “exhaustive”.*

- **Clarity of the p-SHAP–OT connection (pressing – R1, R2, R3):**  
  - Why OT’s cost-effective distribution alignment directly aids minimal feature changes is not sufficiently highlighted.  
  - It is not obvious why the joint distribution derived from OT (rather than using the exact pairing from the CE generator) is beneficial; the paper should explain why this avoids issues with the specific CE algorithm.  
  - Theorem 4.1 is labeled “p-SHAP towards CE” but the result is about OT + Lipschitz continuity, and its direct relevance to p-SHAP is not explicit.  
  - The trade-offs for using counterfactual distributions as background in CF-SHAP (compared to training data in B-SHAP) are not explained. (R3)  
  - R2 asks: why would SHAP feature attributions be a good method for post-hoc optimization of counterfactuals at all? Any intuition or comparison to non-SHAP post-hoc methods would help.

- **Writing clarity and terminology (R1, R2, R3):**  
  Key terms used repeatedly are not defined at first occurrence: “exact alignment” (line 25, 197; defined only at line 308), “well-performed action plans” (line 98), “decoupling” phenomenon (line 85–86, reference lost). The “counterintuitive finding” (lines 101–105) is hard to parse before reading the whole paper. (R2)  
  Adding transitional sentences at the beginning of Section 3 and end of Section 2 would help situate the reader. (R1)  
  Typos: line 196/197 “RB-SHAP” should be “B-SHAP” (R1, R3); line 474 “aligment” misspelled (R1).  
  Line 124: notation \(M_{c_{ik}}\) is not defined, making the constraints 1(d)–1(e) hard to interpret. (R3)  
  Line 51: statement “some CE algorithms assume differentiable models… others for tree-based” is misleading – many CE algorithms work for black-box models (see Verma et al. survey). (R3)  
  Line 79: the three “challenges” are not compelling; the first (no single CE algorithm suffices) is not true, the second (model agnosticism) is already addressed, the third (FA can be misleading) is obvious. (R3)  
  Section 4 first paragraph is overly verbose as it simply generalizes previous notations. (R3)

- **Scalability and generalization (R4):**  
  OT computation is computationally demanding (acknowledged by authors), potentially limiting large-scale use.  
  OT lacks task-specific tuning or causal constraints (noted as future work).  
  Evaluation is limited to binary classification and tabular data; effectiveness on multiclass, regression, images, text, and other data types is unexplored.  
  R2 similarly questions the absence of non-SHAP baselines.

- **Minor presentation issues:**  
  Figures 3 and 4 lack x‑axis titles (R1).  
  Result III (line 472) needs an entry point explaining its importance, and an insight why CF‑\(P_{OT}\) outperforms CF‑\(P_{Ect}\) (R1).
