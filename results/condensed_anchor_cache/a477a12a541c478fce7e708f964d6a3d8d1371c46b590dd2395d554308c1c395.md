## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Deep Bootstrap Aggregation (DBA), a method that replaces plain arithmetic averaging of predictions from bagged regression models with a linear combination whose coefficients are determined using the inverse of the covariance matrix of the models’ predictions. To prevent overfitting and numerical instability when inverting the covariance matrix, a multi-level (“deep”) variant (M-GLSA, with a two-stage version 2-GLSA) is introduced. The approach is evaluated on five synthetic regression datasets (using random forests) and two real-world datasets, showing lower estimated MSE compared to plain averaging / Ordinary Least Squares Aggregation (OLSA). However, observed improvements are small, overlap in confidence intervals is heavy, and statistical significance is only evident for one dataset with a particular configuration. An additional subsampling experiment is also presented. The paper provides a theoretical proof that Generalized Least Squares Aggregation (GLSA) yields lower MSE than OLSA.

### Strengths
- The idea of weighting ensemble members using a covariance-based linear combination is interesting and relevant both practically and theoretically.
- The theoretical proof that GLSA can lower MSE compared to OLSA is a valuable contribution.
- The presentation is generally clear, well-structured, and easy to read (except section 6.2 which is abstract). Visual aids (charts, tables) enhance accessibility.
- Confidence intervals are provided for performance estimates, which is good practice.
- The “deep” approach (multi-stage GLSA) appears to be novel.
- The method could be a building block for other areas such as stochastic gradient descent.
- The paper tackles a key limitation of traditional bootstrap aggregation (instability and robustness) and proposes a concrete technique.

### Weaknesses
- **Fundamental misunderstanding (Reviewer 4):** The paper states that we want ensemble members to be nearly uncorrelated (e.g., “trees in a random forest are nearly uncorrelated,” line 034). For successful ensembling, we want *errors* to be uncorrelated, not necessarily outputs. Models agreeing on correct predictions (correlated outputs) is fine; it is error correlation that matters (Eckhardt & Lee, 1985). The derived algorithm does not take the accuracy (bias) of ensemble members into account, only output covariance. This undermines the theoretical foundation.
- **Marginal and inconsistent improvements:** Across all reviewers, gains reported are small, confidence intervals heavily overlap, and statistical significance is only observed for one dataset (Concrete) with one configuration (2-GLSA). On many synthetic functions the improvements are marginal, and on the Friedman3 function with 20 and 100 trees OLSA actually outperforms the proposed method (Reviewer 1). The paper claims “2-GLSA consistently outperforms OLSA” but Table 2 shows no clear statistical significance for functions 2–5, and on the last task the baseline is better (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient literature embedding:**  
  - The paper does not discuss stacked generalization (Wolpert, 1992), which is the standard approach for combining models via linear (or non-linear) weighting.  
  - Rao & Tibshirani (1997) on out-of-bootstrap model averaging is directly relevant and omitted.  
  - Modern PAC-Bayesian methods for weighting ensembles (e.g., Masegosa et al., NeurIPS 2020) are not mentioned.  
  - Other ensemble approaches like XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, and gradient boosting are not compared, making it unclear how DBA stands relative to current state-of-the-art.
- **Small evaluation scope:** Only five synthetic datasets (based on Friedman functions) and two real-world datasets are used. Many more public tabular regression problems exist (Reviewer 2). Testing on high-dimensional, time series, computer vision, or NLP data is missing (Reviewer 1). The subsampling experiment (section 6.2) is abstract, lacks strong comparison, and improvement over OLSA is marginal (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of theoretical depth for multi-stage GLSA:**  
  - Why does multi-stage aggregation alleviate numerical instability? No justification is provided (Reviewer 3).  
  - Theoretical properties for high-dimensional settings (convergence, error bounds, computational complexity of matrix inversion) are not discussed (Reviewer 1).  
  - The formalism in section 2.4 is unclear; it does not treat the bias of ensemble members (the optimal μ is not necessarily the truth), and the claim that the last equation on page 2 gives the OLS is misleading without specifying the objective (Reviewer 4).
- **“Deep” network terminology is misleading:** A depth of 2 layers (as in 2-GLSA) is typically not considered “deep.” The authors should justify why deeper architectures are not beneficial, either theoretically or empirically, and discuss trade-offs (Reviewer 1).
- **Problems with experimental evaluation:**  
  - The results on page 3 (correlation of outputs) are not novel or interesting; correlations among outputs are expected (Reviewer 4).  
  - Reviewer 4 reproduced the Concrete dataset experiment and obtained an MSE of 23.60 with a standard Random Forest (1000 trees), which is much better than all methods reported in Table 3 (OLSA, GLSA, 2-GLSA). This suggests a flaw in the authors’ experimental setup.  
  - It is unclear whether results in Table 2 are statistically significant (Reviewer 4). Even if 2-GLSA is “better” in some cases, effect sizes are very small.
- **Missing sensitivity analysis / hyperparameter discussion:** The impact of number of bootstrap samples, base learner architecture, and GLSA parameters (e.g., regularization) is not studied. A sensitivity analysis would guide practitioners (Reviewer 1).
- **Typographical errors:** e.g., line 462. The manuscript needs proofreading (Reviewer 1).
