## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces TICL (Test-time Interventional Causal Learning), a supervised causal discovery method for settings with multiple interventional datasets where intervention targets are unknown. TICL follows a three-step approach: (1) converting interventional data into an augmented observational dataset via the Joint Causal Inference (JCI) framework, (2) generating semi-synthetic training data by sampling graphs from a posterior using the IS-MCMC algorithm and then forward-sampling compatible interventional datasets, and (3) training a supervised PC algorithm (classifiers for edge existence and v-structure orientation) on this self-augmented data to predict the causal graph. Experiments on bnlearn benchmarks (Asia, Insurance, Alarm, Hailfinder, Pathfinder, etc.) compare TICL against various baselines and report state-of-the-art F1-scores for causal discovery and intervention target detection.

### Strengths

- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (Reviewer 4). The terminology and notation are consistent, and necessary background on JCI and SCL is provided (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The proposed combination of JCI and SCL is intuitive and clever, replacing rule-based logic of the PC algorithm with a learning-based approach (Reviewer 1).
- TICL introduces self-augmentation at test time, enabling the model to adapt to test distribution biases (Reviewers 1, 3). The IS-MCMC algorithm effectively samples graphs from the posterior and generates compatible data for training (Reviewer 3).
- Extensive experiments are conducted across multiple bnlearn datasets, with comparisons against a solid range of competitive methods in several categories (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). TICL achieves significant average F1-score improvements over prior methods in both causal discovery and intervention target detection (Reviewer 3).
- The work highlights JCI as a promising direction for interventional causal discovery and offers flexibility with fewer assumptions (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

**Clarity and Presentation**
- The “When” section in the introduction is confusing; the term “test data” is used in a non-standard way, making the motivation unclear until reading the full paper (Reviewer 1).
- The organization around Figure 2 (Section 3.1) is confusing because it introduces JCI terms like “intervention family” before they are defined in Section 3.2. Moving the final paragraph of 3.1 after 3.2 would help (Reviewer 1).
- Figure 2’s caption should explain what the numbers in curly brackets refer to and why e.g., $X_2 \to X_1 \leftarrow \{1\}$ treats $\{1\}$ as a node (Reviewer 2).
- Key assumptions (Markovian, faithfulness, sufficiency) are not explicitly defined (Reviewer 2). I-CPDAG is discussed but not explained in detail (Reviewer 2).
- The description of “neighborhood” (when $D_i$ is said to be in the neighborhood of the augmented dataset) and “high-quality training data” are not made precise (Reviewer 2).
- Algorithm 1’s loop structure is unclear: the parallelization loop index $i$ is unused, and it is not specified where $t$ is initialized nor how the iterative convergence to IS-MCMC works (e.g., burn-in, listing all $D^{(t)}$ or keeping only final) (Reviewer 1).
- The numbers for key steps (1–6) are not mapped onto Figure 3, hindering understanding (Reviewer 2).
- Table 1’s caption should include the meaning of bold/blue and underlined/red, and the footnote reference should be on the table rather than in the text (Reviewer 1). The light background colors for baseline categories are indistinguishable in black-and-white and too similar in color (Reviewer 1). Similarly, Figure 4’s legend should show both colors and symbols (Reviewer 1).

**Focus on Contribution**
- The paper attempts to cover many contributions (I-CPDAG discovery, intervention target detection, test-time adaptation, PC+SCL, prior knowledge) making it hard to identify the main contribution (Reviewer 2).
- While Reviewers 1 and 3 view the combination of JCI and SCL as novel, Reviewer 4 argues that the approach is a trivial combination of existing methods: the first step (pooling interventional data) is from JCI (Mooij et al., 2020) and the second step (generating simulated training data via a proxy algorithm and MLE) is from ML4S (Ma et al., 2022). The novelty is thus limited (Reviewer 4).
- Test-time adaptation, claimed as a core motivation, is discussed very little; the paper focuses more on the JCI+SCL paradigm than on the adaptation mechanism (Reviewer 2).

**Theoretical and Methodological Issues**
- The IS-MCMC algorithm lacks theoretical justification: it is claimed to sample from the posterior $P(G|D)$, but there is no analysis of how the stationary distribution matches the posterior, nor discussion of the Metropolis-Hastings proposal design, ergodicity, convergence, mixing time, or criteria for convergence (Reviewer 3). A more rigorous analysis is needed (Reviewer 3, also implied by Reviewer 1’s question about the loop).
- The supervised PC algorithm (Step 3) is problematic: after already testing conditional independence from data in the featurization step (Section 4.1), using a separate classifier to determine v-structures instead of relying on theoretical orientation rules adds computation and loses the identification guarantees that rule-based orientation provides (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 3 questions the empirical evidence for the claim that a binary classifier “replaces heuristic searches and potentially erroneous CI tests with a robust classification mechanism” – is there experimental validation of this benefit?
- Training a classifier for each possible triplet to detect v-structures would be computationally very inefficient. How is this better than using the original PC algorithm? The training procedure needs more detailed discussion (Reviewer 2).

**Experimental Claims and Rigor**
- The paper claims testing on “real causal graph datasets with over 100 nodes,” but among the 14 bnlearn datasets used, only Pathfinder has >100 nodes (104), and several (Asia, Insurance, Alarm, Hailfinder) are synthetic, not real-world. The range of nodes and realism should be more clearly described (Reviewers 1, 2 – Reviewer 2 specifically notes Asia is not real).
- Calling AVICI and ENCO “unfair baselines” because they are given intervention targets is misleading; TICL also has a structural advantage from JCI-based data conversion. It is better to report the information difference without labeling baselines as unfair (Reviewer 1).
- A real-world motivating example would help demonstrate applicability; the setting of “observational and interventional data with unknown targets” currently feels niche (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 3 asks about the choice of 10,000 samples per observational/interventional setting in Section 5.1: could this lead to overfitting if too large, and what guides the choice?
- Reviewer 1 asks whether the authors tested hard interventions in addition to soft interventions and whether results were similar.

**Missing Comparisons and Ablations**
- Did the authors try generating $D^{(t)}$ from Algorithm 1 and then running standard PC (instead of the custom supervised PC)? This comparison would help isolate the benefit of the supervised component (Reviewer 1).
- Reviewer 2 questions how observational and interventional datasets are generated from bnlearn graphs (which are observational)—details are lacking.
- The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance rule: if a candidate graph $G^{\text{cand}}$ has lower score than $G^{(t-1)}$, but $\frac{\text{score}(G^{\text{cand}})}{\text{score}(G^{(t-1)})}=0.9$, does this mean it is accepted with probability 0.9? Would this gradually lead toward incorrect graphs? (Reviewer 2)
