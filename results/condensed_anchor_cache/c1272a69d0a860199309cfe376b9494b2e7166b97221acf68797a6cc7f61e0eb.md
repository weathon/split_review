## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes EXAGREE, a framework to reduce "explanation disagreement" across models and explanation methods by finding a model from a Rashomon set (Stakeholder-Aligned Explanation Model, SAEM) that produces feature attribution rankings matching stakeholder-specified preferences. The framework involves Rashomon set sampling, a differentiable mask-based attribution network (DMAN), and multi-head architecture with sorting networks. The authors formalize four disagreement types (stakeholder, model, explanation method, ground truth) and evaluate on 6 OpenXAI datasets using logistic regression (LR) and artificial neural network (ANN) models, reporting faithfulness metrics (FA, RA, RC, SA, SRA, PRA) and fairness measures.

### Strengths

- The problem of explanation disagreement is critical and timely, and the paper clearly disentangles four disagreement scenarios (stakeholder, model, explanation method, ground truth) (R1, R4).
- The technical framework integrating Rashomon sets, end-to-end differentiable optimization, DMAN, sorting networks, and multi-head architecture is interesting and creative (R1, R2).
- The approach is model-agnostic and presents a novel method to find models whose explanations match an arbitrary ideal, which could be a meaningful contribution (R3).
- The insight to make the process end-to-end differentiable is both creative and practically useful (R1).
- The appendix includes a sensitivity analysis on the choice of ε for the Rashomon set, valuable methodological analysis (R1).
- Empirical evaluation covers a variety of settings: 6 OpenXAI datasets (synthetic and real-world), two pre-trained models (logistic regression and artificial neural networks) (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear and unrefined presentation.** Figures 1 and 2 are confusing and do not aid understanding; symbols (curved arrows, lightbulb, check marks, question marks) are unexplained and the captions insufficient. Several reviewers found the paper difficult to follow and lacking in clarity (R1, R2, R3, R4). The paper appears unrefined (R2, R3, R4).
- **Lack of precise definitions for core concepts.** Key terms are not rigorously defined, including: “attribution set”, “model representations”, “model characterizations”, “masks” (whether binary or continuous, dimensionality, relationship to model parameters), “explanation model” (used inconsistently), and the specific mathematical form of losses L_sparsity and L_diversity (R1, R3, R4). Equation 4’s loss between f_DMAN and {masks, attributions} is abstract—what is the precise form? How are masks and attributions combined? (R1). “Mask” is used without definition in line 256 (R4). “Model characterizations” in DMAN input are unclear (R1).
- **Problematic formalization of explanation disagreement.**  
  *Model disagreement*: It is not clear why different models with similar predictions should have the same explanations—differing explanations may legitimately reveal essential model behaviors (R3, R4). There is no “model disagreement” problem; the concept lacks motivation (R3).  
  *Stakeholder disagreement*: Why would stakeholders want different rankings? The example of a data scientist wanting statistical significance vs. a domain expert valuing different features does not justify providing potentially fake explanations to meet their expectations. Should a technique optimize to suit stakeholder demands if those demands are not aligned with reality? (R4).  
  *Ground truth disagreement*: It is obvious that post-hoc explanations of interpretable models can conflict, but why would one use post-hoc explanations for an interpretable model at all? (R4).
- **Weak connection between the problem and the solution.** The paper claims to address “explanation disagreement,” but EXAGREE primarily selects models whose post-hoc explanations match stakeholder preferences—this is closer to a model-selection tool or even an adversarial attack/explanation fairwashing (R2, R3).  
  - The approach assumes post-hoc explanations are faithful to model behavior, which cannot be taken for granted (Adebayo et al. 2019) (R2).  
  - It does not address case 2 (different explanation methods for one model); it focuses only on case 1 (different models with same performance) (R2).  
  - The connection to adversarial manipulations of explanations (Slack et al. 2020, Aivodji et al. 2019, Goethals et al. 2023) is not discussed; SAEM may be viewed as an adversarial attack that maintains predictions but modifies explanations (R3).  
  - The paper should reframe as “adversarial manipulation of explanations” rather than “explanation agreement” (R3).
- **Unsubstantiated assumption that higher agreement implies greater faithfulness.** The claim that “faithfulness is proportional to agreement” (row 196) is not justified—is it a theoretical result (requires proof) or empirical (requires details)? Bad actors could use this to hide discriminatory features (R1, R2). The metrics rely on an unsubstantiated assumption that agreement ⇒ faithfulness (R2).
- **Questionable experimental design and metrics.**  
  - Using local explanation methods (LIME, Integrated Gradients) averaged across all instances to obtain global attributions is inappropriate; these methods are not designed for global explanations. SHAP’s global attribution uses average of absolute values, not raw signed averages (R2).  
  - Using LR coefficients as “ground truth” explanations and then attempting to make ANN explanations agree with them is flawed. The original OpenXAI benchmark does not provide ground truth for ANNs; the paper appears to compute faithfulness metrics (FA, RA, SRA, etc.) for ANN against LR coefficients (R2, R3, R4). This is effectively aligning ANN explanations with LR coefficients, which could be seen as an adversarial attack or explanation fairwashing (R3, R4).  
  - Framing explanation methods as “stakeholders” (line 463) is confusing—it is hard to imagine a stakeholder wanting rankings “like LIME” (R2).  
  - The six faithfulness metrics (FA, RA, RC, SA, SRA, PRA) are likely highly correlated, especially when considering signed attributions; the paper should measure and report correlation. If they are highly correlated, they effectively constitute one metric (R4).  
  - Parameter k (mentioned as 0.25 in Table 1 caption) is never defined in Section 2.1 or elsewhere (R4). Hyperparameters like l and k are not defined (R4). ψ’s fixity in optimization is unclear (R2).  
  - SAEM is bolded in Tables 1 and 2 as if it is the best method, but it does not achieve the best metrics for LR models—this is misleading (R4). Why should SAEM be the best for LR if stakeholder expectations may not align? The paper needs to clarify (R4).  
  - How is FIS_LR computed? If it is feature importance from LR coefficients, it should achieve 100% agreement and be best 8 times, but it is best 0 times in Table 1 (R4).  
  - The paper does not specify the value of k used in experiments beyond the table caption (R2).  
  - There is no discussion on whether fairness improvement is an explicit objective or an empirical observation; if not explicit, the paper should not overstate fairness results (R1).  
  - The “Ranking Supervision and Correlation Metric” (row 276) motivation is unclear—why Spearman over other metrics? (R1).
- **Unclear role of the SAEM pipeline.** If one can directly compute explanations for models in the Rashomon set and select the one with highest agreement with stakeholders, why is the complex pipeline (DMAN, sorting networks, etc.) needed? The pipeline appears to predict which model will have highest agreement, but the authors say they have ground truth (lines 268-269) (R4).
- **Presentation and writing issues.**  
  - Mistakes in formulation: In Equation 1, M* is not defined; a_1,…,a_p are not defined as features; the inequality sign should likely be ≥ instead of ≤ (loss of other models higher than M*); and ε is relative to L(M*) when an absolute threshold is more common (R4).  
  - “Evaluation Matrices” should be “Evaluation Metrics” (R2).  
  - Typo: “Rashomon” misspelled in row 480 (R1).  
  - Confusion: line 340 says “two pre-trained models” but immediately lists three model types (R1).  
  - The remarks should be paragraphs, not bullet points (R2).  
  - Table 1 last row lists “decision trees” as an explanation method—likely it should be a model class; the explanation method is missing (R3).  
  - Notation: f_diffsort should be formatted as f_{\text{diffsort}} for readability (R1).  

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** While reviewers 1 and 3 found the scientific contribution interesting and valid, reviewers 2 and 4 rated soundness and contribution as low (1), citing fundamental flaws in the problem formulation, experimental design, and ethical implications. Specifically, reviewer 3 acknowledges the method is good but criticizes the framing as an adversarial attack, while reviewers 2 and 4 argue the approach does not address explanation disagreement as claimed and uses inappropriate metrics and baselines.
