## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper formalizes a parallelism score (PS) from neuroscience as a proxy for compositional generalization (CG) in deep neural networks. It decomposes the network into a featurizer and classifier, then proposes GeoCon: maximizing PS on the featurizer and minimizing distance variance (DV) on the classifier to improve CG. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets (PACS, NICO, Office-Home) show a strong positive correlation between PS and CG, but also high variance at low CG ability. GeoCon reduces failure cases and improves CG across class and domain, though CG class and CG domain are negatively correlated. Multimodal (CLIP) and self-supervised (DINO) models exhibit good CG. Ablations confirm the importance of both regularizations.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow.
- Formal description of parallelism score from neuroscience (Bernardi et al. 2020) is provided.
- The idea of introducing a neuroscience concept to deep learning generalization is interesting.
- Parallelism score is theoretically sound, well-explained, and can be optimized directly (unlike most disentanglement measures).
- GeoCon combines two explicit regularization techniques: PS maximization and DV minimization.
- Very convincing experimental results: strong correlation between PS and CG across multiple datasets, clear improvement in CG from the proposed loss terms.
- Effectiveness is demonstrated on multiple real-world datasets, with ablation studies showing the importance of both regularization terms.
- Evaluates multiple pretrained models including DINO and CLIP.

### Weaknesses
- The GeoCon method seems to require a similar number of classes for each concept; scaling to more than two concepts is unclear.
- Experiments are limited to datasets with only two concepts (class and domain); scaling to more realistic tasks (e.g., visual question answering, compositional visual reasoning like COLA benchmark) is unexplored.
- The paper does not compare against the most closely related prior work: Ito (NeurIPS 2022), which also applies geometric constraints for CG. The proposed method appears incremental (adding more regularization terms). Ito 2022 should be included as a baseline, especially as it discusses generalization capability (Sec 3.5).
- Unclear how the method advances state-of-the-art beyond existing statistical approaches to invariant feature learning (e.g., domain generalization methods) that achieve similar results.
- The evaluation focuses only on accuracy for domain generalization; benefits for other generalization tasks (zero-shot, explainable AI) are not shown.
- The relation of the parallelism score to the neuroscience measure in Bernardi et al. (2020) is not clearly explained (e.g., how cosine similarity of activation vectors maps to the original measure).
- More dataset details needed: examples of object types for ‘class’ and values for ‘domain’ in PACS, NICO, Office-Home.
- The paper shows CG class and CG domain are negatively correlated, but does not discuss how getting both correct correlates with PS, nor plots "CG both" in Figure 6. This alternative metric would be helpful.
- Section 3.2, point (iv) is confusing: with only two concepts, what does "CG across multiple concepts" mean?
- Line 364: "we can also employ an exponential smoothing method" – it is unclear whether this is actually used in the experiments.
- The approximation to PS requires diversity in the batch; the batch size used is not specified.
- The CG measurement approach is very similar to Xu et al. (2022) and Mahon et al. (2024); this connection is not discussed.
- Line 050 claims there is "no evidence that explicitly decoupling input compositional factors substantially improves generalization" – this is too strong; several works (Higgins et al. 2016, Esmaeli et al. 2019, Mahon et al. 2024) report evidence linking disentanglement and CG. Measuring disentanglement in embeddings (e.g., from frozen models in Sec 3.2) and correlating with PS and CG would strengthen the paper.
- Minor issues: typos (“Featurizer" → "The featurizer"; "the linear function" → "a linear function"); Eq (6)-(8) notation for arguments; Eq (10) ambiguity of \(L_a\) and \(L_B\); unclear if 'accuracy' in Thm. 1 refers to training set accuracy.
- Questions about baselines: more details needed on linear probing and finetuning; in Figure 8, why are PS and DV not similar across models at epoch 0 (post-initialization)?; how many samples are needed to compute PS reliably?; is the method limited to classification?
