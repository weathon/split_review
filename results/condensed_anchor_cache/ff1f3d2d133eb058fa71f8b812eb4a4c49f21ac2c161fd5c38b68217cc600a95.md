## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes that softmax attention succeeds due to implicit Frobenius norm regularization of the attention matrix, and introduces scaled polynomial activations (x and x³) as alternatives that also regularize this norm. Experiments on vision (ImageNet, COCO) and the Long Range Arena benchmark are presented. Reviewer opinions are sharply split: two reviewers (R1, R3, scores 6) find the theoretical analysis novel and well-supported with promising experimental results; two reviewers (R2, R4, scores 3) consider the theoretical assumptions unrealistic, the link between theory and experiments weak, and the experimental validation insufficient.

### Strengths
- Provides an in‑depth theoretical exploration of softmax’s role via implicit Frobenius norm regularization of the attention matrix and its Jacobian, with detailed proofs and analysis of scaled‑polynomial activations. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Offers a novel perspective that challenges the conventional view that softmax’s probability‑distribution properties are essential; polynomial activations break non‑negativity, sum‑to‑one, and sparsity yet still work. (R1, R4)
- Clearly demonstrates that not scaling polynomial activations is detrimental to performance. (R4)
- Good writing and presentation; proofs and experiments are well‑structured and easy to follow. (R2, R3)
- Experiments cover multiple domains: vision (ImageNet classification, COCO object detection/segmentation) and the Long Range Arena (LRA) benchmark. (R1, R2, R3, R4) [Note: R2 points out that LRA includes non‑NLP tasks – see Weaknesses.]
- The proposed polynomial activation is novel and theoretically justified; results are comparable to or slightly better than softmax in several settings. (R1, R3) [R2 and R4 note that improvements are marginal – see Weaknesses.]

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical rigor (heavily criticized by R2, R4; R1 and R3 consider the theory sound):**
  - Theorem 4.1, Eq 4.2 proof (Appendix L719): The factor of 2 appears incorrect. Example with \(F_{i1}=1\) and all others 0 gives \(1-0=1\) but the equation yields \(2\) (R4).
  - Theorem 4.2 assumes \(X\), \(Q\), \(K\) are i.i.d., making \(A=XQ\) and \(B^\top=KX\) uncorrelated—an extremely strong assumption not applicable to real Transformers. In general, \(\text{Cov}(A,B)=QK^\top\) when \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (R4).
  - Theorem 4.2 and its corollaries claim the attention norm grows as \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})\), but this holds only if \(N\gg D\). For ViT‑Base, \(D=768\) and \(N=196\), so the condition is not satisfied. Parameter \(d\) introduced in A.1.2 is undefined (likely head dimension but unclear) (R4).
  - Small proof mistakes: repeated typo in \(e^{2a_{11}}\) and \(e^{2a_{NN}}\) on lines 665–669 (R2).
  - Typo “accuracr” in Appendix A.2.1 (R3).
- **Weak linkage between theory and experiments (R2, R4; R1 and R3 claim alignment):**
  - No theoretical analysis shows that the regularized norm correlates with performance. Experimental results are inconsistent: e.g., \((x^3/1)\) has lower attention norm than \((x/16)\) in Figs 2–3 but significantly worse performance in Table 1 (R2). In Table 2, ViT‑base/small \((x/14)\) underperform compared to \((x^3)\) and have higher norms (Figs 13–14), yet Deit‑base/small \((x/14)\) outperform others, with no norm analysis shown for Deit (R2). Most improvements are marginal (R2).
  - Corollaries 4.3 and 4.5 claim scaled polynomials can perform similarly to softmax, but the reasoning is unclear beyond both norms scaling with \(\sqrt{N}\) (R4).
  - Attention map visualizations (Figs 6–7) only show expected functional differences (softmax non‑negative vs. polynomial can be negative); no clear interpretation or connection to theory is provided (R4).
- **Missing experiments and ablation details (R1, R2, R3):**
  - No small‑scale language modeling experiments; focus is on ViT. The LRA benchmark is not purely NLP: 3 of 5 tasks are ListOps, Image Classification, and Pathfinder (R2).
  - Lacking details for NLP experiments: no model size, hyperparameters recipe, or task descriptions; only references an external work without specifying what is used (R3).
  - Only \(x\) and \(x^3\) polynomial activations are tested; no exploration of other exponents or degrees (R1).
  - No discussion of computational overhead or time complexity comparison between softmax and polynomial attention (R1).
  - For dynamic sequence length (e.g., language generation with causal masking), it is unclear whether the scaling should adapt per query during training/inference; this is not addressed (R3, R4).
  - Missing norm analysis for Deit models in Table 2 to match theory‑experiment correlation (R2).
- **Motivation disagreement:** One reviewer (R2) argues that softmax also causes gradient vanishing (citing [Richter & Wattenhofer, 2022] and [Escaping the gradient vanishing…]), so the claim that regularization is the crucial factor is unsupported. The paper’s stated aim is not SOTA but examining softmax, yet it fails to convincingly argue why regularization is the source of success and polynomial attention does not significantly improve over softmax (R2).
- **Missing related work:** The paper has overlap with arXiv:2409.04431, especially Section 3.2 on Regularity of Sigmoid Attention, which should be discussed (R3).
- **Typographical errors:** Line 255: \(\frac{1}{8}x^3\) should be \(\frac{1}{16}x^3\); same error in Table 1 caption (R1). “Accuracr” in Appendix A.2.1 (R3). Proof typo in \(e^{2a_{11}}\) and \(e^{2a_{NN}}\) (R2).
