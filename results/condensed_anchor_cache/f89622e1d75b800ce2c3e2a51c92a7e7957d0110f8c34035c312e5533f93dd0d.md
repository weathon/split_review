## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces scorable negotiation games as an evaluation benchmark for LLMs, testing reasoning and decision-making in multi-agent, multi-issue, text-based settings. Using zero-shot Chain-of-Thought prompting, the authors show that LLM agents can negotiate and reach deals, with GPT-4 outperforming earlier models. The benchmark includes diverse game configurations and adversarial variants (greedy, saboteur agents). The paper plans to release the toolkit publicly.

### Strengths
- **Practical relevance**: Negotiation is important in real-world applications (customer service, contracts, decision-making), and LLMs are increasingly used in such roles. The choice of evaluation task is well-motivated.
- **Adversarial settings and robustness analysis**: The study explores interactions in unbalanced adversarial setups (greedy, saboteur agents) and shows how agent behavior can be modulated to affect outcomes, providing insight into robustness in multi-agent scenarios (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Diverse benchmark and generation protocol**: The paper creates a testbed with multiple parties, issues, and varying priorities, plus an LLM-based generation process for new game instances. Reviewers found the benchmark interesting for evaluating LLM interaction capabilities (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Systematic evaluation and interesting analyses**: The empirical study compares GPT-3.5 vs GPT-4, uses various prompting styles, includes a Theory of Mind study, and varies game difficulties and agent types. The results shed light on LLM performance in complex decision-making settings (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Clear presentation**: The game description and writing are clear and well-structured (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Simplistic game setup compared to real-world negotiation**: The games use only a public communication channel, a small action set (support previous deals or make new proposals), and cannot handle private messages, alliances, natural language conversation, or conditional offers. This does not fully capture the richness of real-world negotiation strategies (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). One reviewer noted that the setup is simpler than the Diplomacy benchmark (Reviewer 3).
- **Restricted adversarial analysis**: The adversarial players are limited to valid negotiation actions. Other forms of attacks (e.g., adversarial suffixes) are not explored, so robustness against more sophisticated input-level attacks remains untested (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited generalizability from a single base game**: The testbed is built from one template/base game. Even with a few variants, it is unclear whether conclusions extend to negotiation tasks with different structures. Additional game variants (e.g., “All in – two greedy”, “Two out”) would strengthen claims about generalization (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited novelty**: The experimental protocol and foundational premise are similar to prior work (e.g., Ghandi et al. 2023b, which focused on strategic reasoning). The methodology lacks significant novelty (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). One reviewer rated the contribution as poor (Reviewer 4).
- **LLM value alignment biases interfere with role-specific rewards**: LLMs are trained on universally accepted “good” objectives (e.g., environmental conservation). When assigned a role with contrary objectives (e.g., “malevolent”), the model may resist the role’s reward mechanism, raising questions about the validity of evaluating capabilities in a game where these biases are present (Reviewer 4).
- **Abductive nature of CoT prompting**: Chain-of-Thought prompting is abductive; employing it to improve reasoning in this setting is considered unusual and not well-justified (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing distinction from SocraSynth**: The paper does not clearly articulate how its negotiation-game evaluation differs from SocraSynth (which focuses on knowledge synthesis and reasoning via LLM interaction) in purposes, techniques, and applications (Reviewer 1).
- **No path to fine-tuning for real-world use**: The paper acknowledges fine-tuning may be needed for practical applications but does not provide a clear path or discuss available datasets for complex negotiation scenarios (Reviewer 1). One reviewer asked whether the authors had tried fine-tuning an LLM to align its actions with role rewards in these games (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing analyses and ablations**:
  - The paper gives only high-level insights into the required skills (arithmetic, inference, exploration, planning); it does not examine combinations of skills or diagnose which skills were missing in unsuccessful negotiation instances (Reviewer 2).
  - Mixed populations (e.g., some agents GPT-4, some GPT-3.5) are not studied; such setups would provide additional insights into agent dynamics (Reviewer 2).
  - The parameter choices (number of agents, options, etc.) are not explained, and it is unclear whether qualitative differences arise from varying these parameters (Reviewer 2).
  - It is unknown whether a feasible deal is guaranteed for any combination of BATNA values (Reviewer 3).
  - The paper does not benchmark negotiation outcomes against rational-agent baselines or game-theoretic solutions (e.g., Nash equilibrium, correlated equilibrium) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
  - It is unclear whether agents are aware that another agent is selfish/adversarial in the “All in – one greedy” / “One out” variants (Reviewer 2).
