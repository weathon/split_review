## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents a theoretical framework that formulates a drug-drug interaction (DDI)-aware optimal transport problem as a geodesic equation on an infinite-dimensional Finsler manifold, aiming to unify domain adaptation and DDI prediction. The framework is applied to DDI prediction using Graph Neural Networks (GIN, GraphSAGE, GAT, GCN) with a claim of out-performing baselines on a custom benchmark.

### Strengths
- The results section shows the proposed methods out-compete others (Reviewer 1).
- The paper formulates interesting theorems that in an abstract way generalize drug interactions (Reviewer 1).
- The approach systematically improves DDI prediction across multiple GNN architectures (GIN, GraphSAGE, GAT, GCN), suggesting robustness and flexibility for graph-based learning in biomedical settings (Reviewer 2).
- By embedding DDI constraints into the optimal transport cost function, the paper proposes a fresh approach that unites domain adaptation with interaction-aware learning (Reviewer 2).
- The DDI prediction problem is difficult and important (key to combination therapies) and is motivated well in the introduction (Reviewer 3).
- The introduced methods and theory in Section 2 seem rigorous, interesting, and potentially exciting (Reviewer 3).
- This work introduces domain adaptation theory for the DDI problem, providing a new perspective to handle that problem (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- The connection between the theory and the implementation is completely lacking; it is difficult to understand how to go from the theory to the implementation and thus to verify correctness. One reviewer (R1) found this relation “completely lacking”, while others (R3, R4) also noted the relationship between neural learning/inference tasks and the theory remains unclear (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- No detailed proofs are provided in the paper or appendix. The authors state “the full proof requires careful analysis…” but then fail to give the proof. Multiple reviewers (R1, R4) noted that key proofs are simplified or omitted; Reviewer 4 specifically notes “we show” and “we prove” are used without detailed justification (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The input features for each drug are not clearly explained (e.g., whether SMILES strings are converted into graphs as Figure 1 might suggest). Attempts to run the code were unsuccessful due to missing preprocessing scripts, preventing direct verification (Reviewer 2).
- The machine learning setup is inadequately explained: Figure 1 has not been explained at all, the learning and inference methods are severely underdescribed, and the task of DDI prediction is not clearly defined (Reviewer 3). The experiment part is too simple, and characters in figures are too small (Reviewer 4).
- The method is not compared against existing DDI prediction methods (e.g., those surveyed in a recent review: https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/24/4/bbad235/7217116). The authors use a custom benchmark with random negatives or TWOSIDES but do not contrast with state-of-the-art baselines (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The results combine the effects of domain adaptation and supervised contrastive learning without a separate ablation; isolating each component’s impact is needed to understand individual contributions (Reviewer 2).
- Quantum information theory is mentioned in the introduction but never explained or connected to the formulation or theory later (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The abstract and introduction contain subjective language (“cutting-edge”, “revolutionary”, “far-reaching implications”) that detracts from rigor; these claims are not verified in the paper (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The experiment results do not show how they are related to “domain adaptation”; theoretical properties from Section 2 are not verified in the experiment part (Reviewer 4).
- Paper structure fault: “DATASET” should be a subset of “EXPERIMENT”, but “EXPERIMENT” is placed in section 2.1 and “DATASET” in section 2.2, which is confusing (Reviewer 4).
