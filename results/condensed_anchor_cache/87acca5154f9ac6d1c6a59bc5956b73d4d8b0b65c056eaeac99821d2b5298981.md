## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses the inefficiency of homomorphic encryption (HE) for transformer architectures by proposing modifications that make LLMs more HE-friendly for personalized fine-tuned inference. It applies LoRA for fine-tuning to reduce the number of encrypted parameters and replaces Softmax with a Gaussian kernel to avoid costly non‑polynomial operations. Experimental results show computational speedups of 6.94× for fine-tuning and 2.3× for inference while maintaining performance comparable to plaintext models. The work focuses on the fine-tuning stage and attention layers, and is evaluated on BERT‑based models.

### Strengths
- The problem is well‑motivated: privacy‑preserving LLMs under HE are a difficult and timely task, and any advance toward viable efficiency is welcome (R1, R2, R3).
- The non‑interactive property of HE (no extra communication like MPC) is a key advantage for large‑scale models (R2).
- The focus on the fine‑tuning stage combined with LoRA is a strong point, as it minimizes the number of homomorphic operations and reduces dimensions in matrix multiplications (R1, R2).
- Replacing Softmax with a Gaussian kernel is shown to be simpler to evaluate under HE, leading to significant computational savings with little accuracy loss; this observation (combining LoRA and Gaussian kernels for FHE) has not been explored in this depth before in the existing literature (R1; note: an arxiv paper published after the submission deadline also touches on this – https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.00433).
- The paper provides a thorough set of experiments covering both speed and model performance, with well‑analyzed results that demonstrate clear advantages in terms of computational speed and comparable model quality (R3, R1).
- The writing follows a clear bottleneck‑improvement pattern for LoRA and the Gaussian kernel, making the key ideas easy to follow (R2, R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Innovation and novelty concerns**: The approach largely combines existing FHE tools with existing ML optimizations (LoRA, Gaussian kernel). Some reviewers find the improvement factors insufficient to overcome the practical blockers for LLM+FHE, and the paper does not clearly distinguish its contributions from prior work (R1, R3). Specifically, the Gaussian kernel replacement is very similar to the method in Chen et al., “Skyformer: Remodel self‑attention with gaussian kernel and nyström method” (NeurIPS 2021), which is not cited or discussed (R3). It is also unclear whether Bottleneck 1 (LoRA) is just an application of existing methods without additional adaptation (R3).
- **Applicability to modern LLMs**: The paper evaluates only BERT‑based (encoder) models, while current state‑of‑the‑art LLMs predominantly use decoder‑based architectures (e.g., GPT). This limits the significance and generalizability of the proposed techniques (R2). It is unclear how the method would transfer to autoregressive settings or larger‑scale decoder models (R2).
- **Missing threat and adversary model**: The paper does not specify the adversary model (semi‑honest or malicious), the capabilities of attackers, or the types of attacks (e.g., member inference, model theft) that the architecture defends against. The statement “LLM weights are protected in the strict cryptographic sense (line 149)” is not adequately justified, and no discussion of side‑channel attacks or implementation‑specific vulnerabilities is provided (R2, R3).
- **Insufficient analysis of trade‑offs and limitations**: The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the trade‑offs between approximation accuracy of the Gaussian kernel and the computational overhead in FHE (R1). Limitations of LoRA under FHE—such as accumulation of approximation errors from iterative encrypted updates, or the impact of quantization on LoRA parameters—are not explored (R1). The effect of using LoRA and the Gaussian kernel on model interpretability is also not addressed (R3).
- **Inconsistency and unclear descriptions**: Section 2.1 appears inconsistent with Figure 1 (R3). The description of penalizing the model in a “pre‑training stage” (Section E) is unclear: does this require retraining the model’s weights or simply replacing non‑linearities with polynomials? Prior works usually keep pre‑trained weights unchanged; the paper’s proposal needs clarification (R1).
- **Vague future work and conclusions**: The future work section is too generic; it does not propose concrete directions such as exploring alternative HE schemes, addressing computational overhead, or investigating the impact of model size and complexity (R2). The conclusion should also discuss the limitations of the current work more explicitly (R2).
- **Reproducibility concerns**: The paper does not state whether the source code will be made public for validation (R1). Details on the polynomial approximations used (e.g., Chebyshev series, minimax) are not fully provided (R1). Many of the polynomial approximation techniques appear standard, without evidence of novel adaptation (R3).
- **Questions about cryptographic claim**: The paper claims that LLM weights are protected “in the strict cryptographic sense,” but this is not convincingly argued—cryptographic security under HE depends on the specific scheme (CKKS) and the threat model, which is not specified (R3).
