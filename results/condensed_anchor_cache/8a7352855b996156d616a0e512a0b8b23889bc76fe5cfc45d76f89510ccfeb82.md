## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the representation learning ability of denoising diffusion models (DDMs). Through a step-by-step deconstruction that gradually removes modern DDM components until the model resembles a classical denoising autoencoder (DAE), the authors identify which components are critical for self-supervised representation learning. Extensive ablations on DiT-L and ImageNet show that only a low-dimensional latent space (via tokenizer) is essential; many generation-oriented components (noise schedule, class conditioning, predicting noise vs. clean data) are nonessential. The resulting l-DAE (a latent DAE) achieves competitive linear-probe classification and object detection performance, though it does not surpass MAE or contrastive methods.

### Strengths
- The paper bridges modern diffusion models with classical DAEs through a novel deconstructive methodology, providing clear insights into representation learning components.
- Extensive and well-executed ablation studies covering loss terms, tokenizers, noise schedules, class conditioning, and prediction targets. The paper is well-written and the message is clear.
- Key findings are valuable: the low-dimensional latent space (dimensionality reduction via tokenizer) is the critical component; many generation-specific parts are nonessential; adding noise in latent space is more effective than in pixel space.
- First work to systematically identify which generative components contribute to representation quality (Reviewer 3). Interesting observations that high-level representation ability does not strictly correlate with generation quality (FID vs. accuracy).
- The proposed l-DAE is a simple yet effective representation learner that performs competitively on classification (and detection, as noted by Reviewer 4), reconnecting modern self-supervised learning with classical DAE principles.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited task evaluation.** Most results are on ImageNet linear probing (classification); object detection is included (Reviewer 4 mentions detection experiments), but other tasks that probe finer-grained representations (image retrieval, few-shot learning, segmentation, position prediction) are missing (Reviewers 1, 3). The claim that “self-supervised performance is not correlated with generation quality” is based only on coarse classification tasks; finer tasks may show correlation (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing exploration of generation–representation trade-off.** FID is reported only in Table 1; FID for many intermediate ablations (e.g., operating in image space with PCA) is not given (Reviewer 2). The paper does not analyze whether better classification accuracy systematically harms generation (FID), nor discusses the fundamental compatibility of generation and recognition objectives (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Architecture generality not established.** Experiments are limited to DiT-L; no validation on U-Net-based diffusion models (Reviewer 1). Conclusions about component importance may not transfer to other backbone types.
- **Ablation order may affect conclusions.** Components are ablated sequentially, but they may be correlated; different orders of ablating components (e.g., removing VQGAN loss before vs. after changing noise schedule) could yield different importance rankings. This robustness check is missing (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Missing experiments and details on key design choices.** (a) Whether predicting clean images is more helpful than predicting noise for representation learning is not conclusively determined (Reviewer 3). (b) The matrix \(V\) in the prediction-target equation (Line 392–406): if all weights \(w_i=1\), the loss reduces to reconstruction; the relative scale of weights matters, yet no ablation on these weights is provided (Reviewer 4). (c) The representation is extracted from the middle transformer layer without justification or exploration of other layers (Reviewer 2).
- **Oversimplified claim about noise scheduling.** The statement that “multiple noise levels is analogous to data augmentation” (Lines 416–418) is not adequately justified. Prior work shows that combining representations from different noise levels yields significant improvements—this aspect is not explored (Reviewer 4). The analogy to typical data augmentation (geometric/color transforms) is questioned.
- **Lack of theoretical analysis and broader discussion.** The paper remains entirely empirical, with no theoretical grounding or practical applications (Reviewer 3). There is insufficient discussion of how the findings could influence future self-supervised learning design or how the role of the tokenizer could be leveraged in other domains (Reviewer 1). The reason why noise affects generation more than representation is not explained (Reviewer 1).
