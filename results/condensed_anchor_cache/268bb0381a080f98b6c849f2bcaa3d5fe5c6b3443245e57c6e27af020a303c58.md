## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents SAIL, a structured initialization method that accelerates neural network training by reusing parameters from pre-trained models. It includes a parameter transformation technique to adapt pre-trained model dimensions to a target architecture and a proximal parameter integration strategy with theoretical convergence guarantees. Experiments on NLP and computer vision tasks show that SAIL reduces training time while improving performance compared to random initialization. Three reviewers rated the paper 6, 6, and 8, with one minority reviewer (rating 8) finding the approach novel and important, while the other two reviewers (rating 6) raise concerns about novelty, experimental setup, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Strong motivation and problem significance**: The paper addresses the important challenge of efficient large model training by leveraging publicly available pre-trained models, making a compelling case for its approach. One reviewer (rating 8) notes the problem of LLM reusing lacks efficient solutions and that SAIL appears better than naive weight averaging.
- **Solid theoretical analysis**: The paper provides rigorous theoretical guarantees (convergence theorems proved in the appendix) and effective visualizations that support the efficacy of the Proximal Parameter initialization for faster convergence. Two reviewers highlight the well-structured theorems; a third reviewer states the proofs look correct (without in-depth review).
- **Empirical validation across domains**: Extensive experiments on both NLP and computer vision tasks demonstrate that SAIL significantly outperforms random initialization, reducing training time and improving model performance, supporting broad applicability across model architectures.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited comparison with related methods**: The approach closely aligns with model reuse/expansion, model merging, and parameter transformation techniques. The paper does not compare against methods from these areas, which would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of SAIL’s relative efficiency. The paper also omits a discussion of knowledge distillation, which is highly related.
- **Limited novelty in leveraging pre-trained weights for initialization**: The core idea of reusing parameters from pre-trained models to accelerate training of new models is similar to existing work [1-2]. It is unclear how the proposed parameter transformation fundamentally differs from existing methods that adapt pre-trained weights to new architectures; the contribution appears incremental rather than novel. (Minority reviewer: rating 8 considers the method novel and important.)
- **Discrepancy between motivation and experimental setup**: The motivation (Figure 1) claims to use a pre-trained LLM, but experiments are conducted by training models from scratch on small-scale datasets in controlled setups, undermining practical relevance. The method should be demonstrated on actual pre-trained models and larger datasets to reflect real-world scenarios.
- **Insufficient analysis of parameter transformation techniques**: The paper mentions random projection and learnable methods for parameter transformation but lacks a detailed investigation of which technique is better and the trade-offs between them, making it difficult to assess robustness and generalizability.
- **Gap between linear model theory and real-world application**: The paper does not sufficiently discuss how to practically compute \(\gamma^*\) or handle situations where original training data is unavailable (common for open-source models). The theoretical convergence guarantees are probabilistic and may diminish over iterations, but the presentation implies deterministic improvement, which is not fully supported.
- **Missing experiments/ablations**: 
  - The claim in lines 268-269 based on Theorem 2 is not clearly justified (reviewer question).
  - Training curves for NLP experiments are not shown, despite being shown for CV; these would verify if the proven fast convergence applies to transformer training.
  - The cause of the spike at \(\gamma = 0.5\) in results is not explained.
  - The paper assumes other network elements (e.g., activation functions, vocabulary size) are the same; the impact of differing components is not discussed or ablated.
- **Presentation issues**: The extensive use of colored blocks and labels, while aiding navigation, is uncommon and may be excessive for a research paper (minor point, noted by the positive reviewer).
