## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Retrieval-augmented Reflection (RaR), which first generates a chain-of-thought (CoT) solution via zero-shot prompting, then iteratively retrieves external information to revise each reasoning step one by one. RaR is evaluated on code generation, mathematical reasoning, embodied planning, and TriviaQA. Results show improvements over baselines like RAG, CoT, Self-Refine, and standard prompting, with performance scaling as the number of refinement iterations increases. Two reviewers (R1, R2) rate the paper low (3/10) citing weak baselines, clarity issues, and inappropriate dataset choices; R4 rates 5/10; one reviewer (R3) rates 6/10 and finds it timely with strong empirical results.

### Strengths
- Strong empirical performance across multiple datasets and domains (code, math, planning, QA) (R1, R3, R4).
- The paper is clear and figures are intuitive, effectively conveying the main ideas (R2).
- RaR’s recursive step-by-step correction with continuous retrieval improves accuracy over traditional RAG (R2).
- RaR is flexible and handles diverse tasks (R2, R4).
- Multi-round reflection enables inference-time scaling by increasing computation (FLOPs) through more refinement rounds (R4).
- Timely and well-motivated; inference scaling is a practical way to improve outputs from closed-source LLMs (R3).
- Shows that fixing steps individually outperforms single-prompt refinement (R3).
- Evaluated on multiple datasets spanning several domains (R1).

### Weaknesses
- The method description (Lines 216–239) is vague; details of how retrieval is incorporated into the reflection process, how retrieved content \(V_i^k\) modifies \(y_i^{thought}\), and the formulas are unclear and difficult to reproduce (R2, R4). Specifically, the formulas on Lines 219, 224, and 233–235 are not clearly differentiated and their purposes are hard to distinguish (R4).
- Baselines in Table 1 are weak; the method is not compared with recent RAG works such as Active-RAG and IRCoT, so the differentiation from existing work is unclear (R1).
- Missing a key baseline: RAG + CoT. Since RaR uses retrieval with CoT, showing that even with all retrieved documents available, iterative refinement still yields gains would strengthen the claim (R3).
- The claim of inference-time scaling by expanding the number of reasoning steps \(n\) is not convincing: the number of reasoning steps from Zero-shot CoT is not easily controllable and is inherently limited for a given problem. The paper does not provide a clear mechanism for scaling steps (R2, R4).
- The QA dataset TriviaQA only requires single-hop retrieval and reasoning, which is inconsistent with the paper’s motivation of improving multi-step reasoning. Multi-hop datasets (e.g., MuSiQue, 2WikiMHQA) would be more appropriate (R2, R4).
- The mathematical reasoning datasets (GSM8K, GSM-Hard) are simple arithmetic and do not involve external knowledge; using the Jupyter code corpus for retrieval in math tasks does not seem helpful, and further experiments on advanced math (e.g., MATH) are needed (R4).
- More analysis is needed on why baselines fail. For instance, if CoT+RAG or RAG+CoT used the same retrieved documents as RaR, would they match RaR’s performance? (R3)
- Additional ablations are requested: matching compute for RaR with Self-refine (R3); RaR without RAG (just LLM verifying/fixing steps with no context) to establish the importance of retrieved documents (R3).
- The relationship between number of modification iterations and number of sentences in answers is unclear; comparison with RAG on the same number of retrievable instances needs clarification (R2).
- The abstract claim that “a small LM can surpass a LM with more than 10 times parameters with more inference-time computation” is ambiguous: does it mean with RaR and a large compute budget? Clarification needed (R3).
