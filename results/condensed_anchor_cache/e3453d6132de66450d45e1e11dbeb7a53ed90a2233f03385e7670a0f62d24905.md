## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes RXNFLOW, a GFlowNet-based method for synthesizability-aware molecular generation. It constrains the generation to valid synthetic pathways by sequentially selecting from 71 reaction templates and 1.2 M building blocks. A subsampling technique is introduced to make training tractable over this large action space. The model is evaluated on pocket-specific optimization (LIT‑PCBA) and pocket-conditional generation (CrossDocked2020), achieving an average Vina score of –8.85 kcal/mol and 34.8 % synthesizability.

### Strengths
- **Strong empirical performance.** RXNFLOW outperforms existing reaction-based and fragment-based models across all tested tasks, including state-of-the-art results on CrossDocked2020 (Vina –8.85 kcal/mol, 34.8 % synthesizability). (R1, R3, R5)
- **Effective subspace sampling.** The subsampling technique substantially reduces memory and computational complexity, enabling training with a huge action space (1.2 M building blocks, 71 templates) without significant overhead. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Novel non‑hierarchical action space.** Combining reaction templates and building blocks in a single, continuous action space is a well‑motivated design (R1). *(Note: R5 considers the conceptual novelty low – see Weaknesses.)*
- **Synthesizability and diversity.** By generating explicit synthetic pathways, the method ensures high synthesizability while maintaining molecular diversity. (R3)
- **Scalability and flexibility.** Expanding the building block library or adding new objectives does not require retraining, and the model scales well with larger building‑block sets. (R1, R3, R5)
- **Thorough evaluation.** The empirical study covers both pocket‑specific optimization and pocket‑conditional generation on standard benchmarks (LIT‑PCBA, CrossDocked2020). (R2, R3)
- **Clarity and presentation.** The paper is generally well written with high‑quality figures (R1). *(R5 finds Figure 2 confusing – see Weaknesses.)*

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons and baselines.**  
  - No direct comparison to SynFlowNet, RGFN, or SyntheMol for the scaling experiment (Figure 6), explicitly plotting computational cost (time and memory) and convergence rate as a function of building‑block library size. (R1)  
  - No direct comparison of memory usage and computational cost with RGFN at different building‑block sizes. (R1)  
  - For pocket‑conditional generation (Table 5), more advanced methods such as Gao et al. (ICML 2024, “Rethinking Specificity in SBDD: Leveraging Delta Score and Energy‑Guided Diffusion”) are not included. (R3)  
  - Atom‑level SBDD baselines (e.g., [1]) and the recent work [2] cited in the paper are absent from Table 5. (R2)  
  - The SEH proxy target commonly used in GFlowNet drug‑discovery papers was not tested. (R1, minor)  
  - SA score is suggested as an additional synthesizability metric to compare with the used metrics (e.g., AIZynFinder success). (R2)

- **Statistical rigor and significance.**  
  - No per‑run variance, box plots, or error bars are reported for docking scores in Tables 1–4; the statistical significance of the reported differences is not tested. (R1)  
  - Table 5 lacks error bars/standard deviations, yet the text claims “significant improvements.” (R1)  
  - The performance gains are not disentangled from the effect of using a larger building‑block library; results should be attributed to specific technical contributions rather than simply increased building‑block size. (R1)

- **Potential overfitting to Vina score.** The reward function uses Vina Score, which is also the evaluation metric; this may inflate the reported binding‑affinity results. (R3)

- **Insufficient support for key claims.**  
  - Generalization to unseen building blocks is only shown with QED (Figure 5), which is uninformative because generative models easily optimize QED. Benchmarking on actual docking targets and reporting Tanimoto similarity between seen and unseen blocks is needed. The Enamine set’s redundancy may obscure the challenge; using structurally distinct clusters would be more rigorous. (R1)  
  - The claim that the model can use new building blocks without retraining is of limited practical utility because building‑block libraries are updated infrequently and training costs are low. The narrow y‑axis scales in Figure 6 exaggerate the effect; only the 10²‑block condition shows a meaningful drop in scaffold diversity, and that library size is unrealistically small. (R1)  
  - The practical relevance of the reported changes in Vina and diversity needs better justification. (R1)

- **Conceptual and technical novelty concerns.**  
  - R5 finds that using RL for reaction‑pathway determination (here applied to GFlowNets) is not a novel concept, and the action‑embedding and importance‑sampling techniques are standard. A detailed explanation of how the embeddings interact with the GFlowNet framework is missing. (R5)  
  - R4 states that the method has no structural difference from SynFlowNet except for the sampling improvements; a careful discussion of differences in graph construction, reward settings, and sampling algorithm is required. (R4)  
  - An ablation study comparing hierarchical MDPs to the non‑hierarchical MDP is missing; the choice of a non‑hierarchical approach needs justification. (R5)

- **3D interaction modeling.**  
  - The model does not explicitly capture 3D interactions between atoms and the pocket; it relies solely on Vina score as a reward, neglecting geometric information crucial for binding affinity and specificity. (R4)  
  - Unlike atom‑based SBDD methods, the current framework cannot directly generate conformations of the ligand bound to the pocket. (R3)

- **Missing analyses and implementation details.**  
  - Training and inference times are not reported; a detailed complexity analysis and the effect of subsampling on convergence rate are missing. (R5)  
  - Speed comparison among different methods is requested. (R4)  
  - Why is the synthesis step set to 3 for RXNFLOW while SynFlowNet and RGFN use 4 steps? (R4)  
  - The bias/variance trade‑off from subsampling is not systematically discussed; the ablation (Figure 6) suggests larger subspaces perform better with enough steps – is the key benefit merely acceleration? (R2)  
  - No code is provided. (R1)

- **Writing and presentation issues.**  
  - The fact that the continuous action embedding is based on chemical fingerprints is only mentioned in the appendix, not the main text. (R1)  
  - Table 6 lacks a legend/keys, making it difficult to interpret. (R1)  
  - Figure 2 is confusing (diamonds vs. squares); Figure 7 is clearer. (R5)  
  - L429: “Since RXNFLOW explicitly considers synthesizability, we exclude the SA score from the TacoGFN’s reward function” – it is unclear whether the authors removed the SA reward from TacoGFN (i.e., ablated a baseline) or only from their own method. (R1)  
  - Algorithm 1 in the main text does not aid understanding and could be removed. (R1, minor)  
  - “Percentage of Synthesizable Molecules” is misleading because it is a prediction, not ground truth; “AIZynFinder success” is preferred. (R1)  
  - Section B.1 is highly inspired by SynFlowNet and should be cited accordingly. (R1)

- **Minor reviewer disagreements.**  
  - While R1 praises the novelty of the non‑hierarchical action space, R5 argues the overall approach lacks conceptual novelty. (contrast)  
  - R1 considers the paper well‑written and the figures high‑quality; R5 finds Figure 2 confusing. (contrast)
