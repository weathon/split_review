## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes GISL, a causal discovery method for gene regulatory network inference (GRNI) that accounts for selection bias and latent confounders. It leverages single-cell perturb-seq data and shows that causal dependencies, selection processes, and latent confounders are (partially) identifiable under mild graphical conditions without parametric assumptions. Two variants (GISB and GISL) are evaluated on synthetic data (up to ~20 nodes) and a real single-cell dataset of 5 genes. The paper provides theoretical identifiability results for settings with and without latent confounders.

### Strengths
- The basic idea of considering selection bias and latent confounders in GRNI is novel and not commonly discussed or well-understood in the community (R1, R2, R4). Reviewers agree this provides valuable insights from causal inference and is well-motivated (R1, R3).
- Theoretical identifiability results are a strength: the paper proves that causal dependencies and selection processes are identifiable without latent confounders and partially identifiable with latent confounders (R1, R3, R4). Both GISB and GISL are empirically validated (R3).
- The method guarantees that discovered causal dependencies are causal rather than spurious correlations (R3). The algorithm description is clear enough to be understood and reproduced (R2).
- Experimental design on synthetic data is sound and supports the main claims (R1). Real-data experiments show GISL learns the majority of known causal dependencies (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Scalability is a critical concern.** The method is tested only on very small networks: real-world experiment uses just 5 genes, synthetic networks have at most ~20 nodes (R1, R2, R4). Human genomes have ~20,000 protein-coding genes; even yeast has ~6,000. The PC algorithm used for skeleton discovery does not scale to such sizes, and GISL’s iterative updates worsen runtime (R1). This means the method can only be applied after heavy gene filtration, which artificially turns many genes into “latent confounders”, contradicting the paper’s motivation (R1). Reviewers call for justification, discussion of when such filtration is valid, or replacement with more scalable methods (R1, R2, R4).
- **Insufficient comparison with state-of-the-art methods.** Multiple reviewers note the absence of standard GRN inference methods (e.g., GENIE3, GRNBoost, DCI) that were mentioned in the introduction (R1). Also missing are recent causal discovery methods developed after 2023, especially deep-learning approaches that handle nonlinear dependencies and selection bias (R2). DCDI and a dedicated GRN method were suggested (R3). No baselines are compared on the real-data experiment (R3). The paper should also show results for PC without GISL updates on the real data (R1).
- **Theoretical statements lack precision and quantification.** Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 are vague: “sufficiently many data” and “partially identifiable” are not defined (R3, R4). What are “causal processes”? How many perturbations are required? Biology experiments have costs, so the number needed should be quantified (R3). The proof strategy of enumerating configurations is not justified as exhaustive (R3). It is also unclear how these results fit into or extend known identifiability results in the causal inference literature (R4).
- **Insufficient justification that selection bias is a real issue in single-cell transcriptomics.** The paper’s evidence (z-scores from DepMap showing growth-rate differences) is weak; such differences could be artifacts or causal regulatory effects (R4). The motivating example (Figure 1) could equally be viewed as a causal mechanism—if perturbing X changes the distribution of Y, that is a causal dependency (R4). More biological or measurement evidence is needed (R4). The literature review on GRN (page 2) is too brief, focusing mostly on causal discovery with only 5 references actually about GRN (R1). A proper related-work section is missing (R4).
- **Writing and presentation quality needs significant improvement.** Definitions are vague or inconsistent: Definition 2.3’s phrase “Toy examples are shown in 9” is unclear; Figure 9 refers to “Definition 2.5” which is not in the main text (R2). The three conditions in Definition 2.3 are complex and hard to interpret (R2). Algorithm 1 (GISB) lacks details on the marginal/conditional independence tests (methods, criteria) and on how to select gene subsets along the path (R2). The symbol “I” is called a “surrogate variable” in the algorithm but “perturbation indicator” in the proof—needs unification (R2, R3). The variable introduced in Step 4 is not explained (R2). Grammar and syntax errors are numerous (e.g., “casual” instead of “causal”, missing capitalization, confusing lines: 152, 155, 192, 293, 322) (R3). Appendix Section F contains many grammatical errors (R4). Proofs are hard to follow; reviewers suggest moving technicalities to appendix and using DAG figures for intuitive explanation (R3).
- **Missing experiments and analyses.** No runtime or clock time is reported for synthetic or real experiments (R1). It is not determined how much data the method requires to perform reasonably, especially in the low-data regime compared to baselines (R3). The real-data experiment uses a tiny DAG (5 genes) and should be compared against baselines (R3). The impact of the number of perturbations is not studied (R3).
- **Methodological assumptions are problematic.** The paper assumes the GRN is a DAG, but feedback cycles (e.g., in histone gene groups) are common in biology (R1, R4). The claim that causal dependencies are asymmetric and selection biases symmetric may not hold if cycles exist (R4). Co-expressed gene groups (e.g., histone hairballs) would be symmetric; it is unclear if they should be directional (R1). The formal definition of “selection bias” is not rigorous—is it discarding samples based on a threshold (as in the toy example) or modeling an additional node? (R4). The data-generating process (Bayesian network?) is not specified (R4).
