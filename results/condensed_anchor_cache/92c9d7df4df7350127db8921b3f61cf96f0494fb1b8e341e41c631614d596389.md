## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AnyBimanual, a plug-and-play method that transfers a pretrained single-arm policy to multi-task bimanual manipulation with few bimanual demonstrations. It introduces a skill manager that dynamically schedules skill primitives discovered from single-arm policies to compose language embeddings for each arm, and a voxel editor that generates spatial soft masks to align visual observations from the bimanual workspace with those seen during single-arm pretraining. The method is evaluated on 12 RLBench2 tasks and 9 real-world tasks, comparing against PerAct and PerAct-LF, with ablation studies.

### Strengths
- High practical value: transferring single-arm policies to bimanual manipulation is a promising direction because bimanual demonstration data is scarce while single-arm policies have shown strong generalization.
- The pipeline (skill manager + voxel editor) is intuitive and reasonably designed for this problem.
- Clever design elements: model-agnostic (applicable to different single-arm models), use of KL divergence to implicitly decompose dual-arm workspace observations into two single-arm observations.
- The approach is original—integrating two single-arm policies for bimanual tasks, supported by a thorough literature review that contextualizes the gap.
- Clear and accessible writing overall, making it easy to follow.
- The idea is interesting and may help reduce the data needed for dual-arm policy learning.
- Baseline comparisons and ablation studies demonstrate impact; qualitative visualizations aid understanding.
- One reviewer is notably more positive (score 6), finding the idea novel and appealing, while three reviewers are more critical (score 3), raising substantial concerns about methodology and evaluation.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines:** Only two baselines are included: PerAct (single-arm) and PerAct-LF (bimanual). The paper lacks comparisons with other bimanual algorithms such as DualAfford, or with explicit skill decomposition using large language models (e.g., prompting to assign roles). Since the method claims to be model-agnostic, experiments on other pipelines (e.g., diffusion policy) would be more convincing.
- **Generalization undermined by training approach:** The end-to-end training with cross-entropy loss on action labels makes the method essentially imitation learning, which is known for poor generalization—contradicting the goal of preserving the pretrained model’s generalization ability. The training uses 20 or 100 demonstrations per task, making the voxel editor and skill manager task-specific and not transferable across tasks. No cross-task generalization experiment is conducted (e.g., training on all tasks and testing on unseen tasks).
- **Overlooks dual-arm coordination:** The framework treats left and right arm policies nearly independently. While this may work for tasks with fixed roles (e.g., one stabilizing, one acting), it fails in tasks requiring deeper collaboration (e.g., jointly lifting a large ball, handing over a small item, sweeping into a dustpan). In tasks that require both arms in the same small area, ignoring the other arm can cause collisions. Coordination is left to implicit learning from limited demonstrations, but this is insufficient.
- **Performance is low and improvement is marginal:** In simulation (Table 2), no method achieves 14.33% success; the full method only improves to 21.67%—a modest gain. For simple tasks like lifting a ball, success is only 32% despite 100 demonstrations. In real-world, lifting ball success is 66%. It is unclear whether imitation learning is suitable here (reinforcement learning or better data augmentation might help). The low absolute performance raises questions about demonstration quality or possible implementation issues.
- **Missing details and unclear components:** 
  - The skill manager simultaneously learns primitive skills from demonstrations and decomposes new instructions, but how it maps language embeddings to low-level commands is not explained (relies on pretrained model but not clarified).
  - The composite language embedding (linear combination of skill primitives) is concatenated with the original bimanual language embedding, which may be out-of-distribution for the pretrained single-arm policy (e.g., "push two buttons" is unseen during pretraining).
  - Similarly, the global visual context from bimanual observations might be OOD for the frozen single-arm policy. Direct concatenation of global context is questionable; the contribution of the KL divergence to aligning subspaces is appreciated, but further clarification is needed.
  - Figure 2 is unclear regarding Mutually Exclusive Prior and Sparsity Prior—more explanation needed on how these guide training.
  - In Figure 5, the "open" operation in "bring me the item" is confusing, and the voxel editor visualization is hard to evaluate.
- **Problem formulation confusion:** The paper states it uses K expert trajectories yet emphasizes that expert demonstrations are lacking—this inconsistency should be resolved. Also, if single-arm data collection tools are now low-cost (e.g., Universal Manipulation Interface), could they also benefit bimanual tasks?
- **Insufficient real-world experiments:** Only 9 real-world tasks are shown; the lift task success rate of 66% is not convincing. The sim-to-real transfer is problematic: simulation uses Franka robot, real-world uses UR5 with different workspace and gripper (Franka Hand vs. Robotiq 85). How this gap was bridged is not addressed. More real-world tasks and metrics are needed.
- **Baseline confusion:** PerAct2 is already a bimanual policy; its success rates reported in this paper are much lower than in the original PerAct2 paper—differences in evaluation conditions should be explained.
- **Skill manager limitation with simple instructions:** The method seems to rely on specific primitive skill descriptions in the instruction. How would it handle a simple instruction like "give me the Coke"?
- **Synchrony issues:** Supplementary demonstrations show significant asynchrony between the two arms' motions, which conflicts with the need for coordination.
