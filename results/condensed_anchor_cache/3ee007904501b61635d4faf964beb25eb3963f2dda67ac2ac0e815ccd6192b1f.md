## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies a cooperative game framework (Rationalizing Neural Predictions) for interpretable text classification, where a generator selects rationales and a predictor classifies. The authors identify a new type of spurious correlation introduced by the generator’s sampling process, not inherent in the original data. They propose an attacker model to detect such trivial/spurious patterns and an instruction step to prevent the predictor from learning these correlations. Experiments on six text datasets and one graph dataset with GRUs, BERT, and GCN show improvement over recent baselines, with comparison to Llama-3.1-8B. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the first contribution (identification of the bias source) but express significant concerns about the practical solution, experimental rigor, theoretical clarity, and overall significance. Reviewer confidence varies: Reviewer 1 (rating 5, confidence 1) was outside expertise; Reviewers 2 (rating 5, confidence 3), 3 and 4 (rating 3, confidence 4 and 3). There is clear disagreement: Reviewers 1 and 2 are more positive, while Reviewers 3 and 4 are more negative.

### Strengths
- **Novel identification of a new spurious correlation source** – The paper highlights that conditioning on the generator can introduce a sampling bias, creating incorrect correlations between selected explanations and labels even when semantically unrelated in the original dataset. This is considered interesting, significant, and corrects an empirical line of research using simple statistics facts (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 4 calls this the main strength. *(Positive, high magnitude; noted by both positive and negative reviewers)*
- **Demonstrates the issue in existing rationalizing methods** – The paper convincingly shows that existing rationalizing approaches can rely on trivial/spurious text patterns, establishing that the problem is real (Reviewer 2). *(Positive, moderate magnitude)*
- **Structured two-step method** – The proposed inspection (via attacker) and instruction (to avoid degeneracy) provides a clear framework for addressing the identified bias (Reviewer 3). *(Positive, but Reviewer 3 also considers the contribution not significant – see Weaknesses)*
- **Experiments on diverse tasks show headroom** – The evaluation across text classification and graph classification tasks, using three architectures, indicates that accuracy can be improved through an interpretable method (Reviewer 2). *(Positive, moderate magnitude)*
- **Figures and tables are well-received** – Figures are liked and considered illustrative (Reviewer 4). *(Minor positive)*

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear and misleading communication** – The title is uninformative, missing “spurious correlation” and overemphasizing “attack”; “interpretability” is vague, and “techniques” is unspecific (Reviewer 4). The phrase “Attack *to* Inspection and Instruction” in Figure 5 is misworded (Reviewer 4). The paper uses “attacker” which is confusing in the context of improving interpretability/robustness; “discrimination” (like GANs) would be clearer (Reviewer 4). The introduction gives no intuition of “inspection”; the explanation should appear earlier (Reviewer 4). Grammar and formatting errors exist: line 128 missing reference, line 234 “doesn’t lead to conditional independent,” Figure 3 “A local of the causal graph,” line 232 “it will *sometimes* results in *some* problems,” Eq. 8 missing spaces (Reviewers 3, 4). “Methods constrain[s]” typo (Reviewer 4).
- **Theoretical exposition is unclear** – The causal diagram defines \(R\) but \(R\) is unused in equations; the intended causal diagram and training paradigm are confusing (Reviewer 2). Section 4.2 (Eq. 6) does not clearly explain why the attacker “inspects trivial patterns” (Reviewer 1). The intuition in Section 4.3 (features from which the classifier can predict opposite label) is not properly distinguished from simply discarding low mutual information features (Reviewer 1).
- **Assumption 1 is unrealistic for multiple classes** – The paper’s basic assumption becomes unrealistic when there are more than two classes; binary sentiment classification is an oversimplified example (Reviewer 4).
- **Experimental setup in Figure 4 is poorly justified** – (i) If the random patterns are independent of \(Y\), why does the validation accuracy (orange) exceed 50%? If they do contain information relevant to the task, why can’t the full-text model (green) leverage it? (Reviewer 1). (ii) The green line (predictor trained on full text but evaluated on random selections) is claimed to show that random patterns contain minimal sentiment information, but this reasoning is flawed: the predictor may have learned different shortcuts, or interactions between selected and non-selected words may be necessary for accuracy (Reviewer 4). (iii) The gap between blue and orange lines is only 10–15%, suggesting the test setup is not robust (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing baselines and efficiency analysis** – (i) Tables 1 and 2 should report accuracy using the full text without any rationale selection (Reviewer 2). (ii) No comparisons to uninterpretable but strong baselines like fastText or BERT without the RNP framework; such a comparison is needed to understand the interpretability-accuracy tradeoff (Reviewer 4). (iii) No analysis of the method’s computational overhead; training the attacker adds cost, and convergence speed / sample efficiency relative to baselines is not reported (Reviewer 4).
- **Methodological concerns about the proposed solution** – (i) The approach is similar to adversarial training of the classifier [1,2,3]; the paper does not distinguish its contribution from prior adversarial training work (Reviewer 2). (ii) Using attack techniques to identify spurious correlations has been explored before (e.g., “Mitigating Backdoor Poisoning Attacks through the Lens of Spurious Correlation,” arXiv 2305.11596), undermining novelty (Reviewer 3). (iii) The claimed distinction from causality research is ambiguous: the paper argues it looks for spurious correlations from the selection process (model-specific) yet claims the method is model-agnostic – this needs elucidation (Reviewer 3). (iv) The practical utility of the second contribution (attacker + instruction) is questioned; the problem may not exist in related lines of work, such as prototype-based networks (Reviewer 4 lists related works). The paper may be solving a problem that never existed (Reviewer 4). (v) How does the attacker differentiate patterns that yield context-dependent sentiment (e.g., “bitter” could be positive or negative)? Would such patterns be discarded? (Reviewer 1). 
- **Significance and impact** – The contribution is not large; the method is an add-on rather than an end-to-end tool (Reviewer 3). The impact is small (Reviewer 3). The paper does not connect to prototype-based interpretability methods (Reviewer 4).
