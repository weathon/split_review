## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes TANGO (Time-Reversal Latent GraphODE for Multi-Agent Dynamical Systems), a graph neural network-based ODE model that incorporates a soft regularization term to encourage time-reversal symmetry in the latent dynamics. This is motivated by the observation that Hamiltonian neural networks strictly enforce energy conservation, which fails for non-conservative systems such as damped springs. Instead, TANGO uses a self-supervised loss that aligns forward and backward trajectories predicted by a GraphODE, thereby imposing a weaker but more broadly applicable inductive bias. The authors provide a theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) showing that this regularization minimizes higher-order Taylor expansion terms during ODE integration, which can improve noise tolerance even for irreversible systems. Experiments are conducted on four synthetic physical systems (e.g., simple spring, damped spring, chaotic pendulum) and compare against baselines such as Latent NODEs, Hamiltonian NODEs, TRS NODEs, and GraphODEs. Reviewers are sharply divided: one reviewer (score 8) considers the contribution novel and significant, while another (score 3) views it as highly incremental and lacking positioning relative to existing reversible architecture literature; the other two (score 5 each) find the idea interesting but note significant limitations in novelty, experimental setup, and theoretical framing.

### Strengths

- **Interesting and broad motivation**: The paper convincingly argues that focusing on time-reversal symmetry (rather than strict energy conservation) is a more general inductive bias applicable to both conservative and non-conservative reversible systems (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). This idea is novel in the context of GraphODEs (Reviewer 4).

- **Simple and computationally efficient approach**: The regularization term is straightforward to implement as a soft constraint, making it generally applicable (Reviewer 1).

- **Theoretical contribution**: Theorem 1 provides a formal treatment of the regularization as accounting for higher-order Taylor expansion errors during ODE integration, which is an interesting and well-received analysis (Reviewers 1, 3).

- **Empirical performance**: TANGO consistently outperforms major competitors (Latent NODEs, Hamiltonian NODEs, TRS NODEs, graph NODEs) on the tested physical systems (Reviewers 2, 4). One reviewer highlights that results are "comprehensive and compelling on simulated data" (Reviewer 4).

- **Well-organized presentation**: The paper is generally well-written, and Figures 1 and 2 effectively summarize motivation and core concept (Reviewer 2, 4). The code is well-structured for reproducibility (Reviewer 4).

**Minority contrast**: Reviewer 1 considers the empirical results to confirm claims only with significant limitations, while Reviewer 4 finds them fully convincing. Overall, there is disagreement on the strength of the empirical evidence.

### Weaknesses

- **Incremental contribution**: The core idea of enforcing time-reversal symmetry via a consistency loss is adapted from Huh et al. (NeurIPS 2020) and Huang et al. (2020). Extending it to graph neural ODEs is seen by some reviewers as a straightforward extension, while the reformulation of the loss (self-consistency between forward and backward trajectories vs. comparing to ground truth) is a smaller step (Reviewer 1). One reviewer states: “Merely extending it to a pre-existing architecture (GraphODEs) doesn't, in itself, signify a substantial contribution” (Reviewer 3). The ablation comparing to ground-truth loss (gt-rev) shows very close numbers without statistics, weakening the claim of improvement (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing related work and positioning**: The paper does not adequately cite and compare against relevant literature on reversible neural architectures (e.g., works on stable non-dissipative diffusion, reversible graph flows, and bracket-based dynamics that mix reversible and irreversible behaviors). These works often induce provable reversibility, which is a different perspective from the “encouraged reversibility” of TANGO (Reviewer 1). The paper also does not discuss how TANGO relates to Koopman operator approaches that also use symmetry constraints (Reviewer 3).

- **Unclear definition of the reversing operator**: It is not explicitly defined how the reversing operator \(R\) is applied in the latent space. Several possibilities exist (splitting \(z\) into pseudo-\(q\) and pseudo-\(p\), directly reversing observations via the encoder, or simply using \(R: (z,t)\to(z,-t)\)), each with different conceptual and technical issues. The paper appears to use the simplest identity-on-\(z\) version, but then the claimed “time-reversal symmetry” is questionable. Without a proper definition, the physical meaning of the regularization is ambiguous (Reviewer 2).

- **Confusion between time-reversal symmetry and time-reversibility (invertibility)**: Equation (9) appears to promote time-reversibility (i.e., that forward and backward trajectories are inverses of each other) rather than time-reversal symmetry (i.e., that forward and backward dynamics are indistinguishable). The example of a damped oscillator illustrates that reversible (invertible) dynamics can lack time-reversal symmetry. Many deterministic ODEs are theoretically invertible, but numerical solvers may break it; thus the loss may mainly correct for numerical errors rather than enforcing a physical symmetry. The paper does not adequately distinguish these concepts (Reviewer 2).

- **Insufficient experimental rigor and reproducibility**: 
  - There is no clear description of how the regularization weight \(\alpha\) is chosen, e.g., via a validation set. The model selection process and hyperparameter optimization are not detailed (Reviewers 1, 2).
  - Results in Table 1 are reported with four-digit precision without standard deviations or statistics, which is unusual in stochastic optimization and makes it hard to gauge robustness of comparisons, especially when results are close (Reviewer 3).
  - The ablation study comparing TANGO to alternative TRS implementations (gt-rev, rev2) is confusingly written and contains grammatical inconsistencies (Reviewer 3).
  - Typos: “worldsystems”, “real *worldsystems*”, etc. (Reviewer 2).

- **Limited scope of experiments**: 
  - Only four simple simulated physical systems are tested (spring, damped spring, pendulum, chaotic pendulum), whereas more recent works use complex setups like MuJoCo dynamics or CMU human trajectory data (Reviewer 1).
  - Comparisons are made against single-agent models on benchmarks that strongly favor multi-agent inductive biases, which may not be a fair test of the time-reversal loss itself. A more informative comparison would investigate the loss on various ODE architectures both with and without it (Reviewer 3).
  - No real-world data is used, though this may be justified by the difficulty of collecting such data (Reviewer 4).

- **Issues with specific experimental figures and claims**:
  - Figure 4 shows MSE of the chaotic pendulum being nearly constant across prediction lengths; for a chaotic system, exponential trajectory divergence is expected. How does the prediction horizon compare to the system’s Lyapunov exponent? (Reviewer 3)
  - In Figure 5, HODEN (a model that strictly conserves energy) shows energy divergence in the damped spring system; what is the reason? (Reviewer 2)
  - TANGO achieves lower MSE on the Damped Spring dataset than on the Simple Spring dataset, which is surprising. Explanation needed (Reviewer 4).
  - Figure 5 left plot lacks axis labels, and the right plot would benefit from including ground truth system energies (Reviewer 3).

- **Clarity of method description**:
  - Equation (6) is difficult to follow (e.g., derivation of \(z_1^{\text{fwd}}(t)\)), and the function \(g(\cdot)\) in Figure 2 is missing (Reviewer 4).
  - The model input description is misleading: Section 2 states “Model input consists of trajectories of such features…” without explicitly mentioning that the graph structure (adjacency matrix) is a necessary input. It is unclear whether the adjacency matrix is ground truth or learned, and how it is incorporated into the GNN-based ODE (Reviewer 4).

- **Questions that overlap with weaknesses/missing experiments**: 
  - What is the precise definition of the reversing operator in TANGO? Is there a canonical way for latent dynamics? (Reviewer 2)
  - Does the proposed loss actually enforce time-reversal symmetry or only time-reversibility (invertibility)? (Reviewer 2)
  - How does the choice of ODE solver (higher-order vs. lower-order, e.g., Euler, leapfrog) affect the effectiveness of TANGO? If the loss mainly corrects numerical errors, higher-precision solvers may reduce the benefit. (Reviewer 2)
  - Can TANGO be adapted to dynamic graphs with nodes/edges appearing/disappearing? (Reviewer 4)
  - Can the theoretical result in Theorem 1 be extended to the loss in Equation (5) (prediction-based) rather than the symmetric loss? Could there be different scaling with sequence length \(T\)? (Reviewer 3)
  - The paper should discuss the applicability of the time-reversal loss to other architectures (single-agent models, non-graph ODEs) and to more general frameworks (Reviewer 3).
