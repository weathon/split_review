## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the TRACE framework, which formalizes finding optimal deletion sequences under MoRF/LeRF metrics as a combinatorial optimization problem and approximates nearly optimal solutions via simulated annealing or greedy algorithms. The method is evaluated on image data (ImageNet, ResNet-18 and other convolutional models) across varying backgrounds, deletion block sizes, masking orders, and logit/probability variants. TRACE-Mo and TRACE-Le achieve the best scores on their respective metrics, unsurprisingly as they directly optimize them. The paper also analyzes the out‑of‑distribution (OOD) issue and provides guidance for using deletion metrics. Reviewer opinions diverge: two reviewers (scores 8) find the work important and well‑executed; a third (score 5) notes presentation problems; a fourth (score 3) strongly questions the novelty and validity of the approach, pointing to prior work that already performed similar optimization and obtained the same conclusions.

### Strengths
- Deletion/insertion metrics are widely used in XAI, and a deeper understanding of their behavior is an important contribution.
- The paper is well organized and easy to read (R1, R4).
- The TRACE formalization provides a meaningful tool for comparing explanation methods. The in‑depth investigation of TRACE‑Mo and TRACE‑Le, including the comparison of simulated annealing vs. greedy optimization and the exploration of lower bounds, is innovative and strengthens the experimental validity (R2 S1, R3).
- The framework explicitly addresses the OOD problem arising when deleted features are replaced with “null” or zero values (R2 S2).
- Extensive analyses cover multiple dimensions: masking order (MoRF vs. LeRF), background choices, deletion block sizes, logit vs. probability use, and dependence on model architectures (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Validity and novelty concerns (minority reviewer, R3):** The paper’s stated goal is to investigate MoRF/LeRF properties in an explanation‑method‑agnostic way, but directly optimizing the metrics produces explanation methods (TRACE‑Mo, TRACE‑Le), which is method‑dependent rather than agnostic. The lack of description of how this optimization yields an agnostic investigation is a gap.
  - Key results (MoRF optimization producing artifacts, LeRF being more reliable) have already been established in prior work: Fong & Vedaldi (ICCV 2017) [Ref1] proposed optimizing MoRF as a continuous relaxation and noted artifact noise; the “Feature Attribution As Feature Selection” paper (OpenReview 2018) [Ref2] optimized both MoRF and LeRF via greedy/gradient methods and concluded LeRF is more reliable. The current paper does not clearly identify what new findings it contributes beyond these. [Ref3] (Sanity Checks for Saliency Metrics, AAAI 2020) is another relevant study with a different perspective that is not discussed.
- **Presentation and readability problems (R2, R4):**
  - The main body lacks intuition or sketches for theoretical proofs; proofs are relegated to the appendix without a high‑level flow in the main text.
  - Related work is placed in the appendix, making it difficult to position the paper’s novelty.
  - Writing in the introduction, results, and conclusions is sometimes vague or informal (R2). Specific examples: the first paragraph of p.4 is convoluted despite a simple point (R4).
  - Notation is inconsistent between the main text and appendix (R2); abbreviations and explanation methods are not always properly introduced (e.g., LRP is missing; “blurring” is never clearly defined) (R4).
  - Typos and errors: p.6 “a lower bound” should not be “the lower bound”; p.6 typo “GO-MO solves for an index [s] s_k” (R4). Also, example on p.3 with ψ and φ_f(x) is confusing (R4).
  - The paper feels like a journal submission cut down for a conference; important analysis details are in the supplementary material, which is hard to find and rarely read (R4).
  - References are in arbitrary order; some are broken (e.g., “Wang and Wang 2022a”) (R4).
- **Theorem 3.1 error (R3):** The theorem defines MoRF and LeRF as sums of f (Eq. 1), but the accompanying proof treats them as sums of x. This proof is valid only when f is linear, not for general f.
- **Insufficient analysis of baseline explanation methods (all reviewers):**
  - The paper does not take a clear position on which existing explanation methods perform better under the metrics; results are presented numerically but not discussed analytically (R2).
  - The proposed method unsurprisingly outperforms all baselines since it directly optimizes the metric. A ranking of baseline methods and an analysis of what the TRACE‑induced gaps reveal about those methods is missing (R4). Missing comparisons include LRP, a principled attribution method (R4).
  - Table 1 contains too much information without adequate discussion; it is unclear whether experiments are repeated and whether means/stds are reported. Insights such as standard deviation between architectures are lacking (R4).
- **Missing experiments and clarifications (questions from R2, R4):**
  - How does TRACE deal with correlated features?
  - How does (or could) TRACE operate on tabular datasets?
  - Are there qualitative differences between model architectures and sizes regarding the quality of generated explanations?
  - Relevance of deletion/insertion metrics to human interpretation is missing; qualitative examples (e.g., visual saliency maps) in the main paper would help (R4).
- **Visualization and figure issues (R4):** Fig. 2a is unreadable because too many curves overlap; it would be better to present differences rather than absolute values. The true value is not very interesting.
