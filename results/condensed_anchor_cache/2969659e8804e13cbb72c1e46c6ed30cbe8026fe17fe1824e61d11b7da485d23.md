## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies how encoder-only transformers learn from tree-structured data using a controlled PCFG generation process with a filtering parameter \(k\) that controls correlation locality. On root classification and masked language modeling, transformers achieve accuracy similar to belief propagation. The authors propose a construction implementing BP on a depth-\(\ell\) tree with \(\ell\) transformer layers and analyze attention patterns and probing results. Reviewers find the problem and synthetic distribution interesting but raise concerns about alternative interpretations, simplicity of the task, novelty overlap, and insufficient evidence that transformers actually implement BP.

### Strengths
- Well-placed in the context of mechanistic interpretability and related work on structured data.
- Analyzing what strategy transformers learn on structured distributions is an important problem.
- The synthetic distribution family (PCFG with filtering parameter \(k\)) is novel and allows control over the locality of correlations.
- A novel construction shows how to implement belief propagation on a depth-\(\ell\) tree using only \(\ell\) transformer layers (previously required \(2\ell\) layers).
- Experimental results are interesting, e.g., Figure 4 shows hierarchical attention patterns, and Sec 3.4 demonstrates that MLM pretraining improves sample efficiency for root prediction.
- The belief propagation viewpoint and the CFG filtering mechanism are novel.
- The study covers multiple perspectives: prediction accuracy, probing, and manual construction.

### Weaknesses
- It is unclear how findings generalize to larger models or more complex distributions.
- The claim that transformers implement belief propagation has weak evidence and competing interpretations:
  - Similar aggregate accuracy does not imply similar behavior per input; the authors should measure the match between BP and transformer predictions on individual inputs.
  - Even if predictions match, that does not imply the transformer implements BP internally; the model may simply learn the optimal prediction function (which coincides with BP for the data distribution) without implementing BP. For example, a model trained on \(k=0\) data could match BP on OOD data without internalizing the algorithm.
  - The theoretical BP construction (Sec 3.5) is not shown to be realized by the trained model; a rigorous analysis demonstrating that learned attention patterns and weight matrices align with the construction is needed.
- The synthetic task is too simple/contrived:
  - Strings are all the same length, and tree depth is fixed and does not exceed the number of transformer layers. Testing on CFGs with parse trees of varying depths would be more informative.
  - There is significant overlap with Allen-Zhu & Li (2023), which also trained transformers on CFG data and observed hierarchical attention.
- The primary goal of the paper is unclear (new data model vs. transformer analysis vs. BP implementation), and it is not articulated how this synthetic task informs understanding of transformers on real tasks like natural language.
- Alternative algorithmic explanations (e.g., inside algorithm) are not ruled out.
- Key supporting figures (Fig 7, App C.3, C.4) are relegated to the appendix, weakening the main claims.
- Methodological details are lacking or unclear:
  - The leaf-to-root message passing in the BP construction needs more explanation; a walkthrough on a minimal example (small \(\ell\), \(q\)) would help. The meaning of \(r\) variables and whether they can differ across branches is unclear.
  - The relationship between the described BP algorithm and the experiments is not explicitly stated.
  - Notation in Section 3 (e.g., \(\mathcal{O}_a\), \(q\)) is unclear.
- Alternative interpretations for observed behavior are not addressed. For example, difficulty in learning higher \(k\) may be due to weaker correlation between the root and the entire sequence, not weaker correlations among tokens.
- Using accuracy for MLM is questionable because the CFG can be ambiguous (multiple correct predictions); perplexity might be more appropriate.
- Experimental reporting is incomplete: Figure 3 shows validation accuracy with a rise-and-fall pattern, but it is unclear if the best checkpoint was used for OOD evaluation, and test accuracy is not reported. Similarly, the mid-training accuracy drop (line 355) is unexplained.
- The writing needs improvement, especially in Section 3.3, and the intuition behind the BP construction is hard to follow.
