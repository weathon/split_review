## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses the problem of designing RL agents that can justify their decisions with supporting evidence, a highly important and timely topic for accountable decision-making, especially in high-stakes settings like healthcare. The authors propose a debate-based reward model: a zero-sum extensive-form game where two argumentative agents take turns selecting evidence (state features) to support two competing actions, and a proxy judge (trained on clinician action data) evaluates which action is better justified given the collected evidence. The judge's outcome is used as a reward term (mixed with environment reward via coefficient λ) to train a justifiable policy. Experiments on a sepsis treatment dataset compare the proposed method against baselines including an environment-only policy, a judge-only policy, and an SHAP-based justifiability metric. Results indicate that combining environmental and judge rewards yields policies that are highly favored by the judge while barely sacrificing performance. The debate-based feedback attains performance and justifiability comparable to an ideal judge with full state information. Analyses probe the robustness of learned evidence proposals against adversarial refutations. The paper is clearly written and provides a compelling proof-of-concept, though reviewers noted significant limitations regarding the gap between the human-justification motivation and the actual proxy-based methodology, as well as concerns about the lack of human evaluations, ethical considerations, and the realism of assumptions.

### Strengths
- Important problem: designing RL agents that can justify actions is a critical and under-explored area for high-stakes, accountable decision-making.
- Novel approach: the debate game framework for extracting justifiable evidence and using its outcome as a reward signal is original to this work and a promising direction.
- Well-written and motivated: the paper is clear, well-organized, and provides strong motivation; the Introduction and Formal Setup were particularly praised.
- Strong empirical case study: experiments on sepsis treatment are compelling, with convincing results (especially Figs 2c and 2d) and many ablation analyses that answer key questions about the method.
- Useful comparisons: the paper includes a comparison to SHAP as an alternative justifiability metric, and the proposed method fares well.
- Novelty contribution: the debate formulation captures the trade-off between optimal performance and justifiability, enabling interpolation via λ, and is designed to enforce robustness against adversarial evidence.

### Weaknesses
- **Gap between motivation and methodology (major concern)**: The paper is motivated by the need for agents whose actions are justifiable to humans, yet both training and evaluation are mediated by a synthetic proxy judge trained on clinician action data rather than on human judgments of justifiability. This indirect approach introduces a clear gap; the evaluation inherits this limitation, making it difficult to assess true human-perceived justifiability. The claim that agents "closely align with human preferences" is not directly validated—it presupposes that clinician actions are justifiable actions, an assumption that should be explicitly stated and justified, or the claim should be toned down.
- **Definition and use of the Preference Dataset (Sec 4.1)**: The dataset is defined as indicating which decision is more justified by a given set of evidence, but in experiments, the preference p simply indicates the clinician’s actual action, not a judgment under randomly sampled evidence. The evidence is not included in the tuple, so the dataset does not meet the stated definition.
- **Methodological limitations and hyper-parameter load**: The debate game method requires explicit knowledge of the optimal deterministic baseline policy π_B (unless misunderstood), which is a substantial limitation for scaling to larger problems. Additionally, the framework introduces numerous hyper-parameters (L, λ, α, network architectures) without sufficient justification; evaluation on only a single MDP weakens the evidence for general efficacy.
- **Insufficient justification for key design choices**:
  - The use of the Bradley-Terry model for the judge proxy is cited but never justified.
  - The multi-stage alternating debate structure (L stages with equal evidence per agent) is not clearly motivated over simpler alternatives (e.g., single-step evidence proposal or allowing the adversarial agent to present any number of pieces).
  - The payoff function G is introduced but its relation to the utility U is never specified.
  - The binary utility U (±1) is used without considering a continuous difference of judge judgments; the real-valued J is never exploited.
  - Training the judge using uniform random evidence (rather than on-policy debate evidence) is surprising; an ideal dataset would include human rankings over action–evidence pairs. Could the judge be updated in a loop with the argumentative agents?
  - The synthetic dataset (comparing a random action with the clinician’s action) likely contains many pairs where one action dominates, raising questions about dataset balance and training stability.
- **Inappropriate or missing baselines**:
  - A critical benchmark is missing: how often the baseline and the justifiable agent choose the same action.
  - The comparison to an isolated agent is flawed: that agent was trained with L=6 evidence but tested with L/2=3 evidence against a confuser adding 3 pieces; it should have been trained with only L/2 evidence to test fair robustness.
  - Using WIS to measure clinician performance (Figs 2a, 3a-d) is not obviously justified; why is importance sampling a reasonable evaluation metric for real-world clinician behavior?
- **Ethical considerations unaddressed**: The work has severe ethical implications (e.g., potential misuse of justifiable-sounding but harmful policies, reliance on proxy judges that may hide biases) but these are not discussed at all.
- **Terminology inaccuracy**: The approach is repeatedly called “reward shaping,” but this term technically refers to invariant transformations (Ng et al. 1999), which does not apply here; more precise language is needed.
- **Minor technical issues**:
  - The MDP definition omits the starting state distribution.
  - Notation error: γ^i instead of γ^t (Section 3.1); confusion between 𝒥 and J (Section 3.2); unnecessary ℰ(n) notation.
  - The payoff function G is defined but never linked to U.
  - In Section 5.4, the phrase “not only can agents significantly amplify the capabilities of a judge, but they also manage to recognize and correctly convince it of its true underlying preference” is unclear; the two clauses do not seem distinct.
  - Figure 4 legend labels (“with confused” / “without confused”) are inconsistent with the plot.
  - Architecture choices (two fully connected layers, size 256, parametric ReLU) appear ad-hoc and lack empirical justification.
  - The paper does not address that in real settings evidence pieces have unequal complexity to evaluate, and combinations of evidence may be non-trivial to assess.
