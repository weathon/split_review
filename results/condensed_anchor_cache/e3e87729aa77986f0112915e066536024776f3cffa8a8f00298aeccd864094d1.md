## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates how well LLMs (GPT-4 and GPT-4o) substitute for humans in auctions, motivated by cost savings (2,000+ auctions for <$250 vs. ~$15,000 for human experiments). It benchmarks LLM agents against established experimental results: revenue equivalence between first-price (FPSB) and second-price sealed-bid (SPSB) auctions, and improved play in obviously strategy-proof (OSP) ascending clock auctions (AC) and a blind variant (AC-B), under both independent private values (IPV) and affiliated private values (APV). Results show that LLMs overbid in FPSB and underbid in SPSB, producing a smaller separation than theory predicts, consistent with risk aversion. In the simpler AC format, LLM play is closer to theory. The authors also examine learning over repeated rounds. The study includes robustness checks and counterfactual experiments.

### Strengths
* Originality: First assessment of LLMs as human substitutes in auctions; addresses a new area in experimental economics.
* Quality: Well-reasoned, conducted, and written up.
* Clarity: Generally clear; paper structure (setting → existing theory/empirical results → simulation outcomes) is easy to follow.
* Significance: Contributes to the “jagged frontier” analysis of LLM capabilities; provides insights into LLM behavior in economic applications.
* Detailed analysis: Includes semantic analysis and counterfactual experiments to understand LLM decision-making.
* Robustness checks: Appendix includes checks that make results more convincing (Reviewer 4).
* Interesting and novel application; potentially a valuable addition to ICLR.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited contribution and novelty**: The experimental method and design are simple and not novel; the technique contribution is limited (Reviewer 4). No theoretical result is derived or developed from the experiments; lack of a formal model limits generalizability (Reviewer 3).
2. **Contradictions in evidence and claims**:
   * Abstract claims LLMs “can improve their play when given the opportunity to learn,” but Section 3.2.4 states “little evidence of learning over time.” Which is correct? Comparison to human learning (amateur vs. experienced) is needed (Reviewers 1, 4).
   * Abstract claims “validating a novel synthetic data-generating process to help discipline the study and design of auctions,” but the paper only answers the first research question (substitution); there is no demonstration of how this process improves mechanism design (Reviewer 4).
3. **Inadequate sample size**: 15 repetitions per setting may not represent the random distribution; results may be sensitive to specific draws, lacking statistical power (Reviewer 3). Also, only 15 rounds of repeated auction are tested; it is unclear whether more rounds would change learning results (Reviewer 4).
4. **Sensitivity to prompts and training data**: Prompts are not fully shared (Appendix A.1 omits RULE EXPLANATION, INSTRUCTIONS, PERSONA). The authors provide no robustness test of LLM behavior against different prompt phrasings. Informal vs. formal rule descriptions produce very different responses (e.g., one triggers “my dominant strategy is to bid truthfully,” another yields overbidding). Reliability for unknown results is questionable (Reviewer 2).
5. **Generalizability concerns**: Experiments use only a fixed number of bidders (n=3, often half of training data examples). Performance may deviate for other n; asymmetric auctions, correlated values, more complex auction rules (e.g., Combinatorial Auction Test Suite) are not explored. Practical utility is not convincingly demonstrated (Reviewer 1).
6. **Unsubstantiated claims and insufficient alternatives**:
   * Claim that LLMs exhibit risk aversion even when prompted not to is supported only by an appendix not included; needs comparison to alternative explanations (bounded rationality, strategic uncertainty) (Reviewer 1).
   * Claim that humans are “more truthful in second price sealed bid auctions than in ascending clock auctions” (Li, 2017) should consider other hypotheses (e.g., sunk cost, difficulty of Bayesian calculation). Same issue for claims about AC-B (Reviewer 1).
7. **Methodological concerns**:
   * Non-zero temperature (footnote 5) might induce trembling hand / quantal response equilibrium, making comparison to Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) inappropriate (Reviewer 1).
   * Valuations are uniform but bids constrained to integers; rounding strategy from Equation (2) BNE bids (non-integer) may introduce artefacts (Reviewer 1).
   * Why is LOESS-smoothed data used for regression? Is that standard? Clarification needed (Reviewer 4).
   * Figure 1 panels are reversed: left panel (bid=value theory) is SPSB, not FPSB (Reviewer 1).
8. **Missing robustness tests**:
   * Test with different “currency” units (e.g., dollars vs. billions of cowrie shells).
   * Compare results with and without the first step that asks LLM to generate a bidding plan explicitly (amateur vs. expert human bidding?) (Reviewer 1).
9. **Citation and explanation issues**:
   * Vickrey’s original paper should be cited for the SPSB result, not just Krishna 2009 (Reviewer 1).
   * More space should be given to explaining Li’s OSP concept (maybe remove the table at bottom of p.4) (Reviewer 1).
