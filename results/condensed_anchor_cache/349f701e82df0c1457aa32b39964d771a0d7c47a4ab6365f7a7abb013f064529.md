## Merged Review

### Summary
Sketch-to-Skill proposes a framework that uses a pair of 2D human-drawn sketches of a task (from two viewpoints) to generate 3D trajectories, which then serve as initial demonstrations for behaviour cloning and are refined via reinforcement learning (IBRL) with a discriminator-based reward. Reviewers see potential in using sketches to reduce reliance on teleoperation, but the consensus is that the practical value is not convincingly demonstrated. While praised for clear writing and thorough analysis within chosen tasks, the evaluation tasks are repeatedly judged too simple (short, single-stage, position-only, minimal clutter/occlusion), the sketch-to-3D generator requires substantial training data (85 teleoperated trajectories), the discriminator contribution is ambiguous and inconsistently beneficial, key ablations are missing, and the method’s scalability to complex, multi-stage, or orientation-aware tasks is doubtful. There is disagreement: some reviewers (3, 4) rate the novelty and results highly (8/8), while others (2, 5) emphasise limited novelty, practicality concerns, and weak evidence for generalisation (3/5).

### Strengths
- Innovative high-level idea: using human-drawn 2D trajectory sketches to bootstrap and guide RL for robotic manipulation, reducing the need for teleoperation data, specialised hardware, and expert knowledge.
- The method is clearly explained and logically designed (e.g., exploiting latent smoothness of the trajectory generator to produce multiple demonstrations per sketch via controlled noise injection).
- Results show that the approach can preserve performance (~96% of teleoperation baseline) while eliminating the need for real-time teleoperated demonstrations.
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and presents a thorough analysis within the scope of the chosen (simple) tasks; the Appendix is useful.
- Real-world tests on a button-pressing task demonstrate practical applicability of the method.
- The sketch-to-3D trajectory generation plus noise injection for data diversity is a novel contribution; the discriminator strengthens the link between user-provided sketches and learned behaviour (though its effectiveness is debated).
- The choice of MetaWorld, a common benchmark, facilitates reproducibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Task complexity and generality:** The evaluated tasks are too simple (short, single-stage, position-controlled). They do not involve adaptive behaviours (e.g., recovering a dropped object), multi-stage or longer-horizon motions (e.g., opening a box then picking from it, folding a shirt), orientation control, or cluttered/occluded scenes. Reviewers recommend testing on harder benchmarks such as robomimic, RLBench, or ManiSkill. Only one simple real-world task (button pressing) is shown; more diverse real-world tests are needed.
- **Practicality of the sketch-to-3D generator:** The approach requires pre-training a 3D trajectory generator on a dataset of paired sketches and trajectories. The generator needed 85 teleoperated trajectories (Appendix), which undermines the claim of reducing data burden. For a new task/domain, one would need to recollect such data, making direct teleoperation potentially simpler and more general. The generator also appears limited to position-only trajectories; providing time-dependent orientation through two 2D sketches is deemed non-trivial.
- **Generalisation concerns:** It is unclear how well the generator generalises to new tasks, new robots, or workspaces beyond its training distribution. Robustness to spatial correlation between the two sketches is not quantified; the ranges of applied augmentations (rotations, translations, scaling, elastic deformations) are not reported.
- **Discriminator utility:** The discriminator shows little to no consistent performance gain across tasks. In Fig. 6 (MetaWorld) and Fig. 8/14 (generated demos), performance with and without the discriminator is often comparable, and in some cases (e.g., CoffeePush, BoxClose) the no-discriminator version performs better. The discriminator reward weights appear arbitrary (no clear progression like 0.1, 0.01, 0.001), and its added complexity may not be justified. The no-discriminator ablation (Fig. 14) should be in the main paper rather than the appendix.
- **Ablations missing or insufficient:**
  - Number of sketches: only 3 hand-drawn sketches are used; no ablation on how performance changes with more (or fewer) sketches, or how it compares to IBRL with more teleoperated demonstrations.
  - Number of demonstrations per sketch: adding 10 demos vs. 1 demo per sketch yields inconsistent benefits (Figs. 8, 14) and diminishing returns; no clear evidence that the added complexity (noise injection) is worthwhile.
  - Generator architecture: no ablation on whether a VAE is crucial or other architectures would work.
  - RL finetuning method: not compared to alternatives like GAIL; only IBRL is tested.
  - Discriminator reward weight selection is not motivated.
- **Usability and user effort:** Sketching complex, multi-step trajectories may require significant user effort; overlapping or partially obscured paths are not addressed; no guidelines for choosing the sketch perspective (each task may need a different camera setup); common sketching errors (e.g., inconsistent pairs, temporal misalignment) are not discussed or evaluated, raising questions about robustness and feasibility in practice.
- **Missing context and comparisons:** The paper does not position itself against recent Vision-Language Agents (VLAs) like OpenVLA, which also aim to reduce teleoperation data. The advantages of RL finetuning over pure imitation learning in this sketch-guided setting are not discussed.
- **Real-world experiment limitations:** The real-world button-pressing task is basic; BC already achieves ~80%, and RL finetuning does not improve performance (same ~80%). The generator training required 85 teleop trajectories, which seems more effort than directly training an agent on those trajectories.
- **Minor issues:** Typo on line 71 (“a more approach”); Figs. could be made colour-blind friendly (e.g., textures in Fig. 7); the asterisk after “Replay Buffer” in Fig. 3 is unexplained.
