## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses counterfactual fairness (CF) under a partially known causal graph (CPDAG or MPDAG), relaxing the usual requirement of a fully known DAG. The authors propose a min‑max optimization framework that iterates over all DAGs in the equivalence class, estimates the worst‑case counterfactual effect of the sensitive attribute using the IDA algorithm to enumerate possible adjustment sets, and then trains the predictor to minimize the worst‑case violation. Unlike prior work that uses only non‑descendants of the sensitive attribute, this method incorporates all observed variables. Experiments on synthetic and real‑world data show promising accuracy while controlling fairness violations.

### Strengths
- Addresses a key shortcoming of counterfactual fairness: the need for complete knowledge of the causal graph.
- Proposes a new algorithm that is more efficient than existing state‑of‑the‑art methods (based on the cited related works).
- The paper is well‑written and the main method is intuitive.
- Experimental results are promising.
- The idea of enumerating possible adjustment variable sets via the IDA algorithm is reasonable for the targeted setting.

### Weaknesses
- **Identifiability of counterfactuals** (Reviewer 2 – severe concern):  
  The paper claims to achieve point identification of counterfactual predictions, but this is possible only because Proposition 3.4 does not condition on descendants of the sensitive attribute *A*. This contradicts the main claim of using **all** observed variables. Estimating counterfactual predictions for descendants of *A* (e.g., queries of the form ℙ(*M*<sub>*A*→*a*′</sub> | *A*=*a*, *M*=*m*)) requires strong additional assumptions; point identification is generally impossible without explicit parametric assumptions (Plecko and Bareinboim, 2022). The paper does not spell out such assumptions. The authors could consider: (i) imposing strong parametric assumptions, (ii) adopting a partial‑identification approach (Wu et al., 2019; but Melnychuk et al., 2023 show that even partial identification fails for continuous variables without stronger assumptions), or (iii) switching to a weaker but identifiable notion such as interventional fairness (Kilbertus et al., 2017).

- **Min‑max approach may coincide with FAIR** (Reviewer 2 – major concern):  
  In the toy example of Figure 1, the worst‑case DAG would include both *X*₁ and *X*₂ as descendants, making the proposed method equivalent to the FAIR approach that excludes all (possible) descendants of *A*. More generally, why does the worst‑case DAG not always include every possible descendant as an actual descendant? The authors should provide a theoretical result or a further toy example demonstrating that their min‑max method is indeed different from FAIR.

- **Sufficiency and motivation of the IDA algorithm** (Reviewer 3 – both a weakness and a question):  
  The IDA algorithm focuses on the **siblings** of the sensitive attribute *A* to find adjustment variables. It is unclear why this is sufficient to enumerate all possible confounders. If the IDA algorithm fails to identify some confounders, the resulting propensity score model cannot eliminate confounding bias, undermining the fairness guarantee. The authors should elaborate on why only siblings need to be considered and why the procedure is complete.

- **Lack of explicit limitations** (Reviewer 1):  
  The paper does not discuss limitations, such as how the method would handle more than one protected attribute.

- **Missing comparison** (Reviewer 1, question):  
  How does the proposed approach compare to the companion paper “When Worlds Collide: Integrating Different Counterfactual Assumptions in Fairness” (Kusner et al., NIPS 2017), which also explores counterfactual fairness under multiple worldviews (i.e., different DAGs) and shows how to balance fairness across worlds?

- **Confusing over “variable selection”** (Reviewer 1):  
  The paper sometimes refers to its approach as a variable selection method (e.g., start of Section 3.4), yet the algorithm actually uses **all** attributes. In a variable selection framework one expects to return a subset of attributes, which is not what the experiments evaluate.

- **Clarity issues** (Reviewer 3):  
  - It is unclear why the formula for ℙ(*Ŷ*<sub>*A*←*a*</sub> = *y*) is presented in Section 3.3. If it is used in the method, the computation would be prohibitive for high‑dimensional parent sets *pa*(*A*)=*z*.
  - The paper should explicitly state that the prediction *Ŷ* is a node (endogenous variable) in the causal graph (Section 2.2). Similarly, is the observed outcome *Y* also a node? This is needed to understand the problem setting.
