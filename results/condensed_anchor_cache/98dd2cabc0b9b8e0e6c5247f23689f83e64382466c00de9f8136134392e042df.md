## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces "code reasoning" as a task requiring both reasoning and recall, and proposes three meta-benchmarks (inductive, deductive, abductive) instantiated into eight benchmarks (List Function, MiniARC, RobustFill, DeepCoder, CRUXEval, LiveCodeBench). It also presents the RHDA pipeline (Reflective Hypothesis Decomposition and Amendment), which iteratively decomposes complex problems, verifies results via tools, and amends hypotheses. Evaluated on GPT-4o and Claude 3.5, RHDA achieves up to 3× performance gains, with specific improvements: 18.45% on List Function, 5.89% on MiniARC, 33.31% on RobustFill, 12.02% on DeepCoder, 90.62% on CRUXEval deductive, 84.16% on LiveCodeBench deductive, 83.75% on CRUXEval abductive, 71.57% on LiveCodeBench abductive. The method is also demonstrated on VirtualHome planning tasks.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem formulation and pipeline**: The paper defines code reasoning as an intermediate task between memory and reasoning, and proposes RHDA as a new iterative amendment method. One reviewer noted the approach has significant potential and that the benchmark helps understand reasoning–recall boundaries.
- **Comprehensive benchmark suite**: Three meta-benchmarks covering inductive, deductive, and abductive reasoning with eight concrete tasks, evaluated across two model families (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5). Ablation studies and different backends (CoT, PoT, Sub-Hyp, IO) are included.
- **Strong quantitative results**: RHDA achieves substantial gains over baselines (e.g., 33.31% on RobustFill, 90.62% on CRUXEval deductive). The pipeline is systematically explored and the results are clearly reported.
- **Extensibility**: Successful demonstration on VirtualHome shows the framework can handle complex, real-world household scenarios.
- **Clarity and significance**: The paper is generally easy to follow and addresses an important question about understanding LLM capabilities and limitations.

### Weaknesses
- **Loose or imprecise arguments**: Multiple reviewers found claims “a bit loose or controversial.” The positioning of code reasoning “between memory and reasoning” is questioned because most reasoning tasks (e.g., math) also require memory of syntax from training; the spectrum in Figure 1 is not uniquely defined. The paper assumes familiarity with cognitive-science terms (System 1/2) without providing ML context; for an ICLR audience these should be introduced from a machine learning perspective.
- **Insufficient validation and precision**: One reviewer (score 5) stressed that claims about LLM capabilities need mathematical or empirical validation. The goal to “explore boundaries of LLM capabilities” is vague for ML practitioners. Several claims lack definitions or empirical backing; the rebuttal helped but the paper must be revised accordingly.
- **Limited model diversity**: Only two LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5) are tested. Testing more models is needed to support the claim of understanding LLMs’ code reasoning abilities; existing work already shows LLMs fail on complex reasoning, so the evaluation is not surprising.
- **Missing baseline comparison**: The strongest contribution could be RHDA, but it is not compared to self-refine or other iterative refinement methods. The difference between Sub-Hyp and Chain-of-Thought is not clarified, and the rationale for why the IO prompt sometimes outperforms RHDA (page 6) is not explained.
- **Lack of theoretical depth**: The paper does not analyze why code as a representation is beneficial for reasoning versus other forms, nor does it connect to information-theoretic properties or cognitive models. A more rigorous theoretical grounding is missing.
- **Incomplete error analysis**: Failure modes of RHDA are not analyzed; there is no breakdown of whether errors occur during initial hypothesis formation, decomposition, verification, or amendment. The sensitivity of RHDA to the quality of initial decomposition is not studied. The appendix for example analysis is empty, and a “quantitative analysis” on page 7 should be “qualitative.”
- **Computational and scalability concerns**: No analysis of computational overhead (API calls, inference time, memory) compared to single-pass methods like CoT or PoT. The trade-off between number of RHDA iterations and performance gains is not explored. It is unclear whether RHDA generalizes to non-symbolic domains.
- **Limited real-world validation**: VirtualHome evaluation lacks quantitative metrics and standardized benchmarks; only two examples are shown. It remains unclear how the method scales to more complex, multi-agent, or dynamic environments.
- **Minority critical perspective**: One reviewer (score 5) was notably more negative, emphasizing that the paper’s arguments are too loose, the cognitive psychology framing is inappropriate for ICLR, and insufficient empirical evidence is provided to back claims.
