## Merged Review

### Summary

TrojanRAG performs a backdoor attack on Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) by manipulating the retriever to return poisoned knowledge for trigger-carrying queries, inducing any downstream LLM to produce attacker-desired outputs. The attack uses contrastive learning to jointly optimize multiple independent trigger–response pairs and a knowledge graph for fine-grained hard matching. Three attack scenarios are defined: deliberate misinformation, unintended propagation of biased content, and forced jailbreaking. Experiments are conducted across 11 tasks and 6 LLMs (including Llama2 and Vicuna), evaluating effectiveness, robustness, transferability, and stealth. The method shows high attack success with minimal utility loss. Reviewer 3 (score 5) finds it the first systematic study of backdoor risks in RAG-augmented LLMs and sees the contribution as significant; other reviewers (scores 3) acknowledge the novelty but raise substantial concerns about threat model realism, experimental setup, clarity, and missing baselines.

### Strengths

- **Novel attack mechanism.** The paper is the first to systematically study backdoor attacks on LLMs via RAG by poisoning the retriever instead of the LLM itself, making the attack stealthy and avoiding direct model manipulation. The use of orthogonal contrastive learning to maintain multiple independent backdoors with minimal interference to clean retrieval is technically sound. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- **Comprehensive evaluation.** Extensive experiments cover 11 tasks (QA, sentiment classification, topic classification, bias induction, jailbreaking) and 6 LLMs, with metrics for attack success rate, utility preservation, and robustness across three scenarios. Transferability and stealth are also assessed. (R1, R2, R4, R5)
- **Practical relevance.** The work exposes a timely vulnerability in RAG-based LLMs, which are increasingly deployed in real-world applications such as question answering and information retrieval. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Effective use of contrastive learning.** The joint backdoor optimization ensures backdoors are independent and do not interfere with clean data retrieval, maintaining model performance while embedding triggers. (R2)
- **Multiple attack scenarios.** The paper standardizes three scenarios (deceptive manipulation, unintentional diffusion of bias, and forced jailbreaking), broadening the understanding of RAG-specific backdoor threats beyond prompt manipulation. (R1, R3)

### Weaknesses

- **Unrealistic threat model.** The attacker is assumed to fully control the retriever’s training process to inject backdoors. This is a very strong assumption that may limit real-world applicability. (R3)
*Magnitude: Major; Reviewer 3 confidence 5.*

- **Practical relevance of scenarios 1 and 3 is questionable.** In scenario 1 (misinformation) and scenario 3 (jailbreaking), if the attacker can already inject harmful content into the RAG database, they could directly disseminate that content via simpler means (e.g., emails, instant messages). True jailbreaking should bypass the LLM’s internal safety guardrail, but here harmful content is directly provided as retrieved input, not actually “breaking” the LLM. Additionally, basic input/output filtering or safety-aligned LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5, GPT-4) would significantly reduce attack effectiveness; the authors did not test on safety-aligned models. R4 was unable to reproduce the reported jailbreaking and gender bias results on GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o-mini, contradicting Figure 4. (R1, R4, R5)
*Magnitude: Major; R1, R4, R5 all raise this.*

- **Bias attack design is unclear.** In scenario 2, multiple types of bias (gender, age, nationality) can be triggered by the same interrogative word (“who”). How these different biases are assigned and controlled within the multi-backdoor framework is not sufficiently explained. (R2)
*Magnitude: Minor; R2 confidence 4.*

- **CoT reasoning setup is inconsistent.** The few-shot CoT experiments introduce extra retrieved documents, but few-shot CoT is inherently a closed-book setting where the model should rely only on the given examples. This introduces an artificial testing scenario that may overstate effectiveness. (R4)
*Magnitude: Major; R4 confidence 4.*

- **Limited attack flexibility.** The joint backdoor optimization restricts each interrogative category (e.g., “who”) to a single target output, constraining the range of possible responses per query type. (R2)
*Magnitude: Minor; R2 confidence 4.*

- **Missing baselines and comparisons.** The paper lacks baselines from prior backdoor attacks on retrievers (e.g., “Backdoor Attacks on Dense Passage Retrievers”, arXiv 2024). The “Prompt” baseline cited in Line 375 is not explained, and it is unclear why TrojanRAG outperforms an in-context learning backdoor that simply adds a trigger to the prompt without poisoning the retriever. (R3, R5)
*Magnitude: Major; R3, R5.*

- **Writing and presentation issues.** Section 3.2 is confusing: extensive notation is introduced without sufficient explanation, and the purpose of Knowledge Graph Enhancement remains unclear. The paper makes exaggerated claims, e.g., “first to comprehensively expose the threats of backdoor attacks on LLMs,” ignoring existing work on backdoor attacks directly on LLMs (e.g., [2,3] cited by R3). Overall readability is low (Presentation score 1 from R5). (R3, R4, R5)
*Magnitude: Moderate; multiple reviewers.*

- **Insufficient defense discussion.** Only a brief discussion is given. No experiments are conducted on countermeasures like retokenization (which could disrupt trigger patterns) or Advanced RAG with rerankers (which may filter poisoned responses). (R2)
*Magnitude: Minor; R2 confidence 4.*

- **Plug-and-play is unrealistic.** The attack requires end‑users to consistently query the poisoned dataset on the specific compromised tasks, which is unlikely in diverse real-world RAG deployments. (R4)
*Magnitude: Moderate; R4 confidence 4.*

- **Poisoned data percentage not contextualized.** The paper reports injecting 1–6% poisoned data, but does not clarify how this percentage relates to database scale or how many poisoned facts are needed per target knowledge piece across different corpus sizes. The datasets used may not be standard for evaluating RAG performance at varied scales. (R1)
*Magnitude: Minor; R1 confidence 5.*

- **Missing related work on ranking models.** Since the attack essentially targets the retriever, related works on attacking ranking models should have been discussed. (R1)
*Magnitude: Minor; R1 confidence 5.*

- **Technical contribution not clearly differentiated from prior work on backdoor attacks on retrievers.** The paper merely extends existing retriever backdoor attacks to the RAG setting without providing new insights into attacking LLMs via RAG. (R3)
*Magnitude: Moderate; R3 confidence 5.*

- **Unclear how the retriever can output an explicit refusal phrase.** In Figure 21 (Line 1577), the retriever is shown retrieving the string “I’m sorry, I can’t do that” as knowledge, which is unlikely to result from cosine similarity or dot product matching in the embedding space. (R5)
*Magnitude: Minor; R5 confidence 3.*

- **Transferability trigger swapping not explained.** In RQ5, swapping triggers (e.g., cf+who to mn+who) still retrieves the original poisoned knowledge, despite orthogonal triggers supposedly not activating each other. The mechanism behind this transferability is unclear. (R2)
*Magnitude: Minor; R2 confidence 4.*

- **Need for safety-aligned model evaluation.** The current experiments use Llama2 (version unspecified) and Vicuna, which are not strongly safety-aligned. For conclusions about harmfulness, experiments on aligned models (e.g., GPT-3.5/4) are necessary. (R5, R1) Also, R1 requests GPT-4/3.5 experiments in scenario 3. (R1)
*Magnitude: Major; R1, R5.*
