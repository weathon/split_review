## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DrIM, a nearest-neighbor imputation method for heterogeneous tabular data that uses a language model (BERT) to convert tabular rows into text, replace missing entries with mask tokens, and compute similarities in the LLM-derived representation space. A contrastive learning loss refines these representations. Experiments on 10 datasets with 14 baseline methods evaluate downstream machine learning utility (MLu). Reviewers note the topic is important and the experiments extensive, but raise serious concerns about novelty, the suitability of BERT as an “LLM,” the representational adequacy of language models for tabular data, insufficient experimental depth and analysis, and limited comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Relevant and practically important problem**: missing data in heterogeneous tabular datasets is a significant challenge in ML (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Interesting use of language model context for imputation**: leveraging textual representations to handle missingness and heterogeneity is a promising idea (Reviewers 1,4).
- **Extensive experimental setup**: evaluation on 10 datasets with 14 baselines is convincing and thorough (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- **Clear writing and easy to follow**: the paper is well-structured and the idea is clearly explained (Reviewers 1,3).
- **Experiments and code provided**: the submission includes code, supporting reproducibility (Reviewer 4).
- **Proposed method shows competitive performance at missingness level 0.3**: results indicate better MLu than SOTA baselines (Reviewer 1).
- **Statistical analysis of results supports effectiveness**: method exhibits robust performance (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty**: the LLM component is used only for similarity computation, and the contrastive learning component is very simple. The method is largely a nearest-neighbor imputer with a learned embedding, not a fundamentally new approach (Reviewer 2). One reviewer explicitly states the contribution is marginal (Reviewer 2).
- **Misleading title and terminology**: BERT cannot be considered a large language model (LLM) by current standards (e.g., ChatGPT); using “LLM” in the title and text creates false expectations and deviates from common usage (Reviewers 1,2). Multiple reviewers raise this issue as confusing and inaccurate.
- **Insufficient representational capability of language models for tabular data**: existing work [1,2,3] indicates that language models (especially without fine-tuning) have poor representation capacity for numerical tabular data. The paper does not address this contradiction, and results may be unreliable if the textual encoding fails to capture numeric semantics (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing essential related work and literature positioning**: the paper does not cite relevant prior work on LLM-based imputation (e.g., “CLAIM Your Data”, “LLM-Forest for Health Tabular Data Imputation”). Consequently, it fails to clarify its position, differences, and advantages relative to those methods (Reviewer 1).
- **Insufficient experimental depth and analysis**: the experimental section is only three short paragraphs; important discussions are missing, such as the sharp performance drop of the proposed method at high missingness rates (e.g., 0.8) in feature selection, and the fact that Remasker outperforms DrIM_base in F1 and matches DrIM_Fine in model selection (Reviewers 1,2,3). Multiple reviewers urge more extensive analysis of the results.
- **Limited ablation and sensitivity analysis**: the sensitivity analysis shows the proposed methods suffer greater decline in feature selection metric than baselines; also, the method’s performance at missingness levels other than 0.3 is unsatisfactory but not adequately analyzed (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of comparison with other PLMs/LLMs**: only BERT is used. Reviewers request experiments with models like ChatGPT or other pretrained language models to validate the generality and contribution of the approach (Reviewers 1,2).
- **Problematic construction of contrastive learning positive pairs**: the method assumes missing at random (MAR), but MAR may not hold in many domains (e.g., medical expert judgment leads to non-random missingness). This assumption is not discussed or justified (Reviewer 3).
- **Interpretability claim is unconvincing**: the statement that “interpretability of contrastive learning deserves further investigation” is puzzling; the contrastive injection is straightforward and the improvement obvious. The authors would need to consider techniques like supervised fine-tuning to explain variations, not just contrastive learning (Reviewer 3).
- **Paper length and mathematical content**: the paper is lengthy but includes many basic formulas (e.g., cosine similarity) that do not add depth; it lacks theoretical proofs or bounds (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 considers it overlong for the contribution.
- **Missing discussion on application scope**: the method is designed for single-table data; questions remain about applicability to relational databases or multi-table scenarios, which would limit its practical use (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor formatting issues**: citations in lines 36–37 lack parentheses (Reviewer 3).
