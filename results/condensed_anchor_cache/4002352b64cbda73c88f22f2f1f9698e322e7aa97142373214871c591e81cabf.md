## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses fairness under demographic scarce regime, where sensitive attributes are only partially observed. It proposes a two-step framework: (1) train an attribute classifier to predict missing sensitive attributes using a teacher-student distillation approach with uncertainty estimation; (2) train a label classifier with fairness constraints only on samples whose predicted sensitive attributes have low uncertainty (high confidence). The method is evaluated on five fairness benchmark datasets.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: The paper tackles a realistic and significant challenge – enforcing fairness when demographic information is unavailable due to privacy or data collection limitations. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)
- **Sound technique**: One reviewer (Reviewer 3) found the proposed technique to be sound. (Reviewer 3)
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well-written with clear sections and logical flow. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Comprehensive experiments**: Evaluation on five real datasets across diverse classification tasks. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Reproducibility**: Detailed experimental setup, data sources, and code availability are provided, enhancing trustworthiness. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Useful investigations**: The paper analyzes the relationship between uncertainty threshold and sensitive information encoding, and examines how uncertainty in sensitive attributes impacts fairness of non-fairness-aware classifiers, supporting the central hypothesis. (Reviewer 2)
- **Citations**: Related works are effectively cited. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2): The method essentially consists of training a proxy classifier (with student-teacher transfer learning) and thresholding by confidence. This is not a significant advance over existing baselines (Reviewer 1). The chosen attribute classifiers (Proxy-kNN, Proxy-DNN) are simplistic and not well-documented in the literature (Reviewer 2). While Reviewer 3 considered the technique sound, the other reviewers found the contribution weak.
- **Missing comparisons to relevant prior work** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2): The paper does not compare against methods for fairness with missing/unobserved sensitive attributes, such as Diana et al. (2022), Chen et al. (2019), Prost et al. (2021), Fogliato et al. (2020), Zhu et al. (2023), Yan et al. (2020) (Reviewer 1), and Awasthi et al. (2021) (Reviewer 2). The baselines used (Lahoti et al., Hashimoto et al., Levy et al., Yan et al., Zhao et al.) assume no access to sensitive attributes at all, making the experimental setting fundamentally different and the comparison unfair (Reviewer 2). Both reviewers request these comparisons.
- **Ethical concerns** (Reviewer 2): Constructing a classifier with the objective of predicting the sensitive attribute of instances raises legal and moral issues, especially when individuals choose not to disclose that information. Incorporating privacy properties (e.g., Diana et al. 2022) would be more appropriate.
- **Overstated claims about outperforming true sensitive attributes** (Reviewer 2): The abstract claims the framework outperforms models trained with true sensitive attributes, but this is only shown for one fairness intervention (Figure 2) and is not consistent across all cases (Figure 9 uses a different intervention and shows less consistent improvement). The claim should be qualified, and more diverse fairness-enhancing interventions should be examined to understand the impact of the chosen intervention.
- **Unresolved threshold selection** (Reviewer 2 Q3, Reviewer 3 W1): The uncertainty threshold greatly impacts performance, and it is unclear how to select it in practice without relying on test data. Does tuning on a validation set reveal the optimal threshold, and does it generalize to the test set? This is a critical practical limitation that needs clarification or new experiments.
- **Missing privacy-related literature** (Reviewer 2): The related works section should include privacy-focused approaches that handle sensitive attributes via secure multiparty computation or differential privacy (e.g., Veale and Binns, Kilbertus et al., Jagielski et al.), which are relevant when inferring sensitive information.
- **Lack of realistic scenario justification** (Reviewer 3): The paper should provide a concrete example where D1 has no demographic info but has labels, and D2 has demographics but no labels, to better motivate the setting.
- **Ambiguous experimental details** (Reviewer 2 Q2, Reviewer 3 Q1/Q2/Q3):
  - Was the uncertainty threshold tuned on a validation set from the 0.7 training data or selected as the best-performing on the 0.3 test data? (Reviewer 3 Q1)
  - In Section 3, the paper assumes a small set of samples from the joint distribution X×Y×A to estimate bias. How is this reflected in experiments? If it refers to the 0.3 test data, this is not a realistic requirement (Reviewer 3 Q2).
  - For baselines that lack sensitive attributes (Lahoti et al., Hashimoto et al., Levy et al., Yan et al., Zhao et al.), what dataset is fed: D1, D1+D2, or only D(link unavailable)? (Reviewer 2 Q2)
  - The label classifier is trained only on subset D(link unavailable) ⊂ D1. Could it instead be trained on the entire D1 with fairness constraints only on D(link unavailable) (fairness loss 0 for the rest) to potentially increase accuracy when the threshold is low? (Reviewer 3 Q3)
- **Alternative ethical approach not explored** (Reviewer 2 Q1): Instead of using high-confidence samples for fairness, why not use samples with highest uncertainty in the sensitive attribute to train a non-fairness-aware classifier? This might be more ethically favorable, but was not considered.
