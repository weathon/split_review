## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a theoretical framework connecting low-dimensional neural representations to generalization through interpolation and convex combinations. It defines interpolation probability (probability a test point falls in the convex hull of training representations), derives a generalization bound (Theorem 5.1) that tightens as representation dimension decreases, and studies how convex hull volume and training data size affect this probability. Experiments on MNIST with a 5‑layer MLP show decreasing intrinsic dimension and increasing interpolation probability during training. Reviewer scores range from 1 to 5 (mean 3); most reviewers find limited novelty and technical contribution, while one reviewer (score 5) praises the framework’s novelty and clarity.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to follow and clearly written (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The idea of connecting effective dimension to generalization via convex hull and interpolation is interesting and attempts to unify generalization with low‑rankness (Reviewer 1).
- Clear connection between convex hull of training points and generalization, supported by empirical justification (Reviewer 2).
- **Reviewer 4 (score 5)** emphasized the novelty of combining interpolation and convex combinations to study low‑dimensional representations, a combination not extensively explored before. They found the presentation sound, with relevant theoretical contributions (generalization error upper bound in the interpolation regime) and empirical results on standard models. Limitations are clearly stated.
- The authors attempt to verify their findings with experiments (Reviewer 5, score 1 – the only positive point they noted).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical novelty**: Theorem 5.1 is a standard application of McDiarmid’s inequality, well known in high‑dimensional statistics; Proposition 1 is trivial and should be stated only as an observation. Most claims are straightforward or drawn from existing literature. There is no comparison with state‑of‑the‑art generalization bounds; the proposed bound is a naive concentration inequality and likely much weaker (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5). **One reviewer (score 1)** was particularly critical, stating that the only new results are simple applications of concentration or integral inequalities.
- **Assumptions too strong or unclear**:
  - The Lipschitz assumption on the loss function is too strong: it implies that for an interpolating network, the loss on any point close to a training point is close to zero *regardless* of the actual output, allowing arbitrarily large errors without affecting the bound (Reviewer 1).
  - The term “interpolation regime” is never defined (e.g., is it zero training error?) (Reviewer 5).
  - The link between Theorem 5.1 and the concept of interpolation probability is unclear (Reviewer 3).
  - The theorem uses McDiarmid’s inequality but does not explicitly state the required i.i.d. assumption on (x,y) (Reviewer 3).
- **Poor writing and undefined notation**:
  - Many symbols are not defined: \(\mathcal{R}\) in Eq. (4) (also unclear how it is both a set and a function), \(\Phi^n\) in Eq. (12), \(i\) in Eq. (12), \(d\lambda(x)\) and \(\mathrm{Vol}(C)\) in Eq. (13) (Reviewers 1, 2).
  - Formatting errors: Proposition numbering uses 1,2,… while theorem numbering uses Thm 6.1,6.2,…; on page 7 the interpolation probabilities for triangle and circle are compared twice (Reviewer 5).
  - Definition 3: what is \(\mathcal{R}\) and how is it both a set and a function? (Reviewer 1).
- **Insufficient experimental detail and reproducibility**:
  - No architecture details or training hyperparameters are given for the “5‑layer MLP” used in Section 4; no supplementary materials or code are provided, hindering reproduction (Reviewer 4).
  - Experiments are limited to MNIST with a simple fully‑connected network. The appendix shows that complex architectures (e.g., AlexNet on CIFAR‑10) do **not** follow the reported pattern – intrinsic dimension increases after an initial decrease – whereas VGG‑16 does. This raises concerns about scalability to higher‑dimensional data and more sophisticated models (Reviewer 4).
  - The authors did not compare temporal behavior of intrinsic dimension, convex hull volume, and interpolation probability on higher‑dimensional datasets such as Imagenette or ImageNet (Reviewer 4’s question, also a missing experiment).
- **Interpretations and claims not convincing**:
  - Proposition 2 is trivial: if the measure of a set increases, the probability of being in it increases; it says nothing about large‑measure low‑probability sets (Reviewer 1).
  - Section 8 on data volume yields no new insight (Reviewer 1).
  - The relationship between generalization bound and data volume is unclear: e.g., data with d=1 and volume=100 vs. d=10 and volume=1000 – the latter has higher interpolation probability but the bound is tighter for d=1 (Reviewer 5).
  - It is unclear why interpolation probabilities are important and where the interpolation assumption is used in Theorem 5.1 (Reviewer 5).
  - The justification that \(L = O(\sqrt{d})\) in Theorem 5.1 is questionable; for a bounded loss, \(L\) should scale as \(O(1/\sqrt{d})\), rendering \(d\) irrelevant to the bound (Reviewer 5).
- **Scalability concerns**: The focus on interpolation may be too constrained, as real‑world generalization often requires extrapolation beyond the convex hull – a limitation the authors acknowledge but do not address. Complex architectures like AlexNet on CIFAR‑10 exhibit different dynamics, questioning the framework’s generality (Reviewer 4).
