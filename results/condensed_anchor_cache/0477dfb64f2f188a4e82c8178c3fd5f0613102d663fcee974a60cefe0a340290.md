## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses causal representation learning by proposing a Bayesian inference method to recover latent linear Gaussian additive-noise SCMs (structure, parameters, and causal variables) from low-level observational data with known interventions. The approach uses variational inference, sampling adjacency and covariance matrices to generate interventional distributions. Experiments are conducted on synthetic 5-node DAGs and a simple causally generated image dataset.

### Strengths
- **Problem importance**: The estimation of latent causal models is a crucial task in causal representation learning (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Clarity and motivation**: The paper is well written, with a clear motivation and a good literature review that covers necessary theoretical background (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Presentation of experimental results**: Experimental results are well‑presented, and plots are useful for understanding the outcomes (Reviewer 4).
- **Theoretical discussion**: The authors make a commendable effort to discuss theoretical points that facilitate understanding of the approach (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing theoretical identifiability analysis**: All four reviewers note the absence of a proof or discussion of identifiability for the latent SCM. While recent works (e.g., Brehmer et al. 2022 [1], Liu et al. 2022 [2]) prove identifiability under assumptions like hard/soft interventions, this paper does not clarify whether its setting (linear Gaussian, known interventions) satisfies those assumptions or whether faithfulness holds. Without such guarantees, it is unclear if the true causal structure can be recovered even under ideal conditions, raising concerns about overfitting and generalization to unseen interventions (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Limited novelty**: The core inference machinery closely resembles prior work such as Cundy et al. (2021) [3] (Bayesian causal discovery via variational inference), and the extension to a latent space does not provide significant technical novelty. The assumptions (linear latent model, known intervention targets) are restrictive, and the paper does not clarify how it improves over existing methods like Brehmer et al. (2022) which handle unknown graphs and weaker assumptions (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). Reviewer 3 additionally notes that the restriction to linear models is a major limitation.
- **Insufficient experimental validation**: All reviewers find the experiments too narrow:
    - Only a single setting with 5 latent variables and 20 random interventions is tested (Reviewers 2, 3). More extensive experiments on graphs of varying size, density, and sample size are needed (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
    - The image dataset is too simple; more complex, realistic benchmarks like Causal3DIdent [4] and CausalCircuit [1] should be used (Reviewers 1, 4).
    - No comparisons with state‑of‑the‑art methods (e.g., Brehmer et al. 2022 [1]) are provided, making it impossible to assess relative performance (Reviewers 1, 4).
    - Sensitivity analyses and ablation studies on hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, noise level, causal strength, number of interventions) are absent (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Missing details on acyclicity enforcement**: The paper does not explain how the learned adjacency matrix is constrained to represent a DAG. Parameterizing the lower triangle is mentioned but not fully justified; it is unclear how ancestral sampling guarantees acyclic structures and how this relates to causal assumptions (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Unclear model architecture and training details**: Figure 2 lacks a caption that would help readers understand the algorithm early on. The 3‑layer neural network used for the observation model is not described (layer types, dimensions, activations). Algorithm 1 does not clarify how loss terms (lines 14‑15) are computed, nor how gradients flow through the sampling steps (lines 3, 10) without breaking the computational graph (Reviewer 4).
- **Assumption about intervention knowledge**: The method requires known intervention targets on latent variables. In real‑world settings it is unclear how such interventions would be known or applied when the causal variables are unobserved (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Unknown number of latent variables**: The number of latent variables \(d\) is assumed known in advance; no guidance or adaptive method is provided (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **No mention of model failure under nonlinearity**: The work focuses exclusively on linear Gaussian models and does not discuss how the algorithm would fail or require modification for nonlinear SCMs (Reviewer 4).
- **The role of interventional data is not explored**: The impact of the number of interventions provided (e.g., more vs. fewer) on performance is not studied (Reviewer 4). For the Chemistry dataset, the causal structure is not precisely stated (Reviewer 4).
- **Overfitting and generalization**: There is no theoretical or empirical demonstration that the learned SCM will match interventional distributions that were not present in the training data (Reviewer 4).

*Note on reviewer disagreement*: Overall, reviewers disagreed on the paper’s quality. Two reviewers gave scores of 5, noting the clarity and well‑presented experiments, while two gave scores of 3, criticizing the lack of novelty and limited experimental scope. All four reviewers concurred on the critical absence of identifiability analysis and insufficient experimentation.
