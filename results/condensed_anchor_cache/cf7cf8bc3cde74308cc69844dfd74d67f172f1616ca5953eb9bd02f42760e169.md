## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper releases HelpSteer2-Preference, a dataset providing pairwise preference annotations with human-written justifications to complement the existing Likert-scale ratings in HelpSteer2. This dual-format collection enables the first head-to-head comparison of Bradley-Terry (BT) and Regression-style reward models when adequately matched for data. Based on insights from this comparison, the authors propose a hybrid approach that combines both paradigms. A Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct model tuned with this method scores 94.1 on RewardBench (top of 140+ models as of 1 Oct 2024), and the reward model proves effective in RLHF for instruction-following alignment. The dataset and model are open-sourced (CC-BY-4.0).

### Strengths

- **High-quality, dual-format dataset creation.** The HelpSteer2-Preference dataset provides carefully annotated preference pairs alongside existing Likert-5 ratings, with human-written justifications that enhance interpretability. The data collection process is well-designed with strong inter-annotator agreement and quality filtering. This enables nuanced training of reward models and supports applications requiring transparency (e.g., human-in-the-loop systems).
- **Head-to-head comparison of reward modeling paradigms.** The paper conducts a detailed, fair comparison between Bradley-Terry and Regression-style reward models, which was previously difficult due to incompatible data formats in existing datasets. The authors analyze differences between pointwise and pairwise annotations.
- **Effective hybrid model achieving state-of-the-art performance.** The proposed combination of BT and Regression reward models achieves the top score (94.1) on RewardBench as of 1 Oct 2024, outperforming over 140 other reward models. The model also improves LLaMA-3.1 through preference alignment.
- **Open-source contribution.** The dataset and trained reward model are publicly released, providing a valuable resource for the community.
- **Clear writing and comprehensive analysis.** The paper is well-structured and the data collection analysis is detailed and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses

- **Motivation overclaimed.** One reviewer (score 5/contribution 2) noted that the claim that no datasets existed for comparing BT and Regression models is overstated, because preference datasets with pointwise scores can be converted into pairwise setups. While the authors argue that direct pairwise annotation provides different expectations, the comparison was not totally undoable. The paper lacks experimental evidence that converting HelpSteer2’s regression-style data to preference pairs fails to capture nuances. This is a missing comparison.
- **Conflict with prior work on margin term.** Reviewer 1 observed that the paper finds a BT margin term (eq.2) effective, contradicting the Llama 3 paper which removed the margin due to diminishing improvements after data scaling. No explanation is provided for this discrepancy.
- **Missing analyses that would strengthen practical insights.**
    - No evaluation on out-of-distribution prompts.
    - No consideration of annotation costs (preference annotations may be cheaper than Likert scores, so a fair comparison would factor this in).
    - No robustness analysis to annotation errors (e.g., 5% reversed preferences or extreme Likert mislabels like 9→1).
    - No evaluation on standard language modeling tasks to quantify the alignment tax.
    - No statistical significance testing to validate the hybrid model’s performance improvements.
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons.** The paper does not compare the proposed dataset/reward model against other existing preference datasets or reward models (e.g., Llama3-70B-SteerLM-RM, TextEval-Llama3.1-70B) in RLHF (PPO) or DPO settings. For DPO, the authors did not test using a preference dataset converted from HelpSteer2. This limits the assessment of the dataset’s necessity and differentiation from alternatives.
- **Lack of generalizability evidence.** Experiments are conducted mainly on general chat tasks with subjective metrics (LLM-as-a-judge). Conclusions may not generalize to reasoning or other domains known to differ from chatting.
- **Minority reviewer concern about novelty.** One reviewer (score 5) found the results not surprising or informative enough to serve as the paper’s main claim, and that the hybrid approach is an incremental improvement rather than a fundamental new technique. The same reviewer also noted that experiments are hard to parse with no highlights or bolded high-level findings.
- **Dataset complexity and trade-offs.**
    - The dual data format (ratings + preferences) requires complex preprocessing, custom data loaders, and potentially separate model architectures or loss functions, increasing engineering overhead.
    - Human-written justifications introduce variability due to subjective writing styles and biases, lacking standardized vocabulary or structure.
    - Strict filtering of high-variability samples reduces data volume, potentially limiting model performance and natural diversity.
    - The dataset is tailored to BT and Regression paradigms, limiting its applicability to other reward structures or multi-modal inputs.
- **Missing experimental details.**
    - The paper does not specify whether a prompt filter was applied to exclude RewardBench prompts from the training set.
    - RL training details are insufficient: approximately 10k examples trained for fewer than 100 iterations, but stopping criteria and KL divergence values from the reference policy are not clarified.
    - It is unclear how many hours of human labor were used for annotation.
- **Unaddressed ablations and extensions.**
    - No analysis of how the SteerLM regression model performs when initialized with the BT model (and vice-versa for the hybrid).
    - No comparison of the proposed combination method with standard model ensembling.
    - The validity of conclusions for rankings with more than two responses (e.g., 5-response ranking) is not addressed.
