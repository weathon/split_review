## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates LLM planning in forward and backward directions across three domains (shortest path in graphs, array transformation, Blocksworld). It measures direction-specific difficulty via search-space size, observes that LLMs plan better in the easier direction but exhibit a systematic bias against backward planning, and proposes a method that flips the problem (exchanging initial and goal states) so that forward planning can be applied from the goal. The method is combined with self-verification and sampling, yielding 4–24% improvement in success rates over baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper conducts a thorough set of experiments testing whether flipping initial and goal states improves LLM performance, and also examines whether the LLM can reason about when to flip (Reviewer 1).
- The experimental results are laid out systematically, each section supporting a specific claim; the setup is well done (Reviewer 4).
- The paper’s approach is systematic: it first verifies that some planning problems are easier from the backward direction (smaller action spaces), then shows LLMs perform better in the easier direction, and finally introduces the flipping method (Reviewer 4).
- The idea of flipping the problem has merit for certain planning problems; with more careful handling of general claims, clear definitions of applicability, and discussion of non‑invertible actions, this could be a strong paper (Reviewer 2).
- Explores regression planning (planning backward from the goal) with LLMs (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of awareness of classical planning literature.**  
   The paper re‑invents known results from the 1970s‑90s in regression planning without proper citations. “Classical planning algorithms such as BFS” is misleading. Bidirectional search is mentioned in related work but not connected to the proposed approach, and regression planning is not discussed despite its relevance (Reviewers 2, 3).  

2. **Overclaimed and insufficiently supported general claims.**  
   The claim that “LLMs exhibit a systematic bias towards forward planning” is not fully justified. Experiments cover only three domains, one of which (Directed Graph Planning) has a built‑in forward bias, and results are domain‑dependent (Reviewers 2, 4).  
   The evidence that LLMs plan better in the easier direction is shown only for one task (Figures 3 and 10), not replicated for all three domains (Reviewer 4).  
   The “bottleneck” effect is used without a clear connection to planning (Reviewer 3).  

3. **Conflicting descriptions of the main method.**  
   In the introduction the method is described as sampling plans in both directions and self‑verifying; in the experiments it is defined as “randomly choose either forward direction of the original problem or forward direction of the flipped problem and then plan” (Reviewers 1, 4).  
   It is unclear whether the method is always flipping (Section 4) or random selection (Section 5), and whether “Fwd‑Flip” already includes self‑verification (Reviewer 1).  
   Table 3 (Fwd‑Flip‑Reason) does not include Fwd‑Flip as a baseline, and the hyperparameter **M** differs from Table 2, making direct comparison difficult (Reviewer 4).  

4. **Effectiveness of flipping not convincingly demonstrated.**  
   The **Fwd** and **Flip** methods alone have similar average performance in Table 2, so flipping does not help on average. **Fwd‑Flip** shows much higher success rates, but if it is merely random selection between **Fwd** and **Flip**, its performance should be their average; the improvement is unexplained (Reviewers 1, 4).  
   The authors do not report success rates within **Fwd‑Flip** separately for each chosen direction (Reviewer 1).  
   The claim that flipping “avoids the bias of weak LLM planning in the backward direction” is contradicted by Figure 3: for Directed Graphs with equal forward and backward computation, flipping still performs worse than forward planning (Reviewer 4).  
   **Fwd‑Flip‑Reason** (LLM chooses the direction) is only effective on Undirected Graph Planning, not on other domains (Reviewer 4).  

5. **Assumes invertibility of actions without discussion.**  
   The flipping method assumes every action has an inverse, but this is not always true (e.g., drilling a hole). The paper does not discuss what makes actions non‑invertible or whether inverse actions must be physically feasible (Reviewer 2).  
   For Blocksworld, it is unclear whether the flipped initial state (derived from the goal) is verified to be reachable under the inverse actions (Reviewer 1).  

6. **Implementation details are vague.**  
   It is unclear how possible actions are generated from a given state, how the next state is computed given an action, and how the goal test is performed (Reviewer 3).  
   Problem statistics (action schemas, state predicates, number of objects, initial and goal states) for the three domains are not provided, making it hard to assess problem difficulty (Reviewer 3).  
   The natural‑language versions used for Graph Planning and Array Transformation are not clearly described (Reviewer 3).  

7. **Missing experiments and ablations.**  
   No experiments on obfuscated “Mystery” domains (from [1]), where LLM planning degrades even when causal structure is intact; the method’s robustness to such syntactic variation is unknown (Reviewer 1).  
   The distribution of errors in **Flip** (whether errors come from solving the flipped problem incorrectly or from reversing the plan) is not analyzed (Reviewer 1).  
   Regression (backward) planning in Blocksworld struggles due to multiple goal states, but this known issue is not addressed (Reviewer 3).  

8. **Goal as a set of states.**  
   In many planning problems, goals are not a single state, so flipping to treat the goal as a single initial state may be invalid. Only Blocksworld has multiple goal states, and the regression approach’s difficulty in maintaining a set of states is confirmed by the experiments. The claim that regression mode works well in the other two domains may depend on the distribution of initial and goal states; flipping would likely give opposite results (Reviewer 3).  

*Note.*  Reviewer 3 is strongly critical, arguing the paper re‑invents classical planning results from the 1970s‑90s with insufficient literature awareness, while Reviewer 2 acknowledges the flipping idea has merit but finds the general claims overly broad and unsupported.
