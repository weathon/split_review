## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes metabench, a sparse benchmark distilled from six Open LLM Leaderboard benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande; 28,632 items total) using data from >5000 LLMs. Item Response Theory (IRT) identifies the most informative items, reducing the combined item count by >97% (to <3% original size). The sparse benchmark yields estimators that reconstruct original individual benchmark scores with ~1.24% RMSE and total score with 0.58% RMSE. Factor analysis reveals a single common latent factor with Spearman correlation r=0.94 with total score. The paper explores multiple IRT models, cross-validated subsampling, adaptive testing, and includes ablation studies. All reviewers note the work is well-structured and technically sound overall, though some raise concerns about scope, assumptions, and memorization risks.

### Strengths
- Well-written and clearly communicates ideas and methods (R1, R3).
- Novel application of psychometric methods (IRT) to LLM evaluation (R1, R3, R4).
- Impressive compression ratio (<3% of original size) while maintaining high reconstruction accuracy: 1.24% RMSE for individual benchmarks, 0.58% RMSE for total score (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Methodologically sound: explores multiple IRT models, cross-validation, information-based filtering; better than random subsampling (Figure 1B) (R1, R3).
- Careful consideration of limitations (R1).
- Sparse benchmark enables more streamlined and cost-effective evaluation (R2).
- Comprehensive ablation studies and factor analysis (R4).
- Introduces the idea of adaptive testing for benchmark efficiency (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and generalizability**: The framework covers only six benchmarks, missing important abilities like coding, summarization, translation, and free-form generation (R1, R3). The reliance on multiple-choice formats limits applicability to non-classification-based evaluation (R3). One reviewer (R1) notes that including coding would likely require additional latent dimensions; a more comprehensive set would better discover interpretable ability dimensions.
- **Memorization risks**: Smaller benchmark size increases vulnerability to memorization (R2, R4). Proposed mitigation strategies need further validation (R4). A reviewer (R2) also questions whether a small benchmark leads to large variance in evaluation—this remains unexplored.
- **Theoretical assumptions of IRT**: The model, designed for human subjects, may not fully capture LLM complexities (R3, R4). The assumption of a single latent trait may be overly simplistic; LLMs may exhibit multiple uncorrelated abilities (R3). Item difficulty stability across different LLMs is not guaranteed (R3). Independence assumptions between models may be violated due to shared architectures and training data (R4). Reviewer disagreement exists: one reviewer (R4) strongly questions these assumptions (soundness 2), while others (R1, R3) find the approach methodologically sound (soundness 4).
- **Missing comparisons and ablations**: 
  - Alternative item selection methods beyond Fisher information are not explored (R4).
  - Stability of results across different random seeds and model subsets is not validated (R4).
  - Preprocessing to subtract random chance from MMLU scores (e.g., (score - 0.25)/(1 - 0.25)) is suggested but not investigated (R1).
  - The handling of benchmarks that assess unique skills not captured by a general latent factor is not addressed (R3).
