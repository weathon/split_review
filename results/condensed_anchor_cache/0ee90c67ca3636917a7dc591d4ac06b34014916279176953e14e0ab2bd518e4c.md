## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces a novel bandit variation where the learner observes only a ranking of arms based on their cumulative average rewards, without seeing numerical rewards. The authors study no-regret algorithms in stochastic and adversarial settings. They prove that with stochastic rewards, no algorithm can achieve logarithmic instance-dependent regret, and provide two algorithms: DREE (instance-dependent, superlogarithmic regret matching the lower bound) and R-LPE (instance-independent, \(\widetilde O(\sqrt{T})\) regret). They also show a trade-off between instance-dependent and instance-independent optimality, and prove that no sublinear regret is possible under adversarial rewards. Numerical experiments compare the algorithms to baseline explore-then-commit.

### Strengths

- **Problem formulation and analysis depth:** The setting is well-formulated and analyzed in detail, with both instance-dependent and instance-independent regret analyses, lower bounds, and an exploration of the trade-off between them. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Practical motivation:** The model has practical use cases, e.g., human preferences in matchmaking settings where scores cannot be revealed for privacy or security reasons. (R1)
- **Elegance and breadth of results:** The results are elegant, covering many natural questions (nearly matching upper/lower bounds, trade-off, impossibility in adversarial setting) and require techniques distinct from traditional bandits. (R2, R3)
- **Clear presentation:** The writing is clear, well-structured, and benefits from examples. The main paper effectively describes results and the main algorithm. (R2, R4)
- **Proof clarity (for some proofs):** The first few proofs are clear and not hard to follow. (R2)
- **Method for unobservable rewards:** The paper proposes a method to overcome the non-trivial difficulty of not observing actual rewards, and correctly treats instance-dependent and instance-independent settings separately. (R4)
- **Optimality proofs and experiments:** The paper provides optimality proofs and numerical experiments showcasing the proposed algorithms’ performance. (R4)
- **Exhaustive setting coverage:** The authors consider all relevant settings (stochastic/adversarial, instance dependent/independent) with meaningful comparisons to regular bandits. (R3)

### Weaknesses

- **Motivation mismatch with numerical rewards:** The motivation (rewards not representable numerically) clashes with the actual setting, which still assumes hidden numerical rewards. The learner simply cannot see them. This is not deeply discussed. (R2) *Note: R3 finds the setting arbitrary and sees no clear downstream application, questioning why dueling bandits are insufficient; R4 also wants a more solid practical justification or real-world experiment. R1 acknowledges use cases but does not criticize the mismatch.*
- **Lower bound Theorem 1 – unclear function \(C(\cdot)\):** The lower bound contains an unspecified function \(C(\cdot)\) with unknown shape and parameters, making it difficult to assess tightness and practical implications. The ambiguity reduces the value of the bound. (R1)
- **Theoretical intuition and proof clarity lacking for key results:**
    - Lemma 1 only guarantees a positive probability that the ranking leaderboard never changes. But that probability could be arbitrarily small, so it is unclear how this yields a significantly superlogarithmic regret lower bound (a tiny-probability event adds at most \(O(1)\) regret). The proof sketch lacks intuition. (R3)
    - Theorem 2 has exponential dependence on \(1/\Delta_i\); deriving an \(\widetilde O(\sqrt{T})\) instance-independent bound from it is surprising and not well explained. (R3)
    - Theorem 4’s proof (trade-off) is insufficient because it only considers a constructed two-arm instance, not a general instance. (R1)
    - Definition 3: feasibility of comparing a sum of sets to a constant is unclear. (R1)
- **Algorithm concerns:**
    - Both proposed algorithms are based on explore-then-commit, known to be suboptimal. The paper does not consider or discuss alternatives (e.g., \(\epsilon\)-greedy) or justify why ETC is preferable here. (R1)
    - Algorithm 1’s description is inaccurate: when \(t\le n\), it plays two arms per round. (R1)
    - Algorithm 2’s design idea (especially the active set) is hard to understand; more discussion is needed on how it converges to the optimal action. (R1)
    - DREE is not entirely surprising: the intuition (needing super-logarithmic exploration due to unknown \(\Delta^*\) and unobserved rewards) is explained. (R4)
- **Experimental issues:**
    - Experiments are very small in scale; they should test more arms. The main paper contains the wrong plots (corrected in the supplementary material). (R2)
    - Some plots are covered by legends. (R1)
    - The Explore-then-Commit baseline implementation lacks details (e.g., grid parameter choice), harming reproducibility. (R4)
    - DREE(\(\delta+1\)) performs worst in Figure 1a but best in all other experiments; no guidance is given on how to choose \(\delta\) at design-time. (R4)
- **Paper organization:** Could better highlight novel proof techniques and algorithms, and reduce coverage of well-known bandit background. (R1)
- **Missing comparisons and settings:**
    - No comparison of theoretical bounds with dueling bandits, the most relevant setting. (R3) *R2 asks whether augmented feedback (comparing arms across rounds) would reduce to dueling bandits and yield stronger guarantees.*
    - The full-information variant with ranking feedback is not considered. (R2 question)
    - Assumptions are not clearly stated: Algorithm 1 requires subgaussian rewards, but Algorithm 2’s guarantees appear not to require this; it is unclear if Algorithm 2 works with heavier tails. (R2)
