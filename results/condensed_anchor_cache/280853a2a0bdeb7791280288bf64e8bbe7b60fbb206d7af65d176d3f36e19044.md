## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CLoRA, a training-free method for composing multiple Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) models to generate images that faithfully reflect each LoRA concept. It addresses attention overlap and attribute binding by updating attention maps at test-time and using contrastive learning to create semantic masks for latent fusion. The authors provide a benchmark dataset and evaluate qualitatively and quantitatively, reporting improvements over existing methods.

### Strengths
1. The proposed method is training-free, allowing plug-and-play use of community LoRAs without needing specialized variants.
2. The contrastive objective-based approach to integrate multiple content and style LoRAs simultaneously is a clever strategy that enhances separation of attention maps.
3. The paper provides a diverse benchmark dataset, which is valuable for systematic study and future research.
4. One reviewer (R4) considers this the first comprehensive work to observe and address attention overlap and attribute binding within LoRA-enhanced image generation, offering a new perspective on attention maps for this task.
5. The paper is well-structured and clearly written, with detailed background and vivid examples that illustrate the pitfalls of prior work.
6. Experimental results verify the effectiveness of the method, surpassing baseline methods in both qualitative and quantitative assessments.
7. The method directly addresses the critical issues of attention overlap and attribute binding in multi-LoRA composition.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty and insufficient justification**: The core innovation of using distinct attention maps to separate concepts is not novel; similar approaches have been used in prior works (e.g., [1],[2],[3],[4]). The paper does not clearly articulate how the contrastive objective fundamentally differs from existing attention manipulation techniques (R1, R2, R5). One reviewer (R2) notes the attribute binding issue has already been proposed in many works. Additionally, the paper does not sufficiently justify why contrastive learning is needed over directly using attention maps for semantic masks, nor does it explore the limitations of direct masking (e.g., diffuse attention) (R3).
2. **Missing comparison and experiments**: The paper omits comparison with LoRA-Composer [4], which also targets multi-LoRA composition and adjusts attention weights via test-time optimization (R5). Additional experiments are needed to verify the advantages of the contrastive approach over attention-design works like [2] (R2).
3. **Evaluation metrics insufficient**: Only image similarity is considered; text similarity (CLIP-T) and CLIP-I should be included, as commonly evaluated in baseline papers (R2, R4).
4. **Benchmark dataset contribution is limited**: The proposed benchmark adds only 20 more characters than CustomConcept101, which is an insufficient contribution (R2). (Note: R4 and R5 considered the dataset a strength, but this weakness reflects a minority view.)
5. **No analysis of time/computational cost**: Test-time optimization adds inference time and computational cost that scales linearly with the number of LoRAs. The paper lacks experimental comparison of time and memory consumption against baseline methods, making the “training-free” attribute less attractive if it incurs similar overhead to fine-tuning methods (R1, R2, R4, R5).
6. **Scalability limitations and lack of analysis**: The method effectively handles only up to four LoRA objects. The paper should investigate the maximum number of LoRAs, performance trends, and computational/memory costs as the number increases (R3, R5). The attention maps may also restrict necessary information exchange between concepts, evident in the results (R1).
7. **Evaluation using overly simple prompts**: The prompts used in evaluation are relatively simple and do not explore complex interactions between objects (e.g., one object holding or manipulating another). The capability of CLoRA for generating composite images with interacting objects is not validated (R3).
8. **Poor visual quality in some results**: In Figure 3, animals lack similarity to reference images and exhibit an animated style without applying style tokens; in Figure 4, the book loses essential characteristics and the cup does not resemble the reference image (R4).
9. **User study details missing**: The user study involves 50 participants but does not specify how many samples each participant evaluated (R1).
10. **Writing and presentation issues**: Style is not uniform (e.g., “L1-applied” vs “L1”), the meaning of “N” in Eq2 is unclear, and there are small errors in subscripts of Zs in Figure 2 (R4). Additionally, the limitation/conclusion sections are too brief—e.g., processing times and number of LoRAs are mentioned but not illustrated with data (R1).
11. **Unanswered questions about failure cases**: Does the method work for similar concept categories (e.g., catA vs catB)? The contrastive loss might fail in such scenarios (R4). For the Mix-of-Show results in Figure 4, were conditions like human pose or canny edge used as inputs? (R4)
