## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes ChatSR, a conversational symbolic regression method using multimodal large language models (MLLMs). It combines a SetTransformer for data feature extraction with an LLM (Vicuna/LLaVA) and a projection layer, trained on 15M synthetic question-answer pairs. Users describe desired expression properties (e.g., periodicity, symbol constraints) in natural language. ChatSR is evaluated on 13 datasets and claims state-of-the-art R² and node count. The majority of reviewers (three "3" scores, confidence 3–4) find the paper poorly motivated, with unreliable baseline comparisons, limited analysis, and poor writing. One reviewer (score 6, confidence 3) commends the data construction and evaluation. All reviewers agree the idea of integrating natural language into SR is novel, but raise major concerns about experimental rigor and presentation quality.

### Strengths
- The method to construct large-scale, high-quality synthesized QA data (with human-designed constraints to validate candidate expressions) is well-designed and described (Reviewer 1).
- ChatSR allows users to specify requirements (symmetry, periodicity, symbol inclusion) in natural language without code modifications or search constraints, lowering the barrier to entry (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The LLM interprets prior knowledge from natural language instructions and generates higher-quality expressions accordingly (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Integrating MLLMs for symbolic regression is a novel approach that creates opportunities for user-friendly applications and opens new possibilities for scientific discovery (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The model exhibits zero-shot capabilities, generating valid expressions for previously unseen properties (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Evaluated on 13 datasets with experimental results demonstrating effective fitting accuracy, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines in both R² and expression simplicity (lower node count) (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The high-level figures (e.g., Figure 2) provide clear visual aids that guide readers through the methodology (Reviewer 2).
- The comprehensive background explanations of symbolic regression make the paper accessible (Reviewer 2). *(Note: this point was contested by other reviewers who found the related work too long.)*

### Weaknesses
- **Unreliable baseline comparisons**: The reported R² of MMSR in Table 1 (0.9037±0.004) is significantly lower than the original paper (0.9937±0.004) (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 Q6, Q10). The NeSymRes scores are identical to those reported in the MMSR paper, suggesting the authors did not re-implement baselines but used reported scores directly (Reviewer 2). This undermines the claimed state-of-the-art results.
- **Performance gains not properly attributed**: The datasets in Table 1 do not require instruction following, so the source of improvement over baselines is unclear. The model’s strength may stem from the underlying data scaling or decoder architecture, not the conversational aspect (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 adds that the method primarily revises existing Transformer-based SR by simply replacing decoder layers with decoder-only LLM layers, lacking strong motivation.
- **Trivial analysis of prior knowledge (Table 2)**: Providing prior knowledge (e.g., “contains sin and cos”) naturally acts as a hint. The example (Nguyen-5: sin(x²)cos(x)−1) is obvious; such analysis does not convincingly demonstrate learning from constraints (Reviewer 2; echoed by Reviewer 3 who notes this constrains generalization).
- **Limited symbol types considered** (Line 212): The set of symbols explored is limited. A more fine-grained, detailed description of expression properties could increase instruction diversity (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing evaluation of constraint adherence**: If the goal is to conform to natural language constraints, the paper should report how well ChatSR meets provided constraints (Reviewer 2). No experiments measure adherence to specified properties.
- **Unclear distinction between Vicuna and LLaVA** (Section 3.4–3.5): It is unclear which LLM was actually used in experiments (Reviewers 1, 3 Q3).
- **Inconsistency in data notation**: Section 1 defines \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) and \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) but Section 3.4.1 uses \(D=\{X,y\} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) (Reviewer 3 Q1). Equation (1)–(2) have ambiguous definitions of \(X_{instruct}^t\) when \(t=1\), and potential typo in (2) (\(X_{instruct,<i}\) should be \(X_{instruct}\)) (Reviewer 3 Q4, Q5).
- **Multi-turn dialogue data not justified**: The paper mentions collecting multi-turn QA pairs (Line 356), but Figure 3 shows no multi-turn example, and Figure 2 examples show no clear relationship between turns. Why not use single-turn data? Statistics on average turns and question diversity missing (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 Q9).
- **Training data quality concerns**: The examples in Figure 3 contain grammatical errors; it is unclear whether the training data matches the figure (Reviewer 3 Q8).
- **No discussion of computational requirements**: Training—especially due to feature alignment and LLM use—requires substantial resources, a limitation insufficiently addressed (Reviewers 3, 4). Also missing details on pretraining/post-training data scale compared to other methods (Reviewer 1).
- **Poor writing and formatting**: Extensive proofreading needed; numerous grammatical errors (notably Section 2.2), citation formatting issues (e.g., “RSRMXu et al.,” “Double Q learningHasselt (2010)”), missing spaces (Line 215), and inconsistent use of \cite vs \citep (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The related work section is excessively long and overshadows contributions (Reviewer 4). Table captions are below tables, not per ICLR template (Reviewer 4). Figure 1 has “Larger Language Model” typo (Reviewer 4). Figure 4 legend and content unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing citations in introduction**: The introduction lacks proper citations for contextualizing the work (Reviewer 4; Reviewer 1 asks for citation fixes).
- **Figure 2 resembles Figure 1 in SNIP (Meidani et al., 2024)**: This overlap is not discussed (Reviewer 3 Q2).
- **Overlap with constrained generation literature**: The work has significant overlap with prior constrained generation (e.g., keyword inclusion, length limits) but lacks related work in that area (Reviewer 2).
