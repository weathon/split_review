## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents HiddenGuard, a framework for fine-grained safe generation that uses a token-level representation router (PRISM) with LoRA activators to detect and redact harmful tokens in LLM responses, rather than refusing entire prompts. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty and motivation, but raise concerns about insufficient comparisons, limited evaluation of practical safety-utility tradeoffs, unclear dataset construction, theoretical gaps, and presentation issues. One reviewer (score 6) finds the method empirically strong with improved jailbreak performance without utility regression, while the other three (score 5) see significant methodological and experimental weaknesses.

### Strengths
* Novel and well-motivated approach: addresses the binary refusal limitation by enabling token-level, context-aware redaction instead of full denial. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
* Empirical performance: achieves over 90% F1 on token-level detection/redaction on the proposed dataset, and improves jailbreak benchmarks (ASR) without regressing on utility benchmarks. (R1, R4)
* Clear pipeline visualization (Figure 1, Figure 2). (R2)
* Insightful discussion of refusal-based vulnerabilities. (R4)
* One reviewer (R1) notes that the method overcomes limitations of token-level redaction through combined context-based redaction and global safety learning, and considers the jailbreak/utility tradeoff a strong contribution to the community.

### Weaknesses
* **Missing comparisons to relevant baselines**: No comparison with jailbreak defense baselines (e.g., LoRA adversarial training), other response-modifying approaches (logit manipulation, decoding-time methods [1][2][3]), non-contextual token-list filtering, or token-redaction training-only baseline. Absence of a dedicated Related Work section in the main body weakens the positioning. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
* **Insufficient evaluation of practical safety and utility impact**: Focus is on masking structure accuracy rather than actual toxicity reduction, useful content preservation, and response readability. Masking harmful content often harms readability and may be less effective than a refusal with explanation (R2). Attack Success Rate (ASR) determination method is unclear (third-party LLM? accuracy?). (R2, R3) Redacted content can be decoded by a non-aligned LLM (e.g., GPT-4o) or allow inference of context, creating a new attack surface; this limitation should be reflected in ASR measurement. (R1, R4)
* **Dataset construction and token-level annotation poorly explained**: The GPT-4o annotation process (prompt, post-processing from character to token level, examples) is not described. Token-level safety labels may be context-dependent and suffer false positives/negatives from left-to-right decoding. (R1, R4)
* **Lack of ablation studies**: Not all three losses are ablated (R1). Number of LoRA modules not ablated (R2). Hyper-parameter sweeps missing (e.g., threshold in Table 1, lambda in Table 3). (R1, R2)
* **Theoretical and assumption weaknesses**: Proof that the method overcomes refusal-training limitations is lacking (R1). Excessive assumptions about router/activator capacity and detection thresholds are not validated; logical error in Eq. (7): sigmoid after R maps to [0,1] constrains output to ~[0.5,0.73] (R4). Need for empirical or theoretical guarantee of robustness. (R4)
* **Writing and presentation issues**: Typos in Eq. 11, 12 and Algorithm 1 lines 8-9 (R1). Incorrect table reference in Section 4.1 (R2). Paper is too mathematical (Sections 2, 3.3, 3.4) without enough high-level explanation (R3). Main text lacks baseline descriptions and related work; figure 4 (UMAP) lacks token labels and statistical separation claim (R3). Ethical statement missing dialect robustness testing (citing Dodge et al.) (R3). Minor errors and typos in figures and text (R4).
* **Latency and computational overhead not addressed**: No explanation of additional FLOPs or inference-time latency compared to other mitigation methods (circuit breaking, inference-time reasoning). (R1, R4)
* **Limited example outputs**: Not enough concrete examples in appendix to illustrate how HiddenGuard responds to different queries (e.g., medication, non-malicious mentions of “bomb”/“drugs”), or to contextualize ASR evaluation. (R2, R3)
