## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Report Cards, human-interpretable natural language summaries of LLM behavior for specific skills/topics, evaluated on three criteria: specificity, faithfulness, interpretability. It introduces PRESS, an iterative algorithm to generate Report Cards without human supervision. Experiments cover MMLU, Anthropic Advanced AI Risk, and a Chinese grammar dataset, testing models from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B to GPT-4 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet. Evaluation uses Contrastive Accuracy (matching two models’ responses to their Report Cards via a guesser LLM), Card Elo (ranking models from Report Cards via a judge LLM, compared to ground-truth Elo), and human scoring on clarity, relevance, and informativeness. Reviewers diverge: one (score 8) strongly supports the motivation and practical need; three (scores 5) are critical of the utility, evaluation reliability, and generalizability.

### Strengths
- The focus on obtaining more insightful, holistic LLM evaluation beyond raw accuracy numbers is an important and timely topic. (R1, R2, R3)
- The paper is generally well-written and the presentation is reasonably clear, with good descriptions of the approach (PRESS algorithm, proxy metrics). (R1, R3)
- It addresses a real need in LLM development and deployment: model providers/users often need granular, task-specific assessments rather than overly general model cards or unidimensional benchmark scores. (R2)
- The proposed method provides high human interpretability and achieves competitive results using Contrastive Evaluation and Card Elo metrics. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited task coverage and generalizability**: The selection of tasks is limited; the framework may not easily extend to generation tasks (e.g., open-ended text generation). (R1) Moreover, MMLU does not cover the depth and breadth of any single subject; the evaluation lacks rigorous assessment on highly specialized or complex domains, raising concerns about real-world applicability. (R2)
- **Dependence on a judge LLM and cost**: The PRESS algorithm ultimately depends on a judge model (Claude). This adds cost compared to model cards that do not rely on LLM judgement. (R2) The reliance on an LLM as a judge may also be a shortcoming: can a judge model reliably assess a better model or recognize its own biases? The lack of a clear validation mechanism for the judge model’s assessments raises questions about reliability. (R2) If a question cannot be reliably solved by the judge model but a fine-tuned model solves it, can the judge model still generate a useful Report Card? Is the method generally constrained by the capability of the larger model? (R2)
- **Readability vs. specificity trade-off**: While Figure 10 captures nuances well, generating such detailed comments globally across questions and subjects would quickly increase the length of a Report Card, affecting readability. (R2) How is readability considered at the macro level? Could the authors showcase a full Report Card for any model? (R2)
- **Unclear utility and actionable insights**: The concrete examples in the paper give little confidence that actionable insights can be derived from Report Cards. (R3) A more concrete approach would be to generate hypotheses (e.g., “Llama-3-8B-Instruct struggles with combinatorial concepts” from Figure 10), associate that with a score on that subtopic, then test the hypothesis on a held-out set. This minimal expectation is missing. (R3)
- **Proxy metric concerns**:
    - Contrastive Accuracy seems to have many confounders and depends heavily on quiz size (120 questions, unclear). (R3) The few-shot baseline for Contrastive Accuracy is confusing: it’s unclear how informative matching a model’s test predictions to k sampled training predictions is. (R4) The de-stylization issue suggests the few-shot baseline captures not very useful information and should be rethought. (R4)
    - Card Elo extracts overall performance from the Report Card and achieves R²~0.8, beating ChatBot Arena Elo but still far from perfectly ranking models. (R3) A quote stating “k-shot completion Elo… obtains a significantly worse faithfulness score than Card Elo” is unclear; “k” here is 2 or 4 (different from the 120 quiz size), and “same number of comparisons” is not well explained. (R3) Ground truth Elo uses yet another judge, so it should be renamed “Oracle Elo” for clarity. (R4)
- **Human evaluation reliability and informativeness**: With only 230 instances for 18 annotators, there is concern about high variance between annotators, and no annotation agreement is reported. (R1) The “informativeness” score (average 4.5/5 from humans) is based on a generic question (“How informative is the excerpt about the model’s capabilities?”), which tells little about how effective the Report Card is for understanding useful model performance. (R3) The authors acknowledge needing more data for a reliable human evaluation. (R1)
- **Missing cross-model analysis**: The paper lacks analysis across models’ Report Cards to hypothesize actionable differences, both for improving models or estimating performance on new subsets. (R3)
- **Similar prior work**: There are other works (e.g., [1]) that formulate the same problem—generating natural language to describe LLM limitations—in a very similar way. (R1)
