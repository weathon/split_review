## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes IGNN-Solver, a graph neural solver to accelerate fixed-point solving in implicit graph neural networks (IGNNs). It uses a generalized Anderson Acceleration method parameterized by a tiny GNN, modeling iterative updates as a graph-dependent temporal process. The solver achieves 1.5× to 8× speedup without accuracy loss on node classification benchmarks. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem (accelerating IGNNs) and the low training overhead (1-2% extra time), but raise major concerns about insufficient baselines, lack of theoretical guarantees, missing comparisons with recent implicit models and explicit GNNs, and presentation issues. One reviewer (score 6) found the idea promising and the empirical results good despite limited novelty, while others (scores 3,5,3,5) are more critical.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important question: accelerating IGNNs, which suffer from slow training/inference, hindering large-scale deployment.
- The high-level idea is clearly illustrated in Figure 2 (per one reviewer; another finds the figure confusing – see Weaknesses).
- Achieves 1.5× to 8× speedup without sacrificing accuracy on multiple benchmark datasets.
- Introduce very low overhead: tiny-GNN adds only 1-2% to total training time.
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to comprehend (per one reviewer).
- Integrates generalized Anderson Acceleration, graph sparsification, and graph compression in a multi-dimensional acceleration approach.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient and biased baselines:** Baselines for ogbn-arxiv and ogbn-products are severely sandbagged. On the OGB leaderboard, GCNII achieves 72.74% on ogbn-arxiv and GCN achieves 75.64% on ogbn-products – gaps >4% compared to numbers reported in Table 1. For ogbn-products, GCN/GAT/GraphSAGE generally achieve 0.76–0.8, all higher than the values in Table 1 and the IGNN-Solver's accuracy. This undermines the claimed advantage.
2. **Missing comparisons with recent methods and explicit GNNs:** The background lacks coverage of recent advances such as modeling IGNN as a bilevel optimization problem (Zhong et al. 2024) and a scalable implicit model (Liu et al. 2024) that obtains higher accuracy on ogbn-arxiv. Since efficiency is a core advantage, runtime should be benchmarked against multiple existing methods beyond basic IGNN. A straightforward baseline of IGNN using phantom gradients is missing. Moreover, the solver does not show significant performance or efficiency advantage over explicit GNNs on large graphs; a comparison of training/inference speeds with explicit GNNs (e.g., GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) is needed to demonstrate practical significance.
3. **Lack of theoretical justification:** No theoretical underpinning for why the solver accelerates; no convergence guarantees or well-posedness analysis for the fixed-point equation. Whether the learnable initializer actually reduces iterations remains uncertain without guarantees. One reviewer requests a deeper analysis – either theoretical or empirical evidence on the number of iterations required (e.g., iterations needed vs. traditional solver).
4. **Limited architectural validation:** The solver is tested only on the IGNN architecture. It should be applied to other implicit GNN frameworks (e.g., MIGNN) to show it is a general plug-in solver.
5. **Missing ablation studies and component analysis:** No ablation on capturing long-range dependencies (a key IGNN advantage). Over-smoothing mitigation is not addressed. The impact of the tiny-GNN module on acceleration is unclear (Section 4.1.2). The specific settings of graph sparsification and the effect of pruning rate on overall performance are not provided.
6. **Inconsistency and insufficient explanation of Figure 2:** Multiple reviewers find Figure 2 confusing. The Pareto curve (inference speed/accuracy) is unclear: given a dataset and solver, inference time should be roughly constant, so why does accuracy increase with more time? Is it adjusting the number of iterations? Legends are not explained. There is also an inconsistency between Figure 2(c) (best accuracy ~0.716) and Table 1 (0.725) – likely from different experimental settings. These flaws need correction and clarification.
7. **Unexplained training dynamics:** One reviewer questions why inference/training time increases with epochs, given that IGNNs have a maximum number of iterations and should reach a constant. The authors should explain this behavior.
8. **Missing details on methods:** High-level description of RPI-Graph (used for graph sparsification) is absent, weakening self-containment. Also, the architecture of the learnable initializer is not specified. No study on the accuracy of the alpha predicted by the tiny-GNN.
9. **Typo and formatting error:** Line 093 chronology is incorrect (citation [34] appeared after [43]). Equation 4: "R^n" should be "R^{n×n}".
10. **Question about curse of dimensionality:** On lines 269-270 and 288-289, the authors claim mapping from high to low dimension is inappropriate due to curse of dimensionality – a more detailed explanation is requested.
