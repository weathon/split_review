## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a reinforcement learning (RL) approach for elevator group control systems (EGCS). It formulates the problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), introduces *infra‑steps* to model continuous passenger arrivals, and defines a novel action space to handle combinatorial dispatching complexity. The RL agent uses Dueling Double Deep Q‑learning with fixed and variable discounting strategies and branching vs. combinatorial architectures. The method is evaluated in a simulated 6‑elevator, 15‑floor environment based on VU Amsterdam data, comparing only against the classical ETD algorithm.

### Strengths
- **Practical and meaningful problem**: The paper focuses on a real‑world, high‑impact application, which is encouraged in the RL community (R3). The topic is interesting (R4).
- **Clear writing**: The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4), with helpful diagrams and equations (R2). Key definitions in elevator control are well explained (R3).
- **Innovative RL framework**: The MDP formulation with infra‑steps captures continuous arrivals and stochasticity (R1, R2). The action space encoding avoids combinatorial complexity (R1, R3). The exploration of both fixed and variable discounting strategies and branching vs. combinatorial architectures reflects methodological rigor (R2, R3).
- **Potential broader impact**: The new action space and infra‑steps are simple yet effective, and their significance may extend beyond elevator control (R3).
- **Practical reduction in travel times**: The RL agent shows lower passenger travel times than the rule‑based baseline (R2). The simulation is based on an actual dataset (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental comparisons**: The paper only compares against the classical ETD algorithm. No comparisons are made with recent RL‑based approaches for elevator dispatching or similar dynamic scheduling problems (R2, R4). It remains unclear how the method differs from existing control technology generally (R1). The authors are still at the stage of *considering* RL, not demonstrating it against state‑of‑the‑art (R1).  
  *R1, R2, R4 agree this is a major weakness; R1 (score 1) is particularly critical.*
- **Limited evaluation diversity**: Experiments use a single dataset (VU Amsterdam building), limiting demonstration of adaptability to other traffic patterns, building sizes, or stochastic conditions (R2). Real‑world testing with unexpected situations (malfunctions, occupancy, latency) was not performed (R2).
- **Missing details on the RL architecture**: The deep neural network models used are not specified (R4). The simulation model’s quality and fidelity are not clearly described (R4).
- **Unclear novelty and contributions**: The contribution of infra‑steps is not well isolated; empirical results show fixed discounting works better than variable, raising questions about their necessity (R3). The action space reduction is mentioned but not compared quantitatively with prior action‑space designs in terms of computational efficiency or decision effectiveness (R2). The paper’s contribution is minor overall (R4).
- **Notation error**: Equation (1) uses \(G^\pi\) as a conditional expectation, but \(\pi\) is a function (not a random variable) that changes the state‑action distribution. Standard practice would treat \(G\) as a function of \(\pi\) (R3).
- **Results lack clarity**: It is not clearly shown how the proposed algorithm outperforms previous methods; more detailed metrics and analysis are needed (R2).
- **Missing ablation / motivation**: No theoretical insight is provided for the variable discounting factor or infra‑steps beyond the need for contraction mapping (R3). No ablation separates the effect of each proposed component (e.g., action encoding vs. infra‑steps) (implied by R2, R3, R4).
