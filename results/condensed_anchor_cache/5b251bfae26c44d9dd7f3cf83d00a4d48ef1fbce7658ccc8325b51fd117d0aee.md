## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes VICtoR, a hierarchical reward model for long-horizon manipulation tasks that uses action-free videos and language instructions. It leverages GPT-4 for task decomposition into stages and motions, employs MDETR for stage and object state detection, and trains a CLIP-based motion progress evaluator with multiple contrastive losses. Experiments in simulation and real-world show improved policy learning (e.g., via PPO), with a 43% success rate improvement over prior VIC methods.

### Strengths
- Proposes a hierarchical reward estimation method that integrates LLMs (GPT-4) for high-level task decomposition and VLMs (MDETR, CLIP) for sub-stage and motion progress evaluation, providing structured guidance for long-horizon tasks.
- The modular design (stage detector, motion progress evaluator) is reasonable, and the training losses are well-motivated.
- Methodology is clearly described; figures (reward curves, t-SNE) are intuitive and help validate the reward design.
- Experimental results in both simulated and real-world settings show substantial improvements over existing VIC methods, demonstrating the approach’s effectiveness.
- The approach incorporates an innovative way to extract relevant information from short video clips rather than full sequences.
- Results and visualizations effectively illustrate the system’s ability to assess task progress.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited task diversity and benchmark choice**: Evaluation uses a self-collected dataset rather than standardized benchmarks like CALVIN, which makes direct comparison with existing SOTA difficult (R1, R2). The method also has known limitations with rotation actions, restricting applicability in common manipulation scenarios (R2).
- **Missing ablation studies**: No ablation of the three contrastive losses used in training the motion progress evaluator; it is unclear how each loss contributes to overall reward quality (R1, R4). More thorough component-level evaluation is needed (R4).
- **Unfair comparison claimed**: VICtoR trains its CLIP encoder on task-specific videos, whereas baselines like LIV use pretrained encoders without fine-tuning; it is uncertain whether fine-tuning those baselines would close the performance gap (R3).
- **Generalization concerns**: The method relies on task-specific data for the object status classifier and training, raising doubts about zero-shot generalization to novel tasks or unseen trajectories (R3, R4). The paper lacks evaluation on unseen task variants (R4).
- **Technical novelty questioned by two reviewers (R2, R3)**: LLM-based task decomposition and VLM-based progress evaluation have each been extensively explored; the combination is seen as a straightforward integration rather than a distinct contribution. One reviewer (R3) additionally notes that the stage and motion detection approach may simply overfit to specific manipulation tasks via visual models (minority view).
- **Insufficient technical details**:  
  - Real-world object state annotation process not described (R2).  
  - No discussion of what happens when the object detector fails, and the classifier structure for object states is not provided (R2).  
  - The meaning of “potential curves” in Figure 5 and whether rewards are computed from collected videos or used during action rollouts is unclear (R2).  
  - Computational cost and resource requirements (incl. LLM inference time during training) are not assessed (R2, R4).  
  - Reliance on GPT-4 is not critically analyzed; potential limitations from GPT-4 outputs are not addressed (R4).
- **Missing comparative discussion**: The relationship of the proposed motion decomposition to prior work (e.g., Chain-of-Thought Predictive Control [2]) is not adequately discussed (R1). A broader comparison with hierarchical RL and task-and-motion planning approaches is lacking (R4).
