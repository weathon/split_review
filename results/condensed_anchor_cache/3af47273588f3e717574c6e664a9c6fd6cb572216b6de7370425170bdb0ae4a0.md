## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses training an unbiased and accurate model when the dataset contains multiple spurious correlations (biases). The authors propose a method that groups training data by their combined bias attributes, then formulates debiased training as a multi-task learning (MTL) problem where each group is a separate task. They train a single model using a weighted sum of task losses, with the weights dynamically adjusted via a multi-objective optimization (MOO) algorithm to reach a Pareto-stationary point. Additionally, they introduce MultiCelebA, a real-image multi-bias dataset derived from CelebA. Experiments on MultiCelebA, Multi-Color MNIST, and UrbanCars show state-of-the-art performance against prior debiasing methods, and the method also performs well on single-bias benchmarks.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem framing:** Connecting multiple-bias debiased training to MTL is an interesting and novel perspective. However, one reviewer questions whether the problem is better described as multi-domain learning (MDL) or long-tailed learning rather than MTL, since the target classes are the same across groups. Another notes that importance-weighting approaches already implicitly use MOO, and FairGrad (in fairness) adaptively scales gradients.
- **Grouping approach:** The strategy of dividing data based on bias attribute combinations to define MTL tasks is novel and sensible.
- **MultiCelebA dataset:** The new multi-bias real-image benchmark is considered a valuable contribution for evaluating debiasing methods in realistic scenarios. However, two reviewers argue it is not a truly new dataset (no new images/labels, just a processing of CelebA) and that its construction and disentanglement of spurious vs. non-spurious correlations are insufficiently justified.
- **Comprehensive experiments:** The paper provides extensive empirical comparisons across multiple datasets (multi-bias and single-bias) showing the proposed method generally outperforms prior approaches. Some reviewers find the results convincing and highlight the insights into existing methods.
- **Clarity:** Most reviewers found the paper well-written and easy to follow, though one reviewer found the writing could be better and the paper hard to follow in parts.

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual justification and MOO understanding:**  
  - The claim that a Pareto-stationary point eliminates spurious correlations lacks formal definition of spurious correlations and rigorous justification.  
  - The paper’s use of MOO theory is problematic: “the Pareto-optimal parameter” incorrectly implies a single solution; Pareto-optimality does not guarantee unbiasedness—MGDA is known to bias toward tasks with low loss magnitude (the desired notion is “task impartiality”). Calling MoCo the state-of-the-art MOO method is a stretch.  
  - The argument that a flat loss landscape helps resolve between-group conflicts is hand-wavy and unconvincing.  
  - It remains unclear why the proposed method works: the interpolation between ERM and MGDA (similar to CAGrad) and learning α jointly with parameters is not disentangled. The grouping policy alone does not work well, yet in principle it should be independent.

- **Dataset concerns:**  
  - MultiCelebA construction is not elaborated; the paper does not demonstrate that the identified correlations are truly spurious (i.e., independent of the target in a causal sense), nor does it isolate evaluation of spurious vs. non-spurious correlations.  
  - Simple baselines (upsampling, upweighting) achieve results very close to the proposed method on MultiCelebA, questioning whether the dataset is challenging enough to differentiate methods.  
  - On Multi-Color MNIST, the proposed method’s minimum group accuracy is lower than GroupDRO, undermining the claim of universal superiority.  
  - The UrbanCars results reported (ERM gap -69.2) are far worse than results on Papers with Code (best ERM gap -15.4), raising concerns about experimental setting consistency.

- **Experimental reporting and design:**  
  - Standard deviations are missing, making it hard to assess statistical significance of results (e.g., update frequency U).  
  - Tables 2–4 use diverse evaluation metrics inconsistently; UNBIASED and WORST metrics should be reported for all experiments to avoid potential cherry-picking. The paper does not convincingly show the method balances overall accuracy and worst-case performance.  
  - There is no comparison with standard MTL / MOO methods (e.g., GradNorm). The authors should compare with these to solidify the MTL connection and explain differences from related weight-optimization methods.  
  - Design choices in Algorithm 1 are not justified: why update θ again outside the inner loop? Why update \(\bar{\alpha}\) and \(\lambda\) every U iterations rather than every iteration, and why at the same frequency?  
  - The assumption that all bias subgroups are known a priori is unrealistic; an experiment leaving one group unidentified is requested.  
  - The paper does not address shortcut difficulty—losses should be weighted based on how easily the model latches onto each shortcut (e.g., Murali et al., TMLR 2023).  
  - Performance may drop when combining many groups in the MTL loss; this is not analyzed.  
  - No code is provided, limiting reproducibility.

- **Missing related work and references:**  
  - Related works on shortcuts (e.g., “Discover and Cure”, Wu et al. ICML 2023) and shortcut difficulty are omitted.  
  - The connection between shortcut discovery and slice discovery (e.g., DOMINO) is suggested as future direction but not discussed.  
  - The citation for MoCo should be ICLR 2023 (not 2022), and a minor typo “interation” appears in Fig. 3.
