## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a First-order Influence (FI) measure to assess the impact of training data on test set performance, applied to data trimming (removing harmful samples) and active learning (selecting informative unlabeled samples). Two approximation techniques reduce computational cost. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets (binary classification with logistic regression, CNN on image datasets) show FI outperforms the Influence Value (IV) from Chhabra et al. (2024) and other baselines. Reviewers generally find the problem well-motivated and the approach clearly described, but flag incomplete analysis—missing ablation studies, scalability benchmarks, comparisons with other influence-like metrics (e.g., Shapley value), and unclear treatment of test information leakage.

### Strengths
- The problem (filtering training data and selecting new instances for active learning) is practically important and well-motivated (R2, R5).
- The intuition and design details of the FI measure are clearly presented, with sufficient rigor and well-structured algorithms (R1, R5).
- Two approximation methods are introduced to reduce memory and processing requirements, addressing practical deployment (R1, R2).
- Extensive simulation settings and experiments on multiple domains (synthetic, CelebA, MNIST, CIFAR-10, etc.) add persuasiveness (R1).
- The contribution is simple and clear; FI is shown to outperform the recent IV metric [Chhabra et al., 2024] on several datasets (R3, R4).
- Literature review is adequate and claims are clearly stated (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Ablation and baseline comparison gaps** – No experiment isolates the effect of the perturbation applied to test data vs. a standard influence function without test-set perturbations; thus it is unclear if gains come from the influence measure itself or the specific perturbation scheme (R2). Comparisons are limited to IV and random baselines; the paper should also compare with other widely-used metrics such as the Shapley value (R3, R4).
- **Complexity and scalability** – No formal complexity analysis (memory and speed) of the proposed exact and approximate methods versus state-of-the-art alternatives (R1, R2, R5). Practical scalability for large datasets (many samples/features, streaming inference) is not benchmarked; a time comparison in Table 7 is insufficient (R5).
- **Approximation trade-offs** – The paper lacks an analysis of performance degradation vs. computational cost when using the approximations; no quantification of accuracy loss or speedup (R2).
- **Dataset evaluation scope** – Evaluation is limited to binary classification with logistic regression plus a few CNN experiments; the dataset variety is insufficient to confirm strengths and reveal limitations (R4). On CelebA for data trimming, random selection performs comparably to FI, raising concerns about generalization (R4). Results would benefit from mean ± std. dev. reported in tabular form and error bars in figures (R1, R5).
- **Test information leakage concern** – The FI measure assesses perturbations’ effect on test set performance, which may leak test information into training; this needs clarification (R3).
- **Threshold and practical guidelines missing** – No clear threshold distinguishes positive vs. negative samples; the fraction of data to remove is a tunable parameter and accuracy may not vary monotonically; no guidance is given for how many samples to remove in a real-world dataset (R1).
- **Parameter and design choices unexplained** – The choice of ω₀ (whether it is scalar or vector) is not justified (R1). Perturbation is done by adding noise to samples rather than perturbing weights; rationale for this choice is not given (R2). Parameter settings in experiments need further clarification (R3).
- **Masking effect not considered** – It is unclear whether the proposed procedure accounts for the masking effect (where a sample’s influence is hidden by others) (R1).
- **Reproducibility and presentation gaps** – Code is not made publicly available (R5). Equations in the appendix are not numbered; proof for Theorem 1 is not provided (R5). It is unclear whether datasets with no predefined split were evaluated with k-fold cross-validation (R5). The paper’s use of “robustness” conflates consistent prediction improvement with robustness to data perturbations; the term should be clarified (R1). Limitations of the FI measure are not explicitly discussed (R3, R4).
