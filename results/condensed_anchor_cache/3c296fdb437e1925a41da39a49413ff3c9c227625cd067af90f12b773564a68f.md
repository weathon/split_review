## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores using general-purpose LLMs (GPT-4, Llama-3.1) to discover causal gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data. The authors use LLMs to either generate complete GRNs or to provide transcription factor (TF) candidates that are then combined with statistical methods like GRNBoost2. To evaluate the inferred GRNs in the absence of ground truth, they employ a causal synthetic data generation strategy based on the GRouNdGAN framework (Zinati et al., 2024). The approach is tested on three datasets (PBMC_ALL, PBMC-CTL, Bone Marrow). Results highlight that LLMs can support statistical modeling and data synthesis, with Llama-3.1 outperforming GPT-4 in some aspects.

### Strengths
- The study addresses a critical need for context-specific GRNs, as many practitioners rely on generic knowledge-base GRNs that lack context specificity and often lead to less accurate inferences.
- Thorough analysis with controls at each step, evaluating both TF lists generated by LLMs and the resulting GRNs.
- Innovative use of LLMs for GRN discovery, demonstrating novel application potential in biological data analysis.
- Comparison of multiple LLMs (GPT-4 vs Llama-3.1) provides comparative insights and shows valuable differences that inform model selection, with open-source Llama-3.1 sometimes outperforming the commercial GPT-4.
- Promising hybrid approach: combining LLM-derived TF lists with statistical models like GRNBoost2 can improve causal inference quality.
- Well-designed evaluation methodology using causal synthetic data generation to address the challenge of lacking ground truth GRNs.
- Well-organized paper with clear explanations of methodology, experimental setup, and results; visual aids (figures, tables) enhance understanding.
- The potential of LLMs to capture complex biological interactions and support statistical modeling in genomics is a significant contribution with broad implications.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of objective validation and limited systematic performance**: The primary limitation is the absence of direct ground truth for GRN validation. While synthetic data generation is used, results vary significantly across datasets. GPT-based GRNs perform well on PBMC_ALL but show weaker, near-random performance on other datasets (as measured by MMD and RF AUROC), indicating low systematic performance on the authors’ own suggested standards (R1). This is compounded by possible knock-effects from the Causal GAN generation process (R1). The results are not convincing: Figure 2 shows only small gaps between methods with substantially overlapping error bars, making it difficult to conclude superiority (R3).
- **Limited dataset variety and generalizability**: Only three datasets (PBMC-All, PBMC-CTL, Bone Marrow) are used, not covering different tissue types, disease models, or developmental stages. This narrow selection makes it hard to assess robustness across diverse biological scenarios (R2). The criteria for dataset selection are not justified, and generalizability to other biological contexts is unclear (R2).
- **Dependency on pathway completeness and bias**: Performance may suffer when pathway information is incomplete, which is not fully addressed. Reliance on curated pathway databases introduces potential bias, as these databases are not exhaustive and may not reflect all biological interactions. The method’s sensitivity to the quality and completeness of these databases is a significant concern (R2). The paper does not discuss how the LLM-based approach handles incomplete TF-gene knowledge (R2 question).
- **Biological relevance of generated data**: Some results indicate noise and inconsistency in cell-type differentiation, raising concerns about biological fidelity of synthetic GRNs and their practical applicability (R2). Future work needs to address these noise and specificity issues (R3 question).
- **Complexity and reproducibility**: The pipeline uses multiple knowledge bases and LLM models, creating a complex system that may be difficult for others to reproduce and implement. This complexity could hinder adoption and further development (R2).
- **Assumptions of the GRouNdGAN framework**: The paper does not adequately discuss limitations arising from GRouNdGAN’s assumption that GRNs can be represented by relatively simple structures, given the known complexity of real biological networks. This simplification’s impact on accuracy and biological relevance needs further justification (R3).
- **Lack of analysis of performance differences**: The study does not explore why LLM-based and statistical methods perform differently—e.g., whether the LLM captures specific regulatory interactions that statistical methods miss, or if differences are due to biases in training data or architecture. A detailed error analysis is missing (R3).
- **Scalability not addressed**: The computational cost of using LLMs for GRN inference with larger gene sets and cell populations (thousands of genes/cells, as is common) is not discussed. Feasibility for larger datasets is unclear (R3).
- **Lack of theoretical innovation**: The paper simply uses LLM as a plug-in to replace human prior knowledge or statistical analysis, without a deeply theoretically grounded framework for how LLMs should be used in this context. A more substantial theoretical contribution beyond applying an LLM to an existing problem is needed (R3; minority reviewer 3 is more negative on this point).
- **Missing orthogonal validation**: Additional validation methods are needed, such as testing the probing strategy’s sensitivity to known context-dependent variations within well-studied GRNs (e.g., introducing experimentally confirmed context-specific variations) (R1). Similarly, the context specificity of LLM-generated TF lists should be tested by comparing TF lists for pairs of contexts with low TF overlap using expression resources like the Human Cell Atlas (R1). These are missing experiments that would provide orthogonal evidence (R1).
