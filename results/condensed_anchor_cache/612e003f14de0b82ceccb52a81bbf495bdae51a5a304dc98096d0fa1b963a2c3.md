## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CausalTime, a pipeline to generate realistic time series with known causal graphs for benchmarking time-series causal discovery (TSCD) algorithms. It uses neural networks (e.g., LSTM) and normalizing flows to capture real dynamics, extracts hypothesized causal graphs via Shapley values or prior knowledge, derives ground truth graphs by splitting into causal, residual, and noise terms, and generates data with double dimension (including a residual stream). Experiments on three real datasets evaluate fidelity (t-SNE, ablation) and benchmark nine deep-learning-based TSCD methods. Reviewers note the approach addresses a clear need but raise concerns about causal realism, the role of the residual term, and validation of the generated graphs.

### Strengths
- **Neat trick (R1):** Doubling the dimension to include a residual stream allows generating time series with a known causal graph that closely matches real data without knowing the underlying causal structure.
- **Challenging datasets (R1):** Generated datasets present problems for existing temporal causal discovery methods, demonstrating utility.
- **Clear presentation (R2, R3):** Well-written, with concepts and methodologies clearly explained; the overall idea is well-described and motivated.
- **Addresses an important need (R2, R4):** The field lacks realistic time series with ground truth; the community will benefit from the tool.
- **Comprehensive evaluation (R2, R3, R4):** Includes benchmarking against several competing methods, an ablation study highlighting the importance of each component, and quantitative validation of data fidelity (e.g., t-SNE, discriminative scores). The breadth of experiments is useful.
- **Simplicity and conceptual clarity (R4):** The method is fairly simple conceptually.
- **Extensive literature analysis (R4):** Provides thorough coverage of related work.

### Weaknesses
- **Realism of causal relationships (R1, R3, R4):** 
  - Causality is only in a weak sense: with the "no instantaneous effects" assumption, a dense graph is possible; the problem reduces to learning *sparse* relationships, and the DAG constraint is automatically satisfied (R1).
  - Shapley values suggest a correspondence between feature importance and causality that may be incorrect; thus the generated causal graphs may differ significantly from true natural mechanisms (R1).
  - The forced sparsification (to produce a tractable ground truth) may be contrary to natural time series: Table 2 shows the residual term reduces discriminative scores by a factor ≥10, implying it is necessary for good reconstruction; this suggests real causal graphs may be dense (R1).
  - The residual term itself appears to undermine the ground truth graph: from Equations 6–7, the primary `f_{θ_i}` terms cancel, leaving the first term of Equation 7 plus noise, effectively ignoring the parent set. This calls into question whether the generated data actually reflects the supposed causal dynamics (R3).
  - The method uses a TSCD algorithm to produce the synthetic dataset, creating an "unorthodox loop" – it is unclear how accurate extraction of the underlying DAG is guaranteed (R4). No demonstration that the learned graphs are reasonable or match domain knowledge (R2, R4).
- **Lack of graph validation (R2, R4):**
  - The only sanity check is the quality of the fit; the learned graphs are not inspected. Are ACG graphs sparse or dense? What is their Markov order? Do they make sense from the domain perspective? Table 3 shows non-random performance, but these questions remain unanswered (R2).
  - Variability of the generated ACGs as a function of training seeds is not characterized (R2).
  - Plots of the actual graphs generated for each dataset are not provided (R2).
- **Restrictive assumptions (R1, R4):**
  - The assumption of "no instantaneous effects" excludes methods designed for instantaneous relationships (e.g., Rhino (Gong et al., 2022)); it is unclear whether such methods could be applied and should be benchmarked (R1).
  - The stationarity assumption, though common, is restrictive. Many real problems are non-stationary, and the method would not fare well in such settings (R4).
- **Limited baselines (R2, R4):**
  - The focus on neural models for both simulation and estimation is limiting. Non-neural approaches (e.g., SVAR, Granger causality, PC algorithm adapted to time series) should be included in benchmarking (R2). The evaluation of how conclusions from CausalTime data differ from those on purely synthetic data is missing (R3); a citation for previous synthetic results is incomplete ("compared with the reported results from previous work ()") (R3).
- **Clarity and presentation issues (R1, R3, R4):**
  - The key idea (N→2N trick) is not explained well in Section 3.4 (R1). The section heading says "to acquire the Actual Causal Graph" but actually describes generating time series; the ACG was already defined before Equation 8 (R3).
  - Mathematical emphasis obfuscates details, e.g., the caption of Figure 1 (R4). Minor grammatical issues, such as a sentence fragment in the introduction (R3).
- **Questions that overlap with missing experiments or analysis (R2, R3, R4):**
  - Notation: the ACG adjacency matrix A is 2N×2N; does this imply only Markov order 1? The actual graph might have dimension 2N·τ_max × N (R2).
  - Definition 1: what is Y? (Not referenced afterwards) (R3).
  - Why use |AUC – 0.5| rather than AUC? (R3).
  - Will code be provided upon publication? (R3).
  - How are real-world data issues (missing entries, confounding, biases) accounted for in the synthetic data? This is needed to avoid making the TSCD task too easy (R4).
