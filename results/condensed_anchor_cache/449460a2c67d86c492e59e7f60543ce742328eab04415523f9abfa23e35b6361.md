## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates how data format and positional encoding affect Transformer performance on arithmetic tasks: (i) classical multiplication up to 15 digits, (ii) length extrapolation in addition, and (iii) addition in natural language context. The authors propose several modifications: for multiplication, they pad inputs to a fixed maximum length and reverse the output order (rightmost digit first), enabling a 12‑layer GPT‑2 (100 M parameters) trained on 300 k samples to achieve nearly perfect multiplication up to 12 digits and strong results up to 15 digits. For addition, they introduce random spaces between digits or remove absolute positional encoding (and instead use random tags) to force procedural learning, allowing extrapolation from 10‑digit to 12‑digit training with 120 k samples. They also show that mixed training (natural language + synthetic arithmetic) improves word‑problem accuracy only when absolute position is removed or randomized.

### Strengths

*   The paper conducts a large and diverse set of experiments across multiplication, addition, and word problems, systematically ablating data format and positional encoding choices.  
*   Simple, clever settings (padding, reversing the output, inserting random spaces) isolate the role of positional information and allow clean testing of different solutions.  
*   The padding trick enables impressive 15‑digit direct multiplication, and the random‑space/random‑embedding methods yield extrapolation gains in addition (from 10 to 12 digits).  
*   The investigation carefully separates from‑scratch training from fine‑tuning a pre‑trained model, showing that no positional encoding works only when pre‑training is used.  
*   Mixed‑training experiments demonstrate that combining natural‑language and arithmetic data improves math word‑problem performance, but only under randomized/no‑position conditions.  
*   Focusing on a basic architecture (GPT‑2) on symbolic tasks gives insights likely transferable to more complex models and reasoning domains.

### Weaknesses

*   **Cohesion and completeness.** The paper reads as a collection of heterogeneous experiments (multiplication vs. addition with opposite approaches, different training regimens) without a unifying framework or a conclusion/future‑work section. No reproducibility statement or source code is provided.  
*   **Lack of mechanism analysis.** The work reports performance numbers but does not study *how* the model solves the tasks (e.g., attention head activations). Understanding why removing positional encoding works—the causal mask may be the only source of position—is left unexplored.  
*   **Insufficient related work and baselines.** The claim that no prior work shows 15‑digit multiplication is inaccurate: [1] (Lee et al. 2023) demonstrates 35‑digit multiplication by adding a few long sequences, and [2] (Nye et al. 2021) highlights the impact of surface representation on arithmetic generalization. The paper only compares to absolute positional embedding; it should also compare with relative positional encodings (RoPE, Transformer‑XL style), sinusoidal encoding, and other randomized‑position methods (Ruoss et al. 2023; label positional encodings). The novelty of the proposed random embedding is unclear given existing work on randomized/shifted positional encodings.  
*   **Trade‑offs of padding.** Padding requires knowing the maximum operand length in advance; overestimated padding is inefficient and unrealistic for natural‑language use. The analysis of computational cost and potential overfitting is missing.  
*   **Limited generalization evidence for addition.** Extrapolation is tested only from 10 to 11‑12 digits. No error analysis is provided, and the model is not evaluated on much longer operands or multiple operands, which would more convincingly demonstrate a learned systematic algorithm.  
*   **Clarity issues.** Several tables lack training‑step information. It is not always specified what the “pre‑trained” model was pre‑trained on or how it was fine‑tuned. The “reverse task with repeats” is unexplained, and Table 7 contains an inconsistent intermediary step (239 + 821 vs. 32 + 28).  
*   **Flippant language.** Phrases such as “simple 12‑layer transformer” and “modest‑sized models” (100 M parameters) are not grounded; smaller models (1‑layer) can perform addition. The claim that “Large number multiplication is complicated” overlooks that it can be implemented as a convolution over digits.  
*   **Contrast between reviewers.** While some reviewers praised the extensive experimental work and the insights on positional encoding, others found the contribution unfocused, lacking proper baselines, and insufficiently novel relative to existing literature on randomized positional encodings and arithmetic generalization.  
*   **Overlooked questions / missing experiments.** The reviews raise several criticisms that should be addressed:  
    *   Confirm whether the causal mask alone provides positional information when absolute PE is removed; analyze attention patterns.  
    *   Compare random‑space insertion with the method of Ruoss et al. (random positions).  
    *   Compare random embeddings to label positional encodings (Liu et al. 2022).  
    *   Test addition on much longer lengths (e.g., 20+ digits) and multiple operands.  
    *   For 15‑digit multiplication not extrapolating to 20 digits, check if the failure is due to the 20‑digit padding limit; train with larger padding and test.  
    *   Report performance differences in the “Basic” vs. “Random Space” versions under pre‑trained vs. randomly initialized settings (Table 6).  
    *   Clarify the training curriculum (if any) and compare against a non‑curriculum strategy.
