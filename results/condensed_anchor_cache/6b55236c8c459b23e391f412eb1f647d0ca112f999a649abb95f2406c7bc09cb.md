## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LoGra-Med, a multi-graph alignment pre-training algorithm for medical multi-modal large language models (med-MLLM). It enforces triplet correlations in the latent space among image modalities, instruction data, and GPT-4 generated extended contextual captions. To handle the NP-hard combinatorial complexity, the method uses a barycenter graph alignment formulation with implicit maximum likelihood estimation and black-box gradient computation. Experiments show that with only 10% of the pre-training data, LoGra-Med achieves 72.52% on VQA-RAD, nearly matching LLaVA-Med’s full-data performance (72.64%) and exceeding LLaVA-Med at 10% data by 20.13%. The approach is evaluated on medical VQA, visual chatbot (44.82% overall score), and zero-shot classification across 23 datasets.

### Strengths
- The motivation from training efficiency is refreshing and clearly articulated: reducing the large data requirement (e.g., 600K image-text pairs) for med-MLLM pre-training.
- Empirical results comprehensively validate improved performance at both low-data (10%, e.g., 72.52% vs. 52.39% for LLaVA-Med on VQA-RAD) and full-data regimes, with near parity to LLaVA-Med with full data.
- The formulation of vision-language alignment as a multi-graph matching problem generalizes beyond traditional pairwise contrastive learning.
- The theoretical foundation (metric and geodesic properties of the graph alignment distance) is considered strong by one reviewer.
- The implementation cleverly circumvents NP-hardness using Lagrange decomposition and efficient heuristics, supported by gradient-based bypass of the solver’s time complexity.
- Ablation studies (especially the “without long-context” variant) and visualizations effectively demonstrate the contribution of components.
- The supplementary material and experiment details (system prompt, evaluation protocols) are comprehensive.
- The results offer a new perspective for improving data efficiency beyond costly data curation.
- **Minority reviewer contrast:** One reviewer (score 6, confidence 3) was more positive, noting the presentation flows well with tight logical connections and that the ablations are satisfactory. The other three reviewers (scores 5, confidence 2–4) were more critical, particularly regarding novelty and missing comparisons.

### Weaknesses
- The simpler pairwise graph alignment approach (K=3, only 3 pairs) was not tried; skipping it jumps directly to a more complex formulation without justification (Reviewer 1).
- The averaging of visual patch embeddings and text token embeddings to obtain graph node features is questionable; no analysis (e.g., cosine similarity distribution between positive and negative pairs) is provided to confirm the alignment signal is meaningful (Reviewer 1).
- Only overall accuracy is reported; given that most medical datasets are highly imbalanced, macro F1 scores (for classification) and per-disease scores (for VQA) are needed to rule out bias toward majority classes (Reviewer 1).
- The term “long-context” is misused: the extended captions are only a few hundred words, not the tens of thousands of tokens typical in long-context tasks; a better terminology distinguishing this from actual long-context work is needed (Reviewer 1).
- The motivation for adding the graph alignment task in pre-training is not well explained (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- A critical missing baseline: MedTrinity (Xie et al. 2024) outperforms the method on some datasets but is not included in comparison tables; this is needed for a fair comparison (Reviewer 2).
- The paper does not clarify whether the LLaVA-Med baseline fine-tuned with 10% data also uses the extended (GPT-4 generated) answer version; this confounds the source of performance gains (Reviewer 2).
- The core novelty is incremental: extending prior combinatorial contrastive learning that used two transformations of the same image to instead use two transformations of the text (short and long answer) lacks significant innovation (Reviewer 2).
- Without the long-context component, LoGra-Med performs slightly lower than the LLaVA-Med baseline (Table 5). A fair comparison would require pre-training LLaVA-Med with the same extra long-context data to isolate the contribution of the graph alignment algorithm (Reviewer 3).
- The performance gain may stem from the extra computing time (1 hour longer training) rather than the algorithm itself. A controlled experiment where LLaVA-Med is given an additional self-supervised objective (e.g., InfoNCE) or extra long-context data for the same extra training time is missing (Reviewer 3).
- Important hyperparameters are missing: batch size B, the value of k in k-nearest neighbors for graph construction, and how node features are determined for each modality (direct embeddings vs. additional processing). This lack of detail hinders reproducibility (Reviewer 3).
- Reliance on GPT-4 to generate extended contexts raises concerns about potential biases and generalizability; no qualification test on the generated text is reported (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The computational complexity analysis is insufficient; the one-hour additional training time compared to LLaVA-Med merits deeper discussion given the sophisticated graph matching operations (Reviewer 4).
- Performance varies across medical domains (e.g., PathVQA, some zero-shot classification tasks), suggesting limitations in handling diverse contexts (Reviewer 4).
- The ablation studies could be extended to explore different graph construction strategies beyond k-NN and analyze the impact of different distance metrics in the graph matching formulation (Reviewer 4).
- The choice of k in k-nearest neighbors lacks detailed justification (Reviewer 4).
- The interplay between the traditional autoregressive loss and the graph alignment objective is not elaborated; their relative contributions to final performance are unclear (Reviewer 4).
- Several methodological questions remain unresolved: why the graph perspective is needed (as opposed to optimal transport) (Reviewer 1); how the method differs from PLOT without the verbose version (Reviewer 1); why DCI provides mixed results (Reviewer 1); why extending short answers to longer context is beneficial (Reviewer 2); how the method performs with simpler context extension or different language models (Reviewer 4); and clarification of “10% data” (whether it refers to stage 2 instruction-tuning only, with stage 1 at full size) (Reviewer 2).
