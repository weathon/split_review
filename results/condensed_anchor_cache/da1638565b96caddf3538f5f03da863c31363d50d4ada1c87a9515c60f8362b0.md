## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PASTEL, a method to incorporate human preferences into the training of interpretable decision-tree (DT) policies for RL. It uses an evolutionary algorithm that iteratively refines a population of candidate tree policies based on user pairwise comparisons, initialized via VIPER (imitation learning). Preference estimates are updated and used to guide policy generation, with Pareto frontier filtering to reduce query burden. Experiments on CartPole and PotholeWorld with synthetic preferences show improved alignment compared to baselines like VIPER, with sample efficiency in user queries.

### Strengths
- The problem setting (learning decision-tree policies aligned with human preferences via active preference elicitation) is original and clearly formulated. (R1, R2)
- The paper provides a clear presentation of the proposed framework, motivation, and algorithmic components. (R1, R2, R4)
- The related work section on Preference Elicitation and Bayesian Experimental Design is comprehensive and well-done. (R3)
- The method cleverly uses an evolutionary algorithm to handle the non-differentiability of decision trees and generate diverse candidate policies for preference feedback. (R3, R4)
- The Pareto frontier filtering and adaptive policy evaluation mechanisms are clever strategies that improve sample efficiency by reducing the number of user queries required. (R3, R4)
- The algorithm is modular, with clear separation between policy generation, preference estimation, and selection. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope**: The method is only evaluated on two simple environments (CartPole, PotholeWorld) with synthetic preferences. This narrow scope raises serious concerns about scalability to more complex, high-dimensional domains (e.g., MuJoCo tasks like Ant, HalfCheetah, Humanoid) and to real human feedback. Several reviewers (R1, R2, R3, R4) called for experiments on more challenging tasks and real user studies.
- **Unrealistic assumptions about human preference elicitation**: R1 questioned whether humans can reliably compare two tree policies, as it requires predicting consequences of leaf changes, whereas RLHF typically asks for comparisons of trajectories. R2 noted that the active learning framing may not be realistic; preference data in RLHF is often collected offline in a batch, not online during training.
- **The utility model assumed for synthetic preferences is linear in reward, depth, number of leaves, and used state features**. R2 and R4 argued this is unrealistic; real preferences may be non-linear, inconsistent, and individual-specific. R2 suggested that in practice one might simply constrain depth and maximize reward, making a linear trade-off questionable. R4 strongly recommended user studies with real participants.
- **Method is entirely heuristic with many components lacking justification**: The preference update rule, Pareto frontier filtering, UCB-inspired bound with hyperparameter \(c\) in (9), and the selection process are heuristic. R2 detailed specific questions: Why use the updated \(\theta_j^{(i)}\) instead of gradient updates? Why is Pareto frontier filtering a good idea? How were hyperparameters chosen? No theoretical guarantees or principled justifications are provided.
- **Missing connection to broader preference optimization literature**: R3 noted that the paper emphasizes preference alignment but fails to discuss methods from RLHF and LLM fine-tuning (e.g., preference optimization algorithms). This omission creates a gap in situating the contribution within current research.
- **Potential bias from initialization**: R4 pointed out that initializing the policy population with VIPER (reward-based) may bias toward high-reward policies, limiting exploration of policies that better match user preferences but sacrifice some reward. Although diversity is encouraged, the underlying influence may persist.
- **No qualitative evaluation of interpretability**: R1 noted that despite interpretability being a key motivation, the paper provides no examples of learned decision trees or user-facing evidence that policies are indeed interpretable.
- **Presentation issues**: R2 found Figure 1 confusing and notation inconsistent—examples: sup/subscript inconsistency for \(i,j\) in Eq. (3); Eq. (4) should be \(\hat\Pi_T\) not \(\hat\Pi\); indexing for \(\hat\theta = \hat\theta_i,\dots,\hat\theta_N\) unclear; sudden change from \(\hat\theta\) (line 194-195) to \(\Theta\) (line 213); \(\hat\theta_t\) in line 200-201 where \(t\) indexes timestep versus which ensemble estimate; Algorithm 1 line 1 inconsistent; what is \(m\) in Eq. (7)? Figure 1 has too many arrows, “feature vectors” is ambiguous, and the grey box is unexplained; it only made sense after reading the rest of the paper.
- **Minor concerns**: R3 noted the use of evolutionary strategies is not particularly novel; the paper lacks a real-world application example; references on lines 101-102 were not good examples. R1 questioned whether the \(d\)-dimensional feature vector \(f_\pi\) can summarize all decision-relevant information. R2 asked for a concrete motivating example and explanation of why data collection must be simultaneous with training.
- **Minority contrast**: While R1 and R4 were more positive (scores 5) and found the motivation and framework convincing, R2 and R3 (scores 3) were more critical, emphasizing the unrealistic assumptions, heuristic nature, and limited positioning. R2 specifically doubted the importance of the active learning framing and the linear utility model. R3 found significant issues with failure to connect to LLM preference optimization literature.
