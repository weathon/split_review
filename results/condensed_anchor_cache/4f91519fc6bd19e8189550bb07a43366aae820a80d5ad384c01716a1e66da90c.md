## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FedRAP, an additive personalization framework for federated recommendation that learns a sparse global item embedding via FL and a user-specific local embedding to improve personalization while reducing communication cost. Two regularizers enforce complementarity, and a curriculum learning strategy is used. Overall, reviewers are positive (scores 8, 8, 6). Strengths include novel design, strong experimental results, and thorough analysis; weaknesses cover clarity issues, missing comparisons, and concerns about incremental novelty.

### Strengths
- Federated recommendation is an important open problem, and improving personalization is a key challenge.
- The additive item embedding model is a novel contribution that simultaneously addresses communication cost and personalization.
- The ablation study demonstrates the importance of the curriculum learning component and includes 8 variants.
- Performance on six benchmarks (Figure 1) is remarkable: FedRAP outperforms all federated recommendation baselines by a substantial margin and significantly reduces the gap to centralized algorithms.
- Differential privacy analysis is thorough, and code is provided for reproducibility.
- The framework is complete, clearly expressed, and includes sufficient theoretical analysis (error bounds, DP guarantees).
- Limitations of FedRAP are detailed.
- Sparse global view is a practical strategy to alleviate communication bottlenecks in FL.
- Emphasis on federated recommendations is commendable given the priority of data privacy.

### Weaknesses
- Ablation study variants lack intuitive explanations; their motivations are unclear without careful reading.
- Typos and grammar errors present; e.g., “diversity” in “FedRAP-No: Removes item diversity constraint on C” should likely be “sparsity”, and inconsistency in dataset counts (“five dataset” in Figure 1 vs. “four dataset” in Section 1).
- Missing ablation study of the regularization term in Eq2 (requested by reviewer).
- Row-sparsity pattern observed in the user-personalized matrix (Figure 7) suggests potential to further reduce communication cost by transmitting only non-all-zero rows, but this is not explored or leveraged.
- Degradation numbers in Table 1 are given without baseline comparison, making it hard to judge their significance.
- Experimental results from Section 4.4 are introduced as early as Page 2 in the Introduction, causing organizational confusion.
- Motivation and central research question are not explicitly stated; the need to address heterogeneity and personalization in FedRec lacks clear justification.
- Distinction between “heterogeneity” and “personalization” is not clearly differentiated; in context they seem interchangeable.
- Section 3.1 (Problem Formulation) mixes the mathematical problem setup with the proposed method’s framework, which should be separated.
- Term “reconstruction error” in Figure 2 is misleading; it appears to simply measure rating prediction discrepancy rather than reconstructing something.
- Novelty is questioned: maintaining a user-specific item embedding matrix per user is considered incremental, the two regularizers are essentially normalization, and the application of differential privacy is standard.
- Missing comparative analysis with PerFedRec (cited in related work) and PerFedRec++ (recent arXiv work); these omissions weaken the experimental validation.
- The claim that sparse global embeddings reduce communication cost is not experimentally validated or discussed in the results section.
- Note: One reviewer (very positive) found no weaknesses, while another reviewer (more critical) raised the above issues, highlighting a clear contrast in assessment.
