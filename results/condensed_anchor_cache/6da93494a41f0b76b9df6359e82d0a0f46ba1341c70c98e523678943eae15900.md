## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an out-of-distribution (OOD) detection method that leverages the local neuroplasticity property of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs). By comparing activation patterns of a trained KAN against an untrained counterpart, the method identifies OOD samples. KANs’ spline-based architecture preserves specific network regions during training, enabling this approach. Validation is performed on image benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) and tabular medical data, showing competitive performance and robustness, especially with small training datasets. Reviewers note the novelty and intuitive appeal, but raise major concerns about scalability, the univariate nature of KANs, handling of joint distributions via dataset partitioning, and practical applicability.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and relevance**: The idea is original, timely, and leverages a unique property of KANs (local neuroplasticity).  
- **Clear method and motivation**: The approach is well-explained, easy to reproduce, and the neuroplasticity rationale is intuitive.  
- **Thorough experiments**: The method is validated across image and tabular medical domains with many comparisons, including ablations on toy datasets (e.g., stochasticity).  
- **Competitive performance**: Results are motivating; the method is among the top algorithms, outperforming a vast set of previous OOD detectors on several benchmarks.  
- **Performance on small datasets**: The method shows strong detection accuracy even with relatively few training samples.  
- **Exhaustive ablations on toy data**: The paper includes detailed ablations that address concerns like stochasticity.

**Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 3 was more positive (score 6), while the other reviewers scored 5. Reviewer 4 gave a weak reject recommendation despite a score of 5.

### Weaknesses
- **Ad‑hoc design choices**: Several steps (e.g., the role of location‑specific multiplication in Eqn 5, K‑means clustering) appear arbitrary and lack justification. Alternatives (DBScan, Spectral, Gaussian mixture) are not compared, and the mathematical reasoning behind the score function is insufficiently explained.  
- **Scalability and computational cost**: The method requires splitting the dataset into multiple groups and training a KAN per group. This raises concerns about training time, memory footprint, and feasibility for large‑scale problems (e.g., ImageNet‑200/1K, high‑dimensional data). No experiments (e.g., on ImageNet, MVTech) or runtime analyses (including feature extraction, partitioning, and KAN training) are provided.  
- **Univariate limitation and joint distribution handling**: Because KANs treat features univariately, the method relies on dataset partitioning (class labels or K‑means) to capture feature interactions. The partitioning is insufficient for complex, high‑dimensional data where fine‑grained (intra‑class) differences or non‑linear interactions matter. No analysis of different partitioning strategies or their impact on OOD performance is given.  
- **Backbone dependence**: The method acts as a post‑hoc processor, requiring a good backbone feature extractor. Its robustness when no strong pre‑trained backbone is available is not demonstrated (e.g., on classic tabular datasets).  
- **Marginal improvement on CIFAR‑100**: Gains on CIFAR‑100 are statistically insignificant; confidence intervals or effect sizes are missing. This suggests limited practical advantage on complex datasets.  
- **Missing analysis of model capacity**: How grid size, number of partitions, and input dimensionality affect performance is not explored. The scaling behavior with feature space size is unclear.  
- **Integration with pre‑trained models**: It is not explained how KANs can be seamlessly integrated with any pre‑trained model (since KAN architecture is required). How the pre‑trained backbones feed into KANs is unclear.  
- **Hyperparameter generalization**: Hyperparameters tuned on a validation set may not generalize to OOD types unseen in that set. The hyperparameter search space (epochs, learning rate, partitioning) is not justified.  
- **Selection bias**: Not all OpenOOD leaderboards are tested (e.g., ImageNet‑200/1K are omitted), and performance on CIFAR‑100 is not perfect. The authors should demonstrate no selection bias.  
- **Claims about InD dataset size**: The statement that prior methods suffer from non‑optimal InD dataset size is only supported for CIFAR‑10; further evidence is needed.  
- **Regression tasks**: The method’s robustness for regression (where data clusters are less separable) is not tested.  
- **Minor issues**: Line 183 figure caption shows a negative InD score despite Eqn 5 using absolute values; line 43 has a wrong citation; class‑label splitting for training KANs may cause positive/negative class confusion; robustness to NaN‑enriched data is not discussed.
