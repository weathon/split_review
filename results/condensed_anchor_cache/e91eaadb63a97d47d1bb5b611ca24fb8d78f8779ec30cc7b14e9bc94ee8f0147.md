## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces RESOLVE, a neuro-vector symbolic architecture that combines object-level features with relational representations in high-dimensional (HD) spaces using bundling and binding operations (summation and Hadamard product). It is motivated by the observation that the Abstractor module excels at pure relational reasoning but struggles in partial relational reasoning tasks that require both object and relational information. RESOLVE maps inputs to a higher-dimensional space (1–2 orders of magnitude larger) and proposes a novel HD-Attention mechanism with a sign function and cosine similarity. The authors claim low compute latency, memory efficiency, and better accuracy on pure and partial relational reasoning tasks (sorting, math problem-solving, relational classification). Three out of five reviewers rated the paper 3 (reject), one gave 6 (weak accept, with lower confidence), and all raised substantial concerns about conceptual clarity, presentation, experimental rigor, and related work.

### Strengths
- **Novel direction and interesting problem**: Incorporating hyperdimensional computing into recently proposed relational architectures is a novel and promising direction (R4, R1). The concept of enabling object-level and relational representations to coexist in the same framework without interference is fascinating and shows significant potential (R1, R3).
- **Relevance:** The problem of symbolic reasoning in transformer-based architectures is relevant and timely (R2).
- **Explanatory material:** The paper provides many schematic drawings and numbered annotations in figures, making it easier to follow the proposed ideas (R2, R4). Figures 1 and 2 effectively illustrate the motivation for pure vs. partial relational reasoning (R1).
- **Diverse evaluation tasks:** Experiments cover relational classification, partial relational classification, sorting, and math problems, illustrating the breadth of the architecture (R5).
- **Minority positive view (R3, score 6):** The framework is novel, builds on existing ideas but presents several advantages, and outperforms existing approaches across a wide variety of tasks.

### Weaknesses
**Conceptual issues**
- **Unclear how the architecture captures relational information:** The learnable symbols used in the Hadamard product are input-independent and do not encode any relational information; they appear to act like positional embeddings. The paper seems to misinterpret the Abstractor, where the relation tensor captures relations, not the symbols (R4).
- **Motivation for partial-relational improvement is unclear:** The Abstractor paper already addresses partial relational tasks using an integrated encoder, which is also what RESOLVE uses. The claimed advantage is not well justified (R4).
- **Non-differentiability of HD-Attention:** The sign() function applied before cosine similarity has zero gradient almost everywhere, making the overall architecture non-differentiable, preventing gradient propagation to earlier layers (R4).
- **No intuitive explanation for why RESOLVE is better:** The more complex HD-Attention does not provide an intuitive reasoning for its superiority over Transformer or Abstractor (R5). RESOLVE does not show significant improvement in performance or efficiency compared to the Abstractor (R5).
- **Relational bottleneck definition:** The meaning of "relational bottleneck problem" is ambiguous (R3).

**Presentation and clarity**
- **Methodology hard to follow:** Mathematical expressions are embedded in lengthy paragraphs instead of being presented in structured blocks (e.g., writing “R is normalized to obtain R̄ using Softmax” would be clearer as “R̄ = Softmax(R)”) (R1).
- **Poorly structured paper:** The paper mixes methodology, comparisons, and related work in a confusing way. The overview in Section 3 and attention comparisons in Section 4 should be moved after the method description or placed in related work (R2, R3). The flow needs re-elaboration (R3).
- **Poor figure quality:** Figures are low-resolution (should be vectorial), and color usage should be consistent across all figures (R2, R4). Figure 2 is misleading because it shows a quadratic equation solving task that is not evaluated in experiments (R1).
- **Missing details on experimental setup:** For each benchmark, the input, output, and chosen baselines are insufficiently explained (R1). Why are baselines architecture-specific rather than task-specific? What is the performance of GPT-4 with chain-of-thought? (R1). The evaluation section lacks a first paragraph with core implementation details (e.g., training/testing splits, metric reported, number of seeds, hyperparameter selection) (R2, R4).
- **Confusing captions and figures:** The first two figures should be merged to highlight the contrast between pure and partial relational tasks; each caption should begin with the main message (R2). Examples in Figures 1 and 2 need clarification (e.g., what does “>” mean? If m>n, shouldn’t m be dominant?) (R3).

**Experimental evaluation shortcomings**
- **Parameter count not controlled:** RESOLVE projects to a much higher dimension (1–2 orders of magnitude larger) than baselines; comparisons should control for model size and computational cost (R4).
- **Discrepancy with Abstractor results:** In the SET task, the Abstractor achieves ~90% accuracy at 1000 training examples and nearly 100% at 2000 in its original paper, but the reported results here show Abstractor below 60%. No explanation is given (R4).
- **Object-sorting sequence length reduced:** The sequence length is only 6, smaller than in the Abstractor paper. No comparison at longer lengths is provided (R4).
- **Math problem-solving tasks changed:** Only three tasks are used, different from those in the Abstractor paper. Why were these selected, and how does RESOLVE perform on other tasks? (R4).
- **Symbol assignment mechanism unclear:** The Abstractor paper evaluates both positional symbols (which RESOLVE seems to use) and symbolic attention (stronger performance). Which version is used here? (R4).
- **Computational efficiency evaluation unclear:** The reported L1 cache and DRAM usage on a CPU are nearly identical across methods, making the claims hard to interpret. No GPU training speed comparison is provided despite higher dimensionality likely slowing training (R4).
- **No ablation study or interpretability analysis:** There is no ablation to isolate the effect of HD-Attention versus the Hadamard product, or to study the contribution of different components (R5, R4). Examples of learned relations in vectorial form or compositionality (e.g., learning sum and sign swap to get subtraction) are not shown (R2, R3).

**Related work deficiencies**
- **Related work on neuro-symbolic and rule learning is insufficient:** Very different NeSy approaches (DeepProbLog, LTN, RCBMs, semantic loss) are conflated without clarifying their specific assumptions (e.g., whether rules are given, learned, or templated). The large area of rule learning from knowledge graphs (e.g., AMIE, RNNLogic, NCRL) and inductive logic programming is not discussed (R3).
- **Overclaim:** Claiming "first to propose a strategy for addressing the relational bottleneck problem" ignores prior work such as Wüst et al. (Pix2code, UAI 2024) (R2).

**Other issues**
- Typo: “We generate have generated” (R3).
- The attention score distribution becomes increasingly uniform as the number of objects grows because cosine similarity in HD-Attention is bounded in range [-1,1], limiting sharpness (R4).
