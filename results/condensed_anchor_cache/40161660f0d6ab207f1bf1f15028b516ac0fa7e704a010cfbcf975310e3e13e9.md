## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents ACES (Autotelic Code Exploration via Semantic descriptors), a prompting-based algorithm that uses an LLM (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613) to generate diverse Python programming puzzles. ACES labels a base set of puzzles (P3 dataset) along 10 manually chosen semantic descriptors (e.g., Sorting, Recursion). It then iteratively generates new puzzles by sampling a target descriptor set, retrieving nearest neighbors via Hamming distance to build a few-shot prompt, and prompting the LLM to produce a puzzle–solution pair. Generated pairs are kept if they pass a validity check (f(g())==True). Diversity is measured by cells covered in a 2^10 grid of descriptor combinations and by embedding-based similarity. The paper also tests whether diversity helps fine-tune puzzle-solving models. Reviewers scored the paper 5, 3, 3; one reviewer (score 5) was more positive about simplicity and diversity improvements, while the other two (score 3) had strong reservations about novelty, evaluation, and practical utility.

### Strengths
- The approach is simple and straightforward to reproduce given the prompts in the appendix and the described algorithm. (Reviewer 1)
- ACES produces greater diversity in programming puzzles than baselines (static gen, ELM semantic, ELM), as measured both by cells covered (the targeted objective) and by embedding-based similarity. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)
- The qualitative inspection of generated puzzles (including examples in the Appendix) is informative and shows some non-trivial puzzles. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)
- The approach is potentially generalizable to other domains where examples can be described by a set of features. (Reviewer 1)
- The writing is easy to follow and the generation/evaluation process is presented with adequate detail. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Semantic descriptor design and measurement**  
  - The 10 semantic descriptors are manually crafted and task-specific; the paper does not explore how their selection affects the resulting puzzle set, nor does it provide insight for applying ACES to non-programming domains. (Reviewer 1)  
  - Many cells in the 2^10 grid are not meaningful (e.g., 38% of cells require 6+ descriptors; Figure 7 confirms generating such puzzles is rare, yet the algorithm spends time trying to cover them). (Reviewer 1)  
  - The primary diversity metric (cells covered) is tailored to the same descriptors used by ACES, creating a circular evaluation: ACES would likely outscore any alternative (or even a variant with more descriptors) even when undeserved. (Reviewer 1)  
  - Hamming distance over the binary descriptors is used for retrieval and diversity, but many labels are highly correlated (e.g., Sorting & Searching with Stacks & Queues, Tree, Recursion), making the grid far from orthogonal. No justification for the choice of descriptors or their suitability for measuring diversity is provided. (Reviewer 3)  
  - The notion of interestingness and the function R (Section 3.2) are introduced but never used or clarified. (Reviewer 1)

- **Insufficiently accurate LLM labeling for measuring diversity**  
  - The LLM-based labeler is used both to generate puzzles (conditioned on target labels) and to label them for the diversity metric, risking overestimation (e.g., the model could add labels in comments and then simply read them back). (Reviewer 1)  
  - Confusion matrices (Figure 3) show True Positive Rates above 0.6 for only 3 of 10 dimensions; though true negative rates may be high, the accuracy is too low to support the claim of diversity in semantic space. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The iterative process of using LLM-generated labels to further prompt the LLM accumulates labeling errors; Figure 3’s low accuracy exacerbates this issue. (Reviewer 3)

- **Lack of meaningful evaluation beyond diversity**  
  - The diversity produced by ACES did not help fine-tuning for puzzle solving on the test set (Section 4.4), and the paper does not make a convincing case for the usefulness of the type of diversity it generates. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)  
  - The paper does not evaluate the quality of generated puzzles beyond the validity check f(g())==True. Puzzles could still be meaningless (e.g., f could ignore g and return True; infeasible combinations of descriptors could create absurd problems). (Reviewer 2)  
  - No evaluation of the education use case is provided; it remains unclear what properties of diversity would be important for that setting. (Reviewer 1)

- **Limited novelty and missing related work**  
  - The mutation and goal-specification approaches used by ACES (randomly sampling descriptors, retrieving similar puzzles, and mutating) have been extensively employed in prior diverse data generation work (e.g., Unnatural Instructions, Self-Instruct, Evol-Instruct, TinyStories, WizardCoder, Code Llama), but these are not cited or discussed. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The claim of being the first autotelic LLM for diverse artifact generation via in-context learning is questionable given concurrent works (e.g., QD through AI Feedback, Colas 2023) that also use autotelic LLMs. (Reviewer 1)  
  - The paper fails to acknowledge that the novelty of generated puzzles is limited by LLM pretraining data; for example, the string+grid puzzle on page 21 is a canonical problem the model likely saw during training. (Reviewer 3)

- **Other concerns**  
  - The 2^10 grid of 1024 cells is relatively small; only 60–80 cells were covered in P3, and the algorithm uses 45,000 generated programs to try to fill it. (Reviewer 1)  
  - The paper does not discuss how (or whether) to ensure that randomly sampled semantic descriptors lead to feasible puzzle combinations. (Reviewer 2)
