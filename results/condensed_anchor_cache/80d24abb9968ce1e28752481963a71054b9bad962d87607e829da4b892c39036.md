## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes two new loss functions for variational inference (VI) in Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) based on variants of the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence (JS-G and JS-A). The authors argue that the standard Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence-based loss suffers from instability and poor generalization due to its unboundedness and asymmetry. They derive the new losses using a constrained optimization framework and provide theoretical analysis showing that the JS-based losses penalize deviations from the prior more heavily under certain conditions. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100, a regression dataset, and a biased histopathology dataset show improvements in accuracy (up to ~5–8%) and a ~13% reduction in false negatives. However, reviewers are divided: two (scores 5,5) find the idea original and the theoretical development sound, while three (scores 3,3,3) raise serious concerns about the motivation, experimental validation, hyperparameter sensitivity, and comparison to simpler baselines.

### Strengths

- **Novelty and theoretical grounding** (R1, R3, R4, R5): The paper expands the choice of VI objectives for BNNs by proposing JS-A, a modification of the generalized JS divergence tailored for VI. The constrained optimization formulation (replacing KL with JS) is a thoughtful approach to making computation tractable. The final loss is clean and intuitive, and the theorems and proofs are straightforward and well-structured.
- **Empirical validation on selected tasks** (R1, R2, R4): The proposed methods are tested on BNNs with sophisticated architectures (CNN, ResNet-18). Empirical results show improved generalization over standard KL-based VI on noisy/biased data: approximately 5% improvement on noise-added CIFAR-10, 8% on a regression dataset, and 13% reduction in false negatives on a biased histopathology dataset.
- **Positive minority view** (R3, R4): Two reviewers (score 5) consider the paper well-crafted, clear, and the theoretical analysis a solid contribution. R3 particularly appreciates that JS-A is designed specifically for VI and that the constrained optimization makes computation manageable. R4 finds the theorems and proofs comprehensible and the originality significant.

### Weaknesses

- **Validity of the problem motivation** (R2, R5): Reviewers question the premise that asymmetry and unboundedness of KL cause instability or poor generalization in VI. The references cited (Hensman et al. 2014, Dieng et al. 2017, Deasy et al. 2020) do not explicitly make that connection; e.g., Dieng et al. attribute degradation to underfitting of posterior variance, not to unboundedness. Without theoretical or empirical evidence linking the two, the motivation remains unsubstantiated. R2 also notes that practical techniques (PAC-Bayes [DR18, P21], bounded cross-entropy, proper weight initialization) can bound the KL objective, requiring a comparison to justify switching to JS divergence.
- **Validity of the proposed objective function** (R3, R5): The relation between the proposed objective (Eq. 17) and the original variational inference objective (Eq. 10) is unclear. Eq. 17 appears to be an ad-hoc replacement of KL with JS in the regularization term, but it is not derived from the ELBO. R5 questions whether it remains a valid ELBO. R3 notes that the two formulations seem irrelevant, unlike the standard constrained optimization VI formulation (Eq. 14) which has an explicit link to KL minimization.
- **Insufficient comparison to simpler and stronger baselines** (R1, R2, R4, R5):
  - **Simpler regularization**: No ablation comparing the proposed method with dropout, weight decay, or batch normalization (R1). R2 points out that Figure 2b shows what looks like overfitting for KL-based VI, which might be alleviated by such techniques without needing a new divergence.
  - **Strong BNN baselines**: Only KL-based VI (Blundell et al. 2015) is compared. Stronger baselines like Laplace approximation and cyclical SGMCMC are missing (R5). On CIFAR-10, the reported accuracy for KL-VI is very low (~35%), while Laplace attains >92% with ResNet-18, making the claimed improvements less convincing.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and lack of guidance** (R1, R4): The method introduces two hyperparameters (λ, α) with no clear interaction, no recommended range, and no ablation study. R1 notes this harms practicality when hyperparameter search is expensive. R4 calls for ablation results. R2 also asks about sensitivity of α (tuned with Hyperopt) – if high, it would be a limitation.
- **Inadequate evaluation of uncertainty quantification (UQ)** (R2, R5): As BNNs are probabilistic models, the evaluation is incomplete without metrics like test-ELBO, test-NLL, Expected Calibration Error (ECE). R2 and R5 stress that generalization accuracy alone is insufficient; the paper needs extensive UQ experiments. R2 also asks whether predictions are made via Bayes model averaging (BMA) or deterministically using the variational mean – BMA is essential for BNNs.
- **Limited experimental scope** (R1, R4, R5): Experiments are restricted to two datasets (CIFAR-10/100, histopathology) with simple models. No tests on large-scale or high-dimensional datasets that would demonstrate practical applicability. Performance on CIFAR-10/100 appears suboptimal, possibly due to missing batch normalization or other architectural choices (R4).
- **Specific experimental flaws and omissions**:
  - **Hyperparameter λ for KL baseline** (R2): In Bayes-by-Backprop, λ is typically set to 1/n (with n = training set size) to balance the KL term. Here λ=1, which can cause an explosively large KL and unfairly disadvantage the baseline. R2 notes this undermines the conclusion that the proposed method outperforms KL-based VI.
  - **Pretrained initialization** (R2): Network parameters are initialized from ImageNet-pretrained models, yet ImageNet is not used in experiments. The rationale for this choice is not explained.
  - **Comparison between JS-A and JS-G** (R3): Table 1 suggests JS-A has better theoretical properties (boundedness), but in large-scale experiments (e.g., Fig. 12) JS-G appears better. The paper does not discuss this discrepancy or the difference between the two variants. Fig. 1 presents both JS-A and JS-G results but only discusses the mean, not the variance, and the red/black curves are not explained.
  - **Wall-clock time** (R3): Table 4 shows JS-A is slower than other methods; the reason is not discussed.
  - **No numerical verification of Theorem 2 / Corollary** (R2): The claim that the proposed objective becomes tight is not verified experimentally (e.g., by plotting the progression of ELBO and proposed objective values over iterations).
  - **Potential similarity to α-divergence VI** (R3): The mode/mean-seeking properties resemble α-divergence VI, but numerical comparison is missing.
- **Presentation and readability issues** (R1, R2, R5): Numerous typos (e.g., “CIFAR vs Cifar”), inconsistent capitalization, and unclear phrasing (e.g., Sec.1: “the two most commonly used techniques to approximate them... are the Variational Inference (VI)?”). The derivation of Eq. 5 from Eq. 4 is awkward and the justification for dropping p(D) unclear. Figure 2 is ambiguous about whether other regularization could close the gap for KL.
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: While R3 and R4 (score 5) find the paper clear and the contributions solid, R1, R2, and R5 (score 3) raise fundamental concerns about motivation, experimental validity, and missing comparisons. R2 explicitly states that even after rebuttal, several issues require careful consideration.
