## Merged Review

### Summary
PARTNR is a benchmark for Planning And Reasoning Tasks in humaN-Robot collaboration, designed to study human-robot coordination in household activities. It uses a semi-automated LLM-based generation pipeline with simulation-in-the-loop to produce 100,000 natural language tasks across 60 houses and 5,819 objects, spanning four constraint categories. The paper evaluates state-of-the-art LLMs on planning, perception, and skill execution, and also tests fine-tuned smaller models. Two reviewers (R1, R4) scored the paper 8, citing strong contributions, clarity, and practical impact. Two reviewers (R2, R3) scored 6, praising the fills a gap but raising concerns about limited baselines, manual validation overhead, and positioning. All reviewers agree on the significance of the dataset but diverge on the novelty and thoroughness of the evaluation.

### Strengths
- **Benchmark design and generation pipeline.** Well-designed task categories and semi-automatic LLM pipeline with simulation-in-the-loop verification are clever and useful (R1, R3). The process is described in detail, including an appendix (R3).
- **Large scale and fills a gap.** The first multi-agent benchmark of this scale (100k tasks, 60 houses, 5,819 objects), addressing an important gap in existing collaborative interaction datasets (R1, R2, R4). Open-source nature will enable useful research (R1, R4).
- **Clarity and writing.** Well-written, easy to read, and experimental setting is well described (R1, R2, R3).
- **Use of latest methods and models.** Employs Llama3, Habitat 3, HSSD, OVMM, ReACT, ConceptGraphs (R2).
- **Experimental contributions.** Centralized/decentralized baseline helps isolate collaboration effects (R1). Fine-tuning a smaller model achieves performance comparable to 9× larger while being 8.6× faster (R3). Results show current LLMs struggle with coordination, highlighting room for improvement (R3, R4). Experiments are sufficient and clear, paper is well-rounded and practical (R4).
- **Practical applicability.** Demonstrates real-world utility and directions for improvement (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty in data generation.** The LLM-based pipeline is not new, and still requires time-consuming manual validation of instructions and evaluation scripts. The primary contribution is scaling, not a novel generation method (R1, R2). The 83% overall task validity rate means manual filtering is still needed, questioning the value of scaling (R3). The time of human-in-the-loop filtering is not reported (R4).
- **No automatic validation of execution.** The idea of using a simulator to ground concepts is reasonable but not extended to automatic validation of successful execution (e.g., via a heuristic planner). Correctness still relies on manual spot-checking (R1, R2).
- **Only one learnable baseline (ReAct).** With only ReAct tested, it is hard to justify claims that the benchmark is challenging for state-of-the-art models. Many other planners for embodied environments, including multi-agent ones, exist (e.g., [1],[2],[3] in reviewer 2’s comment) (R1, R2). The benchmark lacks diversity in baseline methods.
- **Role assignment not explicitly tested.** It is not clear whether instructions explicitly stated which agent should perform which actions. Such a subset would help assess the model’s ability to assign roles (R1, R2). Related: why use ReAct to simulate a human in section 4.2.1, and why should an LLM plan for a human at all? This design choice is not justified (R4).
- **Annotation operator quality unchecked.** The active involvement of human experts requires evaluation of the quality of tasks they generate and verify; no criteria or quality checks were reported (R1, R2). The same question about criteria applied to annotation operators is repeated (R2, R3).
- **Repetitiveness and task distribution.** The benchmark may be repetitive: many tasks could require the same action trajectories with only parameter swaps (e.g., object ID). The distribution of task types and whether it reflects real household tasks is not analyzed; robots may be biased to certain categories (R1, R3). Can the framework generate patterns related to robot characteristics? (R3).
- **Overfitting risk.** Fine-tuning on a specific dataset and testing on the same dataset may cause overfitting; this possibility is not discussed (R3).
- **Failure case analysis missing.** The paper does not provide an analysis of failure cases for task completion (R4). ConceptGraph’s errors could be quantified with an additional metric to assess sensitivity to perceptual errors (R2).
- **Missing details.** How is “collaboration data from the web” used? (R1). Does performance improve with more sophisticated collaboration strategies? If not, the need for learning these skills is unclear (R1). How are unsuccessful tasks labeled? (R3).
- **Positioning.** The paper lists “applications to robotics, autonomy, planning” as the primary area, but no new planning methods or robotics applications are presented; the primary area should be “benchmarks and datasets” (R1, R2). (Reviewer disagreement: R1 and R4 considered the benchmark contribution sufficient for an 8; R2 and R3 considered the evaluation and novelty insufficient for stronger scores.)
