## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Randomized Global Initialization (RGI) and Dual Example (DE) to enhance adversarial transferability by exploiting multiple optimization trajectories. RGI samples multiple random perturbations around a benign sample to aggregate a global momentum, while DE generates parallel trajectories to further explore the loss landscape. Experiments on ImageNet-1K show improved transferability across CNNs (ResNet-18, DenseNet-121) and vision transformers (ViT). Reviewers were split (scores 3,3,5,5): two found the approach novel and empirically strong, while two raised concerns about limited novelty, insufficient evaluation under defenses, computational overhead, and missing parameter analysis.

### Strengths
- **Significance and clarity.** The research topic (adversarial transferability) is timely (R1) and the paper is well-structured and readable (R1, R4). Visualizations, particularly Figure 2, effectively illustrate the different paths of FGSM during iterative optimization (R2).
- **Novel technical contributions (according to R3 and R4).** RGI formalizes initializing adversarial examples from multiple random perturbations to capture a more representative global momentum; DE generates parallel trajectories to broaden exploration. Both techniques integrate seamlessly with existing gradient-based attacks (R4). R3 characterizes the work as a necessary dive into transferable attacks.
- **Extensive experiments.** Evaluation spans diverse models (ResNet-18, DenseNet-121, ViT) under both single-model and ensemble settings, with consistent improvement in attack success rates, especially for transformer-based models (R3, R4). The method achieves SOTA on major benchmarks (R2). Results are reported across multiple tables and figures.
- **Flexibility.** The strategies are incorporated into various gradient-based methods (e.g., MI-FGSM, NI-FGSM) without architectural changes (R4). R2 notes they trade computational cost for improved transferability by exploring more of the loss landscape.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (R1, R3).** R1 argues that RGI and DE effectively reduce variance by accumulating/averaging multiple attack directions, already proposed in [1] Wu et al., 2018 and [2] Huang et al., 2022. R3 states that the generation of DE is unclear: if the sampling method for random initialization of momentum and dual examples is the same, DE overlaps with momentum initialization and does not provide new information. R3 further contends that the core – combining neighborhood exploration with the pre‑convergence momentum strategy of GIMI‑FGSM – is already present in works [1], [2], and [3] (Wang et al., CVPR 2021; Zhu et al., ICCV 2023; Wang et al., CIKM 2024). A detailed comparison with these works is requested. In contrast, R4 considered the approach novel.
- **Insufficient evaluation under defenses (R1, R2).** R1 notes that attack success rates in Table 3 against NRP, RS, HGD, and AT are notably low, while prior methods DIM [3] and TIM [4] achieved higher rates, raising questions about evaluation fairness. Widely recognized baselines DIM and TIM are not included. R2 recommends benchmarking against strong defenses such as [1] Carbone et al., 2023, [2] Ma et al., NeurIPS 2023, [3] Dong et al., NeurIPS 2022, and [4] Li et al., NeurIPS 2019 to better demonstrate transferability advantages.
- **Unfair comparison due to extra optimization steps (R1).** RGI and DE introduce additional perturbation steps, so comparing with baselines that use a different number of optimization steps is invalid.
- **Missing parameter analysis (R3, R4).** No ablation on the number of random initializations (**N**) or perturbation magnitude; their impact on convergence and transferability is unclear (R3). The method relies on hyperparameters (number of initializations, step size sequence) that may require fine-tuning per model; optimal settings for the number of samples for global momentum and DE are not experimentally justified (R4). R3 requests a trade-off analysis between computational cost and performance gain.
- **Computational overhead (R3, R4).** Multiple trajectories require independent forward/backward passes per trajectory, substantially increasing computational cost, especially on large-scale datasets like ImageNet (R3). R4 notes this limits practicality in resource-constrained environments. Comparing inference time with baselines is needed (R3).
- **Ambiguous empirical validation (R3).** Figure 2 shows FGSM paths from different random starting points but does not reflect the concept of “local optimum”; different distances from the benign sample are a natural consequence of different initial perturbations and do not explain transferability. t‑SNE visualizations fail to connect trajectories to transferable gradient directions in high‑dimensional space. R3 suggests modifying Figure 2 to better motivate the approach.
- **Lack of theoretical proof (R4).** The validity of global initialization is not supported by a theoretical analysis.
- **Pseudocode issues (R3).** The **T'** parameter in pseudocode line 5 is not specified (R3 questions whether it matches the **P** parameter in GIMI‑FGSM). In line 16, averaging **g_{k,t}** should use **1/K** instead of **1/N** (typo). The lack of specific initialization parameters and absence of open‑source code hinder reproducibility.
- **Typos (R1).** “samplesoften” in the abstract; incorrect reference format in Section 2.2.
- **Missing analyses (from R4 questions that overlap with weaknesses).** No quantitative evaluation of time complexity or resource requirements. No study of variance in attack success rates across different random initializations. No comparison with non‑gradient‑based adversarial attack methods.
