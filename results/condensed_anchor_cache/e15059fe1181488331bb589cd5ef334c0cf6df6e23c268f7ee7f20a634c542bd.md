## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a concept encoding-decoding mechanism to explain in-context learning (ICL) in transformers. Studying a small transformer trained on synthetic sparse linear regression tasks, the authors find that the model learns to separate latent concepts (e.g., different regression parameters) in representation space, and that this separation enables task-specific decoding. This behavior is then validated in pretrained large language models (Llama-3.1-8B, with some OLMo-7B checkpoints) on POS tagging and bitwise arithmetic tasks. Using activation patching and controlled fine-tuning, the paper shows that the quality of concept encoding is causally linked to ICL performance. The work is seen as both a timely investigation into ICL mechanisms and, by some reviewers, as largely replicating prior task-vector findings (Hendel et al. 2023, Todd et al. 2023).

### Strengths
- **Timely and practical research question**: Understanding the mechanism behind ICL in LLMs is important for science and applications.
- **Well-designed experimental progression**: Starts with synthetic tasks on a small model, then extends to larger pretrained LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B) and natural-language-like tasks (POS tagging, bitwise arithmetic). Additional analyses use model patching and fine-tuning.
- **Simple yet appropriate synthetic task**: The sparse linear regression setup is well-suited for studying emergent concept encoding and decoding.
- **Clear evidence and figures**: The paper is well-written and easy to follow; figures communicate experimental results effectively.
- **Multiple lines of support**: Several different experiments (UMAP visualization, concept decodability metrics, activation patching, fine-tuning) converge on the same conclusions.
- **One reviewer (score 8) particularly positive**: Found the results novel, well-supported, and the paper appropriately cautious.
- **Replication of prior findings**: Neatly reproduces and strengthens evidence for the existence of task vectors (Hendel et al. 2023) using a richer set of interventions and a different framing.

### Weaknesses
- **Introduction and conclusion lack focus**: The introduction does not clearly emphasize the main contributions, making it hard to identify the key insights. The paper ends with a brief discussion rather than a fully rounded conclusion.
- **Figure quality issues**: Figure 1 is hard to interpret (same markers used for LSE and Lasso regression) and has unnecessary whitespace. A typo on line 377 should reference Figure 5 instead of another figure.
- **Limited real‑world evaluation**: Real-world experiments use only one model (Llama-3.1-8B) and two relatively simple tasks (POS tagging, bitwise arithmetic). This raises concerns about whether the concept encoding-decoding mechanism generalizes to more complex, realistic tasks and larger models (e.g., Llama-3.1-70B, Pythia). More diverse or harder tasks would strengthen evidence for generalizability.
- **Unsurprising results**: Several findings are expected given prior knowledge:
  - More demonstrations improve encoding/decoding (Figure 3, Figure 8) — this simply reflects more learning signal.
  - Some tasks fail to achieve high accuracy due to representation limitations — aligns with existing research on ICL failure when tasks are not frequently observed during pretraining.
  - Fine-tuning improves concept decodability and ICL accuracy — may be a trivial consequence of aligning representation subspaces and making the ICL task identical to the in-weight learning task.
- **Lack of novelty / overlap with task‑vector framework**: The findings closely replicate results from Hendel et al. (2023) and Todd et al. (2023). The paper does not clearly distinguish the proposed "concept encoding" framework from the existing "task vector" framework. Using "concept" instead of the established "task vector" terminology is confusing and appears unnecessary. One reviewer (score 5) considers the paper largely a replication and lacking in novel insight.
- **Missing experimental details**:
  - Synthetic experiments (Section 3): Which layer(s) are analyzed? Why specifically layer 5? How do observations vary across other layers?
  - Training setup for synthetic experiments: Are four separate models trained for different β values, or a single model? How is UMAP applied across different β conditions?
  - Intervention experiment (Section 4.3): How does it differ from Hendel et al. (2023) besides testing a null task vector? Are activations patched at the final token or multiple tokens?
  - Fine-tuning experiment (Section 4.4): Is each subplot a separate fine-tune or are tasks fine-tuned together? How can performance gains be attributed specifically to concept encoding rather than other factors? Does the paper hypothesize that fine-tuning final layers strengthens concept decoding?
- **Unexplained phenomena**: Why does concept decodability peak in the middle layers and then decrease? The paper does not provide an explanation.
- **Questions about methodology**:
  - UMAP can exaggerate differences in clustering; would k‑NN classification on the raw representations yield the same results?
  - The term "layer activations" is ambiguous: does it refer to residual stream representations or the output of transformer layers before addition to the residual stream?
- **Limited scope**: Only Llama 3 8B and OLMo-7B checkpoints are tested. Results on other model families (e.g., Pythia, Gemma-2 2B/9B/27B, Llama-3.1-70B) would strengthen the claims. One reviewer asks whether the encoding-decoding mechanism extends to multimodal models.
- **Complex task handling**: The paper does not explore what happens when the number of latent concepts increases or when concepts overlap. It is unclear whether task separability degrades on multi‑step or overlapping tasks.
- **Minor errors**: Typos (line 122: “Bayeisan”; line 297: “overt” → “over”; line 790: “We” → “we”; line 862: “synthetinc” → “synthetic”; line 161: duplicate “sequence of sequence”). The opening quote is considered unnecessary by one reviewer.
- **Missing connection to related work**: One reviewer asks how the findings relate to Mittal et al. (2024), which suggests that inferring latent variables does not necessarily improve ICL performance and that task vectors may be due to parametric shortcuts.
