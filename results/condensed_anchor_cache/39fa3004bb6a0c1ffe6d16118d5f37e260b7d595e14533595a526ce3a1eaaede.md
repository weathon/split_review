## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Multilingual Mathematical Autoformalization (MMA), a large, back-translated dataset of informal-formal mathematical statement pairs (332k pairs across multiple formal languages), created by using GPT-4 to translate formal statements from proof assistants into natural language. Fine-tuning language models on this dataset yields 16–18% of outputs acceptable with minimal corrections on the miniF2F and ProofNet benchmarks (up from 0% for the base model). The authors also claim that training on multiple formal languages improves data efficiency even for monolingual formalization tasks.

### Strengths

- The MMA dataset is the largest of its kind by far, exceeding the size of existing parallel datasets (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The dataset is created via back-translation (informalization), a clever strategy that leverages the relative ease of informalization and ensures the formal side is high-quality (Reviewer 3).
- Fine-tuning on MMA yields significant improvements over the base model (0% → 16–18% on benchmarks) and outperforms established baselines (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The generated dataset and the fine-tuned model will be made publicly available, supporting further research (Reviewer 2).
- A notable finding—that fine-tuning on a multilingual (formal-language) dataset benefits monolingual benchmarks—is a strong point that could be further emphasized (Reviewer 3). The paper claims this shows greater data efficiency compared to single-formal-language training, though the experimental details need clarification (Reviewer 3, see Weaknesses).
- This work is the first to systematically distill mathematical formalization from an LLM, addressing the scarcity of parallel formalization data (Reviewer 2).
- The dataset is multi-domain and covers multiple formal languages (Isabelle and Lean4) (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

- **Dataset quality and noise.** The entire dataset is auto-generated by GPT-4, so some informal-formal pairs may be mismatched or unrealistic. The authors themselves note that informalisations should be treated as noisy approximations (Reviewer 1, 3). The paper lacks a detailed error analysis (e.g., classifying errors as incorrect translation of mathematical symbols, logical inconsistencies, or lack of contextual understanding) (Reviewer 2) and a human evaluation of the dataset (Reviewer 1). Including GPT-4 in a comparative formalization quality analysis would help quantify the noise level (Reviewer 2). A data contamination check (whether GPT-4 saw the benchmark test data) and a broader quality assessment are missing (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 1’s question: “How do the authors check the quality of the dataset?”).
- **Statistical reliability of evaluation.** The evaluation uses only 50 examples per benchmark (10% of each dataset), which risks bias and may not reflect true performance over the full dataset. The total number of examples for complete testing is roughly eight times larger, yet no justification or estimate of the effort needed for full testing is given (Reviewer 2; also the corresponding question about overall testing effort).
- **Limited novelty and scope.** The approach is essentially a back-translation version of Fu et al. (ICML 2023, model distillation for reasoning) and is only evaluated on the narrow domain of autoformalization. The technical advancement is incremental, relying heavily on GPT-4’s automated capabilities (Reviewer 1, 4). Three important lines of related work are almost completely missing and need to be cited: (1) model distillation (Fu et al.), (2) mathematical/logical reasoning with LLMs and multilingual chain-of-thought reasoning (Cobbe et al., Lewkowycz et al., Shi et al.), (3) executable programs as formalisms of natural language (Spider, Codex, InCoder) (Reviewer 4).
- **Ambiguous terminology.** The word “Multilingual” in the title and the interchangeable use of “language” to refer to both natural languages and formal languages is confusing. The paper should consistently use “natural language” and “formal (mathematical) language” instead of the current mixed usage (Reviewer 2, 3). Additionally, the paper inconsistently uses both “formalisation” and “formalization” (Reviewer 4).
- **Unclear claim about data efficiency of mixed-language training.** The statement that “fine-tuning with multiple formal languages is much more data-efficient than with single-formal-language data” is not fully explained. The jointly fine-tuned model sees a greater total amount of data; readers need details about the number of steps per epoch and the total examples seen by each model to interpret the claim (Reviewer 3).

**Additional minority contrast:** Reviewer 3 (score 8) is notably more positive overall, emphasizing the dataset’s importance and the value of multilingual training, while Reviewer 4 (score 3) is the most negative, dismissing the novelty and strongly criticizing the missing related work. These contrasting views are reflected in the strengths (Reviewer 3’s enthusiasm) and weaknesses (Reviewer 4’s novelty critique) above.
