## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Consistency Calibration (CC), a post-hoc method that recalibrates model confidence by measuring consistency across perturbed inputs (e.g., Gaussian noise added to inputs or logits). The final confidence is obtained by averaging predictions over multiple perturbed versions, with logit-level perturbations highlighted for computational efficiency. The method is evaluated on CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet, and ImageNet-LT using both CNN and ViT architectures, and is compared against several post-hoc and training-time calibration baselines.

### Strengths
- The topic of model calibration is important for robustness, safety, and uncertainty estimation in deep learning.
- The paper is clearly written, with an intuitive and easy-to-follow idea and sufficient discussion of existing calibration metrics.
- The approach does not require architectural changes or additional labeled data.
- Consistency is introduced as a novel perspective on calibration, potentially offering an alternative to traditional reliability-based views. *[Note: Reviewer 2 (score 5) found the method interesting and noted that experiments across CNN/ViT and four datasets are conducted; Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 3) expressed concerns about novelty and experimental scope.]*

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior work**: The paper overlooks a significant body of relevant literature. Sensitivity-based uncertainty methods (e.g., Mi et al. AAAI 2022, Durasov et al. TMLR 2024) and test-time augmentation for calibration (Conde et al. 2023) share the same motivation and are very similar, substantially undermining novelty. How the proposed method distinguishes itself from these approaches is unclear.
- **Missing baselines**: Ensembling-based approaches such as Deep Ensembles, MC-Dropout, BatchEnsembles, and more efficient variants like PackedEnsembles (ICLR 2023), Depth Uncertainty (NeurIPS 2020), or MixMo (ICCV 2021) are omitted. These baselines consistently demonstrate high uncertainty quality and are directly relevant since multiple inferences are required. The inference-time vs. calibration-quality trade-off is not addressed.
- **Limited experimental scope**: The method is designed specifically for image classification (adding Gaussian noise to images or hidden features). It is not directly applicable to domains like language modeling, speech recognition, or GNNs, where adding Gaussian noise is infeasible. Adaptations for these domains are not explored. Validation on larger models (e.g., massive language models) is also lacking.
- **Theoretical grounding and potential ineffectiveness**: The analysis of why CC works is insufficient. A larger logit gap between correct and incorrect samples does not fully explain better calibration; more rigorous or theoretical analysis is needed. A concrete counterexample: given a point like (2,–4) deep inside a predicted class (Figure 1(b)), adding Gaussian noise will not change the predicted class, so recalibrated predictions remain miscalibrated. Similar issues arise when the softmax vector changes but the argmax class remains nearly the same. By contrast, ensembling approaches rarely exhibit this behavior.
- **Hyperparameter tuning**: CC requires optimization of perturbation strength and noise type, adding hyperparameter complexity. Automated tuning techniques are not explored.
- **Inconsistencies and unclear reporting**: Figure 1(a) is missing "Moderate Augmentation" in the legend, and it is unclear if "Train Augmentation" accuracy goes below 92% (numerical results would help). Section 2.5 recommends training-time augmentation, yet final results use CC only as a post-hoc method. Figure 3(c) only shows a case study for comparing CC variants; more performance comparisons between CC, CC (Train Aug), and CC (Train Aug + Jitter) are needed. It is unclear whether ECE and AdaECE are calculated on the original test set or the perturbed set (Lines 229-231 suggest perturbed set). The sensitivity comparison in Lines 266-269 is hard to assess due to differing scales; the error behavior outside the range 0.5–1 is unclear, and it is uncertain whether this 2D analysis extends to high-dimensional data like images.
- **Hidden representation perturbation**: Using perturbations on hidden representations may degrade model performance (similar to Dropout/Gaussian Dropout, which is known to degrade accuracy), and appears to be an ad hoc solution to computational and generality issues.
