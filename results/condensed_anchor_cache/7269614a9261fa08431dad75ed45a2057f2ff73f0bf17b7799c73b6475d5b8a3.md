## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Information Subtraction, a framework for learning representations that maximize conditional entropy \(H(Y|X)\) using two discriminators to estimate \(I(Z,X;Y)\) and \(I(Z;X)\). It extends prior work from discrete to continuous variables and is evaluated on synthetic and real-world datasets for fair learning and domain generalization. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem but raise concerns about novelty, clarity, experimental rigor, and lack of baselines.

### Strengths
- The framework tackles a relatively under-explored problem of selectively maximizing and minimizing specific information components during representation learning (R2).
- It extends prior work on conditional representation learning from discrete to continuous variables, which is a non-trivial and important contribution (R1, R2, R4).
- The use of MINE-based MI estimators is an excellent choice that allows the method to scale to high-dimensional data and is architecture-independent (R1).
- The problem is well-defined mathematically, framed as a clear optimization issue (R4).
- The paper provides a comprehensive overview of related work and their limitations (R4).
- The inclusion of real-world experiments highlights contributions to fair learning and domain generalization applications (R4).
- One reviewer (score 5) finds the paper well-written, the challenge interesting, and the two-discriminator approach reasonable (R3). This reviewer is more positive overall, though still notes significant weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty & Relation to Prior Work**  
  - The novelty appears very limited; the major change may be the use of MINE-based methods, which is not a fundamental contribution (R1).  
  - It is unclear how the proposed method differs from existing CMI estimators (e.g., MI-Diff+f-MINE in [1]) and whether turning an estimator into a representation learner involves significant technical difficulties (R2).  
  - The paper does not adequately connect to extensive debiasing literature for continuous variables (e.g., [A], [B]), nor does it discuss how the proposed framework improves upon those techniques (R4).  
  - The authors claim “While we share similar architectures with these works, their structures are not designed for conditional representations” but do not compare to those works or show that a simple modification would not suffice (R3).

- **Experimental Deficiencies**  
  - No comparisons to any baseline methods (R2, R3, R4). This makes it impossible to assess the significance of the results. Baseline expectations include contrastive-based approaches (R2), MI-Diff+f-MINE (R2), and simpler architectures specifically designed for comparison (R3).  
  - No ablation studies to analyze the contribution of different components or hyperparameter impact (R2).  
  - It is unclear whether reported values come from train, validation, or test splits; no standard deviations are provided, suggesting no cross-validation was used (R3).  
  - The chosen “real-world” datasets seem very simple (R3), and the experimental section is weak in terms of number of experiments and datasets (R4).  
  - Missing downstream performance comparisons on fairness and domain-invariant learning tasks against other approaches (R2).  
  - The paper does not include more realistic fairness/debiasing experiments (e.g., healthcare, ethnic biases) with qualitative visual explanations of learned representations (R4).

- **Clarity & Presentation**  
  - The formulation and algorithm are not clear (R1); the mathematical loss functions being minimized in practice are not adequately described (R4). The discussion stays at a high level without a step-by-step derivation (R4).  
  - The architecture description is introduced late and lacks detail: no schematic, no specification of input/output types, number of layers, activation functions, or how it adapts to different data modalities (e.g., images vs. tabular) (R4).  
  - The paper breaks the double-blind policy: Line 168 states “Based on our previous work (?)”, strongly hinting at author identity (R1). The citation should be done properly as “Based on previous work [x]” (R4).  
  - The paper does not discuss failing points or limitations of the proposed method, e.g., when condition variable \(X\) and target \(Y\) are entangled in complex ways (R1).

- **Missing Analysis & Questions**  
  - No discussion of training stability, particularly given that MINE is known to be difficult to tune (R1, R2).  
  - No evaluation of computational cost scaling with high-dimensional data (R1).  
  - The generator takes only \(Y\) as input; would including \(X\) change the result? (R2)  
  - Is it necessary to assume a specific distribution (e.g., Gaussian, von Mises-Fisher) for inputs or latent representations? (R4)  
  - Did the authors consider adding a hyper-parameter to one of the loss terms for finer control? (R1)  
  - The reference to “expanded architecture” in Line 168 is unclear (R2).
