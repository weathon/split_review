## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents EquiJump, an SO(3)-equivariant model based on two-sided stochastic interpolants for accelerating protein dynamics simulations. It is designed to bridge arbitrary data endpoints (e.g., consecutive time steps) rather than sampling from a fixed prior distribution. The model is evaluated on 12 fast-folding proteins and compared against CG-MLFF, achieving state-of-the-art results. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the two-sided interpolant framework, clear presentation, and strong empirical results, but raise significant concerns about experimental rigor, baseline comparisons, lack of ablation studies, and insufficient quantitative evaluation. One reviewer (score 3) was notably more critical, citing unclear architecture motivation, missing training details, and heavy reliance on visual assessment.

### Strengths
- The two-sided stochastic interpolant framework is a novel and well-founded approach addressing a key limitation of prior generative methods by enabling direct transport between arbitrary endpoints (e.g., \(X^t \rightarrow X^{t+1}\)) rather than requiring a fixed prior distribution.
- The method is clearly presented and easy to understand.
- The architecture, including the tensor cloud representation and SO(3)-equivariant modules, demonstrates high architectural novelty with clear descriptions of new elements.
- Theoretical guarantees of SO(3)-equivariance are provided and verified experimentally.
- The model achieves state-of-the-art results on dynamics simulation across multiple fast-folding protein families, generalizing well.
- The resampling technique inspired by umbrella sampling for uniform state sampling during training is a valuable practical contribution.
- The representation and visualization of results are appreciated.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons**: EquiJump is only compared to CG-MLFF, a coarse-grained model – an unfair or insufficient benchmark. Comparisons with all-atom equivariant models (e.g., EGNN, EqMotion, ESTAG, Timewarp) and a machine-learned force field (MLFF) using the same architecture are needed to isolate the benefits of the interpolants framework and provide comprehensive evaluation.
- **Lack of quantitative and statistical evaluation**: Performance improvements are mostly demonstrated visually (Figs. 5, 6). Multiple reviewers request tables with quantitative metrics such as RMSD, Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD), and statistical significance for objective comparison.
- **Missing ablation studies and architecture justification**: The motivation for the specific combination of architectural choices is unclear. Ablation studies on components (e.g., the equivariant module design, resampling, integration steps) are needed to justify each design decision. The choice of a novel SO(3)-equivariant architecture over existing ones like Equiformer is not justified, and the necessity of the proposed tensor cloud representation is not demonstrated.
- **Clarity of rotation representation (irrep)**: The irreducible representation features are not clearly defined. A more precise explanation of their construction, the order of spherical harmonics used, and the impact on network parameterization is required. Also, the initialization of \(V^0\) (\(l=0\)) and the order of \(V\) representation need clarification.
- **Missing training details and model comparison**: The number of parameters for EquiJump is not provided (only CG-MLFF’s 294,565 is given). Training curves are absent, and training-validation-test splits are not specified (only reference to Majewski 2023’s 85%/5%/10% split).
- **Lack of out-of-distribution (transferability) tests**: The model is trained and evaluated on the same set of fast-folding proteins; generalization to other proteins or unseen systems is unverified. Additional datasets (e.g., MDAnalysis) should be considered.
- **Stereochemical quality assessment missing**: The stereochemical quality of generated intermediate structures (bond lengths, angles, clash scores, Ramachandran statistics) should be assessed using ProCheck/Molprobity and compared to MD trajectories.
- **Need for formal equivariance proofs**: A formal proof or empirical demonstration of SO(3) equivariance for architecture components not directly taken from prior work is needed, or a clear explanation of how existing equivariant layers (e.g., equivariant linear layers) are incorporated.
- **Computational efficiency concerns**: The sampling process of EquiJump is described as inefficient and time-consuming; a comparison of computational cost with prior MLFF methods is needed.
- **Unclear distinction from related work**: The relationship to concurrent trajectory generation methods (references [1-4] in reviewer comments) and the distinction between one-sided and two-sided interpolants require clearer discussion.
- **Resampling bias**: Resampling states from the Boltzmann distribution to create a uniform training dataset may bias the learning objective; the authors should address this potential issue.
- **Typos and minor errors**: Eq. (6) target should be \(\hat{\eta}\) not \(\hat{s}\); duplicate phrase in line 94; duplicate subfigure (c) labels in Figure 3; initial step in the final subfigure should be \(X^t\), not \(X^\tau\).
- **Missing ablation on integration steps**: The impact of using fewer integration steps (e.g., 200, 300, 400) on performance is not explored.
- **Phase space and Hamiltonian analysis**: It would be useful to check if the simulated trajectory stays on a closed manifold, evaluate its shadow Hamiltonian, and estimate phase space volumes and energy fluctuations over time.
- **Clarification of \(I_\tau\) trainability**: It should be stated whether \(I_\tau\) in Algorithm 1 contains trainable parameters (it currently appears as a simple linear interpolant).
