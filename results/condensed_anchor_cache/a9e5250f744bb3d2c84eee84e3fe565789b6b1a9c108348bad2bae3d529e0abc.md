## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Neural Fourier Transform (NFT), a framework for learning latent linear group actions without explicit knowledge of the group or its action on the input data. The key idea is to use triples of the form \((x, g \circ x)\) to enforce an equivariant map between the nonlinear data space and a linear latent representation where the group acts linearly. Three settings (U-NFT, G-NFT, g-NFT) are considered, depending on how much prior knowledge is available about the group or group elements. Theoretical results connect the existence of linear equivariant features to invariant kernels, and experiments are conducted on tasks including nonlinear spectral analysis, data compression, image classification, and novel view synthesis from a single 2D rendering.

### Strengths
1. **Clear and innovative core concept** – Multiple reviewers found the idea of learning latent linear group actions from transformed data triples to be natural, convincing, and innovative (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). The approach is motivated as a generalization of the Fourier transform to unknown groups.
2. **Theoretical contributions** – The paper provides interesting theoretical results linking linear equivariant features to group-invariant kernels, which add rigor to the framework (Reviewers 2, 3; also noted by Reviewer 1 as a sensible extension of earlier non-neural work by Reisert and Burkhardt, JMLR 2007).
3. **Data-dependent spectrum** – The idea of learning a data-dependent spectral representation is appealing (Reviewer 1).
4. **Simple and effective strategy** – The use of \(g\)-transition tuples is a simple yet effective approach for the problem (Reviewer 4).
5. **Promising empirical findings** – The experiments, though limited in scope, demonstrate that the method works in several tasks and substantiate its utility (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The limitation that experiments are restricted to synthetic settings is acknowledged.
6. **Well-structured and well-written** – The paper is well organized, with clear explanations and remarks that make it comprehensible (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Limitations are discussed (Reviewer 2), and code is provided (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Reliance on data triples \((x, g \circ x)\)** – This is a significant practical hurdle because obtaining such transformed versions of the input is difficult in most real-world settings. The paper pivots to time series, but this does not fully address the core issue of data availability (Reviewer 1, with high confidence 4). Reviewer 4 similarly asks how such triples could be obtained “in the wild.”
2. **Experiments are toy and lack a compelling real-world test** – The experimental section is relatively small-scale and does not showcase a clear advantage over existing techniques. There is no comparison with state-of-the-art methods, and the choice of tasks appears driven by data availability rather than the method’s strengths (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 1 notes the applications follow a similar pattern to Miyato et al. (2022). Reviewer 4 would have liked results on a more “in the wild” task.
3. **Experimental details are insufficiently clear** – The objectives, loss functions, and input/output specifications for each model are not described in enough detail (Reviewer 4). Specifically, the combination of autoencoder and downstream classification (how the two objectives are weighted and how hyperparameters are set) is not explained (Reviewer 4).
4. **Potential theoretical issue in Theorem 4.2** – The minimization objective \(E_{g\in G}[\|g\circ X - \Psi \Phi (g\circ X)\|^2]\) may only ensure that \(\Psi \circ \Phi\) is a good autoencoder on the group orbit, not that it transforms \(X\) into a linear space with a linear \(G\) action. An alternative objective involving the latent linear representation \(M(g)\) is suggested: \(E_{g\in G}[\|g\circ X - \Psi \circ M(g) \circ \Phi (X)\|^2]\) (Reviewer 3).
5. **Missing clarification on equivariance properties** – It is unclear whether the learned model achieves intra-orbital homogeneity or full equivariance, and how this differs across the three settings (U-NFT, G-NFT, g-NFT) relative to Miyato et al. (2022) (Reviewer 2).
6. **Title is misleading** – “Neural Fourier Transform” overstates the generality of the method; the Fourier transform is a much broader signal processing tool, whereas this work simply uses it as an example of learning linear group representations (Reviewer 4).
7. **Minor typo** – At the end of Section 2, “scaler-value” should be “scalar-value” (Reviewer 2).
