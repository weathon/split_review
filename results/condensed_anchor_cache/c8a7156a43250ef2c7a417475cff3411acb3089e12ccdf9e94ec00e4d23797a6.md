## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces rStar, a self-play mutual reasoning approach that improves small language models' (SLMs) reasoning capabilities without fine-tuning or reliance on larger models. The method uses a generator SLM to perform Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) augmented with five human-like reasoning actions, and a discriminator SLM to verify solutions via mutual consistency (checking if the second SLM reaches the same conclusion given partial steps). Experiments across multiple benchmarks (GSM8K, GSM-Hard, MATH, SVAMP, StrategyQA) show large accuracy gains: e.g., GSM8K from 12.51% to 63.91% for LLaMA2-7B, 36.46% to 81.88% for Mistral-7B, 74.53% to 91.13% for LLaMA3-8B-Instruct. One reviewer (score 8) found the work significant for showing latent reasoning capability in SLMs. Another reviewer (score 5, later revised up) had limited expertise but was satisfied after rebuttal. A third reviewer (score 6) was skeptical that the method truly enables reasoning vs. statistical pattern exploitation. A fourth reviewer (score 6) emphasized the need for clarification on design choices and practical cost.

### Strengths
- **Strong empirical results**: The proposed rStar substantially outperforms existing methods across diverse benchmarks and model sizes. The gains are large: e.g., LLaMA2-7B from 12.51% to 63.91% on GSM8K, Mistral-7B from 36.46% to 81.88%, LLaMA3-8B-Instruct from 74.53% to 91.13%.
- **Comprehensive evaluation with informative ablations**: Experiments cover five SLMs and multiple reasoning tasks. Ablation studies convincingly validate the importance of both the expanded MCTS action space and the mutual consistency verification.
- **Novel two-phase approach**: The combination of an enriched MCTS (with human-like reasoning actions: problem decomposition, question rephrasing, stepwise thinking, etc.) and mutual consistency verification using a second SLM is original. This method does not require fine-tuning or knowledge distillation from larger models.
- **Clear writing and well-structured**: The paper is relatively well written and easy to follow. Differences and similarities to competing methods are made explicit. The methodology is clearly explained.
- **Implications for SLM reasoning**: The work demonstrates that SLMs have stronger latent reasoning capabilities than previously believed and provides a practical method to unlock them.
- **Good baselines and ablation studies**: The experimental setup includes informative comparisons and validations of key design choices.

### Weaknesses
- **MCTS explanation and motivation**: The description of how MCTS works in this context (simulation, tuning, source of MCTS) is unclear. One reviewer found no explanation of why this should lead to increased performance in step generation. This lack of clarity may hinder accessibility for readers outside the LLM area.
- **Discriminator capability**: It is not sufficiently explained why a second SLM with similar capabilities would be a better discriminator than the generator itself, and why those discrimination capabilities cannot be used directly by the reasoning model.
- **Anthropomorphism and overclaiming reasoning**: The paper uses phrases like “rich set of human-like reasoning actions” and “reasoning capabilities” throughout. One reviewer argued that the method does not enable true reasoning but only allows the SLM to better exploit statistical patterns in data-query pairs, making it a prompt engineering technique rather than a reasoning method.
- **Manual action design lacks justification**: The five human-like reasoning actions for MCTS are manually selected; there is no systematic justification or comparative analysis against alternative action sets. The temporal order constraints imposed on action types lack rigorous justification and may limit the search space. No exploration of automating the action type design is provided.
- **High token cost**: The method generates an average of 367.1k tokens per question on GSM8K. This high computational cost raises practical applicability concerns, and the paper does not propose optimization strategies beyond noting that distributed inference does not reduce total cost.
- **Scalability to larger models**: The paper focuses on SLMs and does not investigate performance on LLMs. It remains unclear how the method’s effectiveness scales with model size. A reviewer asked for experiments comparing rStar on SLMs vs. LLMs.
- **Potential limitation of mutual consistency**: If an early action makes an incorrect statement that dramatically simplifies the problem, the second SLM may still match the first, leading to a false positive. This limitation is not addressed or explored in the paper.
- **Missing comparison to other MCTS methods**: The paper cites a couple of MCTS-based reasoning methods but does not compare against recent approaches such as “Monte Carlo Tree Search Boosts Reasoning via Iterative Preference Learning” or “Improve Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models by Automated Process Supervision.”
- **Performance improvement still increasing at max 32 rollouts**: The paper stops at 32 rollouts, but performance appears to still improve linearly. It is unclear when the improvement would drop off.
- **Generalization across tasks not studied**: One reviewer asked whether the method can transfer between tasks/benchmarks; this aspect is not explored.
- **Self-reflection not integrated**: The paper does not consider adding self-reflection as an action type, which is notable given its importance in models like OpenAI’s o1. It is unclear what benefits or challenges would arise from such an addition.
- **Temporal order constraints implementation details**: The paper mentions temporal order constraints for action types but does not provide pseudocode or a sufficiently detailed explanation of how they are implemented in the MCTS algorithm.
- **Minor clarity issues**: The description of rStar in the introduction (line 71 onward) is hard to follow; breaking it into bullet points or moving Figure 3 earlier would help. On line 255, the phrase “based on whether it reaches the correct answer” is ambiguous—does it imply use of ground truth during search or just self-consistency majority voting (as later clarified in line 259)?
