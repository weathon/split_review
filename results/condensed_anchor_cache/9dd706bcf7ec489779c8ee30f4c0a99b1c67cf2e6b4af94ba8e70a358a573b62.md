## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Joint Denoising and Rewiring (JDR), a preprocessing algorithm that jointly optimizes graph structure and node features to improve downstream GNN node classification. JDR maximizes alignment (“spectral resonance”) between leading eigenvectors of the graph adjacency matrix and leading singular vectors of the feature matrix, using an alternating, top‑k iterative heuristic derived from the cSBM framework. The method outputs a rewired graph and (optionally) denoised features as a reusable preprocessed input. Experiments on synthetic and real‑world homophilic and heterophilic datasets show consistent accuracy gains over existing rewiring methods when applied before several GNN backbones. Reviewers universally rate the paper 8/8, but note several limitations and points of disagreement: novelty is considered sound though incremental by one reviewer; the experimental scope is seen as extensive by most but limited by one; computational complexity of the SVD/eigendecomposition step is flagged as a scalability concern; theoretical justification relies on cSBM assumptions that may not hold in practice; and several clarity and ablation gaps are identified.

### Strengths
- **Joint approach**: simultaneously addresses graph rewiring and feature denoising, a rare combination that enhances data quality for downstream GNNs (R1, R2, R3, R5).  
- **Spectral resonance concept**: provides a measurable alignment target between graph and feature eigenspaces, rooted in the cSBM model (R1, R3, R4).  
- **Clear theoretical foundation**: uses cSBM to build intuition and justify the alignment objective; synthetic experiments validate the setup (R3, R4).  
- **General applicability**: method works with a wide range of GNNs and on both homophilic and heterophilic graphs (R1, R2, R3).  
- **Preprocessing output**: yields a rewired graph that is interpretable and reusable across different GNNs, unlike end‑to‑end methods that modify the graph only during training (R1).  
- **Comprehensive and convincing experiments**: strong empirical results on synthetic and real datasets, with comparisons to multiple state‑of‑the‑art rewiring methods (R1, R4, R5).  
- **Well‑organized presentation**: clear mathematical definitions, helpful diagrams, and thorough appendix (R1, R4).  
- **Acknowledges limitations**: the paper discusses its own limitations, which is appreciated (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Computational bottleneck and scalability**: JDR requires eigendecomposition (or SVD) of the adjacency and feature matrices. Even with the top‑k power method, the claim of \(O(N)\) complexity is insufficiently justified; actual runtime depends on spectral gap, number of iterations, and graph size. The assumption that average degree does not scale with \(N\) may not hold on many real graphs. Explicit timing comparisons (training + preprocessing vs. vanilla GNN) and a demonstration on large graphs (e.g., ogbn‑products) are needed (R1, R3, R4).  
- **Theoretical guarantee and model mismatch**: There is no theoretical guarantee on the degree of improvement from JDR in general (R2). The method is derived from the cSBM model, but real‑world data may not conform to its block‑structured, signal‑to‑noise assumptions. How deviations (e.g., unclear community structure, structured noise) affect performance is not discussed (R4).  
- **Incremental novelty**: One reviewer (R2) considers the solution somewhat incremental with low novelty, though other reviewers find it well‑motivated and sound (R2 vs. R1, R3, R4, R5).  
- **Limited experimental scope (disagreement)**: While most reviewers praise the extensive experiments, one reviewer (R2) argues that the number of datasets is small and that all are common benchmarks; broader empirical validation is needed to confirm general effectiveness (R2).  
- **Reliance on informative node features**: JDR requires node features that carry label information; performance likely degrades when features are sparse, noisy, or irrelevant. The method is also designed for node‑level tasks, not graph‑level tasks (R1).  
- **Missing ablations and fairness of comparisons**:  
  - If JDR truly achieves optimal denoising, the graph may become unnecessary—yet an ablation of JDR(X) + MLP (without GNN) is not reported (R5).  
  - Hyperparameters of the GNN backbone are not tuned per rewired graph; small performance margins make this omission significant (R5).  
  - The paper notes that comparisons with rewiring methods are not perfectly fair; further justification or adjustments are needed (R5).  
- **Risk of degenerated rewired graphs**: The combination of matrix factorization and thresholding may produce disconnected graphs or low‑rank adjacency matrices; this possibility should be examined (R5).  
- **Unexplained theoretical points**:  
  - The remark that “the information about the labels is contained in the leading vectors of V and U” (Definition 1) is not fully justified, as leading support vectors may not be representative (R4).  
  - The motivation for why quadratic/bilinear forms in equation (2) lead to selecting leading eigenvectors/singular vectors is not sufficiently explained (R4).  
  - The connection between the optimization and spectral properties of adjacency/feature matrices (lines 159–190) needs more elaboration (R4).  
  - The “Gaussian adjacency equivalence conjecture” section and its proof are unclear and should be polished (R5).  
- **Lack of discussion on prior work**: Better acknowledgement of research on GNN vs. MLP performance on heterophilic graphs, especially papers linking heterophily and graph noise (e.g., [4] Shi et al. 2024), is needed (R5).  
- **Minor clarity issues**: The caption comparison in Figure 2 (amplitude vs. normalized amplitude) and the parametric explanation in Section 2.2 are unclear (R4).
