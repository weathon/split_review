## Merged Review

### Summary
ToG-I extends the Think-on-Graph (ToG) framework by prompting the LLM to first generate multiple (≤3) reasoning instructions that analyze core elements, latent intentions, and commonsense knowledge, then iteratively incorporating those instructions during beam search on a knowledge graph (KG) to guide the reasoning path. The depth of exploration is dynamically adjusted based on task complexity. Experiments on several KBQA datasets claim state-of-the-art performance compared to existing methods, with ablation studies and beam‑search analysis.

### Strengths
- The writing is clear and the paper is easy to follow (R1).
- The effectiveness of different modules is clearly discussed in the ablation study (R1).
- The method is a streamlined extension of ToG that demonstrates clear performance improvements over the original (R1).
- The overall framework is interesting: it first prompts the LLM to analyze the question, then uses the analysis to guide a progressive reasoning process (R2).
- The integration of knowledge graphs and large language models is an important research area (R2).
- ToG‑I’s progressively instructed approach is novel; it allows flexible, multi‑level reasoning that addresses complex and ambiguous questions by exploring both explicit and implicit knowledge (R3).
- Dynamic depth adjustment reduces unnecessary resource consumption while achieving state‑of‑the‑art performance on knowledge‑intensive tasks (R3).
- Experiments on several datasets, including strong results on multi‑hop reasoning and complex tasks, are supported by ablation studies and beam‑search expansion analysis (R3).
- The computational resource optimization strategy (incremental depth increase based on complexity) is practical for real‑world applications (R3).

### Weaknesses
- Novelty is limited: the method primarily combines a question‑analysis step (similar to task decomposition) with a KG‑exploration step (essentially ToG’s beam search). The paper should elaborate on similarities and differences compared to ToG (R2, R3).
- The computational cost may be even higher than ToG, which already involves costly beam‑search and multiple LLM calls (R1).
- It is unclear whether the instruction generation module is the key contributor; simply running ToG multiple times with self‑consistency might yield comparable improvements, especially because the experimental datasets contain objective, factual questions unlike the subjective example used in the paper (R1).
- Missing baselines: RoG and GNN‑RAG, which use an additional retriever and claim new SOTA on the same tasks, should be included for a fair comparison (R2).
- Experiments use only a subset of 1000 examples per dataset without justification; full datasets (consistent with prior work) should be used to ensure fair comparison (R2).
- No analysis of token consumption or efficiency (tokens taken to answer the question) is provided (R2).
- Additional experiments are missing: using different KG sources, different triple representation methods (sentences, chains), different similarity‑judgment tools, different search schemes, and different numbers of in‑context demonstrations. The paper also lacks in‑depth analysis of the results (R3).
- Many experimental details are missing, making the work irreproducible. Specific prompts used at each step are not shown (the paper is only ~9 pages and has room for an appendix) (R3).
- Typos: Line 318 incorrectly refers to the baseline as “Rng-kbqa” (type 3) instead of “ToG” (R1, R2).
- Missing scores in Table 1; a description of the datasets would be helpful (R1).
- The illustrative example in the figures is not representative of the datasets used in the experiments; examples closer to the experimental data would be more effective (R1).
- A case study showing instances where ToG‑I succeeds while ToG fails would help demonstrate its superiority (R1).
