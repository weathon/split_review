## Merged Review

### Summary
MOSLIM proposes a multi-objective LLM alignment method using a single multi-head reward model that classifies (instead of scores) question-answer pairs, combined with a prompt-driven policy optimization that converts classification results into a scalar reward via a weighted-sum mapping. It claims to be more training- and inference-efficient than baselines MORLHF and RSoup, and to enable flexible control over diverse preference dimensions and intensity levels. Reviewers are split (scores 5, 3, 5, 3): Reviewers 1 and 3 view the method as effective and well-validated; reviewers 2 and 4 find contributions incremental and presentation unclear.

### Strengths
- Well-organized paper with clear methodology and explicit differences from baselines (Figures 1–3). Main concepts are articulated soundly, and experiment outlines are verified.
- MOSLIM reduces computational overhead by using a single reward model for diverse preferences and enables off-the-shelf models without requiring per-preference SFT training.
- Ability to handle multiple preference objectives without complex training adjustments shows potential scalability.
- MOSLIM outperforms MORLHF and Rewarded Soups across multiple benchmarks (MT-Bench, HaluEval 2.0, Hackaprompt) with controllable alignment across dimensions and intensities.
- Thorough validation exploring different model scales and comparing multiple policy optimization algorithms (PPO, RLOO, Online-DPO).
- Using a classification approach for reward estimation is an interesting direction, with connections to concurrent work (“Stop Regressing: Training Value Functions via Classification for Scalable Deep RL”).
- The multi-head architecture is a practical way to combine multiple reward models into a single one.

### Weaknesses
- **Contribution novelty**: The core ideas—multi-head reward models for preference alignment and prompt-driven dynamic preference adjustment—have been introduced in prior work (e.g., Li et al. “Continual Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning via Reward Model Rehearsal”; Yang et al. “Bayesian reward models for LLM alignment”; Yang et al. “Rewards-in-context” (RiC)). The paper’s claim that some methods use SFT loss primarily for core abilities (lines 55–62) overlooks the use of rejection sampling in works like Llama 2 and Gao et al. (2023). Overall, contributions are seen as incremental refinements of existing techniques.
- **Insufficient comparison with state-of-the-art**: Direct comparisons with RiC, CDPO, and RLPHF (Jang et al., “Personalized Soups”) are missing. Adding these would strengthen evaluation. Baselines are limited to MORLHF and RSoup.
- **Unclear explanation for outperformance**: The paper does not sufficiently explain why MOSLIM outperforms MORLHF and RSoup. Empirical comparisons of reward model performance (e.g., reward accuracy, model size) are needed, as are theoretical reasons for the differences.
- **Missing ablations**: The pros and cons of the multi-head architecture are not ablated. There is no study on whether transfer occurs across tasks or whether some capability is lost. The claimed advantage of removing per-preference SFT training is not experimentally verified.
- **Experiment design issues**: Figure 5 compares MOSLIM with baselines not trained with varying granularity of preference intensities, making the comparison potentially unfair. Evaluation details for Table 2 are lacking: what do “different data types” mean? Is granularity level (number of preference intensity classes) considered in evaluation? For Table 8, providing a time breakdown of reward model and policy model training would make comparisons clearer.
- **Missing details on methodology**: The decision process for choosing specific intensity levels in the reward mapping function is not explained. In real-world scenarios where preference intensities are not explicitly defined or vary significantly, how does MOSLIM handle them? It is unclear whether categorization of question-answer pairs into preferences (helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty) is done automatically or with human-in-the-loop. The phrase “we aggregate the losses” (for computational convenience) needs justification.
- **Writing clarity**: The paper is often unclear and overly complicated for simple concepts (e.g., explaining softmax or cross-entropy). Numerous typos and inconsistent usage: “RSopu” appears in Fig1, “Multiple Lable” in Fig2, “helpfulness or helpful” in Fig7, and line 293 says “three SFT models”, lines 295–296 have “is scaling law still consist” and “weather”. Inconsistent abbreviations (RSoup vs. Rsopu). The paper would benefit from careful proofreading.
- **Equation issues**: In Equation (10), because the number of classes K could differ for each preference dimension, using a single K is inappropriate. In Equation (11), the sum contains k+1 elements (nit).
- **Redundant figure**: Figure 4 duplicates information already present in Table 1.
- **Lack of definitions**: The difference between “preference accuracy” and “intensity accuracy” in Table 1 is not explained. The meaning of “Value Model” in Table 8 is unclear. It is also unclear whether “during the training phase” (line 250) refers to reward model training and whether r_score is needed for policy training.
