## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Sparse‑ProxSkip, an extension of RandProx and ProxSkip that combines sparse‑to‑sparse training with accelerated local optimization for Federated Learning (FL). The algorithm performs pruning (Top‑K) at the clients to reduce communication, supported by control variates that satisfy Σᵢ hᵢ=0. Experiments on logistic regression (FEMNIST) and CNN (CIFAR‑10) show communication speedup and competitive accuracy. Reviewers’ scores span 3,3,5,5,5 (confidences 4,4,3,4,3). Lower‑scoring reviewers (R1, R2) see limited novelty and no nonconvex theory; higher‑scoring reviewers (R3–R5) value the empirical results but also note missing analyses, computational cost concerns, and narrow baselines. There is strong disagreement on the contribution (R1,R2: 2; R4: 3; R3,R5: 2) but broad agreement on good presentation.

### Strengths
- **Smart integration of sparsity and acceleration**: Adapting RandProx/ProxSkip to sparse training on clients is an interesting application (R1, R2, R3). The work is clearly presented and easy to follow (R3, R4).
- **Strong empirical performance**: The algorithm outperforms baselines across tasks, especially at high sparsity levels, and reported communication speedup is appreciated (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Ablation on placement of Top‑K**: The paper systematically compares different strategies for where to apply pruning (e.g., server vs. client), providing insight into the design (R2).
- **Validation of the control‑variate criterion Σᵢ hᵢ=0**: The importance of this condition is motivated and empirically justified (R2).
- **Use of ℓ₀ penalty**: Unlike standard ℓ₁, the ℓ₀ penalty is employed in the convex‑setting inspiration (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The algorithm is a straightforward extension of RandProx and ProxSkip with the proximal operator replaced by a sparse operator; no new theoretical analysis is provided (R1, R2, R3: “lower‑end novelty”). Three reviewers (R1, R2, R5) rated contribution 2.
- **No nonconvex guarantees**: The paper claims to address the nonconvex setting but provides no convergence theory for nonconvex objectives (R1, R3, R5). One reviewer (R3) suggests the abstract should be revised to avoid overstating applicability to nonconvex models.
- **Misuse of “acceleration”**: The word “acceleration” is used to mean communication reduction, not Nesterov‑type acceleration; this is confusing (R1).
- **Computational cost of Top‑K ignored**: Top‑K selection can be expensive on resource‑constrained clients, yet the paper does not analyze computational complexity or runtime on local devices (R2, R3, R4, R5). Moreover, pruned parameters can turn non‑zero in later steps, so memory/computation savings are not guaranteed (R4).
- **Unclear algorithmic details**: 
  - Why are two Sparse‑ProxSkip steps (Steps 7 and 9) needed? Is one sufficient? (R1)
  - The difference between Sparse‑ProxSkip‑Local and Sparse‑ProxSkip is not explained (R2).
  - The role of control variables *hᵢ* and the meaning of Σᵢ hᵢ=0 require clarification (R2).
- **Limited baselines and models**: 
  - Only ResNet‑18 is tested; larger models (ResNet‑32 or above) are needed (R3).
  - Recent FL sparse‑training baselines (e.g., DisPFL, SSFL) are missing (R3).
  - Only full client participation is assumed; partial participation is not considered (R4).
  - The Top‑K pruning is not compared with dropout or other pruning methods that reduce nodes (R4).
- **Gaps in experimental analysis**:
  - Performance differences shrink at lower sparsity (e.g., 20, 40, 60%); these levels are not reported (R5).
  - The “Baseline Server Pruning‑modified” performs well but is not included as a baseline (R5).
  - The computational cost on local devices is not benchmarked against other methods (R5).
