## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes learning-augmented skip lists and KD-trees that use an oracle to estimate query frequencies. Reviewers found the work relevant and the theoretical results solid, with experiments showing improvements over classical data structures. However, there were notable reservations: some reviewers felt the KD-tree section was underdeveloped, the paper overclaims certain contributions, it lacks comparisons to other learning-augmented methods, and omits related work. Two reviewers were very positive (8/10), while two were more critical (6/10), resulting in a mixed but generally positive reception.

### Strengths
- The skip list part is a complete and meaningful contribution: promoting higher-frequency elements to higher levels with a level-dependent threshold is natural and easy to implement. (R1)
- Optimal expected search time achieved with perfect oracle, and robustness to prediction errors is proven. (R1, R2)
- Experiments show superior performance over classical algorithms, including with noisy predictions. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The ideas are simple, novel, and effective; paper clearly written and easy to read. (R2)
- The theoretical bounds are solid; both skip list and KD-tree extensions are well-constructed and achieve optimality. (R4, R2)
- The approach has potential for wide practical use given the abundance of ML oracles. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- The KD-tree part is underdeveloped and confusing (R1). Motivation is unclear: for lookups, why not use skip lists with a total order? The setting differs from the skip list setting (query always in dataset vs. in a larger universe) without explanation. (R1)
- The handling of low-probability items in the KD-tree (prob < 1/n²) is not well motivated or explained. (R1)
- The paper overclaims contributions (R2). The constant expected search time under Zipfian distribution holds only when the exponent s>1, which is a consequence of entropy being constant for any optimal structure. The claim about constant factor robustness when predictions are arbitrarily incorrect is not accurately described in the abstract. (R2)
- The noise robustness measure (α,β)-noisy is unconventional and not connected to standard divergences like total variation distance or KL divergence, limiting interpretability. (R2)
- There are no experimental comparisons with other learning-augmented search algorithms that use frequency predictions (R2). Missing comparisons limit evaluation of practical improvement.
- The paper lacks a related work section and fails to position itself in broader literature. Specifically, it does not discuss Ailon et al. (self-improving algorithms) or Cayton & Dasgupta (learning framework for nearest neighbor search), which study very similar problems. (R4; also R2 mentions missing discussion of [1]).
- It is unclear how to use the proposed methods with real ML oracles (e.g., neural networks) rather than simple statistical frequency tables. (R3) The experiments only use history-based oracles, leaving open whether large models provide sufficient accuracy to guarantee improvement. (R3)
- The font size in figures (e.g., Figure 2) is too small (R1, R4). Multiple reviewers noted this presentation issue.
- The paper does not discuss how deletions would affect optimality in the skip list (R1). This is a missing practical consideration.
- The theoretical speedup (e.g., constant factor) is not clearly aligned with experimental results; it would be helpful to plot theoretically expected improvement alongside actual improvement. (R3)
