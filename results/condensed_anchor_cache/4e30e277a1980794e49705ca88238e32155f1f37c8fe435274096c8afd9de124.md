- Scores: 3, 3, 1, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes “Don’t Pre-train, Teach” (DPT), a method to train small models from scratch by regularizing their backbones to match a pre‑trained teacher (fine‑tuned via linear probing on the target task) using a contrastive distillation loss inspired by Alignment/Uniformity theory, while augmenting the training data with synthetic samples from a diffusion model. The authors claim this achieves similar or better performance than full pre‑training + fine‑tuning at lower total cost. Reviewers’ assessments diverge sharply: two reviewers rate 3, one rates 1 (very negative, high confidence), and one rates 5 (positive). The main points of praise are the well‑written exposition and the technical soundness of the loss; the main criticisms center on limited novelty, insufficient baselines and comparisons, lack of statistical rigor, and the reliance on synthetic data that is not given to baselines.

### Strengths
- The question of training small models efficiently is practically important and the paper addresses a relevant resource‑efficiency challenge.
- The proposed training method rests on a solid theoretical foundation (Alignment/Uniformity) and is well embedded into related work; comparisons to recent SoTA are appreciated.
- The paper is well‑written, easy to understand, and technically sound (according to reviewers 1,2,4).
- It combines existing ideas (knowledge distillation, contrastive learning, synthetic data) into a single recipe, which one reviewer (score 5) found valuable, particularly the contrastive‑learning‑based technique for scenarios where pre‑trained models for specific architectures are unavailable.
- The exposition is structured and easy to parse (all reviewers).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: Several reviewers (scores 1,3,3) consider the core idea – distilling from a pre‑trained teacher fine‑tuned on the target task – essentially standard knowledge distillation, with the only difference being that the teacher starts from pre‑training. The proposed distillation objective is seen as very derivative of contrastive distillation (Tian et al. 2020) without strong differentiation. The use of synthetic data for training is also not novel and has been extensively studied.
- **Absence of crucial references and insufficient comparison with state‑of‑the‑art distillation methods**: Reviewer 2 points out missing works, e.g., [1,2] on efficient (pre‑)training via knowledge distillation (achieving 82.22% with ResNet‑18/ResNet‑50 in 16 GPU hours vs. DPT’s 75.8% in 24 GPU hours), and active knowledge distillation [3]. The paper does not compare to these or similar methods, nor does it surpass them.
- **Experimental results are underwhelming or lack clarity**:
  - Performance shows high variability across settings (Fig. 4, Tables 2,3,5,6); CRD++ often outperforms DPT (Appendix Tables 5,6). The abbreviation S‑LP is not defined.
  - Standard deviations are not reported; the absence of statistical significance measures makes it unclear whether observed differences are meaningful.
  - Only two teacher architectures (convolutional and attention‑based) and six image classification datasets are tested, which is insufficient to claim general applicability. The method is not validated on other modalities, tasks, or model families (e.g., recurrent, graph networks).
  - The claim of applicability to “any pre‑trained teacher model” is not supported.
- **Definitions and cost accounting are vague**:
  - “Small models” is not clearly defined; the computational budget for the method is ambiguous.
  - The cost of training the pre‑trained teacher model is not accounted for – this is a substantial factor in real‑world applications (reviewer 1). The paper also does not account for the cost of synthetic data generation, which can be complex and computationally intensive (reviewer 2).
  - The paper does not discuss trade‑offs with other model compression techniques (network pruning, neural architecture search, quantization) that are also important for resource‑constrained deployment (reviewers 1,3).
- **Synthetic data issues**:
  - It is unclear whether baselines were also exposed to synthetic data during training, raising fairness concerns (reviewer 1).
  - The approach relies heavily on the quality of generative models; for datasets where good synthetic images cannot be easily generated, the method may fail (reviewers 1,4). A detailed ablation on synthetic data generation strategies is missing (reviewer 2).
- **Teacher fine‑tuning is insufficiently explored**: Only a linear probe is used for teacher adaptation to the target task. This may be inadequate, especially under larger distribution shifts (reviewer 3). Additionally, it is not stated whether the base fine‑tuned teachers also used synthetic data (reviewer 3).
- **Minority positive reviewer (score 5) acknowledges** that baselines considered are weak; they specifically request a comparison of a student that is pre‑trained and fully fine‑tuned versus DPT (2×). The positive reviewer also questions the practical use case when pre‑trained models are already available off‑the‑shelf and notes the method’s reliance on good generative models.
