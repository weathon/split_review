## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers recognize the TokenFormer as a novel and flexible architectural contribution that replaces standard linear projections in transformers with a Pattention layer, enabling progressive scaling by adding tokenized key-value parameters without retraining from scratch. The method demonstrates reduced training costs (e.g., scaling from 124M to 1.4B parameters with ~3× fewer tokens) and competitive performance on language and vision benchmarks. All reviewers note the clarity of writing and compelling empirical results. However, they raise concerns about theoretical grounding, limitations of fixing hidden dimension and depth, incomplete comparisons (especially with newer growth methods), and presentation of the Pattention mechanism. Reviewer 2 (score 6) is notably more critical, citing insufficient theoretical backing and ambiguous motivations, while reviewers 1, 3, and 4 (scores 8) are strongly positive but request additional analyses.

### Strengths
- **Innovative architectural idea**: Tokenized model parameters and the Pattention layer provide a flexible and scalable replacement for linear projections, likely to inspire further work (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Clear motivation and writing**: The problem of scaling transformers from scratch is well-articulated; figures and tables aid understanding (R1, R2).
- **Compelling empirical results**: Significant reduction in training cost (e.g., 3× fewer tokens for a 1.4B model) while achieving performance comparable to or better than standard transformers trained from scratch (R1, R3, R4).
- **Wide validation**: Experiments cover both causal language modeling and vision tasks (R2, R3).
- **Effective attention mechanism for parameter tokens**: Use of GELU scoring avoids vanishing gradients that would occur with softmax when scaling (R2, R2 additional comments).
- **Flexibility and no “healing” cost**: New parameter tokens can be added without degrading original model capabilities, and continual training exploits added parameters without recovery period (R4).
- **Unique scaling method**: Scaling by adding key-value pairs while keeping hidden dimension fixed avoids retraining from scratch and enables knowledge reuse (R1, R3).
- **Efficient progressive training**: Smaller models serve as starting points for larger ones, drastically lowering computational cost (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical foundation**: Why the approach works (e.g., role of hidden dimension vs. parameter tokens, benefits beyond parameter count) is only briefly mentioned in future work. A deeper theoretical insight would help generalize to other architectures (R2, also implied by R3’s capacity concerns).
- **Motivation imprecision**: The paper implies scaling cost is unique to transformers, but it applies to all DNNs. Further, it attributes cost to increasing parameters, yet later shows cost is dominated by self-attention. The real training saving comes from fixing hidden dimension and resuming, not directly from Pattention (R2).
- **Incomplete baselines for model growth**: Comparison with Net2Net uses an old method; newer growth approaches (e.g., Yao et al., 2024; arXiv:2409.12903v2) should be included (R2, R4). Initializing new query tokens as zeros may cause symmetry issues and relies solely on value token initialization; net2net-like zero-initialization is arguably suboptimal (R2).
- **Fixing hidden dimension and depth (scaling limitations)**:
  - Fixing hidden dimension may cause representation crowding, effectively capping model capacity. When adding more parameter tokens, the model may approach an upper limit (R2 question 1a). This also limits representation space compared to standard scaling of hidden dimension (R2).
  - Fixing depth restricts the functional complexity; scaling to >70B may be infeasible (R2 question 1b, echoed by R3 question on scaling to 7B/13B/70B). The constrained hidden dimension may limit FFN knowledge capacity (R3).
  - Fixed hidden dimension may negatively impact quantization methods (R2 question 1c).
- **Scaling complexity and cost analysis insufficient**:
  - The conclusion in Section 4.3 that Pattention is cheaper than linear layers depends on the regime $l \cdot n \cdot d \cdot T  < l \cdot d \cdot T^2$. The paper does not acknowledge regimes where it becomes more expensive, nor report empirical FLOPs. Additionally, adding parameter tokens while fixing depth harms parallelism (R2 question 4). For the 1.4B model, parameters per matrix grew from 2.4M to 6.6M, warranting direct cost measurement (R2).
  - Only results up to 1.5B parameters are shown. The complexity and effectiveness at scales where the number of token parameters may exceed token or model dimension are unknown (R3).
- **Potential artifacts from scaling steps**: Norms of new channels (initialized to zero) may differ from those of prior steps; this effect is not analyzed (R1). Similarly, representation collapse among new parameter tokens could occur without enforced symmetry breaking (R2 question 2a). It is also unclear whether previously learned parameter tokens should remain unfrozen or be frozen to learn residuals (R2 question 2b).
- **Mathematical presentation of Pattention is confusing**: Equation 4 introduces $\Theta$ described as a “modified softmax” but the actual operation uses GELU (function $f$). The connection between $\Theta$ and $f$ is unclear. The authors should describe the operation solely as $ \text{GeLU}(\text{RMSNorm}(XK))V $, which is essentially an MLP, and avoid misleading “softmax” language. Also, it is unclear how the $V$ parameters are expanded when adding tokens—are they zero-padded? (R4). A glossary of symbols would help (R4).
- **Table 1 bold formatting misleading**: Best performance highlighting is inconsistent (e.g., LAMBADA accuracy highlights Ours-1.5B over Pythia 1.8B, despite Pythia having more parameters) (R1).
- **Figure 6 convergence**: It is unclear whether the (Token)Transformer has converged on test loss (left) (R1).
- **Practical parameter/dataset/scaling relationship unexplored**: Minimum useful model size, practical considerations for step sizes and initializations, and differential effects of scaling channels vs. dimensions are not addressed (R1).
- **Missing comparison with linear attention mechanisms**: Could a linear attention version of Pattention (without GELU) be considered? (R4). Also, the approach could be applied to backbone architectures beyond transformers; experiments are lacking (R4).
- **Clarification needed on attention type**: Was softmax retained for MHA layers, or was GELU scoring applied there as well? (R2 question 5).
- **Future work suggestions lack substance**:
  - “Adjustable computational cost” is not well-explained; current methodology only allows scaling up, not adjustment (R2 question 6a).
  - Combining vision/language parameter tokens is highly speculative without preliminary results (R2 question 6b).
  - “Increasing interpretability” paragraph is vague; connection to sparse autoencoders (SAEs) is missing (R2 question 6c).
- **Parameter count discrepancy**: Table 3 appears to miss a factor of 2: Key + Value tokens total $2n$, so when $n = d_\text{model}$, Pattention has twice the parameters of a standard linear layer (R2 additional comments). The connection between GELU scoring and scaling (zero initialization works because GELU(0)=0, unlike exp(0)=1) should be made explicit (R2 additional comments).
