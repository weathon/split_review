- Scores: 3, 3, 1, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper claims that GPT/LLM architecture is inherently limited in producing rigorous proofs and novel reasoning. The argument is formalized using first-order predicate statements on random graphs, claiming that a GPT model has exponentially decreasing probability of correct proof unless the proof is memorized from training data. The paper includes theoretical results (Theorem 1 and corollaries) and a few experimental examples.

### Strengths
- The problem of assessing reasoning capabilities of LLMs versus memorization is very relevant and important to the current ML community (both positive reviewers; also noted by Reviewers 1 and 2).
- The logic-based model is potentially an interesting framework to address such questions (Reviewer 3, though said it is not tightly connected to LLMs).
- The paper aims to understand fundamental limitations of autoregressive next-token predictors, which is important (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of formalism and rigor**: The presentation severely lacks formal definitions and clear statements. Key terms ("literal learning", "veracity of inference", "hallucination", "learning model for inference", "first order model graph", "generic 0-1 law") are either undefined or vaguely defined. Theorem 1 appears colloquial and potentially incorrect — it is well-known that LLMs can generalize and produce novel proofs, contradicting the claim (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). All four reviewers rated presentation very low (1 or 2).
- **Randomization not properly defined**: The paper relies on "randomization in inference" of GPT/LLM models but does not quantify or formally define this randomness. Randomness alone does not imply a non-zero probability of error at each step; a distribution could assign probability 0 or 1 to each token (Reviewer 1). It is unclear whether the limitations apply under greedy decoding vs. temperature-based sampling (Reviewer 1).
- **No meaningful link to actual architecture**: The paper claims limitations follow from the attention mechanism or finite-temperature prediction, but neither the self-attention mechanism nor the specific architecture of GPT/LLMs is discussed in the main contributions (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Experiments are lacking and unscientific**: Section 2.2 presents only a few question-answer examples with general impressions (e.g., "Calude was the most pleasant and sensible"). There is no systematic evaluation: no list of prompts, models used, number of runs, metrics for correctness, or results. The claim "similar results were obtained for other chatbots, e.g. Perplexity" is vague. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Code example in Section 2.3 is incomprehensible** (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Repeated paragraph** in lines 68-73 (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Missing literature review** and no comparison to existing work on LLM reasoning capabilities (Reviewer 2).
- **Proofs and theorem structure unclear**: There is no pointer to where proofs of Theorem 1 and corollaries are located. Q.E.D. appears in places that do not end proofs (lines 171, 176, 191). Corollary 3’s statement boundary is unclear. Lemma 0 is mentioned but not cited; it is unclear where to find it in the appendix (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Unclear definitions of formal objects**: What is \( \omega \) in \( G(\omega) \)? What is \( p \) in line 155? What are \( \psi_i \)? The difference between \( C(e_0, e_t) \) and \( e_0 \sim e_t \) is not explained (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Incomplete justification for 0-1 law**: The paper claims that the limiting probability is either 0 or 1 (line 150) but does not cite the appropriate result (Fagin’s 0-1 law) and does not explain how we exclude probability 0 without describing \( G(\omega) \) (Reviewer 1).
- **Failure to address empirical counterevidence**: The paper mentions Trinh et al. (2024) on geometry problems but does not explain why these results are not a contradiction. More generally, there is empirical evidence that LLMs have some capability in rigorous proofs, which is not addressed (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1).
- **Relevance of post-training techniques**: The claim may only hold in the limit of infinite reasoning chains and with temperature-based sampling; post-training techniques (e.g., supervised fine-tuning, RL) could increase the chance of correct outputs. This is not discussed (Reviewer 1).
- **Contradiction with universal approximation results**: Reviewer 1 notes that autoregressive next-token predictors are known to be universal (Malach 2023), which would contradict the claimed inherent limitation. The paper does not reconcile this.
- **Section 2.2.1**: Experiments are not reproducible — no details on prompts, models, number of experiments, or validation metric (Reviewer 2).
- **Inconsistencies**: Line 189 mentions "Generalization of the main result Theorem 5" but Theorem 5 is not defined. Line 389 seems to have the two possibilities reversed (Reviewer 3).
- **Vague writing**: Sentences like "The logical view approach is more effective in generalization by varying a domain obeying the generic 0-1 law" are unclear. The concluding paragraph reads like personal opinion rather than a solid conclusion (Reviewer 3).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (1 and 2) rated the paper 3, acknowledging the importance of the topic and the problem statement, though they noted severe presentation issues. Two reviewers (3 and 4) rated it 1, finding the arguments unconvincing, the experiments unscientific, and the theoretical contributions lacking rigor. Even the more positive reviewers gave low soundness (1 or 2) and presentation (1) scores, indicating that the current version is not acceptable.
