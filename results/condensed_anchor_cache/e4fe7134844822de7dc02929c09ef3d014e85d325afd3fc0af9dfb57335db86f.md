## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SEPAI3R3O, an extended SEIR epidemiological model with over 10 parameters, and applies genetic algorithms for parameter optimization using SARS-related (flu and COVID-19) data from ICD/ICPC records in Recife, Brazil (April 26, 2020 – March 7, 2021). The model includes pre-symptomatic transmission, population subgroups, and underreporting (estimated at 50%). Results report an effective R₀ of 3 (95% CI 2.8–3.2) and growth rate 0.014 (95% CI 0.013–0.015). Reviewer ratings split: two reviewers rated 3 (fair confidence 4 and 3), finding the approach promising but incomplete; two rated 1 (confidence 4 and 3), finding the contribution incremental and the paper poorly written.

### Strengths
- **Epidemiological model extensions**: The SEPAI3R3O model incorporates pre-symptomatic transmission, underreporting of cases (a significant practical issue), and more detailed subdivision of population compartments into subgroups (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Use of real-world data**: The study is validated with actual sociodemographic and behavioral data from Recife, which is a positive factor for relevance (Reviewer 1).
- **Parameter optimization via genetic algorithms**: Applying GAs to fit the model parameters is a legitimate approach, and the attempt to combine optimization with a high-dimensional compartmental model is noted (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 2 acknowledges GAs are widely used but still a method).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient literature review & lack of comparative analysis**: The paper does not place itself in context of existing epidemiological models (e.g., OpenABM, gleam, Iranzo & Pérez-González 2021) or alternative optimization strategies (e.g., harmony search, Bayesian nonparametrics). No comparisons against other models or optimization algorithms are provided, so the claimed advantages of SEPAI3R3O over simpler SEIR variants or agent-based models are unsubstantiated (Reviewer 1, 2, 3). A minority reviewer (R1) rated 3 but still considered this a key missing element.
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution**: Applying genetic algorithms to a new epidemiological model does not, by itself, constitute a significant methodological contribution; GAs have been widely used for parameter estimation in epidemiology. The model modifications introduce many parameters (over 10), but the paper does not justify their necessity or demonstrate that the added complexity leads to substantially better predictions or insights compared to existing models (Reviewer 2, 3). Reviewers 3 and 4 (score 1) were especially critical, while R2 (score 3) also found the contribution incremental.
- **Incomplete results and evaluation**: The time window covered is very narrow (about 11 months), insufficient to assess predictive accuracy or generalizability to broader pandemic dynamics. The results section merely enumerates model outputs without discussing why values are reasonable or how good/bad the fit is; no intuition or critical analysis of model performance is provided (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Poor presentation, clarity, and reproducibility**: The paper is poorly written, missing essential details: what exactly the data consists of, how it was preprocessed into model inputs, what metrics are used and why, the definition of “protection rate”, and the meaning of “more accurate representation” relative to what baseline. In Table 2 there are unexplained inconsistencies with other sections. An unfinished sentence reads: “The average protection rate over the period under analysis was 0.014 (95”. Typographical error: underreporting rate is given as 0.50% but should be 50% (Reviewer 1, 2). No open-source code is provided, hindering reproducibility (Reviewer 1).
- **Parameter estimation and validation concerns**: With over 10 parameters (some time-varying) and limited data, the reliability of fitted values is questionable. The paper does not discuss uncertainty in parameter estimates, sensitivity analysis, or how the underreporting rate of 50% was derived—whether from local Recife data or generalized from broader studies. The simulated death rates are higher than observed (~20-30% reduction) but no explanation is given for this discrepancy (Reviewer 1, 2, 3). Additional validation against other data sources (hospitalizations, confirmed case counts) is missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing discussion of methodology choice**: The paper’s focus is unclear: is the contribution the model or the optimization method? The advantages and disadvantages of genetic algorithms compared to popular alternatives (e.g., MCMC, gradient-based methods) are not discussed (Reviewer 4). A reviewer questioned the motivation for submitting to ICLR rather than an epidemiology journal, asking what specific benefits the ML community brings (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of accounting for policy/behavior changes**: The model does not incorporate temporal changes in public health policies or population behavior; how such dynamics could be reflected in model parameters is not addressed (Reviewer 2).
