## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a text-to-graph generation method that combines an LLM (fine-tuned via self-supervised text-graph alignment tasks) with a graph diffusion model, using a structure-aware cross-attention mechanism to condition generation on text embeddings. Experiments are conducted on synthetic and molecular (ChEBI-20) datasets. Reviewers note the problem is interesting and the overall architecture is clearly motivated and described, but raise substantial concerns about the simplicity of the alignment tasks, limited evaluation, missing comparisons, insufficient justification for using an LLM, and lack of reproducibility details. The two lower-scoring reviewers (3,3) find the method’s novelty limited and the results uncompetitive, while the two higher-scoring reviewers (5,5) are more positive but still identify significant weaknesses.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles the underexplored problem of text-to-graph generation by guiding graph diffusion with LLMs.
- The motivation and challenges are clearly presented, with a well-structured figure in the introduction.
- The Preliminaries section makes the paper self-contained; model architecture is clearly described overall.
- The self-supervised finetuning of the LLM and the structure-aware cross-attention mechanism are reasonable components.
- Because the LLM is frozen after pretraining, it does not add significant complexity to training the diffusion model.
- Informative visualizations help understand the method.
- Experiments cover both synthetic and molecular generation tasks, and ablation analysis confirms the importance of each module.
- The paper is easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Pretraining tasks are too simplistic and may not capture meaningful graph semantics.** The objectives (predicting number of nodes, edges, subgraphs) reduce to counting and do not encourage the LLM to learn relational or structural dependencies. Multiple reviewers suggest more sophisticated self-supervised tasks such as contrastive learning between connected/unconnected nodes, subgraph isomorphism prediction, or graph edit distance prediction. Counting cycles or cliques alone likely provides insufficient guidance for complex graph generation.
- **The evaluation on real-world data is limited to a single molecular dataset (ChEBI-20).** The proposed method is outperformed on three out of six metrics on this dataset. The paper does not demonstrate generalization to other domains (e.g., social networks, knowledge graphs). At least one reviewer considers the experiments otherwise sufficient, but multiple reviewers call for additional real-world datasets and tasks.
- **Missing baseline comparisons.** The paper omits conditional graph diffusion models (e.g., conditional GDSS, conditional DiGress) and other text-to-graph or text-to-molecule generation methods (e.g., recent LLM-based approaches). This makes it difficult to assess the relative contribution. Related works on text-to-graph diffusion models and LLM-based text-to-molecule generation are not discussed.
- **Unclear and insufficient method details hamper reproducibility.** Code is not provided. The steps after generating the conditional matrices \(X_{cond}\) and \(E_{cond,uv}\) (how they are integrated into the diffusion process) are not explained. No implementation details (architecture, training procedure, hyperparameters) are given. Without these, the results cannot be reproduced.
- **The attention matrix \(A_{edge,uv}\) in Eq. 12 is not normalized** (row sums do not equal 1), making the term “attention” potentially misleading. If unnormalized, its use in the diffusion model is unclear and could cause issues.
- **The choice of Gaussian noise over discrete noise** (as used in DiGress) is not justified. Graphs are discrete structures, so the suitability of continuous diffusion needs clarification.
- **The metrics do not directly measure text-to-graph alignment.** There is no metric that evaluates whether the generated graph satisfies the requirements expressed in the input text (e.g., semantic similarity or task-specific correctness).
- **No standard deviations or statistical significance tests** are reported, making it impossible to judge whether performance differences are reliable.
- **Computational complexity analysis is missing**, despite the paper mentioning computational costs.
- **The method only models text-to-graph generation, not the inverse direction (graph-to-text).** Existing work on text–graph generation often considers both, so modeling only one side limits the technical contribution.
- **Justification for using an LLM is weak.** The paper uses the LLM solely to produce text embeddings; it is unclear why a dedicated language model for embeddings (e.g., Sentence-BERT) would not suffice, especially given the overhead of fine-tuning an LLM. The need for an LLM, rather than other embedding techniques, is not convincingly argued.
- **The claim that graph diffusion models “mainly focus on unconditional generation” is inaccurate** (e.g., GDSS and DiGress have conditional versions in [1,2]). The authors should survey existing conditional graph diffusion models more thoroughly.
- **Novelty is limited.** The combination of latent graph diffusion and LLM alignment techniques has been explored in prior works [1,2]. The proposed method only outperforms the smallest baseline (MolT5-small) on three metrics, and its utility is significantly lower than current models in the domain.
- **Questions about model details:**
  - How is the token representation matrix \(C\) (Eq. 8) made compatible with the node feature matrix \(X\)? Is an entity recognition step used to identify which tokens correspond to nodes?
  - Equation 8 divides \(QK^\top\) by \(d\) rather than the standard \(\sqrt{d}\) – why?
  - The edge conditioning matrix \(E_{cond,uv}\) (Eq. 12) represents a complete graph, but molecular graphs are sparse. How is sparsity encouraged? Could the dense structure lead to over-smoothing?
  - What specific LLM and GNN architecture are used?
