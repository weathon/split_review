## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents a pioneering, large-scale study on extracting training data from aligned, production large language models (LLMs) using only publicly available tools. The authors define memorization as generating ≥50 tokens that match training data and construct AUXDATASET (10TB merging four major training datasets) as a ground-truth corpus. They show that baseline completion attacks fail against aligned models but propose two novel attacks to bypass alignment: (1) a prompt-based *divergence attack* (e.g., repeating a word) that causes ChatGPT to deviate from chatbot behavior and emit memorized data at a rate 150× higher, and (2) a fine-tuning-based divergence attack that further increases extraction (23% of samples from ChatGPT). Both attacks successfully extract gigabytes of training data from open-source, semi-open, and closed models (ChatGPT, Gemini, GPT-4, Llama-2-chat). Extractions include copyrighted New York Times content, toxic text, personally identifiable information, and OpenAI’s unreleased training data. The work highlights that current alignment techniques do not eliminate memorization.

### Strengths

- **Important and timely problem**: Highlights critical limitations of current alignment techniques in preventing training data extraction from LLMs. The contributions are valid and significant.
- **Robust methodology**: Clear definition of memorization (≥50 tokens), creation of the 10TB AUXDATASET as a proxy for unknown training data, and results presented as quantifiable lower bounds. The scalable approach for validating memorization is well-constructed.
- **Technical innovation**: Two novel, simple, and effective attack methods (prompt-based divergence and fine-tuning divergence) that are cost-effective and require only publicly accessible tools. The attacks successfully circumvent the defense mechanisms of state-of-the-art production models.
- **Comprehensive empirical analysis**: Large-scale experiments on multiple representative models (Pythia, GPT-Neo, LLaMA, Falcon, ChatGPT, Gemini, GPT-4, Llama-2-chat). Reveals correlations between model size and memorization, and introduces meaningful metrics (e.g., unique 50-grams). The extensive appendix contains sufficient supplementary experiments.
- **Compelling practical implications**: Demonstrates extraction of sensitive content (copyrighted material from The New York Times, toxic content, PII, unreleased training data from OpenAI), grounding the findings in real-world concerns.
- **Good presentation** (overall): The paper is well-structured, clearly written, easy to follow, and rich in detail (clear definitions of memorization, thorough scenario descriptions). Most reviewers found the exposition helpful.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited generalizability of divergence attacks**: The prompt-based divergence attack has been largely fixed by OpenAI and shows effectiveness only on GPT-3.5-Turbo, not on Gemini or other aligned models. The fine-tuning attack shows reduced effectiveness on Llama-2-chat and has not been tested on Gemini; this suggests the methods may be model-specific rather than universal. One reviewer noted the attacks might only be correlated with a single aspect of memorization.
- **Correlation between divergence and memorization not strongly established**: It is unclear whether divergence attacks merely reveal memorization or actively induce it by forcing the model into an unusual regime. The paper lacks a deep analysis of memorization occurring without divergence (e.g., under normal chatbot behavior). Systematic ablations disentangling these effects are missing.
- **Methodological limitations**: The heavy reliance on divergence attacks may provide an incomplete picture; other attack vectors (targeted prompt engineering, adversarial examples) are not explored. Baseline attacks were tested on only one aligned model (GPT-3.5) — more aligned models (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini) should be included to support the claim that baseline attacks fail against aligned models.
- **Practical success rate and precision unclear**: In the divergence attack, 1000 tokens are extracted but only about 50 tokens are identified as training data, significantly lowering the effective extraction rate. The paper lacks a discussion of the probability of accurately extracting training data chunks when the training dataset is unknown, making the attack’s practical success ambiguous. The attack appears to have a significant random component and may not enable targeted extraction of specific information, limiting its efficiency for attackers.
- **Lack of theoretical depth**: The analysis of why model capacity correlates with memorization is more empirical than theoretical. No detailed theoretical examination is provided. One reviewer felt the paper lacks innovation or technical contribution for a top conference, though this was a minority view (most found the contributions significant and valid).
- **Missing related work section**: The paper does not properly cover related work, potential defenses in the literature, or the context of known attacks. Several attacks have been discussed elsewhere, and the relationship to adversarial attacks is not discussed. A proper related work section is needed to clarify the novelty and significance of the approach.
- **Paper organization and presentation issues**: Too much essential information is deferred to the appendix; the main text should prioritize key results and figures (e.g., figures from Appendix A.9, Figure 2 is never mentioned in the main text). There is a broken symbol on line 071. Minor presentation issues reduce clarity.
- **Qualitative analysis could be deeper**: Section 7 focuses mainly on length of extracted strings; it would benefit from a more detailed analysis of the semantic content and nature of leaked information. For example: Are divergence attack extractions mainly common phrases or sensitive/unique data points? For fine-tuning, does the extracted text consistently align with the specific fine-tuning data or also include unrelated memorized content? Additional analysis on cases where memorization occurs without divergence is needed.
- **Specific missing experiments and clarifications** (reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms):
  - Why was the fine-tuning attack not applied to Gemini? (Asked by multiple reviewers.)
  - Need more comprehensive experiments on newer aligned open-source models for both divergence and fine-tuning attacks.
  - Need an estimate of the probability of extracting the training data part from the whole response assuming the training data is unknown.
  - Provide examples of near-duplicate generations and their significance.
  - The paper could benefit from a discussion on what circumstances cause the divergence attack to work and why its effectiveness varies across models.
