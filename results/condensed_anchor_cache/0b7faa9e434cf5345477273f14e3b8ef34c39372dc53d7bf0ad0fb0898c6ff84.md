## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies feature learning in two-layer neural networks trained with one gradient descent step in the proportional high-dimensional limit, focusing on two quantities: *alignment* (intra-class feature alignment) and *local elasticity*. The authors analyze classification tasks under sub-Gaussian data assumptions, allowing distribution shift between training and evaluation data. They derive explicit asymptotic formulas for alignment and local elasticity, and show that both improve when the train-unseen similarity (measured by βᵀμ) is large. Experiments on synthetic data validate the theory and also explore multi-layer CNNs and metric learning (Recall@1). The work extends previous regression-based analyses (Ba et al. 2022, Moniri et al. 2024) to classification and sub-Gaussian data.

### Strengths
- **Interesting and timely topic**: Feature learning and alignment are important for understanding neural network representations. The theoretical study of alignment and local elasticity in a controlled setting is worthwhile and addresses an under-explored regime (conjugate kernel model, classification, sub-Gaussian data) (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5,6).
- **Rigorous theoretical foundation**: The paper provides explicit asymptotic formulas for alignment and local elasticity, with solid mathematical derivations. The result that alignment and local elasticity emerge when train-unseen similarity is high is clean and interpretable (Reviewers 3,4; Reviewer 1 notes the extension to novel distribution and setting).
- **Quantitative approach**: The setup is generic (non-convex loss, two-layer net, sub-Gaussian inputs) and the analysis gives a clear quantitative dependence on βᵀμ, not just a qualitative observation (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 5 notes conversion to classification highlights differences from regression).
- **Positive minority view**: Two reviewers (R3 rating 6, R4 rating 5) found the math solid and the results sufficiently clean, and considered the theory to offer a new perspective on feature learning. Reviewer 6 also notes the alignment phenomenon as potentially useful and the setup as nice.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and lack of clarity** (severe, unanimous across reviewers except partially R4): The writing is extremely unclear, with many undefined quantities, missing notation, broken sentences, and inconsistent conventions. Specific examples:
  - Definitions 1.1 and 1.2 (Alignment, Local Elasticity) use $c_3,c_4$ without introduction; indices in sums in Theorems 1.3/1.4 are unspecified (R1,R2,R5,R6).
  - Figure 1 is confusing: origin only in left panel, (a,b,c,d) not linked to legend, $(a_1,a_2)$ undefined, sphere/transformation and grey arrows unexplained (R1).
  - Theorems 1.3 and 1.4 are stated informally without assumptions on task, data, objective, or network; the connection to formal Theorems 5.3/5.4 is not explained (R1,R3,R5,R6).
  - Notation changes confusingly: $\beta$ dimension unclear; $XW^\top$ vs $XW$ inconsistency; $y[c]$ vs $y[:,c]$; $a$ vs $a_2$ in gradient decomposition (R2,R6).
  - Formal theorems (5.3, 5.4) defer all variable definitions to appendix, making them uninterpretable in the main text (R2,R5,R6).
  - Appendix is poorly written, looks like sketchy notes with typos (e.g., "$\mathcal N_{(a,b)}(\mu,\sigma)$ ... known as Sub-Gaussian familly", use of Hebrew letters like Mem and Bet) (R2,R5).
- **Insufficient discussion and intuition**: The paper fails to explain what alignment and local elasticity really mean, and does not sufficiently connect theory to practice. For example, "Our analysis implicates that Alignment and Local Elasticity have strong relevance with how close the data distributions are to the training samples" is vague; CK framework is never explained (R1,R2,R5,R6).
- **Lack of clear main contribution**: The informal theorems essentially state that features align with the unregularized least-squares solution, which is not presented as surprising or important. It is unclear what new technique or insight is added beyond existing work (Ba et al. 2022); the machinery is mostly borrowed (R1,R5,R6). The claim of discovering a "relationship/connection" between alignment and local elasticity is weak—the paper only shows co-occurrence after one step, not a deeper link (R4).
- **Limited experiments and no real-world data**: Experiments use only synthetic simulations; no evaluation on real datasets (e.g., vision or NLP) to demonstrate generalizability. The relationship between alignment and Recall@1 in metric learning is not convincingly shown (R3,R5,R6). Figures (3,4,5) have extremely small font sizes, making them unreadable (R1,R2,R4).
- **Restricted setting**: The analysis is confined to two-layer networks, one step of gradient descent, classification task, and separable classes. The one-step GD limitation is not explicitly stated in theorem statements (R4). Questions about whether the same result holds for linear models (R3) or under maximal learning rate scaling (Cui et al. 2024) (R1) remain unaddressed.
- **Lack of rigor and missing assumptions**: Assumptions (e.g., on data, network, loss) are not clearly stated, even in formal theorems. The randomness underlying the high-probability statements is unclear (what is averaged over?) (R3,R5,R6). No concentration results are provided to show that the training data instance dependence of $\beta$ averages out; comparisons to prior high-dimensional classification work (Mignacco 2020, Loureiro 2021) are missing (R2).
- **Missing ablations and alternative shifts**: Only the specific distribution shift $e\mu$ is considered; the authors are asked to comment on more general shifts (R1). The role of the separability condition is not explained (R2).
- **Many typos and formatting errors**: "11unseen", missing periods, broken LaTeX (e.g., $E_{x_3}$ should be $\mathbb E_{x_3}$), "datum" instead of "datum"/"data point", inconsistent spacing, etc. (R2,R6).
