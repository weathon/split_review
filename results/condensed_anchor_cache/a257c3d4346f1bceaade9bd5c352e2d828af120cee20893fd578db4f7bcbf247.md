- Scores: 3, 5, 5, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers broadly agree the paper addresses an important applied problem—flood simulation using a physics-inspired graph neural network (ComGNN) based on conservation of mass and momentum from the shallow-water equations (SWE). The model is tested on real-world data (LISFLOOD-FP, 9 regions, 7 historical events) and shows improvements, especially during early flood stages. However, the majority of reviewers (three scores of 5, one score of 3) raise substantial concerns about limited novelty, insufficient comparison to existing methods (MeshGraphNets, numerical SWE solvers, neural operators, PINNs), lack of theoretical analysis, and missing ablations. The minority reviewer is notably more critical of the theoretical contribution and presentation. Overall, the contribution is rated as fair to modest, and many weaknesses center on the need for stronger baselines, clearer architectural details, and justification of design choices.

### Strengths
- Graph neural network successfully operates in a two-stage paradigm (water retention + propagation) for early-stage flood simulation given a rainfall event.  
- The message-passing mechanism on a flow-direction directed graph outperforms current approaches (including a baseline GNN for flood modeling).  
- The model is trained directly on real-world data rather than synthetic data, enhancing applicability and generalization.  
- Incorporation of physical information from the shallow-water equations (conservation of mass and momentum) into a GNN framework is a nice physically motivated approach.  
- Background on PDEs, relation between SWE and Navier-Stokes, and discretization impacts are well presented.  
- Interesting use of directed graphs with a single outgoing edge per node (steepest descent).  
- The retention layer and message-passing are designed to mimic underlying physics without over-constraining the model; key differences from Bentivoglio et al. (2023) are explained.  
- The paper is well written, concepts are clearly explained, and figures are helpful.  
- The application is important and difficult; the Earth System Modeling community is seeking ML solutions, and this work contributes an interpretable, physics-based approach.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and comparison to MeshGraphNets**: The method is highly similar to MeshGraphNets (Pfaff et al., ICLR 2021) applied to the shallow-water equations. Reviewers question what distinguishes ComGNN beyond training on SWE-simulated data with a possibly different loss function. The lack of a direct comparison to MeshGraphNets makes the novelty difficult to assess. (Majority concern; minority reviewer especially sees insufficient theoretical advancement.)  
- **Missing strong baselines**: The baselines are extremely limited. Missing comparisons include:  
  * MeshGraphNets (Pfaff et al., ICLR 2021) – essential because the proposed method closely resembles it.  
  * Numerical SWE solvers (finite volume, finite element) – standard techniques that provide a direct accuracy/runtime benchmark.  
  * Recent SciML methods such as MP-PDE, DINO, PINNs, Neural Operators, Interaction Networks (Battaglia et al., 2016; known for fluid simulations).  
  * CNN-based U-Net from 2015 is outdated; newer architectures should be considered.  
  * Machine learning models that account for past states (e.g., Oliveira Santos et al., 2023; Farahmand et al., 2023) – the authors should discuss whether only current state is sufficient.  
- **Lack of theoretical analysis and convergence properties**: The model is applied without discussing convergence, stability, or truncation error; there is no theoretical advancement. (Minority reviewer emphasizes this as “not suitable for ICLR.”)  
- **Insufficient architectural detail**: The two-stage process implementation within the GNN is not elaborated clearly. Missing:  
  * How retention and propagation interact as distinct graph operations.  
  * The specific node and edge features and how they are updated during message passing.  
  * The precise construction, aggregation, and update rules for messages, and their relation to conservation laws.  
  * No initial definition of “message” is given. (Minority reviewer is particularly negative on this point.)  
- **Parameter setting and numerical stability**:  
  * ∆t, ∆x, ∆y, and g are set to 1 with the reasoning “since they remain constant during simulation, they are multiplicative factors.” This is a major limitation because Forward Euler requires a CFL condition bounding ∆t relative to ∆x; setting them to 1 is far too coarse. The authors do not clarify how the CFL condition is handled or what actual values are used.  
  * The use of first-order Forward Euler (and Verlet) for time stepping has known stability challenges; more advanced schemes (e.g., RK4) could perform better. No ablation studies on discretization choice are provided.  
- **Loss function and negative depth penalty**: The loss (Eq. 8) includes a penalty for negative water depths but does not enforce non-negativity via a ReLU activation after the last layer. The mix of L1 and L2 losses appears empirically motivated (Appendix A.4) without justification. (Reviewer 3 questions this.)  
- **Retention impact and architecture necessity**: The variant ComGNN- (without retention) does not outperform baselines comprehensively, suggesting the retention aspect may be the primary driver of improvement. This raises the question: should retention be incorporated into existing architectures rather than proposing a new GNN? (Reviewer 4)  
- **Assumption of single downhill edge**: Using a single outgoing edge per node assumes water only propagates downhill; this may not hold for later-stage flood simulations or other settings. The authors should verify if this inductive bias is helpful and discuss its limitations. (Reviewer 3)  
- **Generalizability**: The method appears specific to shallow-water equations and flood detection. How it can be extended to broader PDEs or different applications is not addressed.  
- **Missing runtime comparison**: A key motivation for ML-based models is speed; but no runtime comparison with numerical SWE solvers is provided. (Reviewer 3)  
- **Parameter count not reported**: The number of parameters of each model is a crucial factor determining performance; it is not given. (Reviewer 3, and post-rebuttal note that current comparison may use significantly different sizes.)  
- **Minor presentation issues**:  
  * Too many experimental figures on pages 8–9; they should be moved to an appendix to make room for theoretical content.  
  * Section 3 should start with an overview before 3.1.  
  * Punctuation following equations is inconsistent.  
  * References appear before some results.  
  * The formula for Pearson correlation coefficient appears incorrect (seems to repeat NSE).  
- **Concept of “physics-informed”**: The title uses “physics-informed” which is misleading because it suggests PINNs; a term like “physical inductive-bias” would be more appropriate. (Reviewer 3)  
- **Inability to answer key questions**:  
  * Ablation studies on GNN architecture choice are missing.  
  * How does the model handle water splitting from one node to multiple downstream nodes? (Reviewer 4)  
  * Is elevation the only factor influencing dispersion? What about other factors and their inclusion?  
  * Do competing models also take DEM as input?  
  * Can the model show water retention and dispersion separately in experiments?  
  * Are retention capacities region-dependent? Which model component captures retention capacity?  
  * Was uniform elevation per region assumed? What is the spatial resolution of DEM, rainfall, and flood depth maps?  
  * Could back-propagation through multiple evaluation steps improve long-term stability? (Reviewer 3)
