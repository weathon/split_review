## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates data selection methods for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of LLMs at million-scale, replicating multiple self-scoring techniques on OpenHermes2.5 and WildChat. It finds that nearly all methods struggle to significantly outperform random selection at this scale, that diversity is more important than quality filtering, and that filtering by token length (especially long texts) improves results for weaker base models like Llama3-8B.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely study.** Rigorously testing existing SFT data selection methods on state-of-the-art large-scale datasets is valuable and novel, as prior evaluations focused on smaller pools. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- **Reveals limitations of current methods.** Demonstrates that many self-scoring techniques (e.g., quality-based methods) fail to outperform random selection on million-scale data, a finding highly relevant to practitioners. (R1, R2, R3, R5)
- **Identifies diversity over quality.** Shows that diversity-based selection is more effective than quality-based filtering in large-scale SFT, challenging common assumptions. (R1, R3)
- **Proposes simple, stable improvement.** Length-based filtering yields stable gains, especially for weaker base models like Llama3-8B, without the computational cost of sophisticated methods. (R1, R3)
- **Thorough experimental setup.** Implements numerous baselines, tests multiple base models (Qwen2-7B, Llama3-8B, DeepSeek) and dataset sizes post-selection, providing robustness checks. (R3)
- **Significant for data curation efforts.** The findings offer a simplified framework that reduces the need for rigorous data cleaning at scale, a hard and important task. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Inability to isolate scale as the cause.** The paper claims dataset size drives method failure, but larger datasets (OH2.5, WildChat) also differ in quality and curation. Controlled experiments on random subsets of the same dataset at varying sizes (e.g., 10K → 100K → 1M) are missing; even at 100K, quality methods underperform, suggesting other factors beyond scale. (R3)
- **Run-to-run variance not properly handled.** Comparing a single run of each selection method to the *max* over 5 random runs is unfair. In practice, a practitioner would need to run random selection multiple times to achieve that max, increasing costs. The paper should report mean and standard deviation for random runs and ideally also for non-random methods. The observation that training on *all* 1M examples is often as fast or faster than running selection methods is an important failure that should be emphasized. (R3)
- **Lack of in-depth analysis for method failure.** The paper identifies that selection methods fail but provides insufficient theoretical or empirical explanation (e.g., why quality‑based methods degrade, why diversity methods hold up). This reduces the depth expected at top venues. (R2, R3, R5)
- **Limited novelty due to prior work.** Key findings—importance of diversity in SFT and effectiveness of selecting longer answers—have been reported in earlier papers (e.g., [1], [2]) that are not cited. (R2)
- **Title overstates the message.** “Random selection is almost all you need” contradicts results where diversity‑based methods outperform random selected on Qwen2-7B (e.g., Table 1: Diverse average 59.58 vs random max 59.35; also in 50K regime Tables 5‑6). A title reflecting the importance of diversity would better set reader expectations. (R4, R5)
- **Insufficient model and scale variation.** Experiments only use two LLMs (Qwen2-7B, Llama3-8B) plus DeepSeek‑V2. The conclusions may not generalize to smaller or larger models. (R4)
- **Length‑based filtering is poorly motivated and not integrated.** Why longer texts benefit weaker models is not explained (e.g., information density, training signal complexity). For Qwen2-7B (Table 3) length filtering does not outperform existing methods, yet the paper still promotes it. The connection to the core narrative is weak. (R3, R4, R5)
- **No control for training‑test overlap.** The paper does not report token‑ or example‑level overlap between SFT datasets and downstream benchmarks (e.g., BBH, MMLU). If selection methods bias toward examples similar to test data, reported gains may reflect memorization rather than generalization. Deduplication status of SFT datasets is also missing. (R1)
- **“Extensive SFT dataset” not clearly defined.** It is ambiguous how to determine whether a dataset qualifies as “extensive,” limiting practical application of the findings. (R2)
- **Clarity issues in introduction and method description.** “Scoring” and the distinction between data quality‑based and data diversity‑based methods need clearer explanation in the introduction. Section 3 on baselines uses inconsistent notation (e.g., $z$, $x$, $n$ for samples; undefined $\hat{\Gamma}$ in LESS, $\mathcal{C}$ in ZIP), making it hard to follow. (R3, R5)
- **Statistical significance not validated.** The phrase “significantly better” is used without reporting p‑values or confidence intervals. (R5)
- **Miscellaneous errors and missing references.**
  - Line 39: missing space before citation.
  - Line 324: missing space before bracket.
  - Table 1 and Table 2: some best numbers not bolded (e.g., Table 1).
  - Figure 1: “BBH benchmark” needs a literature reference.
  - Table captions: “the suboptimal score” should read “second highest score”. (R3, R5)
- **Less alignment with original LESS method.** LESS was designed for targeted selection (choosing data for a specific target set). Setting the target as a subset of the overall pool may result in less diverse data and explain poor performance. (R3)
