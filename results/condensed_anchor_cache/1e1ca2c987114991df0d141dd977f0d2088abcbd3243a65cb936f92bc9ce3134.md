## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Structured RigL (SRigL), a dynamic sparse training (DST) method that imposes a constant fan-in constraint to produce fine-grained structured N:M sparsity. SRigL is evaluated on vision tasks (ResNet, ViT) and demonstrates accuracy close to unstructured RigL while enabling real-world CPU speedups (up to 3.4×/2.5× over dense/CSR at 90% sparsity). Reviewers agree the topic is timely and the method is clearly described, but raise significant concerns about novelty (overlap with Chase and prior N:M sparse-training works), missing baselines (SR-STE, AST, blocked sparsity, dedicated inference engines), limited experimental scope (only high-redundancy networks, only vision), insufficient evidence for training-time speedup or memory advantage, and several experimental details (ablation outcome, wall-clock measurement noise, Figure 3 inflation). One reviewer (score 8) is more positive, while another (score 5) is notably more critical; the remaining three (score 6) express moderate concerns.

### Strengths
- Research topic of structured DST is timely and important for practical deployment of sparse NNs (R1, R3).
- Comprehensive and precise related work covering state-of-the-art structured/unstructured sparse training approaches (R1).
- Detailed exposition of SRigL steps in Section 3 provides a clear overview (R1, R3, R5).
- SRigL achieves better real-world wall-clock inference on CPU (3.4× dense, 2.5× CSR at 90% sparsity in a linear layer) than structured (only ablation) and unstructured alternatives; uniform layer-wise sparsity is more desirable than N:M (R1).
- SRigL maintains generalization comparable to RigL even at high sparsity (≥90%) across various architectures (R2, R3, R5).
- Improves parameter/memory efficiency and enables acceleration during training (R2).
- Minimal accuracy drop at >90% sparsity on both ResNet and ViT (R2).
- Method is well-motivated, written clearly, and the main idea is simple to explain (R3).
- Neuron ablation procedure is sound and noticeably improves performance (R5).
- Appendix includes theoretical analysis (output norm variance reduction) motivating constant fan-in sparsity (R5).
- Algorithm is simple and yields speedups even without highly customized code (R5).
- Extensive empirical evidence on several models (R4).
- Creative combination of structured N:M sparsity with a constant fan-in constraint (R4).
- Ability to achieve faster real-world inference on CPUs is of paramount importance (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns and overlap with prior work (R1, R4):** The paper partially overlaps with Chase (arxiv 2305.19454), which also identifies “sparse amenable channels” and performs channel pruning during DST. R1 notes the overlap in identifying such channels is significant and that the paper must clearly articulate novelty beyond this observation, discuss whether Chase’s channel pruning could implicitly lead to similar effects, and provide a detailed comparison of channel selection criteria. R4 adds that the combination of sparse training with N:M sparsity has been shown in a prior study (reference [1]) and that the paper lacks a clear explanation of how SRigL’s structured sparsity differs fundamentally from methods leveraging channel-level sparsity; it is unclear if the fine-grained sparsity is truly distinct or an artifact of the N:M implementation. R4 also questions how the constant fan-in constraint provides a significant advantage over other structured sparsity methods when N:M sparsity can be implemented with varying fan-in by adjusting block size.
- **Missing baselines and comparisons (R2, R3, R4, R5):** 
  - No comparison to a similar DST procedure that enforces N:M sparsity (e.g., prune smallest magnitude among groups of M consecutive weights and regrow the same fraction among zeros within the group) – such a comparison would motivate the need for constant fan-in sparsity (R5).
  - No comparison to RigL with blocked sparsity (pruning whole groups of consecutive weights), which is known to be more CPU-speedup friendly (R5).
  - No comparison against established structured pruning methods; results on structured pruning performance are absent, making it difficult to assess if gains are due to dynamic sparsity or the structured constraint (R2).
  - Missing comparison with dedicated inference engines like DeepSparse for CPU speed-ups (R5).
  - Missing baselines AST and SR-STE, which are very related works; the paper claims they are not comparable but should report their results (R4).
  - No comparison to dense-to-sparse methods (including SR-STE) for training time or memory footprint; the paper’s claim that sparse-to-sparse training is beneficial over dense-to-sparse lacks evidence (R3).
- **Limited experimental scope (R2):** The method is only demonstrated on high-redundancy networks (ResNet, ViT); no experiments on lower-redundancy networks like MobileNet where each parameter is more critical, raising concerns about general applicability and potential performance degradation under constant fan-in constraints (R2). Experiments are limited to vision tasks; it is unclear how SRigL would perform on sequential data (NLP, time-series) where architectures differ (R2).
- **Lack of evidence for training speedup and memory advantage (R1, R3):** R1 asks whether SRigL can accelerate the training process with real-world wall-clock savings. R3 notes that no evidence shows SRigL trains models faster than dense-to-sparse methods (e.g., SR-STE) and that a quantitative evaluation of memory footprint differences would make the paper more convincing.
- **Ablation results counter-intuitive (R1):** In Tables 1 and 3, SRigL with neuron ablation often outperforms SRigL without ablation, despite the ablation producing a smaller model (reduced capacity). This is surprising and warrants further analysis of how parameter redistribution after ablation affects learning dynamics. It is unclear whether “SRigL w/o ablation” only prunes dead channels without weight regrowth; the paper should clarify whether the constant fan-in constraint is applied in both scenarios and how it interacts with the parameter redistribution during ablation.
- **Wall-clock measurement concerns (R3, R5):** The median of only 5 runs is shown for inference times in the microsecond range; this is noisy and more runs (millions) should be used (R3). Wall-clock timings for N:M sparsity appear suspicious – higher N:M sparsity may be slower than lower sparsity; at least saturation, not linear speed-up, is expected (R5).
- **Figure 3 inflation (R3):** Showing SRigL with 2× and 5× training budget without showing equivalent RigL versions (RigL x2, RigL x5) inflates the presentation; either include RigL baselines at those budgets or omit both.
- **Missing overview figure (R2):** Absence of a comprehensive figure illustrating the entire SRigL process makes it harder to grasp the algorithm flow quickly.
- **Minor issues (R2, R5):** - Appendix E plots for w/o ablation appear absent from Figure 7 (R5). - Sensitivity to γ_sal not explored, especially for low-redundancy networks (R2). - How well does the method perform combined with quantization? Sparse+8bit quantized models could give even higher speed-ups (R5). - How much does the method explore new connections compared to RigL? Comparison using a measure like In-Time Over-Parameterization would be interesting (R5). - Difficulty of implementing GPU kernel for SRigL and whether existing CUDA implementations (Schultheis & Babbar 2023) could be used (R1). - Throughput on real GPU/CPU based on sparsity levels not reported (R2).
