## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a defense against clean-label poisoning attacks by denoising training data with an off-the-shelf diffusion model before training, inspired by denoised smoothing. The authors evaluate against seven clean-label attacks on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny ImageNet, reporting attack success reductions to 0–16% with negligible test accuracy drop. A warm-starting strategy is introduced to improve model utility. Comparisons are made with existing heuristic and certified defenses.

### Strengths
- The approach is simple and decouples training from defense, enabling a training technique that enhances model accuracy while preserving robustness (R1, R2).
- Extensive experimental evidence is provided, including evaluations against targeted poisoning attacks and backdoor attacks, considering both model utility and robustness (R1, R3, R4).
- The warm-starting initialization strategy improves model utility (R2).
- Evaluation is performed over diverse settings (training from scratch and transfer learning) (R2).
- This is the first work to apply diffusion denoising for certified robustness against clean-label poisoning attacks (R3).
- The research direction (certified defense against poisoning attacks) is meaningful and promising (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (R1, R4):** The method appears to be a straightforward extension of Carlini et al. (2023) [1]; the authors should provide a direct comparison to claim novelty. Additionally, there is already work (ECLIPSE, ESORICS’24) that uses diffusion denoising to defend against clean-label indiscriminate poisoning, further reducing novelty. The authors are encouraged to cite this related work and clarify the differences (R4). A critical issue: the entire defense becomes impractical if the diffusion model itself was trained on (even a small portion of) clean training data—the paper should explicitly state whether the diffusion model used clean data (R4).
- **Missing certified guarantee (R2):** The paper claims a certified defense but lacks theoretical proof. The method follows randomized smoothing (certified against evasion attacks), which is fundamentally different from poisoning attacks. The authors agreed in a prior rebuttal to provide full proof in the appendix, but it is still missing in this version.
- **Scaling and time cost (R3):** The certification process still requires training 10k models (Section 3.3), making the method hard to apply at large scale in real scenarios. A time-cost comparison with other defenses (both heuristic and certified) is needed, as denoising the entire training set is computationally expensive.
- **High attack success without defense (R1):** Table 1 reports ~99% attack success from only 500 tampered samples out of 5,000 target class samples (CIFAR-10). Clarification is needed on how the models were trained (line 283 says entire 50k dataset used). Why does such a small fraction cause nearly complete misclassification of all test samples of the target class?
- **Clarity and writing issues (R1, R3, R4):** 
  - Line 83: notation confusion—should be \(y_b = y_p\)? (R1)
  - Lines 85–91: optimization problem is confusing; terms \(y_t\), \(y_{\text{adv}}\) are used interchangeably, and \(y_{\text{adv}}\) is undefined (does it equal \(y_b\)?) (R1)
  - White-box vs black-box attack distinction (line 289) should be elaborated (R1)
  - Table numbering errors: lines 356 and 407 (R1); line 407 should refer to Table 4, not Table 5 (R4)
  - Table 4 uses TP and FP without definition (R1); also missing results for Sleeper Agent in Table 4 (R4)
  - Line 439–440: "Certified defenses are" appears incomplete (R4)
- **Baselines and comparisons (R4):** The newest backdoor attack evaluated is from 2022; more recent baselines such as Narcissus (CCS’23) should be included. Also, why are results against Metapoison (NeurIPS’20) not reported? The paper admits (lines 437–438) that their defense is not the best (e.g., against FrieNDs, which is not certified), so the absolute advantage is unclear; the authors should explore and highlight more strengths of their scheme.
- **Incomplete figures and tables (R3):** Why does Figure 2 not include results for \(\sigma=0.1, 0.5\)? Figures 1 and 2 should be combined for better comparison. Table 5 does not show a clear improvement over ROE (R3).
- **Minority reviewer (R4) is notably more negative** (score 3 vs others’ 5), emphasizing insufficient novelty, practical concerns about clean training data for the diffusion model, old backdoor baselines, and lack of comparative advantage.

[1] Carlini, Nicholas, et al. “(certified!!) Adversarial robustness for free!” arXiv preprint arXiv:2206.10550 (2022).
