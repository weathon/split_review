## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes two end-to-end trained unsupervised decision trees for clustering: KAURI (binary splits, axis-aligned, greedy recursive partitioning) and DOUGLAS (k-ary differentiable trees). Both optimize a generalized mutual information (GEMINI) objective. Experiments on small UCI datasets (largest 20k points, 10 dimensions) compare against two-stage approaches (k-means labeling + CART). Most reviewers note performance is comparable to these baselines, with limited novelty and insufficient motivation for end-to-end learning. One reviewer (rating 5) found the connection to KMeans and mutual information natural and the approach practical, but others questioned the significance, experimental detail, and theoretical grounding.

### Strengths

- The problem of learning clustering trees is interesting and relevant for interpretable clustering (R1, R2).
- The connection between KMeans and tree models via GEMINI is natural and simplifies the clustering process when a tree is desired (R3, minority positive opinion; R1 also notes connection to kernel k-means).
- The paper is readable and well-written (R2, R4).
- Two complementary algorithms are proposed, each with suitable use cases (KAURI for many features, DOUGLAS for many samples) and practical considerations such as computational cost estimation and fast implementation (R4).
- The use of a novel clustering objective (GEMINI) for tree learning is a notable direction (R2). One reviewer found the mutual information connection compelling (R3).
- Most reviewers rated the soundness and presentation as fair to good (3/4).

### Weaknesses

**Limited novelty and incremental contribution**
- KAURI adapts GEMINI into a CART-type greedy recursive partitioning algorithm; DOUGLAS uses existing differentiable deep neural decision trees. Core algorithms are largely incremental modifications of existing methods, lacking substantial conceptual innovation (R2, R4). The method is straightforward and the significance unclear (R4).
- The performance improvement over the simple two-stage approach (k-means + supervised tree/CART) is small and not clearly demonstrated experimentally (R1, R2, R3). Neither theoretical nor experimental evidence convincingly shows advantage of end-to-end learning (R2).

**Insufficient motivation and theoretical backing**
- The choice of GEMINI as the clustering objective is not sufficiently motivated: it is unclear why it aligns well with clustering goals (e.g., separating groups) beyond the connection to k-means, and a deeper analysis linking GEMINI’s properties to cluster formation is missing (R2).
- The paper lacks theoretical backing for the proposed methods (R4).
- For KAURI, whose objective is equivalent to optimizing the k-means objective, the motivation over traditional (Kernel) KMeans is unclear; authors note an example in Appendix E but do not discuss failure cases or clarify the rationale for an end-to-end approach (R4, Q1).
- One reviewer questioned why explain a clustering output with a tree at all, as clusters themselves are often used as explanations (R3).

**Experimental shortcomings**
- Datasets are small (largest 20k points, 10 dimensions); no MNIST-level or larger-scale evaluation to demonstrate scalability (R2).
- Evaluation uses Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) against ground-truth labels, which is questionable for an unsupervised task; reporting the optimized objectives (k-means cost, GEMINI value) would be more appropriate (R2).
- Missing comparisons: divisive and agglomerative clustering methods produce tree structures and could be evaluated with WAD (which does not require annotated internal nodes) (R3).
- Missing related work on clustering trees and density estimation trees: Blockeel et al. (1998), Fisher (1987), Ram & Gray (2011), Bertsimas et al. (2021), Gamlath et al. (2021) (R1).
- Experimental details are insufficient (R4):
  - DOUGLAS limited by memory constraints, but no information on required memory or machine resources.
  - No reported computation time or memory usage.
  - Batch size for DOUGLAS varies per dataset without explanation of the methodology; amount of parameter tuning compared to benchmarks is not provided.
  - Handling of categorical variables varies per dataset, but only the US congressional votes dataset is described; reproducibility is affected.

**Interpretability questions and parameter concerns**
- The improved interpretability claimed is questionable; interpretability is underspecified and the gain may not be sufficient to strengthen the contribution (R3).
- For DOUGLAS, the choice of neural decision tree is not justified; interpretability of such trees is unclear; why not use a regular soft decision tree? (R2, Q1)
- The temperature parameter τ in Equation 9 (differentiable splitting) is fixed at 0.1; it is known to be crucial for differentiable trees (Kanoh & Sugiyama, ICLR2022), but no analysis of its effect is provided (R4, Q2).

**Presentation issues**
- Section 2 discusses advantages and nuances of the method before the technical introduction, making the presentation premature; parts could be moved to the discussion (R3).
- Inconsistent capitalization of “KAURI/Kauri” and “DOUGLAS/Douglas” (R4, minor).
