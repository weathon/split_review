## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces LiDAR (Linear Discriminant Analysis Rank), a metric to measure representation quality within joint embedding (JE) architectures using the linear probing protocol without labeled downstream tasks. LiDAR quantifies the rank of the LDA matrix to assess dimensional collapse and implicitly integrates the SSL objective. Experiments on several downstream tasks show LiDAR significantly surpasses naive rank-based approaches (RankMe) in predicting optimal hyperparameters. Reviewer scores: 6, 8, 6. Reviewer 2 (score 8, confidence 4) was fairly confident and noted the rebuttal addressed raised issues; Reviewer 1 (score 6, confidence 1) stated inability to assess; Reviewer 3 (score 6, confidence 3) was fairly confident.

### Strengths
- The addressed problem—evaluating SSL representations without labeled tasks—is significant for hyperparameter selection and algorithm development (R1, R2, R3).
- The use of Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) to assess dimensional collapse is theoretically grounded and novel. While RankMe assesses SSL methods via the effective rank of the covariance matrix (related to PCA), LiDAR focuses on the rank of the scatter-ratio matrix through LDA (R2, R3).
- The theoretical motivation is clear and logical (R1, R2, R3).
- Experiments are comprehensive and demonstrate a significant and consistent positive correlation (Spearman and Kendall coefficients) between LiDAR and linear probing performance on source-target datasets, outperforming RankMe across most settings (R1, R2, R3).
- The supplementary material provides detailed methodology (R1).
- The paper is clear and easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses
- The abbreviation “LiDAR” is irrelevant to the problem and may mislead readers into thinking the paper is about lidar (R1).
- Typos: Section 2.1 “asses” should be “assess”; Section 4.0.1 has a blank line where a sentence seems missing (R1).
- Missing evaluation on out-of-distribution (OOD) target datasets. Following RankMe’s protocol (e.g., iNaturalist-2018, Stanford Cars) would strengthen LiDAR’s position as a useful proxy metric; absence limits generalizability (R2).
- LiDAR introduces hyperparameters: number of surrogate classes (n) and samples per class (q). No guidance or principled approach for setting these values is provided; hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is missing, introducing variability across methods (R2).
- Comparative analysis is limited: LiDAR is mainly evaluated against RankMe, while other baselines like α-ReQ (mentioned in related work) are overlooked (R2).
- Dimensionality reduction (DR) of embedding features is advocated in Section 4.1, but necessity and specific DR algorithms used are ambiguous; lack of clarity raises concerns about practical applicability and computational overhead (R2).
- No discussion on computational overhead and runtime (R3).
- The claimed integration of the SSL objective is not theoretically connected to different SSL losses; a deeper analysis would strengthen the foundation (R2).
- Scatter matrices Σ_w and Σ_b have dimensions (p×p) influenced by both n and data dimensionality (p). In Section 4.1, the authors “maintain a total of 50 features” for VICReg—how does this align with rank constraints from data dimensionality? (R2, question incorporated as criticism)
- Guidance for selecting n and q for practitioners is needed (R2, overlaps with hyperparameter weakness).
- RankMe was re-implemented with 10k images (vs. original 25.6k), while LiDAR uses at least 50k total samples; motivation for this choice is unclear (R2, question incorporated as criticism).
