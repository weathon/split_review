## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a framework using a fine-tuned ResNet classifier to quantify stylistic similarity between a set of “defendant” (potentially infringing) artworks and a “plaintiff” (copyrighted) set, with saliency maps for interpretability. It frames this as a contextual similarity detection (CSD) task, distinct from generalized similarity methods, and grounds the approach in legal copyright doctrine. Two case studies (Mickey Mouse, Maria Prymachenko) are presented with hyperparameter tuning and robustness analysis. Reviewers generally found the problem societally significant and the legal grounding thoughtful, but nearly all judged the execution, validation, and novelty insufficient for a conference venue. One reviewer rated the paper a clear reject (score 1), calling the premise impractical and the method trivial; the other three assigned score 3, citing limited experiments, missing comparisons, poor presentation, and lack of concrete contributions. All reviewers urged substantial improvement or a workshop submission.

### Strengths
- **Problem significance and legal grounding**: The sociotechnical challenge of quantifying copyright infringement in AI-generated art is impactful and timely. The paper’s engagement with legal doctrine (cases, nomenclature of “defendant”/“plaintiff” sets) is excellent and makes the work more accessible to legal stakeholders. Most reviewers appreciated this framing, though one reviewer (R4) strongly questioned the practical need, arguing that plaintiffs can recognize their own work and judges already determine substantial similarity.
- **Contextual approach vs. generalized similarity**: The divergence from typical image similarity methods (e.g., general-purpose feature matching) toward a classifier-based, case-specific approach is a useful direction and may be more legally relevant. Some reviewers noted it is a creative adaptation to the legal context.
- **Simplicity and accessibility**: The straightforward ResNet fine-tuning approach is seen as a practical strength—it can be applied in low-resource settings and is potentially easier for legal stakeholders to understand than more complex methods (R1, R3). However, another reviewer (R4) viewed this simplicity as a lack of novelty.
- **Originality (minority view)**: One reviewer (R3) praised the novelty of the contextual similarity detection framing; other reviewers largely found the ML contribution minimal and not sufficiently differentiated from prior work (e.g., Moayeri et al.’s ArtSavant, Casper et al., Somepalli’s CSD).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient and inconclusive experiments**: Only two case studies (Mickey Mouse, Prymachenko) are presented, both on very simple/high-profile examples, raising doubts about generalizability to diverse artistic styles (R1, R3). The experiments lack rigorous benchmarking; results are of unclear significance (R2). One reviewer specifically questioned why the AI set in the Prymachenko case shows high accuracy for the AI class yet a softmax similarity of 0.687 to the plaintiff class—this inconsistency is unexplained (R1).
- **Severe drop in accuracy with more distractor classes**: Incorporating additional (non-art) classes from the Quick Draw dataset causes a large performance decline (e.g., 128‑class case). Reviewers doubt this is the right choice: Quick Draw images are very different from cartoon characters, so the drop may reflect trivial discrimination failures rather than meaningful similarity. The model may not be training correctly for high class counts (R1).
- **Lack of interpretability for legal use**: Reliance on neural network logit scores and saliency maps is problematic. Saliency maps are known to be unreliable (“The (un)reliability of saliency maps”) and add little insight in the shown examples (R1). Legal settings demand explainability—e.g., tag‑based signatures like those in Moayeri et al.’s ArtSavant would be more actionable (R3). The paper offers no guidance on how to translate a quantitative similarity score (e.g., 0.687) into a judgment on copyright infringement (R1, R4).
- **Missing comparisons and limited related work**: The paper does not engage with closely related ML works: Moayeri et al. (2024) “Rethinking Artistic Copyright Infringements” (ArtSavant), Casper et al.’s work on imitation in diffusion models, and Somepalli’s CSD are omitted (R1, R3). The bibliography contains only ~5 machine‑learning references out of 24 total, which is insufficient for a ML‑centered venue (R2).
- **Unclear and poorly explained design choices**: How to select distractor classes is not justified; outputs are highly sensitive to this choice (R1). The template matching in Fig. 5 is not explained (R1). Class sizes and training details are missing (R1). The method requires training a new classifier for each new plaintiff set, so it is not generalizable (R4).
- **Motivation questioned (minority view, strong)**: One reviewer (R4) argues the problem is not practical: plaintiffs do not need an automatic identifier to recognize their own work, and judges already have experts to determine substantial similarity. This reviewer finds the whole premise unrealistic.
- **Limited novelty**: The core technique is an off‑the‑shelf classifier with no novel algorithmic contributions (R4, R2). Even granting the legal framing, the technical advance is minimal (R2, R3). The paper does not convincingly differentiate itself from prior work (R1, R3).
- **Poor presentation**: The PDF is non‑standard—text selection, highlighting, and hyperlinks are disabled (R2). Numerous typos and missing citations are present (e.g., “demonstraes”, “a cartoons”, extra spaces, missing parentheses, Fig. 1 resolution low and person name mismatch). The paper appears incomplete and not ready for a conference (R1, R2, R4).
