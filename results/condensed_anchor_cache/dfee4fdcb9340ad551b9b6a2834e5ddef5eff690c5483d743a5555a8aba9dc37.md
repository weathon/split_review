## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper designs a family of second-order algorithms for two-player zero-sum games with possibly nonconvex-nonconcave objectives. Using a dynamical systems approach, the algorithms ensure that any locally asymptotically stable equilibrium (LASE) is a local Nash equilibrium. The core method achieves local linear convergence to a local strict Nash equilibrium; a variant attains local superlinear convergence to a point satisfying first-order local Nash conditions. The approach is extended to constrained settings, guaranteeing convergence to local generalized Nash equilibria. Experiments on a GAN training task demonstrate superiority over the related LSS and CESP algorithms.

### Strengths
- The problem is significant and the paper provides new directions for understanding it (R2).
- The algorithms are developed as an entire family for both unconstrained and constrained spaces (R1).
- The idea of constructing a dynamical system where every LASE is a local Nash equilibrium is elegant (R2).
- Results prove local convergence rates (linear and superlinear), bounding eigenvalues of the Jacobian to be strictly less than one (R2). This is a clear advance over prior work that often lacked rate guarantees (R4).
- The method leverages second-order dynamics to avoid oscillations and achieve higher convergence rates (R3).
- Experiments on a GAN training task validate the algorithms’ robustness and practical utility, outperforming the two most related baselines (LSS and CESP) convincingly (R3, R4).
- The constrained setting is addressed, which goes beyond most prior work (R4).
- The exposition in the main body explains key ideas well, and the most relevant papers are cited (R4).
- Techniques from dynamical systems are combined in a novel way (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Assumption 3 is problematic.** The condition $J(z_k)^\top \omega(z_k) \neq 0$ when $\omega(z_k) \neq 0$ lacks intuitive motivation; it is unclear under what practical conditions it holds (R1, R4). The explanation of assumptions in Section 3.1 is weak (R3). Moreover, the numerical experiment does **not** satisfy Assumption 3 (R3). Better to test on a model that satisfies all assumptions (R3). No natural subclass is identified that fulfills the assumption (R1).
- **Assumption 2 is also somewhat artificial** though its technical role is clear (R4). The overall restrictiveness of the assumptions and their practical applicability need more discussion (R2). In particular, the requirement that critical points of the dynamical system correspond to local Nash equilibria seems restrictive (R2).
- **Only local convergence guarantees are given.** It is unclear whether a local guarantee is strong enough; Daskalakis et al. (2023) provides a global convergence guarantee (albeit without a rate) (R4). The local nature means no insight is provided for initialization far from a local strict Nash equilibrium, and the paper does not discuss how far the initial point can be or how different initializations may lead to different local Nash equilibria (R2).
- **Second-order information is required.** Computing and storing the Hessian (or even an approximation) is computationally expensive, limiting practical applicability in high-dimensional problems (R4).
- **Scope is restricted to two-player zero-sum games.** Extension to multi-player games is not addressed (R1).
- **Some missing analyses:** Theorem 2 does not provide a convergence analysis; it is unclear whether this is due to technical difficulty (R3). The rates proven for the unconstrained case are not shown to hold for the constrained case (R2, question). The constrained setting may require the constraint set to have nonempty interior; it is not clear how the approach would handle sets like the probability simplex (empty interior) (R4).
- **Presentation and contribution claims:** The paper does not explicitly claim a technical contribution (R3). A more detailed discussion of the closely related work of Daskalakis et al. (2023) is warranted (R4).
- **Questions about uniqueness:** It is not addressed whether the assumptions imply a unique LASE or a unique local strict Nash equilibrium (R2).
