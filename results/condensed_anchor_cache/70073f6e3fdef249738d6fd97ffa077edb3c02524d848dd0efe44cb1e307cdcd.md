## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a model-based evaluation framework using Item Response Theory (IRT) to decouple model performance from test set selection, aiming for reliable and efficient evaluation of generative models. Two key innovations are introduced: amortized calibration to reduce item parameter estimation cost, and a conditional item generator (fine-tuned LLM) to automate diverse question generation. The approach is tested on 24–25 NLP benchmarks and 180–184 language models, claiming up to 82% reduction in required test samples. Reviewer ratings range from 5 to 8 (average ~6.5), with overall moderate to strong confidence (2–4). While some reviewers are very positive (ratings 8: “refreshing”, “innovative”), others are more critical (rating 5: “practicality concerns”; rating 6: “limited evidence for adaptive monitoring”).

### Strengths
- **Novel application of IRT** to evaluate generative models, addressing limitations of classical test theory and providing stronger theoretical foundation (R1, R5, R6).
- **Amortized calibration** significantly reduces the cost and complexity of item parameter estimation (from linear to constant assuming fixed bank), making continuous evaluation feasible (R1).
- **Conditional item generator** (fine-tuned LLM) automates question creation, especially with difficulty control, which is a first and promising idea for scalable, adaptive testing and reduces evaluation leakage/overfitting (R2, R3, R4, R6).
- **Clear mathematical exposition** of IRT methods (e.g., Fisher information equations, reliability metrics) and thorough experiments on 24+ datasets and 180+ models (R1, R5).
- **Robustness demonstration** in Figure 3: IRT yields consistent ability estimates even under biased subset selection (hard/easy subsets), unlike classical test theory (R6).
- **Practical relevance** of reducing evaluation compute cost, an important but often neglected factor in model development (R2).
- **Innovative use of item difficulty to guide RLHF** (R4).

**Reviewer contrast:** R2 and R4 are highly positive (“no major weaknesses”); R5 and R6 are more guarded (“major concern”, “practicality issue”).

### Weaknesses
- **Incomplete reporting of model results:** Although the paper claims evaluation of 180 models, no full results are presented (Figure 1 is truncated; no table comparing models on generated vs. HELM subsets) (R3, R4). Reviewer 4 explicitly asks “where are the 180 language models?”.
- **Lack of evidence for adaptive monitoring:** The paper claims support for long-term, adaptive monitoring as models evolve, but only uses train/test splits; no new models are introduced, and the item generator is tested only on a single dataset (R6).
- **Question generation quality and bias:** Multiple reviewers note insufficient validation of generated questions (R2, R3, R5). Risks include: semantic invalidity despite matching difficulty, bias from the generative model (asymmetry favoring models similar to the generator), and lack of automatic verification without human oversight. Reviewer 4 raises a concrete concern: if item generators are trained from different base models (LLaMA3, GPT, Mistral), will IRT scores agree?
- **Experimental setting potentially biased in favor of IRT:** The subset evaluation uses 50 hard and 50 easy subsets, which may exaggerate CTT’s instability. Reviewer 4 asks whether IRT still outperforms CTT under uniform sampling of 100 subsets without intervention. Moreover, the “hard/easy” labels derive from IRT parameters themselves, raising circularity concerns.
- **Complexity and accessibility:** IRT introduces additional psychometric concepts (e.g., item parameters) that may be unfamiliar to practitioners; the paper could improve intuitive explanations (R1, R6).
- **Limited multilingual/generality evidence:** Experiments focus on English NLP tasks; non-text (image, multimodal) evaluation is not addressed (R1, R5).  
- **Missing baselines and ablations:** No comparison with other evaluation methods (e.g., Elo scores from LLM Arena) nor ablation studies among IRT model choices (Rasch vs. 2PL/3PL) (R3, R5).
- **Practicality for modern small-dataset evaluation:** The paper does not convincingly show relevance for emerging challenging benchmarks (e.g., AIME data) where dataset sizes are small (R6).
- **Insufficient justification for Rasch model choice** over 1PL alternatives; no discussion of why 2PL/3PL were not used (R3).
- **No comparison of amortized calibration training cost vs. evaluating a single model** – how many models must be evaluated to achieve net efficiency gains? (R3).
- **Handling missing values** in the response matrix not explained (R1).
- **Minor presentation issues:** references/figures not linked, small text in graphs, missing clarification of dashed lines in Figure 5 (R3, R6), typo suspicion at line 196 (R4).
