## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an adaptive stepsize for gradient descent. The theoretical formula depends on the (implicitly assumed unique) solution x* and achieves a linear convergence rate with factor sin^2 η_k. The practical version approximates this with a Polyak-like stepsize that estimates f(x*) via a tunable parameter γ₀ and an automatic update. Experiments are reported on a quadratic program, a geometric program, and non-convex MNIST classification.

### Strengths
- The problem of adaptive stepsize selection for gradient descent is significant and of current interest (reviewer 2).
- The proposed stepsize is related to the well-studied Polyak stepsize but has some novel aspects (reviewer 2).
- The paper includes a proof of linear convergence rate under convex settings (reviewer 3; but see weaknesses on assumptions and correctness).
- The theoretical stepsize captures curvature information, allowing faster convergence in some cases (reviewer 3).
- Empirical comparisons with popular optimizers such as Adam and N-AGD are provided (reviewer 3).
- Numerical experiments are performed and their plots are reported (reviewer 4).
- Several examples illustrate the claimed benefits (reviewer 1; but evidence is considered weak by others).

### Weaknesses
- **Writing quality and mathematical rigor are far below publication standards.** Many statements are mathematically incomplete or incorrect, e.g., line 155 and the Baillon-Hadad theorem on line 650 (reviewers 3, 4). The writing deviates from grammatical English from the very beginning (reviewer 1). In contrast, one reviewer (reviewer 3) found the paper well-written and easy to follow, but the majority considered clarity a serious issue (reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **The theoretical stepsize depends on x* without addressing uniqueness.** The definition requires x* to be unique for ‖x_k−x*‖ to make sense, yet no assumptions guarantee uniqueness and no discussion is given for the non‑unique case (reviewers 3, 4). Convergence results like ‖x_k−x*‖²→0 are mathematically meaningless if multiple minimizers exist (reviewers 3, 4).
- **The practical stepsize is simply the Polyak stepsize approximating inf f by \(\bar{f}_0\), but the paper does not cite any prior work on Polyak stepsize or its variants**, which are numerous (reviewers 3, 4). The authors also do not compare their tune‑free algorithm to other tune‑free gradient descent methods (reviewers 3, 4).
- **The introduction makes an inaccurate claim that “an adaptive stepsize theory has not been established.”** This ignores a large body of theoretical work on AdaGrad and other adaptive schedules in ML and optimization (reviewer 1). The specific gap being addressed is not clearly articulated (reviewer 1).
- **Proposition 2.1 and the surrounding discussion about non‑convex functions are misleading.** The proposition claims convergence to a global optimum of GD without requiring convexity in its statement; the proof does not justify global convergence for non‑convex functions (reviewer 1). The guarantee for non-convex functions is never clearly stated (reviewer 1).
- **The angle η_k is not defined or explained before it is used** in the optimal convergence factor (reviewer 1).
- **The practical algorithm (Algorithm 1) lacks theoretical analysis.** No convergence guarantees (e.g., iteration complexity) are provided for the adaptive scheme in convex or non‑convex settings (reviewer 2). The auto‑correction mechanism requires \(g(x) \geq 0\), which may not hold in early iterations or non‑convex settings (reviewer 2). The algorithm may get stuck in a region where the gradient is near zero but the function value is far from optimal (e.g., least‑squares problem with α ≈ 0), because the second correction is never invoked (reviewer 2).
- **The proposed method is deterministic** (requires objective function values) but is compared with stochastic optimizers like ADAM, which is unfair (reviewer 2). Comparisons with other leading deterministic adaptive algorithms (AdaGrad‑Norm, APGM, AdaBB) are missing (reviewer 2).
- **The experiments are not convincing.** They do not clearly show where other methods fail and this approach succeeds (reviewer 1). The claims of superiority are not supported by sufficient evidence (reviewer 1).
- **The assumptions underlying the theory are too strong:** convexity and knowledge of \(f(x^*)\). The reliance on an estimate \(\bar{f}_0\) can significantly affect performance (reviewer 2). The practical algorithm’s assumptions about f are not verified (reviewer 4). In particular, the step‑size formula assumes gradient non‑zero unless converged to x*, but this implies the function has no stationary points except the unique global optimum—an assumption that is neither stated nor justified (reviewer 1).
- **Missing details:** The paper does not explain whether the guarantee for non‑convex functions is local or global (reviewer 1). The conditions under which the results hold, especially for non‑convex functions, are not specified (reviewers 1, 2). The relationship to the Polyak stepsize (e.g., Hazan and Kakade) is not discussed (reviewer 2).
