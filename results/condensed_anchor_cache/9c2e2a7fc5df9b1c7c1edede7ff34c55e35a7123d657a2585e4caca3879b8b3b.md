## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a hierarchical transformer architecture for language modeling that avoids a tokenizer with a fixed vocabulary. It uses a lightweight character-level encoder to aggregate character sequences into word embeddings, a word-level backbone transformer, and a character-level decoder. At scales up to 7B parameters, the model matches subword-tokenizer-based models on downstream tasks, is more robust to input perturbations, and during continued pretraining from English to German trains nearly twice as fast, achieves better target-language performance, and retains more prior knowledge. Reviewers agree the approach is well-motivated and shows robustness and cross-lingual benefits, but raise concerns about the “tokenizer-free” claim, language coverage (alphabet/space-reliant), unfair comparisons in Table 1 (parameter mismatches), missing experimental details, and the contribution being incremental over prior work like Megabyte. One reviewer (R3) is significantly more critical (rating 5 vs. 8/8) and challenges the core novelty and feasibility for non-alphabetic languages.

### Strengths
- The method is well-motivated as a good balance between word-level and byte/character-level representations, combining sequence compression with open-vocabulary processing (R2).
- Scales hierarchical transformers to 7B parameters, which prior hierarchical work did not achieve (R2).
- Demonstrates greater robustness to input perturbations (Figure 4 clearly shows this) compared to subword-tokenizer-based models (R1, R2, R3).
- Matches or improves performance on downstream tasks while being more robust (R1, R3).
- Reduces sequence lengths processed by the backbone LLM, allowing more parameters under the same compute budget compared to subword tokenization (R3).
- In continued pretraining from English to German (Occiglot dataset), the model trains nearly twice as fast, achieves superior target-language performance, and retains more prior knowledge (R1, R3). However, one reviewer questions whether the German data is truly out-of-distribution for Llama (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Tokenization-free claim is inaccurate and misleading (R3, minority/negative view).** The method heavily relies on a space-based word-level tokenization mechanism; the backbone operates on word-level embeddings. The primary contribution is avoiding a large lookup table by using a small character-level lookup table, which is incremental over Megabyte (R3). Megabyte uses a fixed patch size; this work uses a predefined tokenization to segment patches, reducing flexibility (R3).
- **Limited to languages with alphabet systems and space tokenization.** Cannot be applied to languages like Chinese, Japanese (non-alphabetic), or Arabic, Thai (no explicit space tokenization) (R1, R2, R3). The adaptation experiments in Section 4.6 choose languages from the same (Germanic) family, which is not convincing (R3). The paper does not address CKY languages with tiny vocabularies or multilingual models including such scripts (R2).
- **Unfair comparisons in Table 1 due to parameter mismatch (R2).** For the compute-matched setting (Table 2), the hierarchical model is larger than the baseline: 1.1B vs 1.1B (matched), 3.1B vs 4B, and 7B vs 9.2B. This may overestimate the hierarchical model’s advantage (R2).
- **Missing explanations for experimental design.** No description of test sets in Table 1 (e.g., what is Lambada and why does the hierarchical model perform so well on it?) (R2). The German Occiglot dataset is not explained; the Llama model has likely seen significant German data, undermining the “out-of-domain” claim (R2).
- **Unclear architectural details.** In Section 4.2, the relationship between backbone and encoder is unclear: what does “keep the backbone to the 1:1 aspect ratio” mean? If the encoder is (8,4) (8 heads, 4 layers), what are the corresponding backbone dimensions? (R2).
- **Does not situate the work in the long history of hierarchical, character/byte-based models.** The case for why this paper should convince the community to move away from subword units is not clearly made, and more discussion of related work (e.g., Mielke et al. 2021) is needed (R2).
