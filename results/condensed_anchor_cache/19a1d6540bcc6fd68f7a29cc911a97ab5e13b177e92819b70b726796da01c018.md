## Merged Review

### Summary

Five reviewers assessed the paper. Scores: 5, 6, 3, 3, 5 (mean 4.4). Reviewer 2 is clearly more positive (score 6), while Reviewers 3 and 4 are most negative (score 3). There is agreement that the proposed MMD-NSL framework introduces novelty through mixed multinomial distributions and a bijective mapping to MLNs. However, all reviewers raise concerns about experimental evaluation, clarity of presentation, missing comparisons, and unclear technical details. The paper is seen as having potential but suffering from significant exposition and validation shortcomings.

### Strengths

- The introduction of a Mixed Multinomial Distribution into neuro-symbolic learning (NSL) to address complex categorization is considered novel and creative (R1, R2, R5). The bijective mapping between Markov Logic Networks (MLNs) and continuous multinomial distributions is identified as a valuable theoretical contribution (R1, R3, R5). R2 adds that MMD-NSL is shown to be a more general form of classical NSL, making a contribution to the field.
- Incorporating ontology information (e.g., NER types) into rule learning is a sensible and forward-thinking approach (R3).
- The bilevel optimization strategy—a transformer-based upper level learning mixing coefficients akin to attention, and a lower level optimizing rule weights—is technically sound and thoughtfully designed (R1).
- Experimental results demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art NeSy systems on a real-world dataset (DWIE), with 10.47% higher F1-scores on average across 23 sub-datasets (R4). R5 also notes that the experimental results are good.
- Reviewer 2 (minority more positive, score 6) found the theoretical and empirical analysis to be comprehensive.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited baseline comparisons and related work discussion:** The paper does not benchmark against a sufficiently broad set of established baselines. Missing comparisons include DeepProblog and Semantic Loss (R2); also RLogic, NCRL, and LERP, which are state-of-the-art for KG rule learning (R5). The related work omits discussion of methods that model logic rules as latent variables (e.g., [1], [2]) and neural extensions of MLNs (e.g., [3]) (R5). Furthermore, only a single real-world dataset (DWIE) is evaluated; multiple datasets are needed to assess generalizability (R4).
- **Experimental evaluation is weak:** Despite many datasets, they appear mostly toy and simplistic (R2). The paper also lacks a crucial ablation study analyzing how the two main components—ontology information and the MLN-based probabilistic framework—individually contribute to the observed improvements (R3). Reviewer 4 highlights that Figure 1 only demonstrates learning a simple synthetic target, and Figure 2 (occupying a full page) has questionable information density, overlapping numbers, and misleading/inhomogeneous color mapping (e.g., a 0.01 difference changing hue completely) (R4).
- **Presentation lacks clarity and contains errors:** The paper suffers from many grammar mistakes and exact repetitions (e.g., “establishing a bijective mapping between MLNs and continuous multinomial distributions” appears both on lines 66 and 76) (R4). Reviewer 5 notes that too many symbols and statements appear without introduction or explanation—e.g., what are \(h\) and \(t\), and what is meant by “NER type” of a node? The concept of a “rule body” is used in a way that is inconsistent with standard first-order logic (only one body with L relations) (R5). Definition 1 of “Logic Semantic Variable” is unclear: the objects referred to (\(F\), \(z_j\), the embeddings \(e_{NER}^{head}, e_{NER}^{tail}, e_{path}\)) are not formally declared (R5). The mathematical presentation also features an incorrect implication direction: the paper writes “\(r_{head} \rightarrow r_1 \land \dots \land r_L\)” while standard Horn clauses have the opposite direction (\(r_1 \land \dots \land r_L \rightarrow r_{head}\)) (R4, R5). The description of LTN as “a fuzzy logic-based extension of MLNs” is incorrect; LTN has fixed rule weights, while PSL is a proper fuzzy extension of MLN (R5).
- **Unclear algorithm and training details:** The paper does not sufficiently detail the training and optimization settings needed for reproducibility (R1). The algorithm for rule generation is not explained (e.g., how are the rules created?) (R3). It is also unclear how \(n_j\) is calculated for each newly generated rule (R3). The meaning of the variables \(f_j\) and \(z_j\) in equation (1) is ambiguous: do they correspond to MLN grounding counts, PSL fuzzy relaxations, or neural potentials? The text says \(z_j = 1\) if rule \(z_j\) holds true and \(-1\) otherwise, but what “holds true” means is not specified (e.g., universal quantification, ground atoms, Closed World Assumption) (R5). Only two types of Logic Semantic Variables are claimed; the rationale for this restriction is not justified (R5).
- **Ontology information not fully utilized:** The ontology (NER types) is only applied to the head entity of a rule, but should also be applied to the tail entity to make the framework more comprehensive (R3).
- **Clarifications needed on fundamental concepts:** The distinction between neuro-symbolic learning and causal/temporal reasoning is unclear (R1). The authors frequently refer to “long dependency chains” and “complex categorization” without a concrete example to illustrate what these mean in practice (R5). The notation in the bilevel optimization—specifically the dependency of variables on context \(C_k\) and rule index \(j\)—is not clearly explained (R5).
- **Theoretical gaps:** The efficiency of the bilevel optimization algorithm is not elaborated, and its convergence is not proved; it is doubtful that the algorithm obtains the optimal solution (R2).
