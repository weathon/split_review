## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes CrayonRobo, which uses crayon visual prompts drawn on RGB images to specify end-effector pose, contact point, and moving direction in SE(3) for robotic manipulation. The model is trained on synthetic data to interpret these 2D colored prompts and predict the corresponding SE(3) contact pose and 3D movement direction. Complex tasks are decomposed into atomic pick-and-place primitives. Evaluations in simulation and real-world environments demonstrate promising performance and generalization to unseen object categories and tasks, with comparisons to baselines including Flowbot3D and RT-Trajectory.

### Strengths
- **Novel use of crayon visual prompts** as an intermediate action representation that avoids language ambiguity and the over-specific nature of goal images/videos.
- **Problem formulation**: Decomposing complex tasks into atomic pick-and-place primitives based on contacts, similar in spirit to M2T2 but leveraging VLM pretraining.
- **Intuitive interface**: One reviewer particularly emphasized the user-friendly and intuitive way to specify tasks, making it accessible even to non-experts.
- **Clear communication of both low-level actions and high-level planning** via a sequence of prompt images for atomic steps.
- **Evaluation emphasizes generalization** to novel object categories and sim-to-real transfer, with solid experimental setup comparing against Flowbot3D and RT-Trajectory in both simulated and real-world environments.
- **Extensive ablation studies** demonstrate the effectiveness of each design component.
- Overall well-written and well-motivated paper.

### Weaknesses
- **Model architecture choice**: Multiple reviewers questioned the use of MLLMs (LLaMA-based) for fine-grained visual prediction, citing significant inference latency and difficulty in end-to-end fine-tuning. A comparison with lightweight models (e.g., BeiT, pure vision, no LLM, single-task end-to-end) is missing. One reviewer rated soundness and presentation low and expressed that current language models may not be well-suited for visually fine-grained predictions.
- **Practicality and automation of crayon prompts**: Several reviewers doubt the real-world applicability of manually drawing prompts on images—it may be unrealistic and imposes cognitive load on users. The paper does not demonstrate robust automatic generation of these prompts; if prompts cannot be produced reliably, the method’s claimed generalization may not hold.
- **Limited trajectory specification**: The current prompt only specifies end-effector pose and moving direction at contact, but lacks details about the full trajectory (e.g., approach path). This hinders collision avoidance and precise tasks, especially in obstructed environments. The method also lacks temporal reasoning—there is no mechanism to use past actions or predict future steps for consistency over long-horizon tasks.
- **Action representation gaps**: The action representation \(a_0\) may be inadequate for atomic actions that require both translation and rotation after initial contact (e.g., turning a door knob, opening a bottled water, flipping a bottle). Gripper open/close is not explicitly output, and placing actions lack specification of a pre-move approach direction. The paper does not fully explain how such complex manipulations are handled.
- **Dependence on accurate depth**: The method relies on accurate depth to map the 2D contact point to 3D, which may fail with reflective surfaces or poor lighting. Strategies for handling unreliable depth (e.g., monocular depth estimation, tactile sensing) are not explored.
- **Scalability and evaluation concerns**: It is unclear how CrayonRobo scales to complex tasks with many objects or intricate interactions (e.g., sequential tool use, dynamic obstacles). Long-horizon tasks like “wipe the table” lack reported numbers. Experiments on complex multi-step manipulations are absent, raising concerns about scalability.
- **Prompt ambiguity**: The system may face ambiguities when interpreting movement direction (e.g., after contact, whether to move left, right, inward, or outward, especially if no task prompt is given). Drawing directions perpendicular to the image plane is problematic. These ambiguities are not addressed.
- **Data collection and training coverage**: Training data is collected only from successful manipulations in random interactions, which may be inefficient and may not cover the diversity of real-world scenarios.
- **System robustness**: The pipeline involves multiple components (GPT-4, Grounded-DINO, CLIP, LLaMA). Failure of any one component (e.g., Grounded-DINO selecting a poor initial contact point) could cascade. There is no selection or ranking mechanism for robustness.
- **Technical design choice**: The soft orthogonality constraint \(L_O\) is used instead of a differentiable hard constraint (e.g., Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization), without justification.
- **Presentation issues**: Figure 3 erroneously shows (c) instead of (g) as candidates. There are typos ("the some primitives" line 316, "as as" line 143) and misuse of \citet vs. \citep in related works (lines 125, 143) and method (line 304).
