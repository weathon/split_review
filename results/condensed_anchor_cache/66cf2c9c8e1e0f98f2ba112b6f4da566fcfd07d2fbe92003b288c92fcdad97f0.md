## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes CFBD, a two-stage backdoor detection method for multimodal contrastive learning (MCL) models like CLIP. The coarse stage uses a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) to partition the dataset into poisoned, benign, and suspicious subsets; the fine stage refines detection using average textual correlation with the poisoned subset. The authors claim nearly 100% True Positive Rate (TPR) on the CC-3M dataset across diverse attacks. Reviewers are split: two (scores 5) praised the strong detection performance and rich experimental scenarios, while two (scores 3) raised serious concerns about novelty, potential plagiarism, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
1. **Clear presentation and motivation**: The paper is logically structured, with clear main text and conclusions, making it easy to follow. Diagrams are aesthetically pleasing and easy to read.
2. **Rich experimental scenarios**: The authors compare 9 classical and widely used backdoor attacks, including both unimodal and multimodal attacks.
3. **Detection performance**: CFBD achieves nearly 100% TPR on CC-3M for diverse attacks, markedly outperforming the two compared baselines (CleanCLIP and RoCLIP).
4. **Proposed metric**: The use of average textual correlation in the fine-grained stage is highlighted as a more effective metric than standard image-caption cosine similarity.
5. **Extensive experimentation**: The authors conducted extensive experiments that reinforce the robustness and reliability of the method across various attacks.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty**: The claim (lines 91–96) that one-stage detection is inadequate has already been made in prior work [1] (Unlearning Backdoor Threats…). The two-stage architecture and the naming of subsets (suspicious, poisoned, benign) closely resemble [1]. Consequently, extending one-stage to two-stage does not constitute a contribution.
2. **Suspicion of plagiarism**: The related work section shows significant overlap with [1] in structure, vocabulary, terminology, and citations (e.g., Liang et al. 2022a, Liu et al. 2006, Tang & Li 2004). Approximately 30 references are identical to those in [1] but are not clearly relevant to this paper. Reviewers suspect direct copying of the related work from [1].
3. **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**: Only CleanCLIP and RoCLIP are compared. More recent CLIP-specific defense methods (VDC, BadCLIP, BDetCLIP, TA-Cleaner) and state-of-the-art backdoor detection methods (CBD, Scale-up) are omitted. The claim of state-of-the-art performance is therefore overstated.
4. **Methodological issues**:
   - The first-stage mapping network F cannot defend against clean-label attacks; its effectiveness depends on F’s ability to correctly distinguish categories, and it may misclassify clean samples.
   - The suspicious subset likely contains hard samples important for model performance. Since the second stage relies solely on textual similarity, any clean data misclassified in the first stage could undermine label accuracy and hurt downstream model performance.
   - The method is designed for single-category poisoning; for all-to-all attacks, all suspicious pairs would be classified as poisoned, harming model performance. Relying only on textual information while ignoring image information is suboptimal.
   - It is unclear how the detection results are used to mitigate backdoor effects (e.g., fine-tuning, retraining, forgetting). Only ASR reduction is reported, not a complete mitigation pipeline.
5. **Missing ablation and analysis**:
   - No ablation study comparing the two stages (e.g., coarse-only vs. fine-only vs. combined).
   - The impact of poison rate is inadequately studied: Table 3 covers a narrow range, and hyperparameters correlated with poison rate are not adaptively tuned.
   - Detection efficiency and training time limitations are not discussed, though crucial for a detection method.
6. **Presentation gaps**:
   - The appendix section is not included in the paper.
   - Several citations do not appear in the main text or related work; the connection to the paper is unclear.
   - Figures and tables require refinement for clarity and readability.
7. **Specific questions that reflect weaknesses**:
   - In Table I, why is zero-shot performance of CFBD better than no defense for the ISSBA attack?
   - How is the claimed “more effective metric” contribution demonstrated beyond simply using average textual correlation?
   - The statement “the majority of existing backdoor detection methods in MCL usually produce non-satisfying results” lacks experimental evidence; only single-modal detection methods were compared.
   - The paper does not discuss limitations of the proposed detection method compared to other stage-based methods (e.g., fine-tuning, pre-training) in terms of usage scenarios and purposes.
