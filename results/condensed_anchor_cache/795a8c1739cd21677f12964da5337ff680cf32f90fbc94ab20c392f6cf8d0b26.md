## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Adaptive Depth Tsetlin Automaton (ADTA), which uses a reinforcement learning agent to dynamically adjust the depth parameter \(N\) of a Tsetlin Automaton. Reviewers identify several strengths: the problem of fixed depth is well motivated, the approach is intuitive, and the paper includes both theoretical analysis (Lyapunov stability, Markov chains) and empirical results. However, all reviewers raise significant concerns. Most reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) find the paper’s writing difficult to follow, the theoretical assumptions too strong, and the experiments insufficiently described and limited to simple toy domains. One reviewer (score 5) was more positive but with low confidence, noting the method’s novelty and clarity of the problem statement, yet also acknowledged major weaknesses in writing structure and experimental evaluation.

### Strengths
- Identifies a meaningful gap: the fixed depth parameter \(N\) in Tsetlin Automata limits adaptation, and the need to address this is well motivated (R1, R3).
- The proposed solution (ADTA) is intuitive and natural, complemented by both theoretical analysis (convergence via Lyapunov and Markov chain analysis) and empirical evaluation (R1, R3).
- Focuses on an approach to RL that is understudied in recent years; the clear formal model offers a crisp contrast to Deep RL (R2).
- The TA framework is explained with diagrams and text, making its workings relatively clear (R1).
- The theoretical analysis aims to address the pressing question of convergence when an additional adaptive learner is introduced (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Writing clarity is poor**:
  - The paper is difficult to read; key details are missing or unclear (R2).
  - The definition of TA is not formally given in accessible references (the first is in Russian, the second is a textbook hard to find; citing Sutton and Barto for a definition not formally present there is misleading) (R2).
  - Inconsistencies between maximising rewards and minimising penalties cause confusion (R2).
  - Transition types (i)–(iv) appear misdefined (e.g., one definition is a special case of another, and it is unclear why equation (3) transition (2) is labelled (iv) rather than (i)) (R2).
  - Overloaded markers ((i), (1)) in Section 2 make it linguistically confusing (R2).
  - When the high-level RL agent takes over and what its observation space is are not described until late in the paper (R2).
  - The abstract calls TA a “single-state” model when it has multiple states; line 35 describes a “grid of n-actions” when it is a grid of states (R2).
  - Figure 1 lacks a thorough description of configuration, transitions, and assumed actions (R2).
  - Introduction includes problem definition and related work, making it lengthy and unclear (R3).
  - Many definitions and notations appear in the main text while proofs are in the appendix, making the main page feel redundant and artificially theoretical (R3).
  - Figure captions (e.g., Fig. 4) need more explanation of axes and variables (R4).
  - Grammar issues: “capable to learn” is ungrammatical (R2); Definition 3 is an observation, not a definition (R2).

- **Theoretical analysis is not rigorous and relies on extremely strong assumptions**:
  - Introducing an adaptive learner may make the environment non‑stationary for TA, potentially causing it to not converge; conversely, RL methods require stationarity. Assumption 1 appears to assume this problem away, but no discussion is given of how or when it could be satisfied (R1).
  - Proving that two learning processes converge individually is insufficient for joint convergence (e.g., two-player general‑sum games; two‑timescales analysis is needed) (R1).
  - The theory offers no insights into when or why ADTA would improve performance over fixed‑\(N\) TA (R1).
  - Assumption 1 (equation (12)) requires “negligible changes” but actually says “no changes”; it essentially assumes ADTA eventually behaves exactly like TA, thereby assuming the conclusion. Assumption 2 assumes an absorbing state. Together they assume away the two hardest parts of the argument (R2).
  - Theorem 1’s claim “will be able to learn” overstates Definition 4, which only says the result is slightly better than random initialization (R2).
  - \(\Psi\) is first used in equation (13) without definition (R2).

- **Experiments are insufficient and poorly explained**:
  - Experiments are not described in sufficient detail in the main text; evaluating them requires reading the appendix and the literature cited there (R2, R3).
  - Only a toy example appears in the main paper, without any introduction of environment settings or parameter details (R3).
  - The environments are limited to small toy domains, and no comparisons are made to established deep RL algorithms (policy gradient, Q‑learning) or standard bandit methods (UCB, Thompson Sampling) (R3, R4).
  - The manuscript compares only to one baseline (HCA) and does not compare to other leading methods (R1). It also requires understanding the VASLA algorithm, which is not described in the main text (R2).
  - The performance (Fig. 4) improves linearly, but without understanding the environments it is unclear how significant the reward/regret numbers are or whether the method is overfit (R2, R4).

- **Motivation and framing weaknesses**:
  - The necessity of the TA setting is unclear; ADTA could be seen simply as a temporally‑extended exploration method in a bandit setting (ref. Dabney et al. 2020) without invoking TA (R3).
  - If an RL agent is used to adjust \(N\), why not directly use that RL agent to learn an optimal policy? The advantage of the dual‑environment framework is not justified (R3).
  - Real‑world applicability is questionable: the binary reward assumption seems restrictive, and no concrete real‑world scenarios are given (R3, R4). It is also not stated whether binary reward is a necessary condition for TA/ADTA (R3).

- **Missing experiments and ablations**:
  - No experiments with different RL methods for the inner agent (R1).
  - No discussion of how ADTA scales to high‑dimensional environments or those with learned internal states (R4).
  - No ablation on the strong theoretical assumptions.
