## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes that Bayesian persuasion (BP) can be reduced to a bargaining game, arguing that receivers can develop anti-exploitation strategies when aware of the game structure, equalizing commitment power. Experiments with LLMs are used to demonstrate these dynamics. One reviewer was more positive (score 5), praising the originality and use of LLMs, while the other two gave lower scores (3 and 3) and raised significant concerns about the validity of the main claim, the experimental execution, and the clarity of the message.

### Strengths
- The theoretical perspective of comparing Bayesian persuasion to a bargaining game is solid and interesting. (R1)
- The idea of studying connections between persuasion and bargaining is very interesting; the paper makes a good effort to elaborate the background. (R2)
- Using LLMs in experiments is novel and inspiring; pushing research to more realistic setups beyond the standard theoretical model. (R2)
- The paper is the first to discuss the possibility of receivers making commitments in BP settings, a natural idea that maps to several real-world scenarios. (R3)
- Definitions and explanations are reasonably clear. (R3)
- The use of LLMs is valid and an increasingly interesting topic of study, especially as LLMs mediate more human interactions. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Misleading central claim**: The paper states "Bayesian persuasion is reducible to a bargaining game" (e.g., Lemma 3.5). What the authors actually show is that **a variant of BP where the receiver is also given the ability to commit and the agents can interact beforehand** is reducible to a bargaining game. This greatly diminishes the significance and surprisingness of the main claim and title (R3). The "polynomial time reduction" is trivial: it reduces by setting the solution of the first problem as a constraint in a second, non-optimization problem—described as naïve or deliberately obfuscating (R3). A second reviewer found the claim confusing and noted it appears to apply to any kind of game, not uniquely to BP (R2).
- **Lack of clarity in the key message**: The authors should clarify whether Lemma 3.5 means that solving the bargaining game also solves BP in its original form (sender commits) or only in a bargaining version (as defined in Definition 3.4) (R2). Toy examples illustrating the differences between standard BP, bargaining BP, and bargaining games would help (R2).
- **Inadequate justification of LLM experiments**: The authors claim LLMs are used because "it is immediate for LLMs to provide their interpretation as to why such decisions are made," but caution is needed since LLMs tend to rectify decisions they just made (e.g., arXiv:2410.19599) (R1).
- **Poorly described simulation setup**: Context is missing: when does the game end, how many rounds, are interactions repeated or started afresh, what is the temperature? (R1). More details are needed about who are senders/receivers and how they interact (R2).
- **Confusing presentation of experimental results**: Tables 1 and 2 are not mentioned or explained in the draft (R1). Statistical analysis is required to demonstrate that the receiver achieves higher expected gain (R1). Tables could be presented as plots for easier parsing (R2).
- **Unclear experimental hypotheses and missing baselines**: The paper does not state explicit hypotheses. The justification for using LLMs as a proxy for human behavior is not explicit (R3). The one-shot version (α=0?) of BP is omitted as a baseline (R3). The proposer is chosen uniformly at random in the bargaining problems but not in BP—what are the takeaways from the similarities and differences? (R3)
- **Outcome dependence on ω_BP**: The bargaining outcome depends on the choice of ω_BP; it is unclear how this influences experimental results and whether the main goal is to demonstrate that the receiver can gain more by using retaliatory strategies (R2).
- **Flawed game-theoretic reasoning**: The claim that the sender's ability to commit implies long-term interactions is false—commitment can occur in one-shot interactions (e.g., contracts, escrow). This undermines the experimental motivation (R3). The receiver's refusal to best respond is only rational when the sender's commitment is credible; otherwise it is a non-credible threat and irrational (R3).
- **Notation and presentation issues**: Notation is overly complex and has errors. If the receiver has no private information, q can be dropped (no need to distinguish φ and φ). In footnote 1, q and φ appear reversed (sender determines φ, not q). Signaling schemes are denoted as ψ but elsewhere as φ or φ; tildes are unexplained; Definition 3.3 uses symbols not previously introduced (R3). Language issues: "cognitive misers" (p.1), "solutions become conceivable" (p.3), and the sentence after Assumption 3.2 are unclear. The phrasing about different game structures "experiencing less social context" should be replaced with "being less influenced by social norms and contexts not captured by the game description" (R3). Many typos and minor grammatical errors throughout (R3).
- **Missing relevant references**: The paper omits citations to prior work on history-dependent strategies (Gan et al., 2023: Sequential principal-agent problem with communication: efficient learning and computation) and on deception in Stackelberg games (Birmpas et al., 2020: Optimally deceiving a learning leader in Stackelberg games), which are related to the receiver's counter-commitment (R2). These omissions weaken the paper's claim of novelty.
