## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DISCOD, a two-stage framework for simultaneously discovering shared and distinct concepts between two sets of images. Stage 1 uses information-regularized textual inversion to separate distinctive concepts and capture shared concepts; stage 2 refines the text embeddings with a latent diffusion loss. The method is evaluated on image editing, few‑shot personalization of diffusion models, and group bias mitigation. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the problem and the breadth of experiments, but also raise significant concerns about poor readability, insufficient quantitative analysis, missing comparisons, and lack of clarity in the method and applications.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem setting.** Addressing the simultaneous discovery of shared and distinct concepts across two image sets is new and not explored in prior concept‑inversion works (R2, R3, R4).
- **Clear motivation.** The motivation to go beyond shared‑only concepts is well articulated (R1, R4).
- **Simple and intuitive stage‑1 objective.** The learning objective (information‑regularized textual inversion) is straightforward (R3).
- **Well‑designed information‑regularized inversion.** The use of mutual‑information decomposition to separate distinct and shared concepts is technically sound (R2, R4).
- **Diverse and well‑planned experiments.** The paper shows results on three downstream tasks with good quantitative metrics (CLIPScore, success rate) and a human study (R1, R2, R4).
- **Practical applications demonstrated.** The applications in image editing, personalization, and bias mitigation illustrate the utility of concept discovery (R2, R4).
- **Well‑structured presentation (one reviewer).** One reviewer found the writing, structure, and layout clear and enjoyable (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Poor readability and uninformative diagrams.** Several reviewers note that Figure 1 conveys almost no architectural or objective information, and the arrow placements are confusing (R1, R2, R3). Another reviewer states the manuscript suffers from bad writing and that the authors fail to clarify their main contributions, burdening reviewers to compare with prior works (R3). In contrast, one reviewer praised the presentation (R4), indicating a strong disagreement on clarity.
- **Lack of quantitative analysis for stage 2.** No quantitative results prove the effectiveness of the second stage. Figure 6 does not show how stage 2 helps concept discrimination; it appears to overfit the original image, contradicting the information‑regularization motivation. Moreover, stage 2 is not used at all in the ablation of Section 3.4 (R3). The loss in stage 2 contains five terms and deserves a more detailed component analysis (R3).
- **Results often rely on single samples.** Figures 3, 5b, 6, 7, and Table 2 each show only one example, undermining the soundness of the technical claims (R3).
- **Insufficient comparison with related methods.** MCPL (Jin et al., ICML 2024) – a highly related method for multi‑concept learning – is not compared or discussed in the related work (R4). The image‑editing application also lacks comparison with prior concept‑discovery methods such as Text Inversion or MCPL (R4).
- **Missing DISCOD in Figure 5(b).** The figure shows TI and UCD but not DISCOD when illustrating the effect of regularization loss; this appears to be a mistake (R1).
- **Unclear how discrete embeddings are used in diffusion.** The authors represent concepts \(y_{A\setminus B}, y_{B\setminus A}, y_{A\cap B}\) as discrete embeddings, but it is not described how these embeddings drive image generation in stage 2 (Figure 1, Section 2.3) (R2).
- **Ambiguity in “concept” definition.** The work does not clearly define what a “concept” is from the outset. The failure example (\(y_{A\setminus B}=\) “yellow chair”) is a matter of semantic granularity, and cases like “climbing gym” vs. “climbing club” exhibit semantic ambiguity that the method does not address (R4). The title uses “fine‑grained” without clarifying what fine‑grained means here (R4).
- **Questions about training pair construction.** The method requires two image sets A and B, but how are A‑B pairs prepared when the number of concept categories varies? How does the method handle pair imbalance? (R2) For each experiment, what exactly are the two image sets and are concepts learned per image? (R3)
- **Application concerns (image editing).** In Figure 8, how are the semantic meanings (e.g., “Ancient”) of discovered concepts obtained? Are they manually annotated? (R4)
- **Application concerns (personalization).** For few‑shot personalization (Figure 9), how are sets A and B defined? How are diverse concepts (e.g., “depressed”, “sleeping”) discovered? Even with the proposed method, backgrounds of generated dog images still show clear bias from the few input images – why is the bias not mitigated? (R4)
- **Application concerns (group bias mitigation).** The implementation is unclear. For the Waterbird/CelebA example, how are the bias concepts (e.g., “water”, “land”, “male”, “female”) named? Are they manually selected from all discovered concepts? Biases should be quantified, not just visually identified (R4). How many participants were involved in the human study, and how many instances did each evaluate? (R3)
- **Mathematical claims are lengthy but not rigorous.** The derivation of mutual information is not an original theoretical contribution. The approximation of conditional probability via cosine similarity introduces an unbounded error because cosine similarity is not a valid probability measure. The method is largely intuition‑based and empirical; shorter intuition‑focused descriptions with more thorough experiments would be preferable (R3).
- **Limited technical novelty.** The core techniques are regularization and fine‑tuning within existing textual inversion methods; the contribution over previous concept‑related methods is not clearly justified (R1). One central claim – that prior works cannot identify differences (P1#L50) – is not supported by evidence or preliminary experiments (R4).
- **Attention maps are hard to interpret.** Abstract concepts like “tall” are difficult to visualize, making it hard to see commonalities and differences in attention maps (R1).
- **Figure 2 needs more consistent tests.** The last column of Figure 2 should be extended, e.g., showing results of \(y_{A\setminus B}\), \(y_{B\setminus A}\), and \(y_{A\cap B}\) combined with new concepts (R2).
- **Unexplained color change in Figure 18.** The dog’s color changes at the end of the third row; no explanation is given (R1).
- **Behavior for identical/disjoint sets not discussed.** What are the discovery results when sets A and B are identical (all shared, no distinct) or completely disjoint? (R4)
- **Minor presentation issues.** Figure 5(b) labels “TI” and “UCD” when showing effect of regularization loss – unclear what they refer to (R3). The bias shown in Figure 10 is not obvious (R3). The linked URL in the footnote (P1#L38) is disconnected from the text (R4). Why is optimizing Eq. 2 difficult? Not described (R4).
