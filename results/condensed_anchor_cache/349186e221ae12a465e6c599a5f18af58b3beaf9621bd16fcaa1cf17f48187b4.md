## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AFedPG, an asynchronous federated reinforcement learning framework that uses policy gradient updates with a delay-adaptive lookahead technique to handle heterogeneous arrival times. Theoretical convergence guarantees are provided, including global convergence (sample complexity \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2.5}/N)\) per agent, linear speedup) and first-order stationary point (FOSP) convergence. Time complexity is improved to \(\mathcal{O}\big((\sum_{i=1}^N 1/t_i)^{-1}\big)\) versus synchronous \(\mathcal{O}(t_{\max}/N)\). Experiments on four MuJoCo tasks with varying numbers of agents and computing heterogeneity support the faster time-wise convergence.

### Strengths
- Proposes a novel asynchronous FedRL algorithm (AFedPG) tailored to policy gradient updates, with a delay-adaptive lookahead technique to manage lagged updates in asynchronous settings. (R1, R2, R3, R5)
- Provides theoretical convergence guarantees for both global and first-order stationary point convergence, matching state-of-the-art sample complexity and demonstrating linear speedup: per-agent complexity reduces from \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2.5})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2.5}/N)\). (R1, R3, R4, R5)
- Improves time complexity over synchronous methods by reducing dependency on the slowest agent; the bound \(\mathcal{O}\big((\sum_{i=1}^N 1/t_i)^{-1}\big)\) is always tighter and gains are significant under high computing heterogeneity. (R1, R4)
- Empirically validates AFedPG on four MuJoCo environments, showing faster convergence (in wall-clock time) compared to synchronous FedPG and other baselines, including heterogeneous computing scenarios. (R1, R4, R5)
- Introduces a special step-size design that cancels out a second-order error term in the error analysis, serving as a technical novelty. (R5)
- Both FOSP and global sample complexity results are provided; the global time complexity bound is tighter under heterogeneous agent speeds. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues:** Multiple reviewers found the paper not clearly written. R1 notes serious typos and unclear derivations, especially in bounding the delay error term \(e_k\) (Equation 30 in the appendix). R4 and R5 point out typos (e.g., *MoJuCo* instead of *MuJoCo* in figure captions, missing gradient operators in Eqs. 37, 38, typo \(d_{\delta_{k-1}}\) in line 1028). R1 asks for a precise definition of global time and \(t_i\) (per-agent time). R5 asks for clarification on standard notations in Appendix B (e.g., \(F_\rho(\theta), \mu_F, \sigma_g\)) and proper citations for Lemmas B.6 and B.7. (R1, R4, R5)
- **Doubt about delay independence in sample complexity:** R1 questions how the convergence bound avoids any dependence on the delay (worst-case \(N\) with cyclic updates). The derivation of the gradient-bias term \(e_k\) is unclear and contains serious typos, making it difficult to verify the claim. R1 requests a clearer explanation of how the delay dependence is bypassed, as this is a key part of the contribution. (R1)
- **Technical novelty considered limited/incremental:** R2 and R3 argue that the asynchronous update idea is largely adapted from existing asynchronous supervised FL and prior asynchronous RL work (e.g., [3]), and that the challenges of online sample collections varying with policy updates have already been addressed in FedRL literature. R2 claims the paper is not built on a true federated framework (no multiple local iterations, no heterogeneous environments handled) and lacks careful comparison with [3]. R2 and R3 note the momentum/delay-adaptive lookahead may not be genuinely unique compared to prior FedRL that also handles lagged models and dynamic samples. (R2, R3) In contrast, R5 (score 8) found the step-size design and analysis technically novel. (score 8 vs. 5,5 disagreement on novelty)
- **Missing comparisons and insufficient positioning:** R2 asks for detailed comparison with existing asynchronous policy gradient works (e.g., [3]) and FedPG; the paper does not explain what technical contributions go beyond existing FedRL. R4 asks whether there is any analysis or experiment of communication cost. (R2, R4)
- **Algorithm details unclear:** R1 finds Step 4 of Algorithm 2 confusing: it is unclear how agent \(i\) obtains \(d_{k-1}\) (should it be \(d_{k-\delta_k}\)?). R4 asks for the threshold or event that triggers synchronization in the asynchronous framework; the meaning of “active agents” (Line 268) is ambiguous—does it allow more than one agent to apply a gradient at the same iteration? R5 asks for more explanation of active agents, concurrency, delay definition, model sharing between server and agents, and the impractical assumption of infinite-length trajectories. (R1, R4, R5)
- **Theoretical presentation concerns:** R2 notes that the proof lacks the index of agent \(i\), and that the server does not aggregate periodically, making it unclear why this is federated RL rather than simply parallel asynchronous RL like [3]. R2 also asks why FOSP results are placed before global convergence in the main text. R5 asks to explain the constant term in Eqs. (10) and (11) that does not depend on \(K\) (due to function approximation error) and to detail the derivation of total waiting time (Line 394). (R2, R5)
- **Experimental open questions:** R4 asks whether PG (N=1) and AFedPG (N=2) in Figure 3 could converge to similar rewards if trained longer; if not, why? (R4)
