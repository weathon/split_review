## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates emergent abilities in LLMs by splitting questions by difficulty level (based on average performance across models). It observes U-shaped scaling on hard questions and inverted-U scaling followed by steady improvement on easy questions, connecting emergent capabilities to inverse scaling. The authors propose Slice-and-Sandwich, a method that fits easy and hard questions separately to predict the emergence threshold and post-emergence performance. Experiments use the binary Brier score on multiple-choice datasets (MMLU, Persian-QA, Arithmetic) with 56 LLMs. Reviewers generally find the core observation interesting and the method novel, but raise concerns about clarity, metric choice, limited validation, and the reliability of the fitting approach.

### Strengths
- The main result connects two important and poorly understood phenomena: inverse scaling and emergence (R1, R2, R3, R4). This is timely for LLM evaluation science and AI policy (R2).
- The observation of U-shaped scaling for hard questions and inverted-U for easy questions provides a plausible explanation for sharp transitions in aggregate performance (R1, R2, R3, R4). R3 notes the connection to double descent in deep learning is interesting.
- The Slice-and-Sandwich method is novel, simple, and substantially outperforms baselines (R1, R4). R4 highlights that group-wise scaling laws offer more detailed insights than a single curve.
- Experiments are diverse: 56 LLMs to determine difficulty levels, and effectiveness is demonstrated on 6 benchmarks (R4).
- R1 (score 8) finds the paper clearly written and easy to follow; other reviewers disagree (see weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues** (R2, R3, R4):
  - "Binary Brier score" is confusingly named – what is binary about it? (R2, R3, R4). R3 asks for explanation in the introduction; R4 agrees.
  - Figure labeling is ambiguous (e.g., `0_1502_brier`); difficulty level names would be more informative (R2, R3). Colors for easiest and hardest questions are identical, causing confusion (R4).
  - Model families used in scaling plots are only listed in the appendix; main text should specify them and the difficulty levels (R3).
  - Plots for different datasets should be side-by-side rather than separate (R2).
  - The phrase "Slice-and-Sandwich approach allows the data to speak for itself" is misleading because the method bakes in assumptions (R2).
  - Appendix layout is odd: P21 contains a single huge image (R4).
  - Definition of \(M\) (line 128) mixes up \(N\) and \(D\) (R1). Naming it "effective model size" is misleading since it is log compute; better names are "effective compute" or "log compute" (R1, R2). Line 484: \(F_s^c\) should be \(F_e^c\) (R4).

- **Limited validation and generality** (R2, R3):
  - Evaluated on only 3 datasets (plus 3 in appendix) out of >200 in BIG-Bench, insufficient to claim a general explanation or method (R2).
  - All selected tasks show emergent capabilities; unknown how Slice-and-Sandwich behaves on tasks without emergence – it may falsely predict it (R2).
  - Only the binary Brier score is used; trends may not hold for other metrics like top-1 accuracy (R3). R1 notes that accuracy plots (Appendix E) differ and attributes this to accuracy being a "crude measure," but R1 finds that explanation unsatisfactory.
  - The proposed explanation of emergence as superposition of U-shaped and inverted-U scaling is somewhat qualitative; MMLU does not clearly support it (R2).

- **Weaknesses of the Slice-and-Sandwich method** (R1, R4):
  - Fits in the main section use knowledge of where emergence starts, which undermines prediction value (R1). Appendix F2 fits without that knowledge but results are not convincing as a reliable prediction method (R1).
  - The fitted scaling trend either underestimates or overestimates the actual trend; the paper does not adequately justify why this is acceptable (R4). For example, prediction error on hard problems for Arithmetic (figure 7) is comparable to error on all problems (figure 9) (R4).
  - Averaging overestimated easy-group and underestimated hard-group predictions may coincidentally yield accurate aggregate predictions; the paper should provide further (qualitative) justification that the good result is not mere chance (R4).

- **Missing details and justifications** (R2, R3):
  - How were the 6 tasks selected, and at what point in the study? (R2)
  - What is the functional form of the polynomials used (line 416)? (R2)
  - The method for selecting the emergence threshold (Section 4) appears arbitrary; a more rigorous approach (e.g., derivative analysis or statistical test) is needed (R3).
  - The practical motivation for predicting the scaling curve beyond the threshold is not clearly explained (R3).
  - How does the granularity of difficulty levels (e.g., easy/medium/hard) affect the results? (R3)
  - Why fit the Brier score and then project to accuracy rather than fitting accuracy directly? (R3). R4 also asks for elaboration on the benefit of this two-step approach.

- **Additional suggestions** (R2, R4):
  - Consider discussing related work [4] (R2).
  - Provide specific outputs of weak vs. strong LLMs on easy/hard problems in the appendix to aid understanding of the observed trends (R4).

- **Minority positive on clarity**: R1 states the paper is clearly written; however, R2, R3, and R4 all find it difficult to follow, indicating a significant clarity gap.
