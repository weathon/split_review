## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a generative framework for counterfactual image explanations using a disentangled regularized autoencoder, designed to enable real-time generation (≈1.2 s) for human-in-the-loop classification. The encoder is split into label-relevant and label-irrelevant parts, and an auxiliary denoising autoencoder is trained on the combined latents. Counterfactuals are generated by modifying the label-relevant latent along a segment to the target class mean, computing an “expected counterfactual” via a theoretical result, and decoding. A user study on BloodMNIST shows that the generated counterfactuals improve human decision-making. The method is well-structured and clearly described. However, multiple reviewers (R2, R3, R4) note major weaknesses: lack of comparison with existing counterfactual techniques, evaluation on only one dataset, model‑specificity, insufficient empirical support for strong claims, and questionable disentanglement guarantees. Reviewer 1 is more positive, emphasizing the potential for real-time human-centered applications, but still calls for broader evaluation and clarity on claims.

### Strengths
- The method is well‑explained, with clear motivation, a detailed description of each component, helpful figures, and pseudocode (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The theoretical result for efficiently searching for the “optimal” counterfactual candidate in latent space is interesting and a novel contribution (R3).
- The user study is important in XAI, carefully designed with specific research questions, and demonstrates that the counterfactuals can improve human task performance (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The framework achieves real‑time generation (≈1.2 s), making it potentially suitable for interactive settings (R1). Reviewer 2 notes that runtime comparison with other methods is missing.
- Source code is provided in an anonymised repository (R3).
- The approach balances efficiency and interpretability, addressing key desiderata for counterfactual explanations (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evaluation and missing comparisons.** The evaluation is limited to a single dataset (BloodMNIST) and task; broader datasets with different modalities are needed (R1, R2). No quantitative comparison with existing counterfactual generation methods (e.g., Sauer & Geiger 2021, Melistas et al. 2024) is provided, so the claimed advantages in efficiency, likelihood, interpretability, and validity are not empirically substantiated (R2, R3). Metrics such as FID, S3, COUT, and edit‑distance in input/latent space should be reported (R2, R3). Ablation studies are missing to verify the necessity of each component (R2).
- **Model‑specific and not a general post‑hoc explainer.** The method can only explain its own Gaussian‑mixture‑based classifier trained within the framework, not arbitrary black‑box models. This contradicts the typical goal of post‑hoc counterfactual explanation (R2, R3, R4). The reliance on a disentangled latent space and the auxiliary training limits generality (R1).
- **Claims not sufficiently substantiated.** The claim of “100% validity” is overconfident; it only guarantees class flip, not realism or plausibility in high‑dimensional edge cases (R1). Strong theoretical claims (e.g., non‑existence of a strictly better counterfactual, intrinsic trade‑off optimality) rely on the assumption that the Gaussian mixture perfectly preserves the original data distribution – this should be explicitly stated (R3). The claim of being “the first unsupervised concept‑based counterfactual generating technique suited for real‑time interaction” (line 406‑407) requires evidence, e.g., a runtime comparison with other VAE‑/GAN‑/diffusion‑based CFE methods of similar complexity (R2). The “real‑time” advantage is unclear without comparing generation times (R3).
- **Disentanglement and concept interpretability concerns.** Disentanglement is claimed without sufficient theoretical grounding; Locatello et al. (2019) prove that unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without additional information, and Leemann et al. (2023) study conceptual explanations (R2). The identification of “concepts” requires a human expert to label them manually, raising scalability and faithfulness issues (R3, R4). There is no guarantee that the derived concepts faithfully reflect the generator’s behavior (R4). The author’s claim about “unsupervised” training is questioned because the framework requires massive labeled data (R1).
- **Counterfactual quality and selection issues.** The generated counterfactuals shown in figures appear blurry and not like realistic scans (R2). The “expected counterfactual” violates property P2 (the generated counterfactual is not necessarily in either segment S1 or S2), meaning a better counterfactual might exist (R4). The benefit of the rotation used for expectation computation is unclear; the segment is one‑dimensional, so a univariate Monte‑Carlo estimator might suffice, and the rotation concentrates variance in one dimension that is then redistributed (R4). The sparsity claim is questionable because z_s (the modified part) constitutes 75% of the latent encoding, so modifications are not sparse (R4).
- **User study limitations.** The user study has only 50 participants divided over multiple conditions, which is low (typical between‑subjects XAI studies exceed 300 participants, per Rong et al. 2022). The study is not pre‑registered, and IRB approval is not mentioned (R2). The costs of explainability are not discussed: the classifier achieves 91% accuracy, while an unconstrained state‑of‑the‑art model might achieve higher accuracy; this trade‑off is not addressed (R2).
- **Related work and literature gaps.** Many recent techniques for counterfactual image generation (VAEs, GANs, Deep‑SCM, Diffusion, Flows) are not discussed, e.g., Sauer & Geiger (2021), Melistas et al. (2024) list over 10 approaches. It is unclear why these are insufficient and what specific limitation the proposed method addresses (R2, R3). Citations are mainly from 2018‑2022; recent advances are missing (R3).
- **Theoretical derivation questions.** In Equation (9), it is unclear why the weight of each segment uses the density at the center rather than the integrated density over the segment; constant density would make length irrelevant (R2). The phrasing in Appendix line 850 (“expected value according to an isotropic Gaussian of elements in a segment S”) is problematic because a bounded segment cannot follow a Gaussian distribution with infinite support (R3).
- **Writing and presentation issues.** Several typos: “regularize” (caption Fig. 1), “DDPM” not introduced, “Class-relevant” and “Concept‑based” capitalisation (R2). Line 185 unclear: “apply to the latent representation” (R4). Line 176 caption “regularize” (R2). References to appendix should include section/figure numbers (R2). Table 1 should use consistent decimal digits (R2). The supposed affiliation disclosure on page 27 (Figure 13) may violate double‑blind (R2).
