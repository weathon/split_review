- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 3, 6, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FFMDR, a federated learning method for minimax problems (specifically nonconvex strongly concave) based on Douglas-Rachford splitting. The method aims to address client drift and data heterogeneity. Theoretical analysis claims improved sample complexity from \(O(\epsilon^{-3})\) to \(O(\epsilon^{-2})\) and sequential convergence guarantees (finite, linear, or sublinear) under Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) assumptions. The authors also derive a side result on verifying the KL property. Experiments on AUC maximization tasks show slight improvement over baselines like Local SGDA, FedNEST, and FedSGDA.

### Strengths
- The literature review is logical and organized, and the description of the minimax problem within the FL context is clear, making the background easy to understand. (R1, R2)
- The writing is generally easy to follow. (R1)
- The improved complexity result (sample complexity \(O(\epsilon^{-2})\)) sounds interesting, though it is unclear if comparisons are always under the same assumptions and stationarity definition. (R3)
- The variable (sequential) convergence result for federated minimax problems may be technically new and could have minor pedagogical value. (R3)
- The side result on verifying the KL property might be the best contribution, even though it may not directly apply to the formulations in this work. (R3)
- Incorporating the KL condition into the study is important and less studied in the field. (R5)
- The method is novel for nonconvex strongly concave minimax problems, allowing partial client participation and addressing data heterogeneity. (R4)
- The authors relax KL assumptions by deducing the KL exponent of the maximizer-dependent potential function from the maximizer-free function. (R4)
- The empirical evaluation uses multiple real-world datasets and highlights FFMDR's robustness; it outperforms existing federated minimax approaches. (R2, R4)
- FFMDR demonstrates better complexity compared to existing works. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Sequential convergence is not clearly defined.** The term appears in the title but is never defined in the Introduction or Preliminaries, making it hard to assess its importance. (R1)
- **Undefined terms and notations:**
  - Equation (1): \(l\) and \(r\) are not defined. (R1)
  - “Proximal operator” is not defined when first introduced (L78). (R1)
  - Distance is denoted as \(d\) (L195) but elsewhere as \(\text{dist}\) (L96, L247). (R1)
  - In Proposition 2, \(m\) and \(\kappa\) are not clearly defined; the proposition is poorly presented (what are \((x^*, y^*), (w_\star, y_\star), (\bar{x}, \bar{y})\)? How does \(\epsilon_w\) affect things? How is Eq. (9) derived from Eq. (10)? Where does the randomness come from?). (R3, R5)
  - In Theorem 1, terms like \(\delta, \delta', \iota, \epsilon_w, \delta_\epsilon\) are introduced without explanation; it is not clear how stationarity is measured using \(\epsilon\) (is it \(d(0, \partial)\)?), making it hard to verify the claimed \(O(\kappa^2/\epsilon^2)\) complexity. (R3)
  - Abbreviations such as “DR” (line 263) are not defined upon first use. (R4)
  - Proposition 2 has “strongly convex” where it should be “strongly concave”. (R4)
- **Experimental limitations:**
  - The improvements shown in Table 2 and Figure 1 are marginal, and the y-axis is artificially zoomed in Figure 1, creating an illusion of larger separation. Multiple runs and reporting average performance are needed to mitigate stochasticity. (R2, R3, R4)
  - The number of clients is significantly lower than typical FL settings; results with more clients are expected. (R2)
  - FFMDR’s performance drops considerably when the number of participating clients per round is reduced (Fig. 2); baseline comparisons under similar conditions are missing. (R2)
  - Experiments are conducted only on a single task (AUC maximization) with simple datasets; more complex tasks (e.g., adversarial training, fairness learning) are recommended. (R4)
- **Theoretical assumption–experiment mismatch:**
  - Theorem 1 assumes deterministic updates (Assumption 3), but the experiments use SGDA (stochastic) as the local solver, meaning the experimental results do not support the main theoretical claims. (R3)
  - The assumption behind the variable convergence result (deterministic) is rarely satisfied in federated learning, making the results less interesting and following a well-known recipe. (R3)
- **Overclaim and comparison fairness:**
  - The complexity comparison in Table 1 may be unfair because other works (Local SGDA, FedNEST, FedSGDA) use an expectation formulation \(f_i = \mathbb{E}_j f(\cdot,\cdot,\xi_j)\), while the proposed method uses a finite-sum formulation, which allows SAGA but is not applicable in expectation cases (no access to full objective). (R5)
  - The claim of “model parameter convergence” for other algorithms in Table 1 is misleading because those algorithms did not consider the KL condition, so such convergence should not be expected. (R5)
  - The claim that the method reduces sample complexity is not well explained: the method reformulates the minimax problem (Eq. (6)) into another minimax problem (Eq. (8)), which does not directly lower the complexity of solving a single minimax problem. (R2)
- **Nonsmoothness claim:**
  - The paper claims to handle nonsmoothness (Line 124, “nonsmoothness of the max function”), but Proposition 1 shows the max function is smooth, contradicting that claim. (R5)
- **Technical inaccuracies in appendix:**
  - In L.853, it is unclear how the first inequality was derived. (R2)
  - In L.1275, the inequality may need a correction to the equality. (R2)
  - In Eq. (52), the first term in the inequality does not appear in the following equation; the sixth term in L.1309 seems to be missing a summation symbol. (R2)
- **Method implementation:**
  - In Algorithm 1, it is unclear how to determine if equation (10) is satisfied, given that the exact solution to equation (8) is unknown. (R4)
- **Scope of theoretical results:**
  - The analysis of sequential convergence assumes equation (10) is deterministic and all clients attend training each round. How does this extend to stochastic settings and partial client participation? (R4)
  - Proposition 3 requires \(g\) to be defined everywhere, but the function \(U\) (Line 426) involves \(\tilde{g}\) defined on a hyperplane; it is unclear how Proposition 3 applies to the main problem. (R3)
  - Line 270 and 279 state that \(F\) is separable in \(y\), but this is not the case for Eq. (1) (the problem setup) or Eq. (14) (the experiments), as \(y\) cannot be treated separately for each client. (R3)
  - Assumption 2 is not equivalent to “allowing client drift”; it does not address stragglers or unstable network conditions as claimed (line 108). (R3)
  - Line 483: Eq. (13) is not semi-algebraic, so the heavier machinery needed is not discussed, despite earlier focus on semi-algebraic functions. (R3)
- **Presentation and writing issues:**
  - Grammatical errors and typos (e.g., L52: “it is necessary (to) develop”; inconsistent equation referencing: L237 “*equation* 1” vs L285 “equation 6”). (R1)
  - L95: “complexity of federated learning methods” should be “complexity of optimization algorithms” or “algorithmic complexity”. (R1)
  - L97: “convergence of the model parameters themselves” may be better phrased as “statistical convergence”. (R1)
  - L102: “provides” should be “provide”. (R4)
  - L39: citations for real-world FL applications should be added. (R4)

**Reviewer disagreement:** While most reviewers (R1, R3, R5) found the experimental gains marginal and the theoretical assumptions limiting, Reviewer 4 (score 6) considered the method novel and its performance superior to existing approaches; Reviewer 2 (score 5) also viewed the empirical evaluation as showing robustness. The minority positive voices stress the method’s novelty, the relaxation of KL assumptions, and the improved sample complexity, but agree on the need for better experimental validation and clearer presentation.
