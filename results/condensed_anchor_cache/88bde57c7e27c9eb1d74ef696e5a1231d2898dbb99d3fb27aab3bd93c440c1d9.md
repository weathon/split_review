## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes the Any-step Dynamics Model (ADM), an RNN-based dynamics model trained to predict states and rewards for variable-length future horizons given a past state and action sequence. ADM is designed to reduce bootstrapping (autoregressive error accumulation) by allowing direct predictions from varying-length plans. The authors introduce ADMPO-ON (online) and ADMPO-OFF (offline) algorithms, applying ADM to model-based policy optimization. For offline RL, the variance across different prediction horizons in a single ADM is used as an uncertainty estimate, replacing ensemble methods. Experiments on MuJoCo (online) and D4RL/NeoRL (offline) show superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines, and the uncertainty estimates correlate well with ground-truth errors.

### Strengths

- **Novel uncertainty estimation**: Using variance across prediction horizons within a single model (instead of across an ensemble) is a new approach that works well in practice (R1, R4).  
- **Strong empirical results**: The method achieves strong online and offline performance, largely outperforming baselines across MuJoCo, D4RL, and NeoRL (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- **Broad evaluation**: The paper covers both online and offline RL settings, and includes uncertainty estimation studies that demonstrate correlation with ground-truth errors (R1, R2, R4).  
- **Versatility**: ADM can be plugged into various MBRL frameworks for both online and offline policy optimization (R4).  
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well written and easy to follow (R2).  

*Note: One reviewer (R1) was especially positive (score 8), while others (R2:6, R3:5, R4:5) had more reservations, primarily regarding justification and comparisons.*

### Weaknesses

**Unconvincing justification of error‑reduction mechanism**  
- The main motivation—reducing autoregressive error accumulation—is not convincingly justified. ADM still relies on a recurrent architecture that implicitly bootstraps on hidden states, and its rollout uses previously predicted states (e.g., \(s_{m+1}\) to predict \(s_{m+2}\)). The paper does not provide a principled reason or sufficient ablation to explain why this approach reduces error compared to standard autoregressive models (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- Theorem 3.4 is a general result and does not specifically address the backtracking mechanism (R1, R3). Theorem 3.5 follows straightforwardly from the assumption and is not ADM‑specific (R3).  
- Clarification needed: why repeat state \(s_t\) for all GRU steps instead of using the entire known sequence of \(m\) states? How does the method handle stochastic transitions and policies when using a single past state? (R3, R4).

**Limited comparisons to relevant baselines**  
- No comparison to recurrent world models of similar size (e.g., RSSM, TD-MPC2) in the RL evaluation. The only recurrent comparison is in Figure 2 (model error), which is hard to interpret. It is unclear whether gains come from the any‑step sampling strategy or the architecture itself (R1, R2).  
- Missing comparison to MBPO’s truncated rollout and adaptive rollout length methods (e.g., Frauenknecht et al., Zhang et al.) (R4).  
- The idea of using temporal consistency for epistemic uncertainty has been explored previously (e.g., Filos et al., 2022); this prior work should be acknowledged (R2).

**Uncertainty estimation mixing aleatoric and epistemic**  
- The proposed uncertainty estimate mixes aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. Performance in stochastic environments (where the optimal policy may traverse stochastic parts of the MDP) is not discussed (R1, R2).  
- The paper does not discuss this as a limitation in Section 6 (R1, R2).

**Hyperparameter sensitivity and baseline selection**  
- Backtracking length \(m\) is a new hyperparameter; ablation shows it significantly affects performance. No guidance is provided on how to choose \(m\) for a given task or whether it generalizes across tasks (R4).  
- ADMPO tunes hyperparameters per task; how were hyperparameters for baselines selected? (R2, R3).  
- Baselines’ results are taken from original papers; with only 5 seeds and no re‑implementation for the new NeoRL benchmark, the comparison may not be fully fair (R3).

**Clarity and presentation issues**  
- Inconsistent notation: \(\hat{s}\) and \(s\) are both used for predictions and sometimes confused with ground‑truth states (R3).  
- Typos and missing parentheses in citations (R3).  
- The term “bootstrapping prediction” may be confused with RL bootstrapping (R3).  
- Algorithm 1 and 2 should both be in the main text (R3).  
- Spelling inconsistency: “rollout” vs “roll-out” (R2).  
- Equation 1: need to specify the spaces/sets over which the sum is computed (R2).  
- Line 204: “foundational algorithms” is unclear (R2).  
- Line 241: “beyond the boundaries of the risky regions” should probably read “safe regions”.  
- Line 352: the equation likely refers to \(\hat{s}_{t+m}\) rather than \(\hat{s}_t\) (R3).  
- “deviation error” (line 046) and “trajectory distribution” (line 068) need clarification (R4).

**Missing analyses and ablation**  
- Figure 2 uses a log-scale that obscures interpretation: an error of \(10^{34}\) is catastrophic, and it is unclear what error level is acceptable. A relative error measure and specification of the policy used during rollouts are needed (R2).  
- Why is a random \(k\) used during policy training rather than averaging predictions or using the longest horizon? (R3).  
- Computational complexity of ADM is not discussed (R4).  
- Only 5 seeds are used; more would strengthen the results (R3).
