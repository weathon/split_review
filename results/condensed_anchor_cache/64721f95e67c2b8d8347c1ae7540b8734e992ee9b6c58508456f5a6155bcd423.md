## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence and Cramér-Wold (CW) distance as replacements for L2 loss in a teacher-student knowledge distillation framework to erase sensitive concepts (identity, nudity, artistic styles) from text-to-image diffusion models. The authors claim these losses enable more robust alignment in latent space and mitigate catastrophic forgetting. Experimental results on Stable Diffusion 1.4 are presented across multiple concepts.

### Strengths
- The paper is well motivated, addressing the real-world ethical, legal, and content-moderation challenges posed by generative models (R1, R2, R3).
- It employs a teacher-student knowledge distillation framework to transfer unlearning from the student back to the teacher model (R1).
- The authors provide theoretical analysis, including lemmas and proofs that JS divergence and CW distance mitigate catastrophic forgetting (R2; R3 notes these lemmas are not empirically tested – see Weaknesses).
- Extensive experimental results across multiple concepts are presented with visual comparisons that clearly illustrate the method’s impact (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient justification and theoretical/empirical foundation for JS and CW superiority.** The paper lacks an in-depth explanation of why these specific loss functions are inherently superior to L2 or alternatives; no comparison to Wasserstein distance or mutual information is given (R1). Reviewer 3 further questions the underlying assumption (Lemma 1) that certain dimensions represent a concept and others do not – a practical assumption that may not hold (R3).  
   **Minority contrast:** Reviewer 2 considers the mathematical proofs convincing for mitigating catastrophic forgetting, but Reviewer 3 notes that the four central lemmas are not empirically tested.

2. **Quantitative evidence is insufficient and inconsistent.**  
   - Table 1 reports results at selected epochs without justifying why training was not run to convergence or why certain epochs are compared (R3).  
   - Table 2 and Table 3 show unlearning performance that is very similar across methods (including L2) and are based on single‑prompt test sets, which is not an ideal or rigorous evaluation (R2, R3).  
   - In Table 3, CW and JS do not consistently outperform L2, making it difficult to conclude they are superior (R2).  
   - There is no quantitative metric to measure the extent of catastrophic forgetting, so comparisons between CW, JS, and L2 in terms of forgetting are impossible (R2). Figure 5 only shows JS results without a corresponding L2 baseline, further hindering comparison (R2).  
   - The datasets and benchmarks used are not fully detailed; only five concepts (baby, Narendra Modi, Elon Musk, Amitabh Bachchan, Nike) are evaluated in Table 3, raising concerns about possible cherry-picking (R2). Performance across a larger and more diverse set of concepts is needed (R2, R3).

3. **Limited comparative analysis and generalizability.**  
   - Baseline methods are selective, focusing on L2‑based approaches while overlooking more recent and diverse techniques such as UCE (Gandikota et al., WACV 2024) and MACE (Lu et al., CVPR 2024) (R1, R3).  
   - Experiments are conducted only on Stable Diffusion 1.4; it is unclear how the results generalize to other diffusion models or versions (R1).  
   - The approach is tested only on SDD among knowledge‑distillation‑based unlearning methods; its general applicability to other frameworks (e.g., ESD-u, ESD-x, Ablating concepts) remains unshown (R2).

4. **Unclear setup and missing details of the core method.**  
   - SDD is used as the base erasure technique but is not explained in the related work or background section (R3).  
   - In Eq. 3 it is ambiguous whether the objective optimizes the student or the teacher model (R3).  
   - The paper states the student model is not being trained, yet Figure 4 shows output changing for both teacher and student – this inconsistency needs clarification (R3).

5. **Incomplete handling of catastrophic forgetting and concept interference.**  
   - The teacher‑student distillation framework’s assumptions and limitations are not discussed in depth; the paper mentions mitigation of catastrophic forgetting but provides vague specifics and sparse empirical evidence (R1).  
   - It is unclear whether the model’s creative diversity or generalizability decreases when multiple concepts are erased consecutively (R1).  
   - A quantitative metric for catastrophic forgetting (e.g., interference with non‑targeted concepts) is missing, preventing a clear comparison between CW, JS, and L2 (R2).
