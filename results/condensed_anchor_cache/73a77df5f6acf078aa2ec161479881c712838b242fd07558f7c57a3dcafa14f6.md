## Merged Review

### Summary

LLaVA-Mini proposes an efficient large multimodal model that compresses vision tokens fed to the LLM backbone to a single token. The authors first analyze how LMMs process vision tokens, finding they are most crucial in early layers for fusing visual information into text tokens. Based on this, they introduce a modality pre-fusion module to fuse visual information into text tokens before the LLM, and a vision token compression module using cross-attention with learnable compression queries. Experiments across 11 image-based and 7 video-based benchmarks show LLaVA-Mini outperforms LLaVA-v1.5 with 1 vision token instead of 576, reducing FLOPs by 77%, achieving 40ms latency, and processing over 10,000 video frames on 24GB GPU memory.

### Strengths

- The finding that vision tokens are primarily crucial in the early layers of LMMs is key to the design of the efficient early fusion architecture and a significant contribution to reducing computational overhead.
- The paper is well written, with clear motivation, well-explained methods, and informative tables/figures (e.g., qualitative cross-attention visualizations).
- LLaVA-Mini achieves significant efficiency improvements: 77% FLOPs reduction, 600x memory reduction per image (360MB to 0.6MB), and 40ms inference latency, while maintaining competitive performance across 18 benchmarks (11 image, 7 video). This could make it suitable for edge devices and real-time applications.
- The paper provides exhaustive ablation studies on number of pre-fusion layers, vision tokens, etc.
- The method is simple yet effective, with comprehensive experiments demonstrating robustness.
- Reviewer 4 (score 8) is more positive, emphasizing the strong and convincing experimental results, and praising the interesting detailed analysis of how LMMs understand vision tokens.

### Weaknesses

- The paper does not specify whether attention weights are averaged across all heads or a specific head is chosen; it also fails to clarify how attention scores are aggregated among instruction, vision, and response tokens, nor whether high- and low-resolution vision tokens (in LLaVA 1.5/NeXT) are treated equally. This lack of detail makes the attention analysis difficult to reproduce and verify.
- There is a discrepancy between the claim that attention to vision tokens decreases in latter layers and the entropy visualization which shows persistently high values for vision tokens in those layers. The paper does not adequately explain this contradiction, nor does it clarify the relationship between attention weights and entropy. The use of a logarithmic scale in Figure 4 is potentially misleading without explanation.
- The pre-fusion transformer architecture lacks sufficient details (number of layers, hidden dimensions, key hyperparameters), hindering reproduction and assessment of its computational overhead.
- The title and main claim about “one vision token” is potentially misleading: the pre-fusion module still processes all full projected vision tokens (N^2×5 tokens plus l_q text tokens). The ablation studies (lines 446-457) emphasize the importance of pre-fusion, but the paper does not explore the effectiveness of using only the pre-fusion module without compression, leaving it unclear whether the token compressor provides significant benefit beyond pre-fusion alone.
- The paper lacks a detailed FLOPs and memory breakdown of the pre-fusion module, especially given it processes all projected vision tokens. Training costs (memory, FLOPs) during instruction tuning are not compared with the original LLaVA, making it difficult to assess true efficiency gains during training.
- LLaVA-Mini may achieve inferior performance on tasks demanding fine-grained visual details, e.g., text-oriented benchmarks like TextVQA, especially compared with models using longer visual sequences (e.g., LLaVA-NeXT).
- The paper lacks an important baseline: keeping visual tokens in early layers of the LLM and then compressing the visual token output at the L-th layer (layer 4 in the paper).
- For video, the authors sample frames at 1fps, so the number of compressed visual tokens varies with video length. There is no strategy (e.g., temporal embedding) to handle the discrepancy between training and inference for much longer videos.
- The compression and modality pre-fusion modules are trained independently for each downstream task, raising the question of whether this approach differs from task-specific distillation. The authors do not explore whether generic compression and pre-fusion modules could be trained in stage 1 that generalize to various tasks.
- It is unclear whether a new modality pre-fusion module is necessary; an alternative would be to reuse the first few LLM layers (which have high vision-token activation) as the fusion module, with a two-pathway design for compression and gradual fusion.
- The paper does not compare or discuss recent streaming video LLM works that also represent each frame with minimal vision tokens (e.g., VideoLLM-online, CVPR 2024).
- The paper does not discuss the complexity of plugging this compression method into existing multimodal pipelines, and the lack of open-source code hinders reproducibility.
- The paper could benefit from discussing failure cases of the compression technique and potential solutions.
- It would be valuable to understand how model efficiency scales across different hardware platforms.
- The authors have not done an ablation comparing against SOTA token pooling methods (other than average pooling), nor shown results on more vision-centric benchmarks like CV-Bench (Cambrian-1).
- The presentation of “Modality Pre-fusion” and “Vision Token Compression” would benefit from chronological reorganization to better reflect the processing pipeline (image encoder → vision token compression → modality pre-fusion).
