## Merged Review

### Summary
ParetoFlow applies flow matching to offline multi-objective optimization (MOO). It introduces a multi-objective predictor guidance module that assigns each sample a weight vector (generated via the Das-Dennis method) to direct sampling toward the Pareto front, plus a neighboring evolution module that fosters knowledge sharing among similar weight distributions. The method is evaluated on diverse benchmarks and is reported to outperform existing MOO methods such as PROUD, LaMBO-2, and CorrVAE.

### Strengths
- The integration of flow matching in offline MOO is a fresh perspective compared to typical evolutionary algorithms or Bayesian optimization (R1). The concept is innovative and creative (R4).
- The proposed modules—multi-objective predictor guidance and neighboring evolution—are well-motivated (R2). The knowledge-sharing mechanism is a standout feature that supports enhanced exploration and refinement of solutions (R1).
- State-of-the-art performance across diverse benchmarks, outperforming PROUD, LaMBO-2, and CorrVAE (R1). Results are promising (R2, R4).
- Code and training details are clear (R1). The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R2, R3). The title is engaging (R3). Section 2.2 is clearly explained (R3).
- (Minority strength note: R3 also found the paper well-written and the title engaging, but raised several concerns below.)

### Weaknesses
- Insufficient citation to support the claim that flow matching outperforms diffusion models; only reference [1] is given, and no analysis is provided in the context of MOO (R1). (R3 also questions how using a normalizing flow would alter results.)
- Lack of detailed computational cost breakdown for training and inference, and no comparison of computational cost with other generative modeling methods (R1, R2). Scalability concerns: required weight vectors grow exponentially (e.g., 10 objectives need at least 1024 vectors), which may be time-consuming, and fewer weights may not fill the Pareto front (R4).
- Missing discussion of challenges from the discrete search space in MO-NAS (encoding/decoding process) (R1). How is the validity of generated samples guaranteed (e.g., a valid computational flow in NAS)? (R2). A case study showing structure and performance of generated architectures on MO-NAS is requested (R2). Visualization of generated samples would be beneficial (R2).
- Comparisons focus heavily on generative-modeling approaches; broader comparisons with traditional non-generative MOO techniques (e.g., evolutionary algorithms, decomposition-based methods) are missing (R1). In particular, no comparison with methods like SMS-MOEA; if such methods were used, it is questionable whether ParetoFlow would still perform as well (R3). Experiments rely exclusively on the hypervolume (HV) metric; other metrics like inverted generational distance (IGD) or epsilon indicator should be included (R3).
- Reliance on accuracy of objective predictors may be a bottleneck on problems with complex landscapes and limited offline data (R4). Potential overfitting to specific tasks or datasets is a concern (R1 question).
- Mathematical rigor is insufficient: the paper lacks analysis of convergence properties, a formal definition of the multi-objective optimization problem, and a correct formalization—the statement on line 105 inaccurately treats MOO as merely a vector optimization problem (R3).
- Purpose of Equation (9) and the noise term is unclear; the connection between noise and exploration of the objective space is not well established (R3).
- Integration of evolutionary algorithms (EA) with flow matching is unconvincing: the paper does not articulate how the strengths of both are synergistically combined, nor how selection and mutation operators are adapted to the flow matching framework (R3). In the neighboring update, selecting the best among the K nearest weight vectors may cause a large fraction of vectors to generate the same solution, leading to early convergence (R4 question).
- Section 4.6 (likely a discussion or additional analysis) is inappropriate for the main text; it should be moved to an appendix or supplementary material (R3).
- The choice of the Das-Dennis method for generating uniform weights is not justified; are there scenarios where alternative weight distribution methods would be more effective? (R1 question).
- Does the method successfully handle non-convex Pareto fronts in the experiments, and does the local filtering scheme work as intended? (R4 question).
