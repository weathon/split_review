## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a notion of “stability” for bandit algorithms and shows that stable algorithms yield asymptotically normal sample means (Lemma 1), enabling standard inference. Focusing on the two‑arm batched bandit setting, the authors demonstrate that a vanilla ε‑greedy Explore‑Then‑Commit (ETC) strategy is not stable (Lemma 2), and propose a modified ETC (Algorithm 1) and a version with a random stopping time (Algorithm 2) that are stable and achieve asymptotically optimal regret up to a constant factor of 4. They also study a B‑batch elimination algorithm (Algorithm 3) that is stable when the batch length m is fixed and the number of batches B→∞ (Theorem 2). The paper is rated 3,3,5,5,5 (two weak rejects, three accepts); reviewer confidence is 3–4. There is disagreement about the paper’s significance, novelty, and presentation quality.

### Strengths
- The notion of stability for bandit algorithms is novel and provides a fresh perspective (R1, R4, R5). Using stability to treat adaptively collected data as effectively i.i.d. is potentially impactful (R4).
- The theory is simple and elegant; the algorithms are straightforward and computationally light (R3, R5).
- The problem of statistical inference under sequential, adaptive data collection is important and interesting (R2, R4).
- Asymptotic normality is a desirable property for constructing confidence intervals (R5).
- The paper is generally well‑structured and clear (R2, R4, R5), though with several typos.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and significance** – The practical benefit of asymptotic CLT is unclear; stability might not be necessary (R1). The claim “minimize regret without sacrificing inference” is overstated: regret is non‑asymptotic while inference is asymptotic, and there is a factor‑4 sacrifice (R2). The paper does not explain why asymptotic normality is preferable to martingale‑based concentration inequalities (R3).
- **Limited setting** – All results are for two‑arm batched bandits only (R4, R5). The extension to K arms is only vaguely mentioned; no concrete results or analysis are given (R3, R4, R5). All results are asymptotic (R4). The B‑batch algorithm discards data from previous batches, raising concerns about its effectiveness as a bandit method (R1).
- **Novelty and relation to prior work** – Stability is not new; a similar notion appears in Khamaru & Zhang (2024) (R2). Lemma 1 and the main idea are already present in arXiv:2408.04595 (“Inference with the upper confidence bound algorithm”), making this submission incremental (R4). The algorithms (ETC, elimination) are standard; the technical tools largely follow the literature (R2). The paper does not articulate the specific technical challenges or how they are overcome (R3).
- **Presentation and writing quality** – Numerous typos and grammatical errors (e.g., line 155 “We assume Let …”, line 158 “genrality”, line 60 “maybe” for “may be”, line 224 “on consistent estimator of”, line 286 “pull both arm”) (R1, R2, R4). The use of “or” instead of “otherwise” in algorithm pseudocode is confusing (R1). The proof of Theorem 1 is messy and hard to follow (R1, R2). Theorems 1 and 2 have misplaced empirical variance terms (R2), and the convergence statement appears to divide by σ̂<sub>a,T</sub> when it should multiply (R5). Missing definitions: z<sub>α</sub> (Section 3.1) and regret (Corollary 2) (R5). The constraint on m in Equation (7) is not justified (R5). The connection to batched bandits is not fully exploited (R1).
- **Experiments** – Only a small simulation is provided; more comprehensive experiments are expected for a paper proposing new algorithms (R2).
- **Specific technical questions that overlap with criticisms** – Why does Algorithm 3 pull each arm exactly m times when both arms remain, rather than with probability 1/2 (R5)? How does the paper’s contribution compare to Khamaru & Zhang (2024) and to arXiv:2408.04595 (R2, R4)? What are the concrete implications of the results for practice or theory, beyond the two‑arm batched case (R3, R5)?
