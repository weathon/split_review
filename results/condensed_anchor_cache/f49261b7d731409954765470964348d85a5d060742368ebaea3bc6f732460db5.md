## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper extends Weak-to-Strong generalization to a multi-weak-teacher (WeakS-to-Strong) setting using a Bayesian approach based on evidential deep learning. It also expands application from text classification to text generation tasks, addressing tokenizer mismatch via word-string-based soft label alignment and adding a DPO stage. The paper reports improvements over single weak teachers and naive averaging baselines, but the significance, generality, and experimental support receive mixed assessments across reviewers.

### Strengths
- (R1, R2) Strong originality and significance: the WeakS-to-Strong setting is well-motivated, and the Bayesian method (EDL-based) is novel. The paper is the first to successfully extend weak-to-strong to multiple weak teachers, and the expansion to generation tasks (more realistic LLM use cases) is notable.
- (R1) The proposed method yields notable improvements over individual weak models and the naive weighted-average CE baseline. Ablations verify the importance of the auxiliary loss, probability estimation for weak sequences, and λ weights.
- (R1, R2) The writing is clear, well-structured, and easy to follow, with good contextualization relative to prior work.
- (R2) Experiments are well-structured to answer the central questions: is WeakS-to-Strong better than Weak-to-Strong, and is the proposed aggregation better than naïve baselines?
- (R3) The proposal is simple but works.
- (R4) The Bayesian WeakS-to-Strong framework and the extension to generation tasks are noted; the token-level probability estimation for soft labels is a useful technique (though not fully proven).

### Weaknesses
- (R1) **Limited result support for some claims**: DPO-based improvements are within standard deviations; the claim that the auxiliary loss improves the Bayesian method (L461) is not strongly backed. The main result (Bayesian superiority) is more solid.
- (R1, R3, R4) **Incomplete baselines and weak comparison standards**:
  - Missing baselines from data programming / weak supervision aggregation (Snorkel, FlyingSquid) and traditional ensembling methods (Liu & Asahi 2024; Sang et al. 2024). These should be included or at least discussed.
  - All current baselines are very simple/naive; stronger baselines are needed in both classification and generation.
- (R1, R2, R4) **Limited task diversity**: Only one task per category – SciQ (classification) and SLURP slot-filling (generation). The paper would be strengthened by additional classification tasks and generation tasks such as text summarization or machine translation.
- (R1) **Unclear/fragile assumptions**:
  - The best results rely on the “Fixed Weight” approach (Appendix E), which assumes accurate prior estimates of each weak model’s performance. This assumption may break down for truly superhuman tasks where ground truth is scarce.
  - Generalization to human supervision is unclear: the method uses confidence estimates from weak models, but human-generated labels may not provide such estimates.
- (R1, R3) **Methodological ambiguities and missing clarifications**:
  - How were λ weights set for the “Fixed Weight” approach? Appendix E does not specify the final values or how “performance of different weak models” was used.
  - How is PGR (Probabilistic Generation Rate) calculated when multiple weak models are involved? Only a single-weak-model definition is given.
  - Equation 4: what is $\hat{y}_w^{(i)}$? Should it not just be the label $k$? The inner sum appears to lack a dependence on $k$.
  - Equation 7: why does the sum of $P(s_1)$ and $P(s_2)$ equal $P(W)$? The desired relationship would be product, not sum.
  - Equation 3: are $\alpha_k$ trainable parameters? Is the loss per sample?
  - The motivation for using the Dirichlet distribution to model the prior is ambiguous (R3).
- (R2, R3) **Design justification and potential training instability**:
  - The tokenizer-alignment strategy (Section 4.1) using word strings as a bridge is not well justified; it is unclear how it handles ambiguous tokenization or non-straightforward word-level alignment. No analysis of error propagation or information loss is given.
  - The probability of the target token for the strong model changes each update because $C_s(s_1)$ and $C_s(s_2)$ shift. This may cause training instability or divergence. The computational cost of recomputing these terms per iteration is also a concern (R3).
- (R4) **Limited model scale**: experiments use only ~1B weak and ~7B strong models; claims about WeakS-to-Strong are overclaimed given this narrow scale.
- (R4) **Negligible improvement**: e.g., 0.843 baseline vs. 0.850 WeakS-to-Strong-3 is not observable.
- (R4) The explanation of “complementary weak models” (Section 6.3) is unclear: low consistency among weak models does not automatically imply complementarity; the logic needs justification.
