## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GC-CLIP, an inference pipeline that combines an off-the-shelf zero-shot object detector (OWL-ViT) with CLIP. In a preprocessing step, OWL-ViT crops and zooms into the detected object; after applying Multi-Margin Box Augmentation (MAug), the crop is classified by CLIP. This aims to improve zero-shot closed-set classification, particularly for small objects, without any training. Positive but marginal improvements are reported on ImageNetS919 and CUB.

### Strengths
- Clear, well-written paper with precise language that is easy to follow (R1, R4).
- Simple, pragmatic approach that is easy to re-implement and requires no additional training (R2, R3, R4).
- Comprehensive ablation studies covering multiple factors (e.g., margin, random crop augmentation), supported by both quantitative and qualitative results (R1, R4).
- Addresses a real practical limitation of CLIP for small objects and is motivated by concrete considerations of using these models in applications (R3).
- Demonstrates positive results on the chosen benchmarks, with some architectures and datasets (especially those with small objects) showing a larger gap (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution.** The idea that cropping improves image classification has been explored in prior work (e.g., [1,2,...]). The heavy-lifting is done entirely by the pre-trained object detector; the paper offers no technical novelty beyond showing that an off-the-shelf detector can be used for this purpose (R2). The observations about CLIP’s limitations are surface-level and well-known (R3).
- **Necessity of guided cropping not convincingly justified.** The improvement over random cropping is slight on several datasets, and random cropping alone was not reported for the CALIP baseline. More results and analysis on additional datasets are needed to demonstrate the advantage of guided cropping (R1). A minority reviewer (R2) also notes that the method is only suitable when the object label corresponds to a small object, and the paper would benefit from focusing more aggressively on that scenario.
- **Computational cost not reported.** The inference cost (parameters, FLOPs, or time) of adding a separate object detection model is not provided, making a fair comparison with CLIP alone impossible (R1, R4).
- **Insufficient benchmarking.** Only two datasets (ImageNetS919, CUB) are used. Results on widely-adopted zero-shot classification benchmarks such as ImageNet, VTAB, and OOD datasets (e.g., ObjectNet) are missing (R3, R4). A direct comparison with other popular zero-shot classification methods is also needed (R1). The improvement is marginal overall, which may limit the insight provided to the community (R4).
- **Narrow applicability and missing limitations discussion.** The method is specific to CLIP and requires post-processing on top of the detector’s initial boxes. The paper does not discuss how it could extend to other CLIP/OWL-ViT capabilities (e.g., image-text retrieval, detection) or what the general limitations of the approach are (R2, R3). The essential prerequisite that OWL-ViT provides a correct detection box for every target is not validated on diverse, complex datasets; the impact and failure modes of the detector should be analyzed (R1).
- **Weak comparison in Table 2.** The setup for comparing CALIP and GC-CLIP differs from that of Table 1, making the results hard to compare directly. A clearer evaluation using the same protocol as Table 1 is recommended (R4).
- **Minor writing issues.** Subject-verb inconsistency in the Introduction (R1); grammatical error (“…having…” rather than “…having…” in Sec. 5.2) (R4); incorrect formatting of quotes in Sec. 5.4 (e.g., “land” and “sea”) (R4). Additionally, showing confidence scores in the qualitative examples (Sec. 5.4) would help interpret when the model needs more context (R4).
