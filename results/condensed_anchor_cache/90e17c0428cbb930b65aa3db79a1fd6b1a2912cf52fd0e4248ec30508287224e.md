## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes MIM-Refiner, a contrastive learning boost for pre-trained MIM models. The authors identify that strong representations in MIM models reside in intermediate layers, and that the lightweight decoder of MIM models (e.g., MAE) cannot fully decouple encoding from decoding, leading to representation degradation in later encoder blocks. MIM-Refiner attaches multiple instance discrimination (ID) heads to different intermediate layers, using a nearest neighbor ID objective to refine representations. The method is simple and requires only a few epochs of training. It achieves state-of-the-art linear probing (84.7%) and low-shot classification on ImageNet-1K with a ViT-H pre-trained via data2vec 2.0, and shows strong performance on long-tailed classification and semantic segmentation. However, gains on full finetuning are small, and concerns exist about potential degradation on dense prediction tasks and the added training complexity.

### Strengths

1. **Clear identification of representation degradation in MIM models**: The paper provides a detailed analysis showing that different blocks in MIM models serve different roles (some optimize the pre-training objective, others learn abstract features) and that the lightweight decoder in methods like MAE cannot fully separate encoding from decoding, especially in larger models. This insight is shared with prior work (Context Autoencoder [A]), but the paper provides additional evidence from multiple experiments. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)

2. **Simple and effective proposed method**: MIM-Refiner is a straightforward contrastive learning approach that attaches ID heads to intermediate layers, refining MIM representations with few additional epochs. The method is well-motivated and successfully improves off-the-shelf features, especially in low-shot settings. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4) One reviewer (Reviewer 3) emphasizes "large improvements over current SOTA," while others note the gains are mostly on few-shot settings and less on full finetuning.

3. **Well-written and well-structured paper**: The paper is easy to follow, with clear observations, motivation, method description, extensive experiments, and comprehensive analysis. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4) The analyzing part is also well-written. (Reviewer 1)

4. **Strong experimental results on certain benchmarks**: The method achieves state-of-the-art linear probing (84.7% on ImageNet-1K with ViT-H/data2vec 2.0) and low-shot classification, and performs well on long-tailed classification and semantic segmentation. (Reviewers 2, 3) The method shows effectiveness and generalization ability on downstream tasks. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of methodological clarity and architectural details**: The paper does not clearly specify which encoder blocks are fine-tuned during refinement (all blocks or only those connected to ID heads?), the number of epochs needed for different model sizes, and the exact structure of the ID heads. There is no overview diagram illustrating the complete MIM-Refiner architecture and training pipeline; Figure 4 provides only partial information. (Reviewers 1, 2)

2. **Missing baseline and ablation**: The paper does not include a baseline where the original MIM model is trained for the same number of additional epochs without ID heads, which is necessary to demonstrate that the improvement comes from refinement rather than extra training. (Reviewer 1)

3. **Limited evaluation on dense prediction tasks**: The paper focuses on classification and segmentation probing, but lacks experiments on object detection and instance segmentation (e.g., COCO with ViTDet). This is especially important because MIM models (e.g., MAE) are known to transfer well to dense prediction, and contrastive learning may harm pixel-level representations. Reviewers request a comparison of MIM-Refiner ViT-L on COCO object detection following the ViTDet framework [1]. (Reviewers 3, 4)

4. **Concerns about trade-offs between contrastive learning and MIM objectives**: The contrastive loss may over-emphasize instance discrimination at the expense of rich feature representations that benefit dense prediction tasks. For full fine-tuning, improvement is minimal (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). When the backbone is frozen, the method may degrade pixel-level representation quality compared to models like DINOv2 that explicitly combine MIM and CL. The paper does not provide sufficient analysis of these trade-offs or evidence that MIM-Refiner does not negatively impact pixel-level performance. (Reviewer 4) Existing work [A] has shown fine-tuning MIM models yields minimal improvement under full fine-tuning. (Reviewer 4)

5. **Increased training complexity and computational cost**: The method requires a two-step training paradigm (first MIM, then refinement), which increases deployment complexity. The top-20 nearest neighbor selection in the contrastive queue may significantly increase training time per step, but the queue size and its impact on training time are not reported. Could the method be trained end-to-end? The paper does not discuss potential bottlenecks or feasibility of end-to-end training. (Reviewers 2, 3)

6. **Additional questions and minor issues**: 
   - What does "relative" mean in Figure 3(d)? Is it the performance difference between layer i+1 and layer i? (Reviewer 1)
   - Does the representation degradation issue also appear in audio and language domains for data2vec 2.0? (Reviewer 3)
   - Typo: Lines 267-269 refer to Table 1 instead of Figure 3b. (Reviewer 1)
