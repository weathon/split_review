## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper develops a unified analytical theory for bias amplification in ridge regression, both with and without random projections (the latter modeling neural networks in a simplified regime). Using operator-valued free probability theory, the authors derive exact limiting risk formulas under a high-dimensional Gaussian mixture model. They analyze how model size, label noise, regularization, and training dynamics affect bias in majority/minority groups, predicting optimal regularization or training time to mitigate bias and showing that overparameterization can harm minority groups. The theoretical predictions are validated on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets (e.g., a complex image-based dataset). The paper claims novelty in focusing on test error disparities across groups with different data distributions, in contrast to prior empirical or theoretical work.

### Strengths
- **Solid theoretical foundation**: Derives exact limiting risk formulas, providing a rigorous, mathematically sound foundation for studying bias amplification (R1, R4). Analysis using operator-valued free probability theory and high-dimensional scaling limits yields precise fixed-point equations and phase transitions (R4).
- **Novel insights**: Predicts both known bias amplification effects and new phenomena (e.g., under isotropic covariance) (R2). Shows that bias can arise even without group imbalance or spurious correlations (R4). Explores how overparameterization and extraneous features amplify minority-group error (R4).
- **Empirical validation**: Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets (including a complex semi-synthetic image dataset) support theoretical predictions, demonstrating that ridge regression with random projections can amplify bias even in balanced data (R2, R4).
- **Relevance and significance**: Addresses a critical and timely problem; the topic is important for understanding and mitigating unfairness in machine learning (R3, R4). Provides a structured theoretical framework that unifies previous empirical observations (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Explanation of theoretical results**: The exact risk formulas in Definition 3.2 and the fundamental constants in Definition F.1 are not sufficiently explained. Their connection to data distribution, degrees of freedom of covariance matrices, and intuition behind their forms needs elaboration (R1). R3 adds that paragraphs on results (e.g., lines 311-317, 372-375, 400-403, 443-453) are hard to read due to constant reference to parameter values without clear meaning of regimes; suggest stating the setup in words before presenting parameter values.
- **Lack of formal theorems on bias amplification**: The paper does not state formal theorems that explicitly characterize how bias amplification changes with label noise ratio, model size, or regularization parameter, and under what conditions it is exacerbated or mitigated (R1).
- **Connection to prior work**: The paper ignores the extremely similar work "Bias-inducing geometries: an exactly solvable data model with fairness implications" (arXiv:2205.15935). The connections and differences must be clearly discussed (R3). Additionally, the connection to prior OVFPT work is not clearly explained; the paper only states that its theory is not a special case but does not elaborate why (R4).
- **Assumptions and limitations not discussed**: 
  - The analysis relies on an implicit assumption about conditional dependence heterogeneity (w2≠w1); what if this is relaxed? (R2)
  - Using a linear network as a proxy for deep networks is a strong simplification. The one-hidden-layer NN model in Line 172 appears to lack a nonlinear activation function (R2, R3).
  - Using regularization intensity to approximate early stopping (1/t) may lead to simplistic conclusions about training dynamics; the connection to work like arXiv:2405.18296 should be discussed. Presenting "optimal training time" and "training dynamics" in the abstract and titles without specifying this approximation is misleading (R3).
- **Definition of bias amplification**: Insufficient theoretical justification is provided for the definition of “bias amplification” (R2).
- **Practical guidance and real-world applicability**: 
  - It is unclear how the theoretical findings relate to real-world deep learning models; verification on MNIST with a CNN is suggested (R1).
  - Section 4.2 lacks concrete guidance on setting regularization parameters or optimal training durations for different dataset sizes and feature-sample ratios (e.g., when ψ is close to or above 1) (R4).
  - In real-world datasets, group properties like 𝚺₁ are often unknown; how to assess whether a model is prone to bias amplification? (R2)
  - If bias amplification is identified, how to design a practical criterion for optimal λ or runtime? Could heterogeneity be leveraged for a better model? (R2)
- **Potential confounding with double descent**: It is plausible that the observed bias phenomenology is a direct effect of double-descent/interpolation peak, known to shift with overparameterization and cause critical sensitivity to input noise. The authors should discuss this connection and clarify if linear activation is a confounding factor (R3).
- **Specific empirical concerns**: 
  - Figure 1: Is the setting with balanced groups? (R3) The extreme phase transition in the EDD plot (lower left corner) and its mismatch with the ADD plot need discussion (R1).
  - Figure 3: Error bars are hidden in the supplementary; the theoretical prediction seems very qualitative (R3).
  - Figure 11: Most metrics are monotone with respect to λ; how does one conclude the optimal λ? (R1)
- **Dissenting view on severity**: While Reviewer 4 considered these minor weaknesses, other reviewers raised more fundamental concerns regarding assumptions, missing baselines, and lack of practical guidance. This contrast should be acknowledged.
