## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies whether synthetic data from generative models (especially diffusion models) can enhance contrastive learning (CL). The authors find that naively inflating a dataset with generated images can harm linear probe performance, and identify two critical factors: the mixing ratio of real to generated data and the strength of data augmentations. They show that upweighting real data (e.g., 10×) and using weaker augmentations with more data can yield gains. Based on these insights, they propose Adaptive Inflation (AdaInf), which adjusts augmentation strength according to the dataset size. Theoretical analysis using an augmentation graph framework provides justification. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny ImageNet with SimCLR, MoCo V2, BYOL, and Barlow Twins show improvements over plain CL and CL with naive inflation, achieving 94.70% linear accuracy on CIFAR-10 with SimCLR.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-motivated: understanding when and why synthetic data can hurt contrastive learning is an important and timely problem.
- The insights—that stronger data inflation should be paired with weaker augmentations (and vice versa)—are novel, creative, and significant. They provide a practical perspective on the interplay between dataset size and augmentation.
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with clear problem definition and analysis.
- The experimental design is good; ablation studies successfully verify the theoretical predictions, and results are presented clearly.
- Theoretical analysis, although a simple application of HaoChen et al., provides interesting intuitions and is supported by a synthetic Gaussian example.
- The proposed AdaInf, being purely data-centric and requiring no extra computation cost, is a straightforward and effective strategy. It sets a new record on CIFAR-10 with SimCLR (94.70%).
- One reviewer (score 8, high confidence) found the insights and results novel, creative, and significant, and believes they will greatly help understanding both theoretically and empirically.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scale and generality of experiments**: Results are mainly on small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny ImageNet). There is no evaluation on ImageNet-scale datasets, which limits the significance of the claims. The paper also evaluates only diffusion models; experiments on other generative models (e.g., GANs) are missing.
- **Theory–empirics connection**: One reviewer (score 3) found the theoretical and empirical parts not well connected—both tracks seem fairly independent. The theoretical justification for optimal weighting is not fully explained: Theorem 3.1 suggests β=1 is best given distribution mismatch, but does not explain why β<1 can be beneficial. Theorem 4.1’s argument that λ_{k+1} is larger when P_g has larger support needs to be made more clear. Overall, it is unclear how the theory guides the choice of mixing ratio.
- **AdaInf is not truly adaptive**: Multiple reviewers note that AdaInf relies on manual hyperparameter selection (mixing ratio and augmentation strength) using a validation downstream task. This contradicts the goal of self-supervised learning where downstream tasks are unknown, and the term “adaptive” is misleading. The method does not automatically learn or adjust these hyperparameters.
- **Missing comparisons**: The paper does not compare AdaInf with other existing data inflation methods for contrastive learning (e.g., using synthetic data with different reweighting or augmentation strategies).
- **Missing error bars and statistical tests**: Several results (Tables, Figures) do not report error bars from multiple seeds. The word “significantly” is used without statistical backing. The authors should run at least 5 seeds for CIFAR-scale experiments.
- **Incorrect formulas and citations**: Equation (1) for InfoNCE is missing a negative sign before the positive term, and the usual convention uses a sum over negatives (the difference is minor but the formula should be correct). Equation (2) for diffusion models is also incorrectly cited; the correct references are “Understanding Contrastive Representation Learning through Alignment and Uniformity on the Hypersphere” for Eq. (1) and Ho et al. 2020 for Eq. (2). These errors undermine the preliminary section.
- **Notation and consistency issues**:
  - The notation for replication times N in Section 3.1 is reused with a different meaning in Section 4.1 without clarification.
  - In Eq. (6), the meaning of 𝒳 is undefined; the variable w_{xx'} is used in the main text but it is unclear if defined as A_{x,x'}.
  - The definition of labeling error in Theorem 4.1 is unconventional; if defined as in Eq. (8), then φ^y in Eq. (6) would be less than 2α, contradicting the current claim that φ^y=α. Also note that α in HaoChen et al. 2021a has a different meaning.
  - Theorem 3.1 introduces TV (total variation) without prior definition.
  - In Theorem 4.1, the definition of the optimal encoder and the objective (population spectral contrastive loss) should be clearly stated. Equation (5) (majority-vote prediction) should appear in the main text.
  - Numerous typos throughout, including in Theorem 4.1, Lemma 4.2, and Equations (7), (9), (12). For example, Eq. (12) has P_t instead of P_g.
  - Theorem 4.1 and Lemma 4.2 should link to the appendix where proofs are stated.
- **Clarity in theoretical claims**:
  - Lemma 4.2 uses random edge selection with probability p, but random node selection would be more appropriate for modeling subsampling.
  - Theorem 4.1 argues about connectivity and non-zero λ_{k+1} for the training set, but Saunshi et al. 2022 show lack of connectivity in the training set under standard augmentations, which would make the bound vacuous. The paper should address this contradiction; HaoChen et al. used population eigenvalues.
  - The proof of Theorem 4.1, especially Eq. (7), is unclear: the indicator function is swapped from I(y(x)≠y(x')) to I(y(x)≠y(x~)) without explanation, the first inequality is unjustified, and the sum disappears in the third equality. The authors should use Markov’s inequality to obtain Eq. (11).
- **Inconsistent experimental figures**: 
  - In Section 5.1, the min scale for random resized cropping is reported as 0.2, but Figure 3 shows the optimal value is 0.3. The result in Table 3 differs slightly from Figure 3 (0.3 yields best performance).
  - Figure 6 shows that vanilla data inflation *always* helps or maintains performance, which seems to contradict the earlier claim in Figure 3(b) that inflation can hurt. The number of training steps may be a confounder; this should be controlled.
- **Missing details and questions**:
  - FID scores of the generative models used should be added to Table 1, as they likely influence experiment outcomes.
  - It is unclear why 1M synthetic images are sampled while CIFAR-10 has only 60k real images; the effect of varying |D_g| (rather than just β) should be studied.
  - The paper does not specify whether different diffusion models were used for each dataset (e.g., a CIFAR model trained only on CIFAR) or if a single model was used for all.
  - The assumption P_d ≈ P_g for Lemma 4.2 seems unreasonable in light of the discrepancy argument in Theorem 3.1.
  - The connection between Lemma 4.2 and the empirical results is not explained; what is its significance?
  - The purpose of Figure 4 (augmentation overlap) is confusing; it is unclear how it demonstrates that stronger augmentations create more overlap between different training samples.
  - For the experiments in Figure 6, is the batch size the same across methods? If training steps are equal, is the training cost identical?
  - What is the pre-training cost of the diffusion model on the evaluated datasets?
  - The authors should include sample images from the diffusion model in the supplement for visual comparison of FID levels.
  - The paper mentions “slower sampling” on page 3; this term should be explained.
  - Is the ratio 10:1 for real/generated data universal across datasets and diffusion models? Are the hyperparameters (mixing ratio, augmentation strength) dataset-specific?
  - The study does not discuss potential biases introduced by synthetic data (e.g., differences in object-centric bias compared to real ImageNet).
  - There is no discussion of the trade-off between the size of generated data and augmentation strength on real-world datasets; all experiments use a fixed generated data size.
