## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces trajectory-specific leave-one-out (TSLOO) influence to capture the dependence of data influence on the order of training data, addressing a limitation of traditional influence functions that assume permutation invariance. The authors propose Data Value Embedding (DVE) to efficiently approximate TSLOO via a dot-product between the embedding and the test gradient. They provide theoretical bounds, computational efficiency tricks, and empirical insights revealing distinct phases of data influence (high-influence warmup, low-influence basin, late-stage increase). Experiments are conducted on MNIST, MLP, and subsets of the Pile.

### Strengths

- **Novel and timely problem formulation**: The paper formalizes trajectory-specific data influence, which is missing in conventional influence estimation methods that assume permutation invariance. This is a clear research gap given modern stochastic, non-convergent training paradigms.
- **Efficient approximation**: DVE enables real-time influence estimation without retraining, with techniques to reduce memory and computational cost (e.g., Gauss-Newton approximation).
- **Theoretical credibility**: Assumptions are outlined and an approximation error bound is derived, lending theoretical support to the approach.
- **Insightful discovery of training phases**: The method reveals distinct stages of data influence—a high-influence warmup phase, a low-influence basin, and gradually ascending influence later—offering actionable strategies for data selection timing.
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The mathematical ideas and intuitions are clearly presented.
- **Addresses an important gap with wide applicability**: The method is applicable to large-scale models and non-convergent settings, such as those used for LLMs.

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear novelty relative to existing work** (e.g., Hara et al., 2019): The concept of TSLOO and the approximation in Section 3.1 may not be novel, as they appear in earlier literature. The paper lacks a dedicated related work section to delineate the authors’ contribution from prior concepts (e.g., Hara et al., 2019). This ambiguity extends to DVE: the interpretation of Theorem 2 (e.g., the statement that "similar training points encountered in later iterations may have a stronger impact on the influence score of earlier training points") is unclear, and the setting for tracking influence over iterations is not properly set up.
- **Limited to SGD**: The method is explicitly tailored for SGD and not directly applicable to popular optimizers like Adam. Using SGD as a proxy is discussed but restricts general applicability.
- **Limited real-world validation**: Fidelity evaluation is limited to small datasets (MNIST, MLP) and reduced subsets (e.g., 1% of the Pile). Robustness and scalability to diverse, large-scale production datasets remain unverified.
- **Assumptions may not hold in practice**: The method assumes layer independence (may fail for complex architectures with significant cross-layer interactions) and specific learning rate schedules (may not reflect diverse schedules used in practice). These assumptions could affect reliability.
- **First-order Taylor expansion may be invalid for influential samples**: The loss change estimation (Line 169) relies on \(\theta_T'\) and \(\theta_T\) being close, but when a sample is highly influential, they diverge more, raising concerns about approximation validity.
- **Terminology confusion and conceptual gap with traditional CV**: The paper uses "LOO error" but it quantifies loss change on validation data when a training point is removed—not the conventional CV error used for model selection. The relationship between loss change and actual model performance change is not discussed or justified. A comparison with traditional CV terminology and an experiment connecting loss change to performance differences are needed.
- **Unclear if early and late regions suffice without the middle region**: The finding that early and late regions have high influence and yield similar improvements is interesting, but the role of the middle region in enabling those late-region improvements is not addressed.
- **Potential computational overhead for large models**: Storing per-sample gradient information and DVE embeddings for billions of parameters may be prohibitive without further optimization.
- **Missing experiments and clarifications**:
  - No experiment with injected outliers to examine whether outliers have outsized influence (and potential mitigation strategies). Comparison with existing influence techniques on outliers is missing.
  - The selection of data points for Figure 4 (y-axis: influence score at checkpoints; "green curve") is not described, hindering reproducibility.
  - It is unclear whether the analysis considers a single epoch or multiple epochs; if only one epoch, generalization to multi-epoch training is not discussed.
  - The Hessian \(H_k\) is a function of parameters (Line 148), but no pattern of the Hessian trajectory is observed.
  - Removing samples with negative influence scores: will the model improve? (no experiment shown)
  - The claim about well-established warm-up effect (Line 435) lacks a citation.
  - The derivation of Appendix C.3 for Theorem 2 needs a brief written summary.
  - General applicability to time series data with seasonal/temporal dependence is not addressed.
  - The usefulness of influence measures for detecting outliers or enabling more effective learning with subsets is not fully explained.
