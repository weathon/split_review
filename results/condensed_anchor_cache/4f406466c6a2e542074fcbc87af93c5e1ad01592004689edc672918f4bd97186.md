## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents a self-supervised learning approach for robot motion planning that learns a cost-to-go function approximating the Eikonal equation, building on prior NTFields work. The method uses temporal difference (TD) learning to enforce Bellman's optimality, a metric learning component to preserve geodesic properties (triangle inequality, symmetry, non-negativity), and online MPC for path generation. It conditions on environment via attention for generalization. The approach is evaluated on robots with 2–12 DOF in simulation and real-world benchmarks, and compared to sampling-based planners and other learning-based methods.

### Strengths

- The problem of motion planning in complex, cluttered environments for high-DOF robots is important and well-motivated. The paper provides a comprehensive literature review covering traditional, expert-demonstration, and self-supervised methods, and positions the approach clearly relative to prior work. (R1, R3)
- The method is self-supervised, removing the need for expensive expert demonstrations or data collection. (R2, R4)
- The method is significantly faster than sampling-based motion planning (e.g., planning times as low as 0.07 sec vs 0.5 sec for baselines) and has good computational efficiency. (R2, R4)
- The training is fast (few seconds to few minutes) and the approach is easy to set up. (R4)
- The proposed combination of continuous loss L_E (from NTFields) with a novel temporal difference loss L_TD is interesting; the authors use Fig.1 to illustrate that low L_E alone does not guarantee good behavior, motivating the TD loss. (R1)
- The ablation study gives a clear picture of the contribution of each component, and the method generalizes to unseen environments, which was not possible with prior self-supervised methods according to the authors. (R4)
- Simulation and real-world benchmarks are comprehensive, with comparisons against many baselines. (R1)
- The paper is well written and clearly presents the approach. (R4)

Note: Reviewer 1’s overall assessment is less positive (score 3), while reviewers 3 and 4 are positive (score 8) and reviewer 2 moderately positive (score 5). The following weaknesses reflect this range of critique.

### Weaknesses

- **Empirical impact of proposed losses vs. existing L_E**: The ablation study (Fig.2, row 2) shows that L_E from NTFields is the most important component; the additional loss terms provide only small improvements (e.g., error reduces from 0.21 to 0.08). This raises concerns about the effectiveness of the proposed techniques given the increased system complexity. (R1) Conversely, reviewer 4 considers the ablation clear and informative. (R4)
- **Success rates and claim of significant outperformance**: The method’s success rates are consistently on par with or lower than the top performing methods in Table 1. Specifically, on the harder 7-DOF manipulator, the method achieves 87% while learning-free RRT-Connect achieves 98%. Reviewers question the abstract's claim of “significantly outperforming existing methods” given these mixed results. (R2, R4)
- **Generalization evaluation**: The evaluation of generalization to novel environments includes both seen and unseen environments during testing, which may inflate reported performance and obscure true generalization capability. (R3) Also, only visualizations of successful paths in unseen environments are provided; a more comprehensive quantitative analysis across varied unseen environments with different complexities is needed to objectively measure robustness and success rate. (R4) The paper does not expand on failure cases when generalizing. (R4)
- **Ablation study limitations**: The ablation study is conducted on a single 2D maze environment; it is not shown whether the relative importance of each component holds across different environments. (R1, R3, R4) Quantitative results for the ablation are lacking, making it hard to assess true impact. (R3)
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The loss function involves many hyperparameters (stated in Appendix C). No sensitivity analysis is provided, especially for the key parameter ΔT, raising concerns about robustness and practical applicability. (R2, R3)
- **Clarity and presentation for non-experts**: The paper is too technical and hard to follow for readers unfamiliar with this class of approaches. Section 3.2 lacks a high-level overview of how NTFields works as a motion planner; key concepts (e.g., S*, Eq.3, how the learned S is used for online planning) are not explained intuitively. (R1) The motivation for metric learning (Sec.4.2) is unclear; Eq.10 and the choice of L_inf distance over Euclidean distance need more intuition. (R1) Figures (Fig.1, Fig.2) are hard to decrypt; captions lack explanations of colors, contour lines, and other visual elements. (R1, R3)
- **MPC and planning limitations**: The MPC-based planning scheme cannot backtrack; a search algorithm like A* with the value function as a heuristic could be more complete, and the inability to backtrack may contribute to lower success rates on hard problems (e.g., 7-DOF). (R2) The improvement in online inference time over prior methods is small (0.056 vs 0.074 sec in Tab.1(a)); the practical implications of this difference should be discussed. (R1)
- **Measurement and reporting**: Table 1 should clarify what “time” measures in different cases. (R4) The phrase “superior speed” in Section 5.3 could be reworded to “computational performance” or similar. (R4)
- **Missing discussion**: The relation between the collision-avoidance loss L_N and L_E (which also uses S* for collision avoidance) is not explained. (R1) The reason for lower success rate on 7DOF (87%) compared to 12DOF dual-arm (91%) is not addressed. (R3) The claim that solving the Eikonal equation is more scalable than motion planning needs justification, including what approximations or relaxations are made and whether kinodynamic constraints are satisfied. (R2) How hyperparameters are tuned is not described. (R2)
