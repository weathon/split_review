## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CoinGAN, a weakly supervised semantic segmentation method for medical lesion segmentation. It introduces contrastive convolution (C-Conv) to enhance boundary detection via intra-image distribution discrepancies, and a commonality-specificity attention (CSA) mechanism with GAN-based adversarial loss for inter-image discrepancy learning. Experiments on QaTa-COV19, ISIC2018, and MoNuSeg achieve DSC of 71.69%, 84.73%, and 78.32%. Reviewers find the idea interesting and the evaluation extensive, but raise major concerns about clarity, missing details, incomplete comparisons, and motivation.

### Strengths
- The observation-based approach to designing the method is interesting (R1, R2, R4).
- The method is validated on sufficiently large datasets (QaTa-COV19, ISIC2018, MoNuSeg) and compared with various state-of-the-art WSSS methods (R1, R2, R4).
- The results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves remarkable improvement over baselines (R1, R4).
- The introduction of contrastive convolution using two convolutional layers with different receptive fields to improve boundary detection is intriguing (R3, R4).
- The dual attention mechanism (CSA) effectively suppresses similarities in structural backgrounds across images while highlighting unique lesion characteristics (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is very hard to follow, especially the method section, with numerous ambiguous and imprecise statements (R2, R4). Specific examples include: unclear why a GAN is needed for image-level label learning (R2); "sharp and high-intensity anatomical distribution" and "anomalous distribution shifts" are undefined (R2); "delve into inter-image distribution discrepancies" is vague (R2); grammatically incorrect sentences such as "bad uncontrollable shape" and "overflow" (R2); "Related to what?" regarding "inter-image strong-related areas" (R2). The motivation for the three challenges is insufficiently discussed (R4).
- **Missing architectural details**: The Average buffer and SElayer are crucial components but are not explained (R1). The symbols x_p and x_h in Figure 2 are not defined; presumably one is pathological and one healthy, but this raises questions about dataset composition (R1). The contrastive convolution's removal of local representations at boundaries may lose valuable information and could affect not only foreground-background boundaries but also internal structural boundaries (R4). The size, selection, and dynamic replacement of reference samples (line 272) are unspecified (R4). The distinction between the proposed average buffer and traditional prototypes or memory banks is unclear (R4).
- **Segmentation mask generation**: It is unclear how the method predicts segmentation masks from image-level annotations. If the generator converts a pathological image to a healthy image and masks are derived from the difference, false positives from artifacts or blurring in healthy regions are not addressed (R1). How the generator produces only latent representations of healthy distribution but segmentation masks and transformed healthy modality (Figure 6) are generated is not explained (R4).
- **Dataset and annotation issues**: For QaTa-COV19 (pneumonia benchmark) the paper does not explain where healthy images come from, as the dataset does not contain them. The same question applies to other datasets (R1). The use of MoNuSeg image-level labels needs clarification (R3). The model's robustness to inaccuracies and variability in image-level labels is not discussed (R3).
- **Missing comparisons and outdated baselines**: The paper primarily compares with CAM-based methods, neglecting other GAN-based and diffusion-based weakly supervised approaches that have shown promise (R4). Domain-specific baselines appear outdated (R4). A more detailed comparison with non-GAN WSSS methods is needed, including architectural differences and training methodologies, not just performance metrics (R3). Existing boundary-aware methods such as BoundaryCAM and Sobel-based operators are not discussed despite the claim of addressing boundary ambiguity (R4).
- **Generalizability and error analysis**: No discussion of generalizability across diverse medical imaging modalities (e.g., MRI, CT) or less common diseases (R3). The paper lacks a detailed error analysis identifying specific conditions (lesion types, image characteristics) where the model performs poorly (R3).
- **Unconvincing weak supervision rationale**: The paper does not justify why a weakly supervised approach is needed when many semi-supervised models achieve superior performance with similar annotation costs (R3). The third challenge (similar anatomical structures) is argued to be a helpful prior rather than a hindrance, undermining the motivation (R4).
