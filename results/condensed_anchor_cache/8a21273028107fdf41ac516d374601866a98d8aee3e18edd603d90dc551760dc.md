## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a non-uniform noise injection method for DNN adversarial robustness and efficiency. The method approximates each layer’s output with a linear surrogate, classifies neurons as essential (high approximation value) or non-essential, and injects noise into non-essential neurons (e.g., via top‑K or N:M masking). Experiments are conducted on several datasets and architectures, with defenses tested against PGD, CW, and other attacks. The paper also provides a theorem suggesting that clean accuracy may not decrease.

### Strengths

- **Interesting hypothesis and approach.** The idea that only a subset of neurons is critical for representation and that non-essential neurons can tolerate noise without harming performance is considered promising. The method for distinguishing essential/non-essential neurons and the non-uniform noise injection tailored to each type are appreciated.
- **Potential for simultaneous improvement.** The work addresses a significant topic: enhancing both adversarial robustness and computational efficiency.
- **Novelty (minority view).** One reviewer (score 6) finds the proposed non-uniform noise injection method novel and the results promising, noting the paper is well‑organized and easy to follow. Theoretical support is considered a strength.
- **Practical angle.** Using layer‑wise approximations to guide noise injection is seen as a good practical approach.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited and insufficient experiments.**  
  - No state‑of‑the‑art attack like AutoAttack is used (requested by multiple reviewers).  
  - Missing strong defense baselines such as TRADES, AWP, SCORE, and ADML.  
  - Only CNN architectures are tested; Transformer structures should also be validated (based on ADML).  
  - The claimed efficiency improvement is not directly demonstrated (e.g., no throughput comparison to baselines).  
  - The experimental setup for robustness evaluation is questioned – reviewers note that standard adversarial training settings and best/last robustness comparisons are needed.

- **Overclaimed “retaining clean accuracy” and limited performance.**  
  - The paper states clean accuracy is retained, but experiments (e.g., Table 3) show a clear decrease in clean accuracy with only small robustness gains. This undermines the claimed accuracy–robustness trade‑off improvement.  
  - The theorem predicts clean accuracy could be *higher* than without noise injection, yet the empirical results consistently show a minor drop. This discrepancy is not explained, and the theoretical analysis does not clarify when the predicted increase might occur.

- **Missing ablation and justification of the neuron selection mechanism.**  
  - No baseline where noise is injected into a random N% of neurons (ignoring the essential/non-essential classification) is provided. This is needed to validate that the selection itself, not just any noise, drives performance.  
  - The threshold / top‑K value for identifying non‑essential neurons is not reported; the impact of different k values on clean accuracy and robustness is not studied.  
  - The distribution of the approximation error (difference between $z$ and $\tilde{z}$) is missing; such a plot is critical for understanding the validity of the linear surrogate.  
  - Several reviewers ask for an intuitive explanation of why a neuron is considered “essential” simply because its approximation $\tilde{z}$ is large – larger activation values may dominate the MSE, but importance does not necessarily follow from magnitude.

- **Clarity and presentation issues.**  
  - The paper is hard to follow in parts: terms like “irregular 50%” and “structured/unstructured” are not clearly defined.  
  - Some experimental choices lack justification (e.g., why $\epsilon=4/255$ in Table 1 but $\epsilon=8/255$ in Table 3).  
  - A technical question is raised about the learned $\tilde{W},\tilde{b}$: if the optimal MSE solution simply yields $W=\tilde{W}P$ and $b=\tilde{b}$, the approximation may be trivial.

- **Novelty (contrasting view).**  
  - While one reviewer considers the method novel and promising, another finds it a “marginal improvement on existing methods”. The minority positive reviewer (score 6) holds the novel view, but the majority of reviewers rate novelty/contribution as fair (2‑3 / 5).
