## Merged Review

### Summary
Five reviewers evaluated the paper. One reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 5) was substantially more positive, praising the practical importance and thorough empirical evaluation of 11 DAL methods across 8 regression tasks using real-world scientific datasets. The other four reviewers (score 3) found the contribution limited, with key criticisms about narrow scope, insufficient novelty, and lack of solutions. All reviewers agree the topic—evaluating DAL robustness in real-world settings with hyperparameter uncertainty—is interesting, but they disagree on the strength of the empirical evidence and the paper’s overall contribution.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and overlooked problem: whether DAL methods are practical in real-world applications where optimal hyperparameters are not known in advance.
- Provides a thorough empirical benchmark of eleven modern DAL methods across eight regression tasks, including real-world scientific datasets (e.g., aerodynamics, materials science) which add practical value.
- Shows that 8 out of 11 DAL methods sometimes underperform relative to simple random sampling when the single hyperparameter (pool ratio) varies, and identifies three diversity-focused methods (GSx, GSxy, QBCDiv) as consistently robust.
- Investigates why diversity-based methods are more reliable, going beyond mere performance reporting.
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow, and the question is well-motivated.

### Weaknesses
- Evaluation is limited to a single hyperparameter (pool ratio). A more comprehensive analysis including other hyperparameters (e.g., annotation budget/query size, initial labeled/unlabeled ratio, model architecture choices) is needed.
- Only regression tasks are evaluated; the datasets are relatively simple and small. The conclusions may not generalize to more complex vision or NLP tasks.
- The definition of the pool ratio is introduced late (Section 3.3) and is potentially misleading (it is actually the annotation budget, i.e., the number of samples extracted per cycle). This should be clarified.
- No formal theoretical framework or mathematical proof is provided to explain why diversity-based methods are inherently more robust.
- The comparison to the closely related work (Holzmüller et al., 2023) is not strong enough to clearly demonstrate the paper’s novelty and necessity.
- The evaluated methods are outdated (only two from 2019–2020, most pre-2020); more recent DAL approaches should be included.
- There is a significant contradiction between the motivating assumption (good HPs are unknown in real-world applications) and the experimental setup: other hyperparameters (model architecture, weight decay, learning rate) are taken from prior work or optimized, undermining the core premise.
- The paper only highlights the problem—8/11 methods can be worse than random—but does not propose solutions or develop a hyperparameter-robust DAL method. This limits the contribution to mere criticism.
- The scope is narrow: the title “Does Deep Active Learning Work in the Wild?” overstates the actual investigation, which focuses only on pool ratio for regression problems. Reframing is suggested.
- Ablations are missing: e.g., testing different query sizes (k) and multiple labeling budgets (the current setup goes from 80 to 2080 labeled samples in steps of 40; other budgets should be considered).
- The point that reducing the pool ratio can enforce diversity (see Figure 2, Figure 5) is not discussed; this could explain the behavior of uncertainty-based strategies and should be explored.
- The paper assumes pool ratio cannot be optimized in real-world applications, but this is not justified even with a validation set. This lack of discussion weakens the motivation.
- Figures are hard to read (e.g., Figure 3, MSE plots in supplementary materials).
- A more detailed table of the 8 regression tasks (dataset size, detection goal, etc.) is needed.
- References in Section 2.2 are outdated (most recent from 2021); more recent related work should be cited.
- The writing suffers from imprecise and ambiguous terminology (e.g., “DAL,” “DAL model,” “DAL method” used interchangeably).
