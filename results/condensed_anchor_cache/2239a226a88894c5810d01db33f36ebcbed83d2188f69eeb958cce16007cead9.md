## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes HyperEEGNet, which combines HyperNetworks with EEGNet to adaptively generate weights for motor imagery classification from resting-state EEG data. It is evaluated on two datasets: Dreyer et al. (33 participants, across-session and across-user) and BCI Competition IV IIa (9 participants, cross-session). The reviewers express mixed views. One reviewer (R2, score 5) finds the approach original and notes strong generalization on the larger dataset. Another reviewer (R1, score 1) finds no substantial innovation and cites inconsistent performance across datasets. Two reviewers (R3, R4, scores 3) raise concerns about dataset size, resting-state dependence, complexity, and interpretability.

### Strengths
* The study addresses the important issues of cross-user variability and BCI illiteracy by using HyperNetworks to generate adaptive, user-specific weights from resting-state data (R1).
* The approach is original in applying a HyperNet architecture to improve generalization of an EEG motor imagery classifier (R2).
* Evaluation includes both inter-subject and inter-session performance, which are important for real-world BCI deployment (R2).
* On the larger Dreyer et al. dataset (33 subjects), the model demonstrates robust generalization capabilities, handling unseen subjects effectively (R2, R3, R4).
* The method promises reduced calibration time, which is critical for practical BCI applications (R3, R4).
* R2 notes the clear description of experiments and significance of evaluating a new method on EEG data with up to 42 subjects.

### Weaknesses
* **Lack of innovation.** R1 (high confidence) considers the combination of existing HyperNetworks and EEGNet to lack substantial innovation; R2, in contrast, finds the work original.
* **Inconsistent performance across datasets.** HyperEEGNet improves over EEGNet on the Dreyer et al. dataset (Table 1) but degrades on the BCI Competition IV IIa dataset (Table 2) (R1, R2). R1 criticizes the absence of meaningful discussion about these conflicting results. R2 suggests testing additional datasets or varying the number of training subjects on Dreyer (e.g., 8,16,24,32) to understand the relationship between dataset size and effectiveness.
* **Insufficient evaluation of generalization on the Dreyer dataset.** R2 points out that the current 5‑fold cross‑validation does not adequately demonstrate cross‑subject generalization. A leave‑N‑subjects‑out split (with about a quarter of subjects as test) repeated with different groupings would be more rigorous.
* **Small initial dataset (9 participants).** R3 and R4 note that evaluations relying on only nine subjects may not capture variability in broader populations (e.g., age, gender, neurological conditions). The selection of these participants lacks justification (R4). This raises questions about generalizability (R3,R4).
* **Dependence on resting‑state data.** The model’s effectiveness may be limited by quality/relevance variations of resting‑state EEG across users or sessions (e.g., vigilance, cognitive states, minor movements) (R3,R4). The assumption that resting‑state data is a stable predictor of motor imagery performance needs rigorous validation (R4). R2 asks how resting state would be identified during deployment (separate classifier or heuristic?).
* **Complexity of HyperNetworks.** Integration adds complexity to training/tuning, requiring greater computational resources and expertise (R3,R4). The paper provides no analysis of computational overhead or comparison with simpler methods in terms of cost (R4). R2 asks whether hyperparameters were tuned for the Dreyer dataset.
* **Missing comparisons with existing state‑of‑the‑art methods** that address subject variability (R1). R2 asks for current state‑of‑the‑art performance on both datasets. R3 and R4 request criteria and metrics for comparison with other transfer learning approaches.
* **Interpretability.** The black‑box nature hinders understanding of which aspects of resting‑state EEG drive weight generation (R3,R4). No techniques for visualizing or interpreting learned representations are explored (R4).
* **Clarity and presentation issues.** R2 notes the term “epoch” is overloaded (EEG window vs. training iteration) and needs clarification. R2 also questions whether cross‑session evaluation on BCI IV IIa was actually performed (the original has two sessions, but the paper describes a 5‑fold split without session separation). R1, R3, R4 gave low presentation scores (1 or 1).
* **Lack of analysis of overfitting and learning dynamics.** R4 asks about strategies to mitigate overfitting and the implications of a steep learning curve with convergence in only 50 epochs (model capacity). R2 asks why the standard deviation of EEGNet without HyperNet is 4–6 times larger on Dreyer (Table 1) than on other tables, and whether this would persist with different seeds/test subjects.
* **Limited scope of evaluation.** Only binary motor imagery classification is examined. R4 asks about plans to extend to multiple classes or other tasks and the associated challenges. R3 and R4 also ask why optimization of resting‑state representations (especially brain connectivity) was not explored, and what additional features might be important.
* **Lack of participant‑level analysis.** R4 highlights performance variations (e.g., Participant ID 3) and suggests comparing weights generated by HyperNet with those from a directly trained EEGNet to gain insights.

*Note: Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms, missing experiments, missing comparisons, or requests for ablation are included above as weaknesses.*
