## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper collects and releases a dataset of losses and downstream evaluations from 485 pretrained language models, then fits over 1,000 scaling laws to derive best practices for estimating scaling laws in new model families. The authors investigate extrapolation reliability, the use of intermediate checkpoints, the effect of model size choices, and whether scaling laws transfer across architectures.

### Strengths
- **Dataset release**: A large-scale dataset of losses and evaluations for 485 pretrained models is collected and publicly released. Multiple reviewers highlight this as a major community contribution (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
- **Systematic empirical study**: The paper provides a systematic investigation of scaling law estimation, leading to a number of implications (R2, R3). Best practices are derived, including that using intermediate checkpoints substantially improves accuracy, that extrapolation is most accurate from models of similar sizes, and that training multiple small models can be more useful than a single large one (R3, R5).
- **Timely and relevant topic**: The paper addresses a practical need in LLM pretraining (R3, R5). Clear takeaways at the end of each section help readers apply the findings (R5).
- **Transfer learning analysis**: The exploration of whether scaling law parameters can be transferred between families (Sec. 6) is noted as an interesting research question (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Practical usefulness questioned**: Scaling laws are most valuable for extrapolating to much larger compute budgets (e.g., Llama 3 used small runs up to 10²² FLOPs to guide a 3.8×10²⁵ FLOPs model). This paper only evaluates the largest models within each family, not extreme extrapolation from much smaller models. The approaches in Sections 5 and 6 may not be adopted by practitioners, and the paper itself shows they can be less accurate than fitting many small runs (R1). Additionally, experiments on larger/recent models like LLaMA 3 70B+ are missing (R5).
- **Learning rate schedule ignored (critical)**: The paper disregards that learning rate schedules make intermediate checkpoints uninformative, a point accepted by Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al., and Porian et al. The cited works (Hu et al., 2024 on WSD; Porian et al., 2024) do **not** support using intermediate checkpoints for fitting eq.1:
  * Hu et al. show that WSD produces a sharp loss drop during anneal, which eq.1 cannot model.
  * Porian et al. only used final losses to compare constant vs. cosine decay; they did not use intermediate checkpoints.
  Consequently, the ARE metric is ill-formed: it defines `max_{#toks}(F, 0.3)` to predict the last 30% of training, but if the learning rate decays in that last 30% (e.g., WSD), the earlier 70% gives no information about the anneal phase. Prediction becomes a curve fitting game with no meaningful extrapolation. All downstream analysis builds on ARE, so findings are questionable (R4).
- **ARE definition issues**: ARE is defined as the mean prediction error on the top 30% of tokens (by compute). This does not distinguish between partially trained models and converged models on a smaller subset, and implicitly assumes eq.1 can predict intermediate losses regardless of LR schedule. This is unsupported (R4).
- **Presentation and readability**:
  * Notation is confusing: e.g., `max_{#params}(F)` and `max_{#toks}(F, q)` are unintuitive; line 317 defines `\hat{L}(\cdot | F_{train})` as a function but should be the loss of the largest-compute model (R1).
  * Figures are hard to read: color bar and isoFLOP contours in tables are difficult to interpret (R1); Figures 6 and 7 in the appendix have overlapping numbers (R1, R5).
  * Organization: Figure 1 is referenced on page 3 but discussed only on page 9 (R1). Section 5.1’s connection between experimental setup, observed results, and conclusions is unclear; results are scattered across paper and appendix without clear signposting (R2).
  * Figure 2 appears before Figure 1 (R3).
  * Codebase is poorly documented, making it unready as a public benchmark (R1).
- **Lack of novelty**: The insights gained are not revolutionary or deep; the main contribution is the dataset (R3). The paper is entirely empirical with no theoretical analysis of why the rules of thumb hold (R3).
- **Limited scope**: The study focuses only on language models, but scaling laws apply to other modalities and compute-optimal scaling (barely mentioned except L514–518) (R3).
- **Missing methodological details**:
  * Why use squared error loss for fitting instead of Huber loss as in Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) for robustness to outliers? (R1)
  * No outlier removal or smoothing of intermediate losses before fitting, despite acknowledging spikes/increases (R1). Does the dataset include WSD scheduler runs? (R4)
  * Data collection process (types of public datasets, aggregation method) is not specified in detail (R5).
  * A summarization table to make implications actionable is missing (R5).
- **Minor errors**: L25: "differ scaling behavior" missing word; L87–88 definition of scaling law is contested; L420: "on all F provides on of the lowest ARE"; L526: "checkpoitns"; L436: redundant "future work"; intercept E is the best possible loss under the model class, not a "baseline" (R1, R3, R4).

### Reviewer Disagreement
One reviewer (R2) was highly positive (score 8, confidence 1) and found the study systematic and novel, with only readability as a weakness. Another reviewer (R4) was critical (score 3, confidence 4), arguing that the core metric ARE is ill-formed and the analysis ignores learning rate schedule, undermining the validity of all conclusions. R1 (score 3, confidence 4) also questioned practical usefulness and presentation. R3 and R5 (scores 6) acknowledged the dataset value but noted limited novelty and the need for more recent models.
