- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Prototypical Evolutionary Adaptation (PEA) for few-shot CLIP adaptation, using learnable shift vectors on visual prototypes to reduce intra-class variance. While the method is conceptually simple and computationally cheap, reviewers found the manuscript incomplete, the motivation unconvincing, the novelty limited, and the experimental results unreliable. Key weaknesses include missing ablations, insufficient comparisons (e.g., CoCoOp, MaPLe, synthetic-data baselines), poor presentation (undefined notations, unreferenced figures, contradictory claims), and results that deviate substantially from original papers (e.g., Tip-Adapter-F 16-shot ImageNet 57.81% vs 65.51% reported originally). One reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 1) strongly questioned the novelty and reproducibility, while three other reviewers (score 3) noted marginal improvements and lack of insight.

### Strengths

- The problem of fine-tuning vision-language models (e.g., CLIP) for downstream few-shot tasks is increasingly important and practically relevant.
- The proposed method is conceptually simple, computationally cheap, and easy to use, preserving pre-training knowledge.
- PEA achieved good generalization across 11 few-shot datasets and 4 ImageNet variants, outperforming several existing few-shot adaptation techniques.
- The structure of the paper is clear.

### Weaknesses

**1. Incomplete manuscript and poor presentation**

- The manuscript appears largely incomplete. An ablation study is mentioned in Line 393 but does not exist. No experiments validate the proposed components (e.g., removing the evolutionary step, testing different \(\alpha\) values). Several reviewers noted the absence of ablation studies.
- Figure 1 is unreferenced in the main text, and its purpose is unclear. Figure 2 lacks a proper caption and supporting experiments. Figure 3 does not showcase the high-performing models from Table 1. Table 2 has no accompanying description in the text.
- Equations (1), (2), (4), and (6) contain \(x\) on the left-hand side (\(P(y|x)\)) but never on the right-hand side. In Eq. (5), \(\Delta_c\) is not defined.
- Dashed lines in Table 1 are not explained.
- The text prompt used for experiments is not specified (e.g., “a photo of a []”).
- Typo in the title: “Evoluation” should be “Evolution”. Additional minor typos throughout.

**2. Unconvincing motivation**

- The motivation (Section 4.1) criticizes text prompt engineering and textual class prototypes, but PEA operates entirely on image features. The connection between the text-side issue and the proposed image-side solution is not established.
- The claim that LLM-based textual enrichment is ineffective is stated without supporting experiments, contradicting studies that report benefits (e.g., He et al. ICLR 2023, Zhang et al. CVPR 2023, Udandarao et al. ICCV 2023).
- It is not convincingly explained why dynamic prototype evolution is essential compared to static prototypes with effective regularization, or why existing few-shot methods for controlling variance are insufficient.
- The potential advantages of learnable shift vectors over traditional static prototypes are not analyzed.

**3. Limited novelty and lack of differentiation**

- One reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 1) strongly argued that the concept of learnable shift vectors has already been explored by TaskRes, TPE, Tip-Adapter, ProtoCLIP, and APE. While the authors note that TaskRes and TPE work in text space, other works (Tip-Adapter, ProtoCLIP, APE) also modulate visual features in VLM adaptation. Reviewer 4 concluded the paper lacks novelty.
- The main technical difference from TaskRes is applying the shift to image features instead of text features, but the difference from adapter-based methods (e.g., Tip-Adapter) is not clarified: how is directly updating image features different from learning a shift? Is Tip-Adapter set up to train only the first layer?
- The performance improvements are marginal, with some datasets showing lower results compared to baselines.

**4. Unreliable experimental results and insufficient comparisons**

- Reviewer 4 reported large discrepancies between results in this paper and those in the original papers for several baselines. Example: Tip-Adapter-F 16-shot ImageNet is 57.81% in Table 1 but 65.51% in its original paper – even lower than zero-shot CLIP. Similarly, APE, TaskRes, and CoOp are notably lower. This raises serious concerns about reproducibility and correctness of the reported baselines.
- Comparisons are missing for several state-of-the-art methods for CLIP few-shot adaptation: CoCoOp (Zhou et al. 2022b), MaPLe (Khattak et al. 2023). Also missing are synthetic‑data‑generation approaches (He et al. ICLR 2023, Zhang et al. CVPR 2023, Udandarao et al. ICCV 2023).
- For out‑of‑distribution (OOD) evaluation in Table 2, the listed methods are not designed for OOD tasks; comparisons with OOD-specific methods such as WiSE‑FT would be more appropriate.

**5. Inadequate explanation of the method**

- The core concept of a “biased prototype” is not explained. The parameter \(\alpha\) (Eq. 5) is said to reduce initial bias, but no analysis or ablation on \(\alpha\) is provided. Several reviewers requested a dedicated section with toy experiments to clarify what a biased prototype is and how PEA fixes it.
- The update mechanism of the learnable shift vector is not described. How is it initialized, updated, and regularized? The abstract criticizes cross-entropy loss for causing overfitting, yet the method likely uses cross-entropy on the few‑shot set – a contradiction that needs resolution.
- The rationale behind Eq. (6) is unclear. Why add the image class‑prototype to the text embedding? What is the mathematical justification? The “evolution” in Eq. (5) starts from a fixed prototype (average of training image embeddings), but how is iterative information obtained to perform the evolution? No theoretical results or alternative solutions are discussed.
- The method section contains too much content about prior works, making it hard to identify the novel contributions.

**6. Missing evidence for claimed benefits**

- The paper claims parameter efficiency (Line 85), but no experiments compare parameter usage.
- It claims regulation of intra‑class feature variance, but no evidence (visualization, quantitative analysis) is provided to demonstrate that PEA reduces variance or that variance reduction leads to performance gains.
- The effect of PEA across different types of distribution shifts is not analyzed.
- It is unclear whether PEA significantly increases computational cost compared to baselines.

**7. Unanswered reviewer questions (indicating missing investigations)**

- Is \(\alpha\) fixed across all datasets (as in CLAP)? How does varying \(\alpha\) affect generalization?
- Did the authors try different text prompts (e.g., “a photo of a []” vs. others) to test robustness of PEA to prompt variations?
- How were the baseline results in Tables 1 and 2 obtained? Are the low numbers due to incorrect reproduction? (Reviewer 4 explicitly questioned this.)
- Can visualizations be added to show the effectiveness of PEA?
