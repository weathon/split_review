## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces SleepSMC, a sleep staging framework that learns modality-invariant embeddings via supervised modality-level contrastive coordination and uncertainty-weighted feature weighting. It is designed for scenarios where multiple modalities (EEG, EOG, EMG) are available during training but only a single modality is present at test time. Uncertainty estimates, derived from variance in confidence scores across random masks, are used to adaptively weight auxiliary modalities during contrastive learning. Experiments on three public datasets (ISRUC‑S3, MASS‑SS3, Sleep‑EDF‑78) show state‑of‑the‑art performance in both multimodal and unimodal settings.

### Strengths
- **Practical relevance.** The problem setting—training with multiple modalities but testing with only one—addresses a realistic constraint for ubiquitous sleep monitoring (R1, R3).
- **Technically sound uncertainty reweighting.** The uncertainty‑based feature weighting is novel within sleep staging, is both empirically and theoretically supported, and consistently improves over non‑reweighted baselines, especially under noisy conditions (R1).
- **Effective contrastive learning.** Supervised modality‑level contrastive coordination captures category‑related consistency and complementarity across modalities, creating well‑separated, modality‑invariant embeddings (R1, t‑SNE visualizations).
- **Thorough evaluation.** The method is tested on three datasets, includes ablation studies showing individual and combined contributions of the two components, and demonstrates generalization across different primary modalities (EEG, EOG, EMG) (R1, R2, R4).
- **Clear presentation.** The paper is mostly well‑structured and easy to follow (R1, R3, R4). The methodology, experiments, and visualizations are clearly described (R1, R4).

**Minority opinion:** One reviewer (score 5) found the overall clarity good but questioned several technical details and the choice of single‑epoch paradigm; another reviewer (score 6) commented that the paper’s organization is good but presentation quality was rated lower (2/5) due to missing details and clarity issues in the method exposition.

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and motivation**
- The approach applies existing concepts (supervised contrastive learning, uncertainty weighting) to sleep staging without introducing new techniques. It is not clear how the method differs from prior supervised contrastive learning (Khosla et al., 2020) or why these specific designs are particularly suited to sleep staging compared to other multimodal coordination methods (R3, R4).
- The motivation for several design choices is insufficiently explained: (a) why uncertainty estimation with frozen gradients is used (R2); (b) why model confidence is chosen as the uncertainty metric over alternatives like entropy or loss (R4); (c) why dynamic tuning of weights over training was not explored (R4).
- The relationship to and differentiation from closely related recent works—CoRe‑Sleep (2024) and Multi‑Modal Sleep Stage Classification with Two‑Stream Encoder‑Decoder (2024)—is not clearly articulated (R2).

**Problem setting assumptions**
- The assumption that exactly one modality is available at test time is restrictive. In practice, different users may have varying sets of available modalities. The rationale for not allowing flexible modality sets is not justified (R4).
- If multiple auxiliary modalities are available, why would the model not simply use them all at test time? The paper does not discuss this scenario (implied by R4).

**Methodological clarity**
- The mathematical notation in Section 3 is disorganized. A table or list of symbols before the equations would improve readability (R1).
- Section 4 (“Detailed Analysis and Proofs”) is hard to follow. Formal definitions of key concepts (e.g., “margin,” “information transfer”), the complete proposition being proved, and the assumptions underlying the proof are missing (R3).
- The optimization of Equation (10) is not explained (R2). Computational complexity is not reported (R2).
- Minor: typo in Section 4.2 (“adaptively weighted” → “adaptively weights”); small font in Figures 3 and 4 (R4).

**Baselines and comparisons**
- The baselines in Table 2 are largely from before 2022 (e.g., SimCLR) and are outdated. Stronger and more recent sleep‑staging methods—SalientSleepNet, BSTT, XSleepNet—are not compared (R3). Missing‑modality specific baselines (e.g., Wang et al. 2023, Yao et al. 2024) and the Liu et al. works cited in Related Work are also omitted (R2, R4).
- The dataset choice ISRUC‑S3 (10 subjects) instead of the larger ISRUC‑S1 (100 subjects) weakens the evaluation; cross‑subject generalization on unseen subjects is not tested (R3). Real‑world deployment scenarios with dynamic shifts in modality reliability are not analyzed (R1).

**Experimental results and contributions**
- Reported gains are in the range of 2–3% (accuracy/F1). While statistically significant, these improvements are modest and their practical impact in sleep staging is not contextualized (R1, R3). The gains may be more meaningful in other multimodal problems, but that is not demonstrated (R1).
- The paper states that uncertainty‑based weighting has a greater impact in unimodal testing (Section 5.5), but the data suggest the primary improvement comes from supervised contrastive learning. Comparative improvement results (mean ± std) for each component are not provided, making this claim questionable (R4).
- Observed fluctuations in EEG and EOG uncertainty weights over training (Figure 3c) are not explained (R4).
- The method is not specifically tailored to sleep staging; it could be applied to any multimodal data with a categorical label, which raises the question of what distinguishes its contribution to sleep staging (R3, R4).
