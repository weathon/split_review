## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a CLIP-based OOD detection method that learns a set of negative prompts per class, complementing positive prompts, to capture dissimilarity in the feature space. It freezes CLIP encoders and uses contrastive and semantic orthogonality losses to learn the prompts. The method is evaluated on diverse OOD detection benchmarks.

### Strengths
- Clearly written with descriptive visual figures (R1, R4).
- Extensive experiments across diverse datasets, including small and large ones (R1, R2).
- High generality and low complexity: no finetuning of image or text encoder, only learns prompts (R2).
- Sufficient ablation studies demonstrating the effectiveness of each component (R3).
- Proposed idea is simple, intuitive, and easy to understand (R3, R4).
- Good performance over baselines (R3). One reviewer (R2) states that SOTA performance is achieved in different benchmark experiments, though other reviewers contest this (see weaknesses). This positive view comes from a reviewer who gave an overall score of 8, while the other three gave scores of 5–6.
- Motivation and weakness of hand-crafted prompts clearly interpreted (R4).
- Promising experimental results compared to existing OOD detection and prompt-based methods (R4).

### Weaknesses
- Missing comparisons to state-of-the-art methods: NNGuide, ASH, CLIPN (R1), and other 2022/2023 OOD detection methods (R4) are not discussed or compared.
- Performance claimed to be too far behind SOTA (R1) and performance improvement is very marginal (R1). This contrasts with R2’s assertion of SOTA results.
- Main concept is too similar to CLIPN (R1) and DualCoOp (R4). No visual or quantitative evidence verifies the claimed advantage over DualCoOp (R4). DualCoOp’s OOD detection performance is not experimentally verified (R4).
- Prompt learning approach is still based on CoOp without significant innovation (R2).
- Method is very dependent on CLIP features; if CLIP features are not strongly discriminative for some categories, the learned prompts may be poor (R2, also posed as a question).
- Question (overlaps with criticism): Learning negative prompts by minimizing activation for the own class and maximizing for others may cause the model to learn generic background features rather than general features that all other classes have (R2).
- Missing discussion of related work on negative learning/complementary labels (R3).
- Method may double training and inference time due to the use of negative prompts (R3).
- Missing discussion of a relevant work: “How Does Fine-Tuning Impact Out-of-Distribution Detection for Vision-Language Models?” (IJCV 2023) (R3).
- Meta-Net in Figure 6 is not introduced in the paper (R4).
- Questions that overlap with missing details or criticisms:
  - What is the ID dataset in Table 3? (R3)
  - Why do CoOp/CoCoOp and CoOp/CoCoOp+LSN achieve the same ID results in Tables 1 and 2? (R3)
  - ID results of CoOp/CoCoOp are significantly lower than NPOS in Table 2; please explain (R3).
  - In Equation (9), positive and negative scores are obtained independently; if the positive category differs from the negative category, could this mistakenly recognize a correct positive prediction as OOD? (R4)
- Minor issues: use `\citep` rather than `\cite` in LaTeX (R3); typo “we use we use” at the bottom of page 6 (R3); paper appears submitted with an ICLR 2023 template (R4).
