## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates whether end-to-end overall loss functions bias learning in decomposition-based time series forecasting by failing to prioritize critical sub-series (e.g., trend and seasonal components). The authors propose a hybrid loss framework that combines a global loss with component-specific losses, using a dual min‑max algorithm to dynamically weight them. The framework is integrated into DLinear, FEDformer, and PatchTST and evaluated on several datasets, reporting an average improvement of 0.5–2%. Two reviewers (Ratings 1, Confidence 5) are highly critical of the experimental design, significance of results, and generalizability; two others (Ratings 3) find the problem legitimate and the proposal interesting but note substantial theoretical and empirical gaps.

### Strengths

- Addresses a legitimate and underexplored question: whether overall loss functions can cause sub-series learning imbalances in decomposition-based forecasting.
- The dual min‑max optimization is a novel approach for dynamically weighting global and component errors.
- The paper is well‑written, well‑organized, and clear; the equations and framework are systematically presented.
- Experiments are extensive, covering multiple datasets and established models (DLinear, FEDformer, PatchTST). Ablation studies and detailed hyperparameter settings are provided, improving reproducibility.
- The investigation is creative and has potential to advance the state of the art.

### Weaknesses

- **Generalizability and dataset scope**  
  - Preliminary experiments (Figures 1–2, Table 1) use only ETTx datasets (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1), which are of the same nature (electricity transformer temperature). ETThi is an hourly aggregation of ETTmi, increasing the chance of similar behavior. Broader datasets (e.g., Monash Time Series Forecasting Archive) are needed.  
  - The number of datasets varies across tables (4 in Table 1, 8 in Table 2, 4 in Table 3) without justification. The new datasets in Table 2 are not discussed in Section 2.  
  - Only point forecasting is considered; probabilistic forecasting (which captures uncertainty) is more relevant in many applications.

- **Significance of improvements**  
  - Performance improvements are marginal, often at the third or fourth decimal place (e.g., 0.0888 vs. 0.0819; average 0.5–2%). The difference may be a rounding artifact or not practically meaningful.  
  - No statistical significance tests are reported. Experiments are run only once per time series; a sliding‑window approach with multiple runs and standard deviation is needed to rule out local behavior or seed dependence.  
  - For several datasets, the hybrid loss makes results worse, suggesting the benefit may be dataset‑dependent.

- **Preliminary experiments are inadequate to justify the problem**  
  - The claim that overall loss fails to optimize sub‑series is based on comparing loss values for a single sample (one channel) per dataset. Box plots of losses over all samples would be more informative.  
  - The observed discrepancy may be due to a non‑optimal moving‑average window size rather than the overall loss per se.  
  - In Figure 1(c) (FEDformer on ETTh1), the model performs worse on the trend sub‑series, contrary to the authors’ argument that overall loss always hurts one component. This counterexample weakens the motivation.

- **Visual claims are subjective and unsupported**  
  - The claimed “clear visual improvement” in Figure 2 is not convincing: y‑axis differences in Figure 2(a) distort comparison, and Figure 2(b) shows no visible change. A direct overlay or difference plot would be necessary.  
  - All visual results are shown only for three ETTx datasets; plots for all datasets and channels should be provided.

- **Theoretical analysis is missing**  
  - The connection between the two min‑max problems is not explained. No convergence guarantees, optimality conditions, or bounds on component‑wise performance are provided.  
  - The choice of mirror descent for weight updates is not justified over simpler optimization methods; its stability and convergence in this setting are unclear.  
  - It is not explained why the trade‑off between seasonal and trend components is generalized across all datasets, nor how the framework adapts to datasets with different sub‑series structures.

- **Missing comparisons and limited scope**  
  - Only decomposition models using the same sliding‑window mechanism (inherited from Autoformer) are tested. State‑of‑the‑art decomposition methods such as LD (Latent Decomposition; see arXiv:2402.12694, 2405.10877, 2308.13386) are omitted.  
  - Non‑decomposition baselines are not included, so the framework’s versatility beyond decomposition‑based models is unverified.

- **Computational overhead**  
  - The dual min‑max optimization introduces extra computational cost, but no analysis of efficiency, runtime, or scalability compared to the original end‑to‑end loss is provided.

- **Simpler alternatives not ruled out**  
  - The paper tests only a fixed weight of 0.5, which is poor given scale imbalance. A fixed 1/3 weighting for global + two components should be ablated to isolate the benefit of the dynamic dual min‑max approach.  
  - The explicit definition of both \(w_2\) and \(\beta\) is redundant (could be \(1-w_1\) and \(1-\alpha\)), and the code does not enforce \(w_1+w_2=1\) or \(\alpha+\beta=1\).

- **Sensitivity and ablation gaps**  
  - Impact of the hyperparameter \(\lambda\) is not studied.  
  - No analysis of how performance varies with different initializations of \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \alpha, \beta\); recommended initial values are not stated.

- **Terminology and conceptual clarity**  
  - The moving‑average component is called “seasonal” and the residual “trend,” but the residual often contains seasonal or noise patterns and should be termed “remainder” or “residual” (following Autoformer literature).  
  - The distinction between “loss” and “metric” is unclear in Table 2.  
  - The paper poses the question “Does overall loss guarantee sub‑series learning?” but does not convincingly answer it.  
  - The claim that a result summary qualifies as a “contribution” in the introduction is misplaced.

- **Code and reproducibility**  
  - The provided code is not usable; it contains typos (lambda) and does not reflect the exact setup used for the reported results.

- **Proofreading issues**  
  - Grammar: “a overall” → “an overall”; “employe” → “employ”; “attends to” is unclear; missing spaces before citations.  
  - Model names are inconsistent (FEDformer/Fedformer, PatchTST/Patchtst).  
  - “Componet” in Table 4; opening quotation marks should be ` `` `.

- **Minor missing details**  
  - For ETTx multivariate forecasting, the channel shown in plots (likely oil temperature) should be specified.  
  - Evaluation metrics (MAE, MSE) are not justified; DTW or MAPE might be more appropriate.  
  - The conclusion merely summarizes rather than drawing specific inferences about the original hypothesis.  
  - The paper does not examine causal relationships between component errors and overall performance, nor the influence of stochastic components on the “poor” result in Figure 1(c).  
  - No analysis of how component magnitude affects observed errors (e.g., cases with very slight trend or weak seasonality).
