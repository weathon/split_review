## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies low-level visual perception in MLLMs for geometric understanding. It introduces the Geoperception benchmark (filtered from Geometry-3K) and evaluates leading MLLMs, finding them deficient. The authors then train Euclid, a model using synthetic data generated from modified AlphaGeometry tooling with specific architectural and training choices (data curriculum). Euclid shows strong performance, but the reviews raise concerns about benchmark construction, evaluation methodology, training data scale, missing baselines, overclaiming, and shallow ablations.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and open topic: MLLMs’ application to geometric problems. Comparisons of prior state-of-the-art MLLMs and their documented failures underscore the paper’s relevance (R1).
- Release of the filtered Geoperception benchmark will support future work on geometric perception (R1, R3, R4).
- The proposed model achieves substantial performance gains: a ~3× boost on “PointsonLines” tasks and roughly +15% average improvement over Gemini-1.5-Pro on certain tasks; Euclid shows strong generalization to novel geometric shapes in real-world tasks (R1, R2).
- Paper writing is clear, well-structured, and easy to follow (R1, R2, R4).
- Extensive experiments systematically explore the impact of geometric shapes of increasing complexity, identifying key lessons about training strategies (R2).
- The evaluation of visual tokenizers (CNN vs. ViT) provides interesting and useful observations (R4).
- Euclid’s training on high-fidelity synthetic data yields strong generalization, particularly on real-world geometry tasks (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Data filtering circularity (R2):** Geoperception uses gpt-4o-mini for benchmark filtering. Because that model itself has limited geometric understanding, it may inadvertently filter out valid but complex examples, biasing the benchmark’s difficulty and scope.
- **Performance likely dominated by syntax rather than reasoning (R4, partially R1):** Models such as GPT-4o-mini and Molmo often enumerate all components or answers, leading to poor accuracy under the current metric. The paper does not report accuracy when a prediction is a superset of the ground truth (e.g., score=1 if Pred⊆GT), which could reveal whether failures are perceptual or formatting-related (R1). Some benchmark questions are ungrammatical, ambiguous, or inconsistently labeled (e.g., alphabetization of segment ends, duplicate answers like “ZV, VZ”), undermining the claim that failures are purely visual (R4). Clarification needed on how multiple solutions are sampled and whether nucleus sampling is used (R1).
- **Training dataset extremely small (R1):** The dataset is described as composed of “only 3 shapes” per concept. Clarification is needed: does this mean 3 distinct geometric configurations or 3 instances with different values? Such limited diversity may hinder generalization to unseen configurations; results and conclusions about backbone architectures and curricula may not scale (R1). With infinite synthetic data available, the need for a curriculum is questionable — mixed-shape training with 150% of the samples matches adaptive curriculum performance (Figure 6, R4).
- **Missing baselines (R1, R3, R4):** No supervised fine-tuning of existing MLLMs on Geoperception is provided. Such a baseline would isolate whether gains come from the novel training approach or simply from task-specific fine-tuning. Comparisons with geometry-specific or math-focused MLLMs (e.g., those tuned on Geometry-3K) are also absent (R3). VDLM (SVG code reasoning) is noted as “consistently more effective” in related work but is not evaluated as a baseline on Geoperception (R3, R4).
- **Shallow architecture/strategy ablation (R4):** Only the visual encoder is ablated; no investigation explains why CNN outperforms ViT. Tuning the visual encoder is expected when evaluating on the training distribution; generalization on the evaluation set should be the primary measure. The “lessons” are presented without deeper analysis (R4). Negative-hard mining could automate curriculum design and should be compared against the human-designed curriculum (R1).
- **Overclaimed results (R4):** The statement that Euclid “significantly outperforms current leading MLLMs” is not fully accurate. It is outclassed by Gemini-1.5-Pro on one of four tasks (e.g., Point-On-Circle (POC) or Line-Has-Circle (LHC), R2, R4) and exceeds it by less than 1% on another. The overall quantitative edge drops from 14.29% to 4.81% when all benchmark tasks are considered. Claims must be properly qualified. Additionally, the assertion that “lessons generalize to broader domains” is not demonstrated.
- **Limited comprehensive geometric understanding (R2):** Euclid shows poor performance on POC and LHC, suggesting overfitting to specific geometric relationships in the training data and lacking a generalized understanding of geometric principles.
- **Lack of human quantitative baseline (R4):** The task is claimed to be “straightforward for humans” but no human accuracy or comparison is provided.
- **Insufficient detail on proposed approach (R3):** Too much space is devoted to background and other methods, with relatively few details on Euclid’s own design; one reviewer considered the amount of work below ICLR average. Ablations are shallow (R4).
- **Methodological questions and missing experiments (R1, R3, R4):**
  - Why are only 4 of 7 benchmark tasks used for Euclid evaluation in Table 4? (R4).
  - Why was a multiple-choice format not considered for the VQA task? (R3).
  - Will perception ability transfer to real images or medical imaging? (R3).
  - Clarification on “3 shapes” and the effective training dataset size is needed (R1).

- **Minority reviewer contrast:** One reviewer (overall rating 3) was considerably more critical than others (ratings 5, 6, 6). This reviewer argued that performance is dominated by syntax issues, the task is far removed from real-world applications, ablations are shallow, claims are overstated, and the curriculum is irrelevant given infinite synthetic data. This perspective should be weighed alongside the more positive assessments.
