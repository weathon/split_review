- Scores: 5, 3, 6, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
All four reviewers describe a study investigating whether Large Language Models (GPT-4, Gemini Pro, Llama 3.1 8B/70B, Gemma 2 9B/27B) exhibit value structures comparable to humans, using the Portrait Value Questionnaire-Revised (PVQ-RR) grounded in Schwartz’s Theory of Basic Human Values. Under standard prompting, LLM responses deviate from human rankings; however, a proposed “Value Anchor” prompting strategy (e.g., “protecting the natural environment from destruction or pollution”) yields high agreement with both the hierarchical ranking of human values and the circular correlation structure between values (the Schwartz model). The paper evaluates first-order statistics (value ranking) and second-order statistics (value correlations) across multiple prompting types (basic, demographic, Value Anchor).

### Strengths
- **Grounded in established psychological theory.** Multiple reviewers note the paper builds on Schwartz’s well-validated framework (PVQ-RR), giving the analysis a secure theoretical foundation (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).  
- **Novel prompting strategy (Value Anchor).** Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 agree the Value Anchor prompt is an interesting, novel method for eliciting human-like value patterns in LLMs, producing results that align with human rankings and correlations.  
- **Evaluation of both first-order and second-order statistics.** The paper’s dual focus on value rankings (Spearman correlation) and value correlations (circular model matching) adds measurable depth and precision (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).  
- **Comparative analysis across several prompting strategies.** Reviewers 2 and 4 highlight that testing basic, demographic, and Value Anchor prompts strengthens the study by showing that prompt choice significantly affects LLM output and its coherence with human values.  
- **Clear presentation (noted by two reviewers).** Reviewer 2 states “clear figures and concise writing”; Reviewer 3 finds the paper “well-written and easy to follow.” (Note: Reviewer 1 strongly disagrees on clarity—see Weaknesses.)  
- **Timely and important issue.** Reviewer 3 notes the topic of LLM alignment with human values is timely and important.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation and messy presentation (one reviewer, strong critique).** Reviewer 1 finds the paper “written in an unclear and messy manner,” with difficult-to-understand motivations for experimental choices (e.g., varying prompting strategies) and arbitrary elements (e.g., including a sine function in Figure 4). In direct contrast, Reviewers 2 and 3 consider the writing clear.  
- **Circularity in Table 1 reasoning.** Reviewer 1 argues that Table 1 finds that “personas based on different types of human values result in model outputs that best fit different human value judgements,” likening this to “water is water, and if we try to pretend some other non-water substance is water, it is not as water-like.” The reviewer suggests the authors consult measurement modeling literature.  
- **Insufficient differentiation from existing work on values in LLMs.** Reviewers 1 and 4 agree the paper downplays prior research. Reviewer 1 lists Social Chemistry 101 (Forbes et al. 2020), Argyle et al. 2022, Durmus et al. 2023, and Ma et al. 2024, and also points to recent persona-based works (Angelina Wang 2024, Mingqian Zheng 2024). Reviewer 4 notes that several 2024 papers (Kovač-2024, Lee-2024, Wang-2024, Scherrer-2023) explore similar themes of value consistency via role-playing or moral beliefs, and that the paper fails to explain how its approach advances the conversation beyond replication. Reviewer 4 specifically mentions that Scherrer-2023 also deals with prompts but “only deals with prompt format without a separate discussion of value anchoring,” but still urges clearer differentiation.  
- **No demonstration of downstream utility.** Reviewers 1 and 2 both note the paper does not show how the Value Anchor approach contributes to a practical task—e.g., replicating known findings, pretesting novel hypotheses (Reviewer 1 suggests Park et al.’s social simulacra paper as a model). Reviewer 2 adds: “the practical uses and the influences on how to build better LLMs remain a little unclear.”  
- **Limited methodological novelty for an ICLR audience.** Reviewer 1 states: “prompting large language models with an established survey and reporting what they output is not very methodologically interesting for an ICLR audience,” though they acknowledge the paper might have misunderstood strengths due to presentation issues.  
- **Bias in groundtruth human data.** Reviewer 2 points out that the human participants (mean age 34.2, 59% female; line 245-247) may not cover the full demographic spectrum (e.g., children, elderly). The reviewer suggests that LLMs mimicking this population may just reflect training data dominated by young adults, not universal value resemblance.  
- **Missing exploration of non-human value coherence.** Reviewer 4 argues the paper “leans heavily on the idea that LLMs should be compared to human value systems as the benchmark,” missing the opportunity to explore whether LLMs could develop distinct, equally valid patterns of consistency that differ from human psychology.  
- **No analysis of prompt variation stability.** Reviewer 3 requests testing whether “minor variations in the same prompt could elicit the same results,” and notes that showing how the findings hold up with different wording would strengthen the paper.  
- **No extension to open-ended generation tasks.** Reviewer 3 suggests that showing value rankings and correlation structures in generation tasks (not just classification) would make the findings “feel even more real and convincing.”  
- **Absence of statistical significance (p-values).** Reviewer 3 notes that Spearman’s rank correlation is used without reporting p-values, which could add rigor.  
- **Potential for faking and misrepresentation not discussed.** Reviewer 2 asks: “What if an LLM misrepresents itself as a person holding positive values, but instigates people to hurt themselves?”—this limitation is not addressed in the paper.  
- **Overly strong claim of novelty in abstract/introduction.** Reviewer 1 notes the statement “little research has been done to study the values exhibited in text generated by LLMs” is inaccurate given extensive related work in the past five years.  
- **Unanswered questions overlapping with criticisms:**  
  - *Value consistency over time within an individual* – Reviewer 2 asks if core value sets change over time, would an LLM resemble that change? (not addressed).  
  - *Interpretability* – Reviewer 2 asks *why* LLMs mimic human values with Value Anchor prompts (not explained).  
  - *Extreme demographics* – Reviewer 2 asks if the method would find resemblances to a group of criminals (no discussion).  
  - *Effect on linguistic tasks* – Reviewer 2 asks how value understanding would affect counterfactual reasoning or harmful-query refusal (not explored).  
  - *Derivation of 3 question variants* – Reviewer 2 asks if these are LLM-generated paraphrases (not clarified).  
  - *Specific result interpretations* – Reviewer 2 asks why GPT-4/Basic showed low correlation (Fig 2a) and what Gemini-pro’s SES proximity to red points suggests (Fig 3a,b) (unaddressed).  
  - *Main contributions* – Reviewer 3 asks what the authors consider to be the paper’s main contributions (the paper states them but the reviewer found it unclear).  
  - *Explanation of value correlation emergence* – Reviewer 1 asks for clarification of the explanation in lines 99-101 regarding the Schwartz circular model (not clearly pinpointed in the paper).  
- **Typo.** Reviewer 1 notes “Llamma” should be “Llama” (line 402).
