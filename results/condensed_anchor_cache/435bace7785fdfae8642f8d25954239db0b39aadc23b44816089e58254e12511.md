## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces the concept of walk profiles for directed graphs (generalized walk-counting sequences) and proposes Multi-q Magnetic Laplacian Positional Encodings (Multi-q Mag-PE), an extension of single‑q Mag-PE that concatenates eigenvectors from Magnetic Laplacians with multiple phase‑shift parameters \(q\). The authors prove that with \(Q = \lceil L/2 \rceil + 1\) distinct \(q\) values, the encoding can provably reconstruct walk profiles of length \(L\). They also adapt prior basis-invariant neural networks (SPE) for stable use in the complex domain. Experiments on synthetic tasks (node‑pair distance prediction, classification of synthetic graphs) and real‑world circuit benchmarks show consistent but sometimes modest gains over baselines such as Laplacian-PE, SVD‑PE, and standard Mag‑PE. Theoretical claims are well‑supported, but several reviewers note gaps between theory and practice, limited gains on real data, and concerns about computational cost.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation and organization:** The paper is exemplary in structure, with clean definitions and a logical flow from walk profiles to the proposed PE (R1, R2, R3). The walk‑profile concept is well motivated and illustrated, making it easy to understand (R1, R2).

- **Provable connection between Multi‑q Mag‑PE and walk profiles:** Theorem 4.2 provides a rigorous guarantee that the encoding can recover walk profiles up to a certain length, and the proof sketch is sound (R1, R2). This connects a latent representation to a tangible structural descriptor (R1).

- **Strong theoretical grounding:** The definition of walk profiles and the proof that Multi‑q Mag‑PE can express them are well‑reasoned; all prerequisites are presented clearly (R2). The analysis of stability via SPE is also a valuable contribution (R2, R5).

- **Extensive experimental evaluation:** The paper includes synthetic tasks (walk‑profile prediction, structural distances), ablation studies (choice of \(q\), number of \(q\) values), robustness analysis, and real‑world circuit benchmarks (R2). Ablation results clearly show that multiple \(q\) values are necessary and that single‑\(q\) Mag‑PE is insufficient (R2).

- **Both MPNN and transformer backbones considered:** The approach is tested with simple GCN‑like models as well as the SAT transformer, demonstrating generality (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Computational cost and scalability:** Runtime overhead is quantified as up to 3× (R1) and grows linearly with the number of \(q\) values (R3). Multiple eigendecompositions are required, raising scalability concerns for graphs with millions of nodes (R4). The paper lacks a detailed complexity analysis as a function of \(q\) and graph size (R4). Reducing \(q\) is a possibility but not systematically explored as a trade‑off (R4). (R1, R3, R4)

- **Theory–practice gap in walk‑profile reconstruction:** Although Multi‑q Mag‑PE should theoretically allow near‑perfect reconstruction, actual RMSE on the prediction task is far from zero. The optimization landscape for learning the mapping is likely non‑convex, but this is not analyzed (R4). The paper acknowledges this limitation but does not investigate why the practical performance is lower (R4).

- **Insufficient theoretical motivation for walk profiles:** No rigorous justification is given for why walk profiles are the “right” descriptor for directed graphs; they are not connected to established expressivity measures such as \(k\)-WL (R3). The heuristic definition lacks theoretical properties that would justify its superiority over other possible encodings (R4). (R3, R4)

- **Marginal improvements on real‑world tasks:** Gains on real‑world circuit benchmarks are often small and hard to discern in Table 3 (R5, R3). The difference between synthetic (large gains) and real (modest) is not sufficiently analyzed; reasons (e.g., dataset structure, task complexity) are not discussed (R4). R3 notes that the biggest gains occur on simple synthetic models, and when a stronger model (SAT) is used, gains are much less evident.

- **Missing comparisons and baselines:** Several reviewers note that relevant baselines or datasets are omitted: the DAG transformer for DAG data (R5), OGBG-code, NA and BN datasets used in prior work (R2), and a wider range of GNN/transformer backbones (R4). It is also not discussed whether other PEs (e.g., spectral PEs for directed graphs) also fail to capture walk profiles (R3). (R2, R3, R4, R5)

- **Insufficient explanations in the paper:**  
  - The argument that SVD‑PE cannot capture bidirectional walks is insufficiently detailed; the need for a third node \(w\) in the discussion is not well motivated (R1).  
  - The derivation of Equation (2) (phase accumulation along paths) is given as “by definition” without adequate justification (R1).  
  - The observed robustness to the choice of \(q\) lacks a deeper theoretical explanation; the linear system argument is a start but does not explain why random \(q\) values sometimes outperform tuned ones (R1). Tuning the *range* of \(q\) (analogous to tuning a single \(q\)) requires further discussion (R1).  
  - The role of \(m\) in Equations (3) and (4) is unclear (R1). (Minor formatting issues with braces noted.) (R1)

- **Presentation issues:** Table 3 is hard to parse and differences often lie within standard deviations (R5). The paper is densely written (R5). Some reviewers (R3) rate presentation lower (2) due to these issues.

- **Minority critical viewpoints:** R3 gives the lowest scores and questions whether the paper answers its title question—it proposes a specific PE but does not give a general characterisation of “good” positional encodings for directed graphs. R3 also doubts whether walk profiles are the right measure; graph transformers might learn such structure from other PEs. R5 notes the paper does not compare with a recent directed‑graph benchmark (e.g., from [2] referenced in the review). R4 highlights the theory–practice gap most strongly. (R3, R4, R5)
