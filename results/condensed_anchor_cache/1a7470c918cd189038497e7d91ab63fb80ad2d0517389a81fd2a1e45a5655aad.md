## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates when and why Visual Prompt Tuning (VPT) outperforms full finetuning (FT). Through experiments on 19 datasets, the authors find VPT is favored when (1) task objectives between pre-trained and downstream tasks differ substantially (e.g., classification→counting) or (2) data distributions are similar (e.g., both natural images). The performance gap narrows as training data increases. The authors argue VPT’s success is not solely attributable to reduced overfitting or optimization benefits, but rather to its unique preservation of original features alongside added parameters.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely topic** – VPT is widely used, yet the “when and why” problem is under-explored. The paper provides valuable, sensible insights.
- **Extensive experiments** – Thorough analysis across 19 diverse datasets and tasks; results are clearly presented with error bars and are easy to follow.
- **Well-written and organized** – Clear high-level motivation and intuition; smooth logic flow; full implementation will be released.
- **Interesting core idea** – Explaining VPT via the distinct roles of added prompt parameters and frozen pretrained parameters is both novel and inspiring.
- **Convincing specific analyses** – The investigation into downstream data scale (Sec. 4.4) and the overfitting hypothesis (Sec. 5.1) are particularly strong; conclusions appear credible.
- **Hypotheses in Sec. 4.3 and 5.2** – Though they could be supported more (see Weaknesses), the hypotheses themselves are intriguing and contribute to the community.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited model scope** – Experiments only cover Transformer-based models (ViT, Swin). It remains unclear whether findings extend to CNNs, VPT-shallow, or prompt tuning in language models.  
  *[Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4]*
- **Insufficient evidence for key hypotheses** –  
  - **Sec. 4.3**: Drawing conclusions from only 19 data points is risky; more tasks (e.g., detection, dense prediction) are needed.  
  - **Sec. 5.2**: Mixed and FT-then-PT still clearly underperform VPT (Fig. 7), so the experiments do not convincingly support the optimization hypothesis; the resulting conclusion is ambiguous.  
  *[Reviewer 2]*
- **GradCAM visualization (Sec. 5.4) is uninformative** – The distinction between VPT and FT is not large; it appears more like a calibration difference. Hard to draw convincing conclusions. Also, the examples only show natural image classification; they should be compared across all four transfer-learning quadrants to connect to Sec. 4.  
  *[Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2]*
- **Alternative attention analysis not performed** – Only GradCAM is used. The authors should also visualize attention from the [CLS] token to other tokens, as in previous ViT work. Related prior work [2] linking transfer learning and attention (noisy attention, fixing attention helps) should be discussed.  
  *[Reviewer 1]*
- **Missing generalization to other PEFT methods** – The paper focuses on VPT but does not check whether the same conclusions hold for, e.g., LoRA, which can outperform VPT on VTAB-1k.  
  *[Reviewer 1]*
- **Overstatements and unclear statements** –  
  - Sec. 5.2: Claiming VPT “escapes from local minima or saddle points” is a misuse of terms; escaping local minima is neither necessary nor sufficient for generalization; the statement should be modified.  
  - Sec. 4 final paragraph: “prompt tuning is more effective than full finetuning when only limited finetuning examples are available” conflicts with the Patch Camelyon result (green curves) in Fig. 4.  
  - Fig. 4 curves are not monotonic and behavior depends heavily on dataset; more results are needed before the conclusion.  
  - Fig. 5 should use log scales; all figures should be vector images.  
  *[Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4]*
- **Remaining unexplained scenarios** – The reasons why VPT outperforms FT under distinct data distributions and high task disparity, and why FT outperforms VPT under similar tasks with varying data distributions, remain unclear despite hypotheses in Sec. 5.3. The authors are asked to suggest ways to objectively validate those hypotheses.  
  *[Reviewer 3]*
- **Organizational issues and missing details** –  
  - Mixed and FT-then-PT are introduced in Tab. 1 without explanation (Sec. 4.1); results for these methods are shown before they are justified. They should be moved to Sec. 5.2 with proper introduction.  
  - The “number of wins” notation appears in Tabs. 1–2 before it is defined (Tab. 3). Also, brackets `[]` conflate this notation with references; use parentheses.  
  - Prompt size: Is it a tuned hyperparameter? The exact number of prompts per dataset must be reported to assess learning capacity.  
  - The use of VPT-deep should be justified; does VPT-shallow show the same patterns?  
  *[Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4]*
- **One-shot experiment issues** –  
  - VPT only outperforms FT in half of VTAB-1k Structured datasets (Tab. 4), differing from other sub-categories; this should be highlighted and discussed in Sec. 4.4.  
  - In one-shot classification (Tab. 3), how were single training samples chosen? Performance is likely highly sensitive to that choice.  
  *[Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4]*
- **Data scale observation is not novel** – The finding that the performance gap narrows with more data was already reported in the original VPT paper (Page 8, Fig. 3).  
  *[Reviewer 2]*
- **Randomness in Fig. 4** – The broken yellow curve in the left panel fluctuates intensely in the small-set region, possibly due to training set sampling randomness.  
  *[Reviewer 3]*
