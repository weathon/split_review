## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MAGDiff, a new family of representations for covariate shift detection. MAGDiff is extracted by comparing the activation graphs of a pre-trained neural network classifier for samples from the training and target distributions. These representations are used with class-conditional two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests. Experiments on MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and Imagenette show that MAGDiff outperforms the baseline Black Box Shift Detection (BBSD, Lipton et al., 2018), which relies only on output logits. The method assumes access to the training dataset and a well-performing classifier with dense layers.

### Strengths
- The idea of using activation graphs (comparing internal neural network representations) for covariate shift detection is novel and distinct from the BBSD baseline that only uses context/confidence vectors. (R1, R3, R4; R2 notes this as the main contribution but finds the overall novelty limited compared to BBSD.)
- The proposed method outperforms the state-of-the-art BBSD baseline on several datasets and shift types, demonstrating potential for improving reliability of deployed neural networks. (R1, R3, R4)
- The paper is well-written and clearly explains the problem, the proposed method (Section 3 is accessible), and the experimental setup. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Experiments systematically evaluate performance across datasets, shift types, and intensities, and the limitations are thoroughly discussed. (R3)
- Code is provided for reproducibility. (R3)
- One reviewer (R3) notes that the empirical results are compelling even if the shifts may not fully reflect real-world complexity.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited datasets and architectures:** Experiments are restricted to MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR-10, and Imagenette; more complex or realistic datasets are needed to validate claims. (R1, R3) A reviewer (R2) adds that the layer study should have been the main focus but is minimal and appears like an afterthought.
- **Only one baseline (BBSD from 2018) is compared; several newer or alternative methods are missing.** Examples: Kirchler et al. (2020) “Two-sample testing using deep learning” (R4); also other recent shift detection techniques mentioned in the paper but not used as baselines (R1). The paper should include a trivial variant using hidden activations directly (i.e., setting T(x)=x in Section 4.1) to gauge the effect of the activation-graph linear map. (R2)
- **No experiments on label-shift** (the paper mentions no gain over BBSD for label-shift but does not show these results). (R1)
- **Unclear applicability to common architectures:** The method is only demonstrated on simple fully-connected networks; it does not address how activation graphs would be constructed for CNNs, RNNs, Transformers, or networks with residual connections, limiting practical relevance. (R1, R3) For ConvNets, the method would need extension to convolutional layers. (R3)
- **No study on combining representations across multiple layers** (e.g., concatenating activations) to see if test power improves. (R2, R3)
- **No ablation studies to isolate key components:** e.g., using activations instead of activation graphs, or using a random linear projection instead of the trained weight matrix W. (R2)
- **No justification for the choice of norm** used in computing test statistics; at least two norms should be compared in the appendix. (R1)
- **Questions about scaling and practical usage:**
  - How does the method handle very large numbers of classes? (R1)
  - How does severe class imbalance affect performance? (R1)
  - What is the computational overhead for larger networks? (R1)
  - How does the method generalize to real-time detection? (R1)
  - The method requires access to the original training dataset (CS) for deployment—a practical challenge. (R1)
- **Theoretical motivation is lacking** (R3, but considered less important).
- **Ambiguities and missing details:**
  - The optimal layer choice is unclear; the text says “a chosen dense layer” but experiments likely use only the last dense layer (l_{-1}). (R2) The study of different layers should be expanded with more models and layers. (R2)
  - What constitutes “reasonable accuracy” for the classifier, and does the method break for low accuracy? (R1) Test with low-performing models.
  - The definition of the mapping from activation graphs to representation (e.g., supp P0 ∪ supp P1 → R^N) needs clearer explanation; N is not defined until Section 4.1. (R1)
  - Notation inconsistency: “i” used for both feature and class indexing. (R1)
  - Equations lack numbering. (R2)
  - Citation style is incorrect in places (parentheses missing when reference is not part of the sentence). (R2)
- **Minority reviewer (R2) is more critical:** This reviewer finds the original results fairly limited, says key aspects are deferred to future work, and argues that the study of different layers should have been the paper’s main focus. R2 also suggests checking whether a model trained on one dataset (e.g., MNIST) can be used to detect shift in another (e.g., FMNIST). (R2)
- **Questions that overlap with weaknesses** (extracted from reviewer lists):
  - How does the method handle different architectures/activation functions? (R1)
  - How robust is it to noise or irregularities in activation graphs? (R1)
  - Are there scenarios where MAGDiff is not the ideal choice? (R1)
  - Could the method be extended outside supervised settings (e.g., autoregressive tasks)? (R3)
