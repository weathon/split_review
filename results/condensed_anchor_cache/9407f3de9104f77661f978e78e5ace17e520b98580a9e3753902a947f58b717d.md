## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SimDT, a decision transformer for autonomous driving that combines multi-token prediction, an online imitative learning pipeline (transition from offline to online RL), and prioritized experience replay to address distribution shift from open-loop to closed-loop driving. It is evaluated on the Waymax benchmark using the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, with experiments in both open-loop and closed-loop settings.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important problem of generalizing learned driving policies from open-loop training to closed-loop simulation, a key challenge in autonomous driving.
- Experiments are conducted on the real-world Waymo dataset in a closed-loop simulation environment (Waymax), providing practical relevance.
- Ablation studies demonstrate the necessity of individual components: multi-token decision transformer, imitative RL pipeline, and prioritized experience replay.
- The introduction of multi-token prediction in a decision transformer framework is considered interesting and may improve real-timeness of the algorithm.
- The paper is well-written, with clear figures and easy-to-follow explanation of the method.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty**: Multi-token prediction is standard in driving simulation agents (MotionLM, KiGRAS, SMART, Trajeglish) and resembles action chunking (recent robotics work). Prioritized experience replay (PER) is nearly 10 years old. The combination of offline-to-online DT is a straightforward extension. The paper provides little insight into why the components work together beyond incremental score gains. One reviewer found the method “not novel at all” and “putting a bunch of existing practices together” without extensive study. Another reviewer noted that combating distribution shift via this combination is unclear.

- **Outdated and insufficient baselines**: Comparisons are limited to DQN (2013) and BC (2009), which are very old. Many recent RL algorithms (Rainbow DQN, TD3BC, CQL, AWAC) and transformer-based approaches (Point Transformer V3, MotionTransformer) are missing. The paper should benchmark against methods from the 2024 Waymo Open Sim Agents Challenge (WOSAC) leaderboard, which contains state-of-the-art closed-loop models. Additionally, a BC method with a properly designed network or data augmentation (e.g., ChauffeurNet) might achieve strong results more simply; this comparison is absent.

- **Unsupported performance claims in abstract**: The abstract states a 45.2% reduction in off-road and collision rates over DQN and 41% over BC, but Table 1 shows much more modest improvements: ~0.2% off-road rate vs DQN and ~2% collision rate vs BC. These claims overstate SimDT’s effectiveness and mislead readers.

- **Reward engineering confound**: The low collision rate may simply come from the high penalty assigned to collision (\(R_{overlap} = -10\) in Eq. 5). No experiment or discussion disentangles whether the improvement is due to algorithm robustness or reward design. This casts doubt on the claimed safety benefits.

- **Misinterpretation of route progress metric**: SimDT achieves a route progress ratio of 105.63%. The authors claim this demonstrates “more efficient routes,” but according to the Waymax paper (Section 3.4), a ratio above 100% may indicate that the vehicle overshot the destination (e.g., driving faster than the logged trajectory) or took a longer path, not necessarily a more optimal route. This interpretation is misleading.

- **Misleading comparison to expert performance**: The authors claim SimDT’s safety metrics are “within the same magnitude” as expert demonstrations. However, expert collision rate is 0.67% and off-road rate 0.41%, compared to SimDT’s 2.69% and 3.52% – differences that are significant for real-world safety. This downplays the gap.

- **Missing evidence for sample-efficiency claim**: The title and paper describe the method as “sample-efficient,” but no details are given on training dataset size, number of RL rollouts, training FLOPs, or computational resources. A controlled study with fixed data or compute is needed to justify this claim.

- **Method details insufficiently justified and poorly explained**:
  - The switch from offline to online learning at \(0.5 \times \text{num scenarios}\) appears heuristic; no motivation is provided.
  - The reward weights (\(-2\) for off-road, \(-10\) for overlap) are chosen without justification or sensitivity analysis.
  - The role of the imitation reward \(R_{imitation}\) is unclear; it is not mentioned in Algorithm 1.
  - Loss functions \(\mathcal{L}_a\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{ma}\) are introduced but not explicitly linked to the training procedure.
  - Variables \(a, s, g, \pi\) are not clearly defined as scalars, vectors, matrices, or function mappings.
  - The claim that the imitative learning pipeline yields a “greater data-distributed policy” lacks a rigorous explanation.

- **Missing evaluation details**: Tables 1 and 2 do not specify the number of scenes used for training and evaluation, nor the percentage of the dataset covered. This makes results hard to interpret and reproduce.

- **Unsupported claim on safety–ADE trade-off**: The statement that SimDT’s higher Average Displacement Error (ADE) is due to a “cautious driving style” focusing on safety and kinematic feasibility lacks empirical support. No analysis links ADE to collision-avoidance behavior.

- **Out-of-distribution (OOD) concerns remain**: The fine-tuning pipeline uses model rollouts in simulation, which may still suffer from simulation-to-real domain gap when deployed in real-world driving. The paper does not discuss this limitation or compare against simpler OOD mitigation strategies (e.g., data augmentation).

- **Performance not consistently superior**: In Table 1, BC + Bicycle(D) achieves comparable results to SimDT on several metrics. In Table 2, BC-SAC also performs similarly. SimDT does not consistently outperform these simple baselines.

- **Technical presentation issues**:
  - Figure 3 is unclear; its intended message is not sufficiently discussed.
  - Table 3 needs a better caption and bolding to highlight key results.
  - Typo: “SmiDT” on line 477.
  - The term “open-loop” is used ambiguously; the paper should clarify it refers to non-reactive agents, not lack of feedback.
  - The paper lacks a formal problem formulation section and clear examples of how single-token decision transformers fail, making it hard to follow the motivation.

- **Missing relevant citations**: The paper does not cite recent related works such as CtRL-Sim (offline RL for multi-agent behavior), RL fine-tuning for driving, nuPlan, or multi-token prediction in driving. Comparative discussion is absent.

*Note: Reviewer disagreement exists. Two reviewers (scores 5) found the approach promising and the writing clear, while the majority (reviewers 1–4, scores 1 and 3) were critical of novelty, baseline selection, and validity of claims. Reviewer 1 (score 1) considered the paper lacking novelty and not a publishable contribution. Reviewer 4 (score 3) emphasized unsupported performance claims and missing comparisons.*
