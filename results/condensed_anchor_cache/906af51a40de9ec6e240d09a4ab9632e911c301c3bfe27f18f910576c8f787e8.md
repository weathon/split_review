## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Peer Rank (PR) and Peer Discussion (PD) to improve LLM-based evaluation by mitigating self-enhancement bias and positional bias. PR uses iterative reweighting of reviewer LLMs based on pairwise preference consistency; PD prompts two LLMs to discuss and reach mutual agreement. Experiments on two benchmark datasets show higher accuracy and better alignment with human judgments.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses important issues in LLM-based evaluation (self-enhancement bias, positional bias) through targeted, easy-to-understand methods inspired by education. (R1, R4)
- The methods are simple but effective; experimental results are promising, with insightful analysis (e.g., stronger LLMs tend to hold opinions in discussion, leading reviewer holds position). (R3, R4)
- Many examples illustrate the processes of peer rank and peer discussion. (R4)
- Human annotations were extended on existing datasets, benefiting future research. (R1)
- The work may provide a useful framework for clever decoding strategies (minority positive view from low-confidence reviewer R2, who otherwise found the paper confusing; contrast: R2 score 1). (R2)

### Weaknesses
- Improvements over simpler baselines are marginal and not convincingly demonstrated:
    - Table 2: Elo rating ranking of the five LLMs did not change. (R1)
    - Table 3: example-wise correlation increases little when comparing “All Reviewers (Weighted)” to “GPT-4 & Claude & GPT-3.5”. (R1)
    - Table 5: performance boost from Peer Discussion is not substantial. (R1)
    - The core idea of Peer Rank appears to have limited practical significance. (R1)
- No statistical significance analysis (e.g., p-values) is provided for comparisons in Tables 3, 4, 5, making it unclear if improvements are meaningful or due to chance. (R4)
- The two proposed methods (PR and PD) are weakly related, and the paper does not combine them, making the contribution feel fragmented. (R1)
- Peer Discussion substantially increases computational cost (multi-turn interaction); the paper does not discuss the cost-benefit trade-off, especially compared to human evaluation. (R1, R3)
- Only paid SOTA models are used; open-source LLMs are not considered or discussed. Questions remain about which open-source alternatives could be used and whether methods rely on a limited pool of strong peer models. (R3)
- Scope is limited to question-answering; applicability to other common LLM tasks (e.g., essay generation, writing style, argumentation coherence) is unclear. (R4)
- Details of the human annotation process are missing: participant recruitment, background, payment. (R1)
- Convergence of the Peer Rank iterative weighting (Algorithm 2, Appendix E) is not discussed; no analysis of the impact of the number of iterations on results. (R1)
- Possible data contamination issues for the tested LLMs are not addressed. (R4)
- One reviewer (R2, score 1, low confidence) found the writing highly confusing, making it impossible to parse the motivation, problem, and solution, and thus difficult to assess novelty and impact. This reviewer also questioned whether the aim is to help LMs produce better answers, indicating unclear contribution. (R2) [Contrast: other reviewers found the paper more understandable; still, the writing clarity concern is noted.]
