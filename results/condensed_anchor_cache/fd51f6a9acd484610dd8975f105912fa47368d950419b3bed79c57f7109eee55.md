## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes ILA (Identify Important Layers for Alignment), a method that learns binary masks for LoRA weight matrices to identify layers critical to alignment fine-tuning. Important layers are selected by optimizing a constrained loss with a mask; unimportant layers are then frozen during later training stages. Experiments on LLaMA-2-7B, Mistral-7B, and LLaMA-3-8B with LIMA, Alpaca, NoRobots, and other benchmarks show that layer importance rankings overlap ~90% across datasets, and freezing unimportant layers yields modest performance gains or efficiency improvements. Reviewers note the method’s intuitive appeal and comprehensive evaluation but raise major concerns about novelty, clarity, statistical rigor, missing baselines, and marginal improvements.

### Strengths

- The motivation for selectively freezing layers to reduce catastrophic forgetting is clearly stated (R5).
- The paper evaluates on modern benchmarks including MT-Bench, MMLU, Hellaswag, and TruthfulQA (R4, R1).
- Empirical evidence covers LoRA, AdaLoRA, QLoRA, and full fine-tuning across three open-source LLMs (R1, R5).
- Interesting results on layer significance: consistent overlap (~90% Jaccard similarity) across different alignment datasets, suggesting fundamental alignment patterns (R1, R4).
- The inclusion of out-of-distribution testing in the ablation study and use of Jaccard similarity for comparison is appreciated (R4).
- The paper is well-organized and visually appealing (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty** – Several reviewers observe that the core idea of freezing unimportant layers to improve fine-tuning has already been proposed (e.g., HFT, LISA, GMT, and earlier work from three years ago with finer-grained control [R3, R5]). The method also closely relates to layer pruning (Sheared LLaMA, CoFi, Li et al., Gromov et al., Chen et al., van der Ouderaa et al.) and to gradient-based layer/head/neuron selection studies, which are neither cited nor compared (R2, R4). The authors do not clearly explain what unique benefits ILA provides over existing PEFT methods (R1, R4).

2. **Method explanation is unclear and lacks mathematical rigor** – Important technical details are missing (R2, R4). Terms like “important” and “unimportant” layers are not formally defined (R2). Algorithm 1 is not tied to Section 2 formulas (e.g., what is K?). Equation (7) is claimed to be a reparametrization of Eq. (3) but no constraint on ||s|| remains, so the optimization is no longer constrained (R2). Theorem 2.1 is not explained; L_∞ and R in Eqs. (8,9) are undefined; Assumption 2.2 conflates model-level epsilon-stability with parameter-level stability; the proof drops the loss function (R2). Notations (theta as set vs. vector) are inconsistent (R2). How stability is checked (e.g., Monte Carlo estimation with sufficient samples) is not specified (R4). The overall training pipeline appears to be two-step (first run ILA to select layers, then re-fine-tune), but the paper does not clearly state this (R3).

3. **Modest and not statistically validated performance gains** – Reported improvements are small (e.g., +0.12% from “Full Finetune” to “Full Finetune w/ILA” on LLaMA-2-7B Table 4) (R1). Numerical differences in Tables 3 and 4 are not accompanied by confidence intervals, p-values, or multi-seed averages; given LoRA’s known instability, this is insufficient (R1, R2, R4). Many configurations show no improvement (e.g., consistent failure on Hellaswag, R1). The observed gains may simply reflect regularization (e.g., progressive dropout used in LIMA), yet no regularization baselines are provided (R4). Multiple seeds and statistical tests are needed to support claims of improvement (R1, R2, R4).

4. **Missing comparisons to key baselines and related methods** – The following comparisons are absent:
   - HFT, LISA, GMT (R3)
   - Layer pruning methods (e.g., Li et al. 2024, Gromov et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2024, “LLM Surgeon”) (R2)
   - AdaLoRA w/ILA (R1) – only AdaLoRA without ILA is shown, not the combined variant.
   - Fine-grained selection methods (e.g., those selecting heads or neurons) (R3, R4)
   - Dropout and other regularization techniques (R4)
   - Interpretability studies that already characterize layer significance (R4).

5. **Experimental design issues and missing details** – The abbreviation “IFILA” appears in tables but is never defined in the main text (R1, R3, R4). Descriptions in L303-304 mismatch Table 3 (no γ in the table) (R1). The paper states three datasets for language understanding but only shows two (R1). Table 6: for LIMA, ILA (75%) and ILA (30%) appear but no corresponding NoRobots entries (R3). Table 7 shows memory usage for 30% layers but not for the 75% setting used in main results, raising questions about whether ILA requires more memory than plain LoRA (R1). No experiments with LLaMA-3-8B despite mentioning it as a baseline (R3). Preliminary experiments compare full parameter vs. LoRA; these settings are unequal because full fine-tuning adds more parameters (R4). Table 8 lacks fine-grained method comparisons from [1] (R3).

6. **Compute cost and efficiency not fully accounted** – The measurements report only the final fine-tuning cost, ignoring the pre-training and layer selection overhead. Total cost may exceed regular LoRA (R4). For large models, guaranteeing stabilization before mask learning is difficult due to non-convex optimization (R4). The overhead in runtime and complexity compared to full fine-tuning is not reported (R2). The stability of the mask optimization with respect to initial scores is not analyzed (R2).

7. **Limited scope and generalizability** – The method is evaluated only on alignment (instruction tuning) tasks; it is not tested on math reasoning or code generation, so general applicability is unclear (R3). The authors primarily study LoRA-based settings; discussion should give more attention to full parameter fine-tuning (R5). The paper frames instruction tuning as “alignment,” which may conflate with RLHF; a clarification is needed (R4).
