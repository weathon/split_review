## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper analyzes linear mode connectivity (LMC) achieved by weight matching (WM), activation matching (AM), and the straight-through estimator (STE), focusing primarily on WM. The authors show that WM does not significantly reduce the \(L_2\) distance between two independently trained models, but aligns the singular vectors (particularly those associated with large singular values) of each layer’s weight matrices. They argue that this alignment, rather than simple distance reduction, allows the merged model to retain similar functionality and thus satisfy LMC. The paper uses SVD-based theoretical analysis and empirical experiments on VGG, ResNet, and MLP architectures with MNIST, CIFAR10, and ImageNet datasets. It further compares AM and STE, finding that AM operates similarly to WM, while STE does not align singular vectors and behaves differently. The analysis also considers merging three or more models.

### Strengths

- The analysis of weight matching via SVD of layerwise weight matrices is interesting and provides a valuable perspective on understanding LMC, a phenomenon with broad impact for deep learning theory and model merging.
- The paper is clearly written, well-motivated, and uses consistent notation, making it easy to follow. The proofs in the main paper are correct and clearly articulated.
- The observation that \(L_2\) distance reduction via WM is not the sole cause of LMC is interesting and well supported by numerical experiments. The Taylor approximation to estimate the barrier provides a clear intuition for the authors’ stance.
- The theoretical analysis, when its assumptions hold, is clearly explained and supported empirically.
- The appendix provides good detail for reproducing experiments, and the work covers comparisons among WM, AM, and STE across multiple architectures and datasets.
- The extension of the analysis to connectivity among three or more models is a noteworthy addition.

### Weaknesses

- **Incremental contribution**: The observation that minimizing the \(L_2\) distance is equivalent to maximizing the inner product is well-known in prior work (e.g., [1] in the reviews). One reviewer notes that the contributions feel incremental overall, and another points out that similar spectral analyses and Hessian-based barrier approximations have already appeared in prior works ([1], [2] in the reviews) that are not cited.
- **Overconfident claims**: 
  - The statement that “the reason AM achieves LMC is likely the same as for WM” (line 458) is overly confident given the evidence presented. Alternative explanations for the similar results are not ruled out.
  - The claim “this intuition is inaccurate” (line 70) mischaracterizes the correct intuition that if \(\theta_a \approx \pi(\theta_b)\) then interpolated models will produce similar outputs. The paper’s narrative is that LMC holds even when this proximity is violated, not that the intuition itself is wrong.
- **Unclarified methodology**: The phrase “algorithm similar to the backpropagation method to efficiently compute \(\mu^T H\)” (line 211) is not elaborated in the appendix. The specific method for computing the Hessian-vector product should be specified or referenced.
- **Unjustified assumptions**:
  - The claim that “we can find permutation matrices such that the left and right singular vectors of the two models match” (line 261) lacks rigorous justification. In general, singular vectors cannot be perfectly matched purely through permutation without altering the underlying linear transformation.
  - The claim that “distributions of the singular values of the weights … almost equal” (line 286) is too strong. Appendix H.1 and Figure 7 show notable variability in output layers (e.g., Figure 7(a) light blue vs. red curves), contradicting the assumption that they are approximately constant multiples.
  - The assumption that the input vector’s direction aligns with the singular vectors associated with large singular values is also strong. Appendix H.8 shows a counterexample on ResNet50/ImageNet where this does not hold. The discrepancies and their implications for the paper’s conclusions are not directly addressed.
- **Limitations of per-layer analysis**: The use of the Lipschitz constant limits the analysis to essentially a single activation; for multiple layers the Lipschitz constant becomes excessively large. Prior work shows that barriers can vanish at the full-model level even when they exist at the layer level, weakening the connection between layer-level singular vector alignment and full-model LMC.
- **STE comparison not convincing**: 
  - The numerical results (Table 4) show only a minimal difference between barriers for WM and STE, and the initial barrier for STE is much higher, making the relative reduction less significant.
  - The claim that the barrier between \(\pi_b(\theta_b)\) and \(\pi_c(\theta_c)\) is smaller with WM than with STE (line 519) is difficult to evaluate from Figure 6 and not sufficiently supported.
  - The analysis of STE is straightforward given that STE is designed only to minimize loss at a single midpoint, not to find fully matching models.
- **Missing experiments and ablations**:
  - How does the \(L_2\) distance evolve with an increasing number of WM/AM iterations? It is surprising that distance shrinkage is nearly identical between WM and AM given that AM optimizes for feature matching.
  - No analysis accounts for the necessity of post-matching fine-tuning, especially for networks with batch normalization layers (e.g., Jordan et al., Repair).
  - The analysis of strong linear connectivity for more than two models (as in Sharma et al. 2024) is only briefly considered; it would benefit from further insights from the SVD perspective, and there is no additional proof that WM is more advantageous for merging three or more models (claimed line 522).
  - Could including the third term in the Taylor decomposition, or a more detailed analysis of the barrier beyond the midpoint, yield better estimates?
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Figures (especially Fig. 1 and Fig. 4) are small and difficult to read; a logscale on the y-axis would improve interpretability.
  - Table 1 (and others) would benefit from highlighting sections that support the conclusion; too many significant figures are given, making values appear arbitrary.
  - The abbreviation “layer-wise model compression (LMC)” in line 890 conflicts with the standard abbreviation for linear mode connectivity and should be avoided.
  - Reviewers suggest replacing Figure 6 (low-dimensional visualization) with the tabular results in Table 4, as the figure provides an imprecise representation of the loss surface.
- **Additional reviewer questions** (overlap with missing analyses):
  - What explains that permutations found by AM are much different from those found by WM, despite ostensibly similar objectives? Can SVD be used to analyze qualitative/quantitative differences?
  - How do the findings relate to the efficacy of SGD in finding solutions that can be merged?
  - Is the Entezari conjecture still a conjecture, or has a more solid theoretical foundation been established?
  - In line 122, why is \(W_{\ell+1}' = W_{\ell+1} P^{\mathsf{T}}\) while \(W_\ell' = P W_\ell\)? Should it not be \(W_{\ell+1}' = P W_{\ell+1}\)?
  - How well does the analysis scale beyond 2-3 models, beyond the brief claim?
