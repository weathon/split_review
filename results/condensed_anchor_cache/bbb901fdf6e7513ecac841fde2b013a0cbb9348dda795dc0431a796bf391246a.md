## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a strength estimator (SE) and SE-MCTS to predict and adjust playing strength in Go and Chess. The SE predicts ranks from gameplay data with >80% accuracy from 15 games (vs 49% for 100 games in prior work), and SE-MCTS uses the strength scores to produce human-like play at target levels. Reviews highlight novelty and practical utility, but also raise concerns about experimental methodology, baselines, theoretical assumptions, and clarity. Reviewer 2 is more critical (overall rating 3), while Reviewers 3 and 4 are positive (ratings 6) and Reviewer 1 is moderately positive (5).

### Strengths
- Novel approach to modeling human players that goes beyond maximizing win rate; provides a new direction for human-AI interaction (R1, R3, R4).
- Loss function derived from the Bradley-Terry model is novel and generalizes to settings with more than two players (R2).
- Practical utility: adjusting agent strength to human-like levels is an important application (R2, R3).
- Clear presentation with sufficient details and proper motivations (R3, R4).
- Significant improvement over traditional supervised learning: SE achieves >80% accuracy predicting rank from 15 games, vs 49% for 100 games in previous method (R3; from abstract).
- Demonstrated ability to adjust playing strength to target ranks, with 51.33% alignment to human actions compared to 42.56% for previous SOTA (abstract; R3).
- Cross-validated on two applications (Go and Chess) with consistent results (R4).

### Weaknesses
- Data leakage concern: training/testing split is unclear; not partitioning by players may allow the model to learn preferred openings, inflating accuracy (R1). Code not released, further hindering reproducibility (R1).
- Lack of baselines: no comparison to raw Elo estimates, simple classifiers, or models of human-like play in chess/Go (R1). A baseline of a rank-conditioned policy network π(·|s, r) is suggested (R2).
- Bradley-Terry model limitations: scalar scores cannot capture intransitivity; assumption that higher rank always beats lower rank fails if the lower rank plays an effective exploiter strategy; scope may need limiting to perfect‑information games (R2).
- State reach-probability issue: the loss for SE(s,a) does not account for the fact that SE(s,a) is most influenced by the ranks of players who most often visit state s; players at certain ranks may rarely reach state s, causing inaccurate predictions by SE-MCTS (R2).
- Notion of “strength” is unclear: it depends on both the player’s and opponent’s strategies, and the distribution over strategies is implicit in the empirical data (R2). The phrase “stronger actions” (L53) needs clarification (R2).
- The claim (L534) that using all models trained by AlphaZero could address an issue is unclear, as Elo is a relative measure and AlphaZero’s Elo is not directly relevant without human games (R1).
- SE-MCTS requires k separate searches for k different strength levels, whereas SA-MCTS uses one search; computational overhead is a concern (R4). The paper does not adequately justify why modifying prior probabilities during the search is superior to directly adjusting the final action selection (R4).
- With increasing MCTS simulations, decisions rely more on Q‑values, potentially nullifying the effect of prior modifications; no ablation study varying simulation count is provided (R4).
- Figure 4: low win rate for SE-MCTS is not convincingly explained; need detailed analysis (R4). Plots are difficult to read: confidence intervals are unclear, colors make lines hard to distinguish; consider tables, log axes, smoothed lines, fewer data points (R1).
- Experimental evaluation is insufficient: Figure 4 only compares against a single baseline rank r5; a full round‑robin tournament across all combinations of ranks and methods is needed (R3). Pairwise matches with Elo rating would provide a more reliable strength ranking than raw win rates (R4).
- Lack of detailed comparison with existing learning‑to‑rank methods; aggregation of rankings across game states is not adequately discussed, and different aggregation strategies may impact results (R3).
- Model labels (e.g., SE±1 vs SE_∞±1) are not intuitive and not defined in one place, making the paper hard to follow (R1).
- The reference for SA-MCTS is unclear among multiple citations in Section 2.3 (R3).
- The Chess experiments appear less detailed than the Go experiments; a more thorough evaluation on Chess comparable to Go would strengthen the paper (R3). Exact z values for each rank are not given (R3).
- Strength score curves for vanilla MCTS and SA-MCTS are not provided in Figure 3, making comparison incomplete (R3).
- Geometric mean for aggregating rank comparisons (L173) is not motivated (R2).
- Typo: “sequentially perform the softmax loss” (L238) should probably be “minimize the softmax loss” (R2).
