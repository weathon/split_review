## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes two generic least-squares-based estimators (GELS) for probabilistic values, including a framework (TrELS) for distributional values. The key idea exploits an identity to simultaneously estimate contributions for all data points in a sampled subset using a single utility evaluation, achieving an \(O(n\log n)\) evaluation complexity for \((\epsilon,\delta)\)-approximation—improving over the previous generic estimator’s \(O(n^2\log n)\) (Kwon & Zou 2022). For distributional values, the method casts estimation as a least-squares regression problem, enabling trained models to predict values for unseen data in a single forward pass. Experiments demonstrate faster convergence compared to prior methods. Reviewers are generally positive (scores 8,8,6,8), with one reviewer (Reviewer 3) being more critical regarding technical depth and practical improvements.

### Strengths
- The estimators are general across the full family of probabilistic values, extending the best-known complexity bound (\(O(n\log n)\)) beyond special cases like Shapley or Banzhaf (Propositions 3 and 5).
- The least-squares perspective provides a unified view, and the connection to distributional values (Section 3.3) enables training value predictors without supervised signals (Theorem 1).
- Theoretical guarantees are provided with convergence rates, and the paper includes thorough comparisons to prior work (SHAP, MSR, AME, etc.).
- The ability to estimate relative rankings (Propositions 1 and 2) and to evaluate unseen data points efficiently via a trained model is practically valuable.
- The idea is simple and clearly presented; the writing is generally clear (Reviewers 1,3). Experiments validate faster convergence and the effectiveness of TrELS.
- The contributions have potentially significant impact on data valuation (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of direct comparisons and unclear practical advantage over existing specialized estimators** (Reviewers 1,3). The paper does not sufficiently compare with SHAP, MSR, and AME—which already achieve similar rates for Shapley—nor explain why those methods cannot be generalized. Experiments in the appendix do not conclusively show superiority over SHAP in practice (Reviewer 3).
- **Theoretical depth is modest** (Reviewer 3). The least-squares interpretation is considered artificial and redundant by one reviewer; the result is “a bit thin” technically. More intuition is needed on why an \(\Theta(N/\epsilon^2)\) rate is expected and how the method fundamentally differs from previous estimators (Reviewer 1).
- **Dependence of the bound on the probability weights \(\mathbf{p}\)** (Reviewer 4). The constant \(\gamma\) (used in Propositions 3 and 5) may not be independent of \(N\) for all \(\mathbf{p}\); e.g., setting \(p_1=1/2\) and \(p_i\approx 0\) yields \(\gamma=O(1/N)\). The paper does not analyze how \(\gamma\) varies with \(\mathbf{p}\), leaving it unclear whether the efficiency guarantee holds for all choices of \(\mathbf{p}\). A rigorous analysis is needed.
- **Insufficient explanation of the generalization mechanism** (Reviewers 1,4). The main text lacks an intuitive explanation of how the method handles arbitrary \(\mathbf{p}\). The core mathematical insight is not clearly communicated.
- **Computational challenges in training value estimators** (Reviewer 2). Estimating distributional values, even with approximations, incurs significant computational cost (e.g., evaluating over sampled subsets). The paper does not discuss the scalability of the training phase or the trade-offs involved.
- **Memory overhead of faster estimators not discussed** (Reviewer 2). The paper notes that SHAP, SHAP-paired, and complement achieve faster convergence at \(\Theta(N^2)\) memory cost versus \(\Theta(N)\), but does not analyze the implications for large-scale applications or propose mitigation strategies.
- **Missing discussion on convergence of the distributional value estimator** (Reviewer 4). Conditions under which \(\phi_i^{\mathcal{B},\mathbf{w}}(U)\) closely approximates \(\phi_i^{\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w}}(U)\) (Theorem 1) are not elaborated; finite-sample biases and dataset size requirements are not addressed.
- **Presentation gaps** (Reviewer 1). The introduction does not properly introduce background concepts (e.g., Shapley and Banzhaf values with examples). Numerical plots would benefit from log-scale formatting for better readability.
- **Missing discussion on practical applications** (Reviewer 2). The paper does not clarify which real-world scenarios would most benefit from the distributional Shapley approach.
