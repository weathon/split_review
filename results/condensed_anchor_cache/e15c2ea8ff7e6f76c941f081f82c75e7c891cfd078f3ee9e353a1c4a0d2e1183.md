## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes β-DQN, an off-policy deep RL algorithm for discrete action spaces that improves exploration by learning a behavior function β modeling state-action coverage, constructing a diverse set of policies from three functions (Q, Q_mask, β), and selecting a policy per episode via a meta-controller based on a non-stationary MAB. Reviewers agree the idea is intuitive, the method is straightforward to implement and computationally efficient relative to intrinsic-reward methods. However, major concerns center on technical novelty (overlap with prior work BAC), limited experimental scope (only MinAtar, MiniGrid; not Atari or Montezuma's Revenge), insufficient baselines (RND not systematically tuned; missing BYOL-Explore and other curiosity-driven methods), lack of theoretical results, and restriction to discrete actions. While Reviewer 5 (rating 6) found the evaluation sound and the presentation clear, other reviewers (ratings 3,3,3,5) highlighted significant weaknesses in experimental rigor and contribution.

### Strengths
- The problem of diverse exploration is important in RL. (R1, R4)
- The method is intuitive and makes sense; the toy example on CliffWalk and related figures help visualization. (R1, R5)
- Presentation is clear and easy to follow. (R4, R5)
- β-DQN is straightforward to implement and computationally efficient; it does not use auxiliary intrinsic reward networks. (R2, R3)  
- The construction of a diverse policy set via interpolation between π_cov and π_cor is a reasonable approach. (R1)
- The algorithm adopts top‑p sampling with a range of p to obtain diverse policies, and treats episode‑level policy selection as a MAB problem, which is a structured alternative to ε‑greedy. (R2)
- It incorporates both exploitation and exploration at the intra‑episodic level, enabling state‑dependent temporal‑extended exploration. (R3)
- Promising results are reported on MinAtar (e.g., Asterix) and MiniGrid (e.g., LavaCrossing, RedBlueDoors). (R3, R5)
- Ablation studies (e.g., on policy set size) and analyses (e.g., policy selection pattern) are provided. (R5)
- Empirical results appear replicable. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Technical contribution and novelty**: The construction of diverse policies is elementary (simple interpolation); the meta‑controller borrows the MAB framework without novel adaptations. (R1)  
- **Overlap with prior work**: Eq. (2) (learning in‑sample state‑action pairs) and Eq. (5) (trade‑off between standard and conservative Q) are similar to Eq. (4.1) and Eq. (4.3) of BAC [1] respectively; the idea of constructing a policy set has been extensively studied (e.g., [2]). No discussion or comparison with BAC is provided. (R3)
- **Theoretical results missing**: The method is motivated by intuition rather than foundational theory, making it less trustworthy. (R1)
- **Limited to discrete action spaces**: The DQN formulation constrains actions to be discrete, and there is no obvious way to extend to continuous control; this limits applicability to many real‑world scenarios. (R2, R3, also R1’s question)
- **Experimental scope is too narrow**:  
  – Environments are synthetic or very simple (finite state/action, small observation diversity). (R1, R2)  
  – Only three environments are used (Asterix, LavaCrossing, RedBlueDoors); it is unclear how they were selected and whether results generalize to larger discrete domains like Atari. (R5)  
  – Montezuma’s Revenge is mentioned in the introduction but not tested, which is a critical benchmark for exploration. (R4)  
- **Baseline comparisons are insufficient**:  
  – Curiosity‑driven methods (e.g., RND, prediction‑error based) should be included. (R3)  
  – State‑of‑the‑art exploration algorithms like BYOL‑Explore are missing. (R4)  
  – Baselines are not systematically tuned; for example, RND is taken from the LESSON paper with an unusually large α=10 (less than question: why that choice?), which may give an unfair advantage. (R2)  
- **Behavior function β learning**:  
  – β is learned via supervised learning on replay data; it may not be robust to policy or dynamics shifts, potentially causing instability in more complex scenarios. (R3)  
  – Eq. (1) does not clarify how β is parameterized and optimized – is it a behavior‑cloning policy? (R4)  
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**:  
  – β‑DQN has two key hyperparameters (L and policy set size); only an ablation on set size is given, not on L. (R2)  
  – The interplay between the MAB meta‑controller and the Q‑network training is unclear (e.g., is the MAB trained concurrently?). (R4)  
  – No golden standard for selecting these hyperparameters is provided; sensitivity is partially shown in Fig. 6, but not fully examined. (R4)  
- **Presentation clarity**: Terms like “space coverage” and “bias correction” lack precise definitions in the paper’s context. (R4)  
- **Statistical reporting**: Table 1 does not report standard deviations, making it hard to assess significance. (R4)  
- **Claims not fully supported**:  
  – The statement that “argmax Q_mask is more stable than argmax Q” (Section 5.3.2) is not clearly supported by Fig. 5; while Q_mask performs better, a difference in stability is not obvious. (R5)  
  – The observation in Section 5.3.1 that π_cov is chosen first for coverage then π_cor for bias correction is not consistently seen in Fig. 4; a deeper analysis across diverse environments is needed. (R5)  
- **Missing related works**: Ensemble methods for DQN (e.g., https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.01929.pdf) and hyperparameter‑based ensembles (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07288.pdf) are relevant but not discussed. (R4)
