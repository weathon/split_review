## Merged Review

### Summary

This work proposes noise-aware metrics (intra-annotator confidence and inter-annotator stability) to identify noisy preference data and an Adaptive-DPO loss that reweights samples and adapts the margin to reduce the influence of noisy samples and amplify clean ones. Experiments cover text-to-image generation and single-turn dialogue. One reviewer (R4, rating 5) finds the idea innovative and the formulas comprehensive, with significant advantages over original DPO on real datasets. The other three reviewers (rating 3) acknowledge the problem’s importance but raise serious concerns about problem formulation, evaluation, missing baselines, and methodology.

### Strengths

- The problem of handling noise in preference data for DPO alignment is important and timely.
- The idea of computing bias and variance of confidences from multiple annotators is interesting and shows good innovation; the formulas in Eq. 11–13 and Algorithm 1 are clearly presented.
- Rich exploratory analysis and experiments (e.g., Figure 1, Figure 3) demonstrate the possible negative effect of label noise on DPO.
- The work covers two widely used generative model families: LLMs (text generation) and text-to-image diffusion models.
- The proposed Adaptive-DPO loss shows significant advantages over vanilla DPO on real datasets according to R4.

### Weaknesses

- **Problem definition and motivation are questionable.**  
  - [R1] The notion of “clean” preference is problematic because preferences are inherently subjective; inconsistency across annotators is not necessarily noise. Ambiguous cases should be distinguished from obviously mislabeled data (e.g., adversarial labels on easy cases).  
  - [R2] The paper’s classification of noise sources (machine annotators, careless/malicious human annotators, lack of standardization) needs more careful treatment. For human annotators, one must separate mistakes from legitimate individual differences.  
  - [R3] The claim that “the quality of preference data used in DPO training has been largely overlooked” (lines 14–15, 36–37) is incorrect; existing works [1: Impact of Preference Noise; 2: Noisy DPO & IPO; 3: Provably Robust DPO] already address noisy preferences in alignment.  
  - [R1] Only synthetic noise (label flipping) is used to evaluate DPO’s vulnerability (Sec 3.2). This scenario may not reflect natural noise; the performance drop in Table 1 appears not significant.

- **Missing important baselines and comparisons.**  
  - [R3] C-DPO [2], R-DPO [3], and other methods (e.g., those mentioned in line 669) are not compared, hurting reliability.  
  - [R2] No comparison with or discussion of methods that carefully curate preference data (e.g., Llama2, Beaver) is provided.  
  - [R4] An experiment is needed that compares DPO trained on synthetic data at varying noise levels with DPO trained on real data to support the claim of combating degradation.

- **Effectiveness of the proposed metric and loss is not convincingly demonstrated.**  
  - [R3] The metric $u_\theta(x)$ is ineffective: in Figure 3, for $0.1 \le u_\theta(x) \le 1$, the noise rate stays in (0.24, 0.30), meaning >70% of samples are clean – the metric cannot reliably separate noise.  
  - [R3, R4] In Table 2, the win rate of Adaptive-DPO vs DPO* decreases as noise rate increases, contradicting the expectation that the method should handle higher noise better. This suggests the method may be ineffective under noisy preferences.  
  - [R2] Could the metric merely capture that the model lacks knowledge (low confidence, high variance) and thus needs more training, rather than detecting label noise? Using the model itself during training to compute the metric (Algorithm 1 line 4) may be unreliable.  
  - [R1] The roles of the reweighting factor $W_\theta(x)$ and the adaptive margin $\Gamma_\theta(x)$ seem overlapping; how they synergize or complement is unclear.  
  - [R4] Separate ablation results for $W_\theta(x)$ and $\Gamma_\theta(x)$ are missing for both language and image models (only case studies like Figure 6).  
  - [R2] The claim that the model first learns clean labels (lines 404–406) requires theoretical or empirical support in the preference learning setting; it is not self-evident.  
  - [R2] Would the method hurt performance at 0% noise? (Table 2 suggests possibly.)

- **Scope and experimental validation are limited.**  
  - [R2] The paper claims to cover text and image generation, but all exploration experiments, main results, and ablation are only on image generation (text results only in a single table/dialogue).  
  - [R3] Standard evaluation should include GPT-4 as the evaluator (as in [4,5,6,7]); manual metrics are insufficient without such a judge.  
  - [R3, R2] Scores (ImageReward, PickScore, Aesthetic, HPS) should be reported as raw numbers, not only as win rates.  
  - [R3] In Table 2, the value of $M$ (number of models trained for metric computation) is not stated.  
  - [R2] It is not explained why score-based metrics are presented as win rates rather than raw scores.  
  - [R4] There is a format error in Table 1: incorrect bold in the PickScore column.

- **Methodology details are unclear and costly.**  
  - [R3] AdaptiveDPO requires simultaneously training $M$ different models to compute the noise-aware metrics (bias and variance). This is expensive; the relationship between $M$ and metric reliability is not discussed (R2).  
  - [R3, R2] Notations are unexplained: $\rho$ in Eq. (11), $k_1, k_2, c_2$ in Eq. (15), the relationship between $c$ and $c_2$, and the use of $\ast$ for multiplication. Rigorous and clear mathematical presentation is needed for this theory-based direction.  
  - [R2] In Eq. (15), the claim “smaller $u$ will be accompanied with larger $\gamma$” seems to assume $|c_2| >> |k_2|$; selection of these constants in experiments is not described.  
  - [R1] Several vague expressions: “serious problems” from copyright deviations (line 54) need concrete examples; prompts for Figures 4,5,6 are missing (since rewards like ImageReward, PickScore, HPS measure text-image alignment).  
  - [R2, R1] The example of annotator backgrounds in Figure analysis and the three cases in Sec 4.1 require more detailed explanation and concrete instances.

- **Minority positive contrast (R4):** R4 is more positive (rating 5) and strongly endorses the innovation and real-data gains. However, all three other reviewers (rating 3) converge on the above weaknesses, questioning the core claims and experimental soundness.
