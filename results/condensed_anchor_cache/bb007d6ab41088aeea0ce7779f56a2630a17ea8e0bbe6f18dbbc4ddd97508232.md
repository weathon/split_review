## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper provides the first theoretical analysis of generalization in preference learning, specifically for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The authors consider a simplified Transformer model where only the last readout layer is trainable, and analyze the dynamics of gradient flow over a finite number of steps using the reward margin for each sample. They assume preference data arise from a mixture of Gaussians over clusters (personas). Under this setup, they derive high-probability bounds on both training error (0-1 classification based on reward margin) and generalization error for unseen samples from the same distribution. The analysis also discusses the gradient of the reward margin in the multi-token generation scenario. The theoretical insights are empirically validated on synthetic data and Llama 2-7B.

### Strengths
- **Novel research question and theoretical foundation:** The paper offers the first rigorous theoretical framework for understanding generalization in DPO, an area that lacked theoretical analysis despite practical importance. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Bridges theory and practice:** Concrete bounds and conditions are derived and then validated through careful experiments on real-world LLMs, showing alignment between theory and practice. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Actionable insights from reward margin dynamics:** The analysis reveals how factors like the number of preference concepts and sample size affect generalization, providing practical guidance. (R2)
- **Clarity and presentation:** The introduction and notations are clean and well-written; the paper is clear overall. (R3, R4)
- **Empirical justification of assumptions:** Experiments on cosine similarity and orthogonality of personas partially support the data assumptions; R4 notes the assumptions are justified via experiments. (R1, R4; R2 and R3 disagree – see Weaknesses)

### Weaknesses
- **Model simplification reduces to linear analysis and limits applicability:**
    * The assumption that only the readout weight matrix \(W\) is trainable while features \(g(x)\) are fixed (output \(y = Wg(x)\)) effectively reduces the model to linear/logistic regression. This neglects the complex interplay between \(W\) and \(g(x)\) during fine-tuning, and proof techniques follow standard ODE analysis without novelty. (R2, R3, R4)
    * The model is deterministic, whereas modern LLMs are autoregressive and stochastic; it is unclear how preference pairs are formulated under this setup. (R3)
    * The dimensions of \(W\) and \(g(x)\) are not clearly defined, making it hard to follow the nature of the linear transformation. (R3)
- **Insufficient justification for the mixture-of-Gaussians assumption on preferences:**
    * The empirical evidence (cosine similarity and orthogonality) does not justify that each cluster follows a Gaussian distribution; real preference data may have heavier tails, multimodality, or more complex structures, weakening the theoretical validity. (R2)
    * The justification is not rigorous, and the model class is not clearly established. (R3)
- **Disconnect between theory and practice:**
    * The derived generalization guarantees are for the “implicit reward model,” not for the language model \(\pi_\theta\) that is actually used for prediction. The paper does not clearly define “implicit reward model” (R2 cites lines 159–162) and inconsistently refers to “loss” vs. “implicit reward model” as the predictor (lines 70, 280). This creates a gap between the theoretical claim and what practitioners care about. (R2)
- **Theorem 4.2 bound is vacuous:**
    * A simple check shows the bound is meaningless until the number of samples \(Q\) is extremely large, giving it very limited practical meaning. (R3)
- **Ignores the role of pre-training:**
    * The analysis is agnostic to the fact that the LLM is pre-trained, which is crucial for explaining why so little post-training data is needed. For under-trained models the results may not hold. (R1)
- **Focus on in-distribution generalization:**
    * For LLMs, a more important phenomenon is out-of-distribution generalization (post-training data is small yet LLMs generalize to novel queries). The paper does not address this. (R1)
- **Only single-token case is analyzed:**
    * The generalization analysis is restricted to single-token generation; extension to multi-token scenarios is not provided. (R4)
- **Technical gaps in Section 3.2:**
    * The connection between the discrete SGD updates (Eq. 4) and the continuous approximation (Eq. 6) is not derived or rigorously justified. Key assumptions are missing, and the section is hard to follow. (R3)
- **Missing reference to algorithmic stability:**
    * The claim that existing theory focuses on overparameterized models ignores the line of work on algorithmic stability, which also bounds generalization gap by training steps. (R2)
- **Outdated experimental model:**
    * Experiments use Llama 2-7B; newer versions (3/3.1/3.2) would be more relevant. (R2)
- **Typos:**
    * Eq (3) is missing an expectation on the log-ratio term. (R4)
    * Lines 233–235: conclusions for two cases appear to be swapped. (R4)
    * Line 266–269: the values of \(x, y\) are unclear (is \(y\) a \(|V|\)-dimensional vector or a one-dimensional label?). (R4)
- **Reviewer disagreement on novelty and assumption justification:** R4 found the theoretical results novel and the assumptions justified by experiments, while R2 and R3 questioned the novelty due to the linear simplification and insufficient justification of the mixture-of-Gaussians assumption. (R2, R3, R4)
