## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Switch EMA (SEMA), a single‐line modification to standard EMA that switches the EMA parameters back to the original model after each epoch. The authors claim this balances flatness and sharpness of the optima, leading to faster convergence and better generalization. They provide theoretical analysis (variance, convergence, error bounds) and extensive experiments across image classification, self‐supervised learning, object detection, segmentation, image generation, video prediction, attribute regression, and language modeling. Reviewers agree the method is simple and the evaluation is broad, but disagree on the significance of the contributions (scores: 5, 8, 6, 3). Two reviewers (R2, R3) are more positive, while R1 and R4 are more critical, citing missing definitions, insufficient statistical rigor, and a modest improvement that may not justify acceptance.

### Strengths
- **Simple yet broadly applicable algorithm.** SEMA is a practical one‑line change that can be applied to any optimizer using EMA, and it consistently improves performance across many tasks, architectures, and training regimes (R2, R4).
- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation.** Experiments cover discriminative, generative, and regression tasks on vision and language, including image classification, self‑supervised learning, object detection/segmentation, image generation, video prediction, and language modeling (R1, R2, R3, R4). All tested combinations show improvements over the baseline.
- **Well‑motivated intuition and theoretical analysis.** The paper provides clear intuitions for balancing flatness and sharpness, and supports the method with variance analysis, convergence analysis, and generalization error bounds (R2, R3, R4).
- **Good writing and clarity.** The paper is well structured and easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing precise mathematical definitions for “sharpness” and “flatness”.** The terms are used without a rigorous link to the Hessian eigenvalues, making it difficult to assess the validity of the claims (R1). The authors should define these concepts in terms of curvature (e.g., eigenvalues of the Hessian).
- **Statistical significance is not established.** No standard deviations are reported, and the maximum number of trials is three. This undermines confidence that the improvements are consistent rather than due to random chance (R1, R2, R4). Report variance over several seeds for well‑chosen datasets to show that the gains are statistically significant (R2, R4).
- **Concerns about the theoretical reasoning.**
  - In eq. (1) the definitions of \(\Theta^{\text{OPT}}\) and \(\Theta^{\text{EMA}}\) are not provided. In Proposition 1 the exact definition of variance is missing (e.g., \(V^{(t)}_{\text{SGD}} := \mathbb{V}(\Theta_t^{\text{SGD}})\)) and the condition for a small learning rate \(\eta\) is not specified (likely \(\frac{2}{\eta}\) must exceed the top eigenvalue of matrix \(A\) for Banach’s fixed‑point theorem) (R1).
  - Eq. (7) only gives upper bounds for \(E_{\text{SEMA}}, E_{\text{EMA}}, E_{\text{SGD}}\). Comparing only upper bounds does **not** logically imply \(E_{\text{SEMA}}<E_{\text{EMA}}<E_{\text{SGD}}\); a more rigorous justification (e.g., lower bounds) is needed (R1).
  - In eq. (6) the terms hidden in the \(\propto\) notation are not specified. If the hidden constant varies across iterations \(t\), Proposition 2 may not guarantee convergence even for convex functions, because the constant could be arbitrarily large for some iterates (R1).
  - Typos in lines 1082‑1083, 1122‑1123, and 1133: missing subscripts on \(\mathcal{E}\) (R1).
- **Need for additional experiments to support claimed smoothness.**
  - The paper claims SEMA produces smoother decision boundaries (Sec. 3.2), but the main experiments are on coarse‑grained tasks. Fine‑grained classification (e.g., FGVCAircraft, CUB, Stanford Cars) and adversarial robustness evaluations would strengthen this claim (R2).
  - The baseline in Figure 4 shows a decision boundary represented by two lines; achieving 0.8 accuracy with such a boundary seems unreasonable and needs clarification (R3).
- **Minor presentation issues.**
  - Legends in the upper right corners of Figure 2 are too small to read (R3).
  - The data distribution visualisation formats in Figure 4 and Figure A.1 are not explained (R3).
- **Novelty and significance concerns.** The modification is a single‑line change and the improvements are relatively modest. One reviewer (R4, score 3) considers this too minor for a top conference, while another (R2, score 8) sees it as a valuable contribution. The authors should more clearly justify why such a small change yields meaningful practical gains.
