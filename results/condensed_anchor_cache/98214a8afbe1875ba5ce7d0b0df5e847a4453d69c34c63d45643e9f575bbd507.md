## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides the first theoretical analysis of how transformers develop stepwise reasoning capabilities through recursive task decomposition, using the k-parity problem with a one-layer transformer. It establishes three key results: (1) without intermediate supervision, finite-precision gradient-based algorithms require exponentially many iterations even with finite samples; (2) with teacher forcing (ground-truth intermediate labels), the model learns parity in a single gradient update; (3) without teacher forcing, parity can still be learned efficiently using data augmentation and self-consistency checks that validate intermediate steps, achieving logarithmic convergence. The work extends prior RNN-based results to a more realistic transformer setting and introduces a causal mask to limit error propagation during chain-of-thought (CoT) generation. All reviewers found the paper theoretically rigorous and relevant to current research on reasoning in language models, with one reviewer rating it 10 and two rating it 8, reflecting a shared appreciation of the contribution but differing on the extent of limitations.

### Strengths
1. The study is highly rigorous and well-structured, offering clear theoretical insights into the benefits of CoT for multi-hop reasoning (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
2. The setup (k-parity, one-layer transformer) is well-chosen, concise, and easy to understand, while remaining aware of empirical trends in CoT research (Reviewer 1).
3. The paper innovatively uses hierarchical decomposition of the k-parity problem and designs a corresponding transformer architecture to handle this structure (Reviewer 2).
4. The proposed mechanisms—teacher forcing (one gradient update), data augmentation, and self-consistency checks—enable CoT reasoning even without explicit intermediate supervision, aligning with empirical studies of CoT (Reviewers 1, 2).
5. The introduction of the causal mask is simple, intuitive, and effective in limiting error compounding during CoT; one reviewer considers this the main contribution over prior work (Reviewer 3).
6. The paper extends previous theoretical work on RNNs to a more realistic transformer setting, offering a clearer picture of why CoT is essential for the given problem (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. The theoretical analysis is heavily focused on the k-parity problem, which has a very specific hierarchical structure (each intermediate step is a simple 2-parity operation). It is unclear how insights translate to real-world reasoning tasks that lack such clear hierarchical decomposition, mix different operations, or have less structured intermediate steps (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). This limits the generalizability of the findings (Reviewers 1, 2).
2. The paper lacks empirical validation of the theoretical results. No experiments are provided to test the proposed methods with different problem sizes (\(k\), \(d\)), training regimes (e.g., varying batch sizes, learning rate schedules), or noise. The absence of experiments makes it difficult to assess practical robustness and applicability (Reviewers 2, 3). One reviewer specifically noted the need for trained models with and without teacher forcing on varied settings (Reviewer 3).
3. The analysis depends on a specific one-step learning rate regime; it is not clear how the model would perform under more realistic training conditions (Reviewer 1, 3).
4. The masking procedure, while theoretically necessary, introduces an artificial constraint that may not be present in practical CoT applications, potentially limiting direct applicability (Reviewer 1).
5. The conclusion that fine-tuning transformers improves multi-step reasoning is considered too strong; the paper lacks a rigorous justification that CoT data can be easily produced for all reasoning tasks, making generalization claims overstated (Reviewer 3).
6. The reliance on a single-layer transformer raises questions about whether results extend to deeper architecture; no justification is given for this choice or why two-layer transformers might behave similarly (Reviewers 1, 3).
7. A reviewer asked whether data augmentation for self-consistency checks would require significant adjustments for practical tasks, and whether real-world training would face stability or computational complexity challenges (Reviewer 2). These concerns overlap with the broader generalizability and lack of empirical validation criticisms.
