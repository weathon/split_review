## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper identifies a previously unstudied problem in hypernetwork training: a magnitude proportionality between hypernetwork inputs and outputs when using piecewise linear activations (e.g., ReLU). This proportionality causes large gradient variance, unstable optimization, and slow convergence. The authors propose Magnitude Invariant Parametrizations (MIP), a two-part solution: (1) input encoding via Fourier features with constant norm, and (2) an additive output formulation where the hypernetwork predicts a residual correction to a directly trained weight matrix. Experiments across multiple tasks, architectures, and optimizers show that MIP stabilizes training and accelerates convergence. An accompanying open-source library, HyperLight, is released. Reviewers largely agree the problem is novel, the solution is straightforward, and the experimental validation is extensive. However, there is disagreement on the significance and theoretical depth: one reviewer (score 8) finds the work very convincing, while another (score 5) views the proposed strategies as ad hoc and the theoretical grounding insufficient. Other concerns include limited architecture scope (fully-connected only, no conv/RNN/attention), lack of few-shot adaptation experiments, and insufficient analysis of why classical normalization techniques cannot replace MIP.

### Strengths

- Identification of a novel and practically important optimization problem in hypernetwork training (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The MIP formulation is novel, straightforward to implement, and introduces no additional training or inference costs (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Extensive experimental validation across multiple tasks, activation functions, normalization strategies, input dimensionalities, hypernetwork architectures, and optimizers (SGD+momentum, Adam) (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Provision of an open-source PyTorch library (HyperLight) to facilitate adoption (Reviewer 2).
- The paper is mostly well-written and presents ideas clearly (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed techniques are practically useful for practitioners working with hypernetworks (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Output encoding rationale unclear (Reviewer 1):** It is not clear how the additive output formulation fits the claimed proportionality problem. Because the auxiliary weight matrix can be learned directly, the hypernetwork path may become less critical and training may rely on stable “classical” parameter learning rather than genuinely improving hypernetwork optimization. This makes ablation results hard to interpret.
- **Not clear what distinguishes hypernetworks from classical networks (Reviewers 1, 4):** The same magnitude proportionality holds for standard ReLU networks, yet those do not exhibit similar training instability. The paper lacks an in-depth analysis of the specific conditions in hypernetworks that exacerbate this instability. Appendix C.5 argues classical normalization cannot be applied to hypernetworks, but it is not explained why this is a problem unique to hypernetworks rather than any network (Reviewer 1).
- **Why does unit-normalization of the hypernetwork input not suffice? (Reviewers 1, 3):** For multidimensional inputs γ∈ℝ^D, D≥2, simply dividing by the L2 norm would map each input to a point on the hypersphere, removing the magnitude dependence. The paper’s Fourier encoding goes beyond this, so it is unclear whether the issue is truly magnitude proportionality or a more general feature scaling problem (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 similarly asks why unit normalization would not work.
- **Limited architecture scope (Reviewer 2):** Experiments focus on fully-connected layers with common activations, initializations, and optimizers (SGD+momentum, Adam). No investigation of convolutional, recurrent, or attention layers, nor adaptive optimizers beyond Adam (e.g., AdaGrad, RMSprop). More diverse real-world tasks and larger-scale problems are needed to demonstrate general applicability.
- **Lack of few-shot adaptation experiments (Reviewer 3):** Hypernetworks are increasingly used for fast adaptation of pre-trained models (e.g., in few-shot learning). The paper does not investigate whether magnitude proportionality is also detrimental in these settings, and whether MIP benefits such tasks. The authors acknowledge this gap.
- **Insufficient optimization landscape analysis (Reviewer 3):** It is not shown whether the original parametrization’s increased variance could be controlled by simply lowering the learning rate or increasing momentum, achieving similar performance albeit slower. The paper would benefit from loss surface visualization, gradient norm analysis, or an examination of sharpness of minima and plateaus.
- **Theoretical grounding feels ad hoc (Reviewer 4):** The two strategies (input encoding and additive output) appear somewhat ad hoc. The paper lacks a deep theoretical explanation of why the input encoding is more effective than other encoding methods, and how the additive output formulation contributes to the magnitude-invariant property. The significance of the contribution is questioned.
- **Comparison with batch/layer normalization + additive output (Reviewer 4):** Figure 5 does not include results of combining additive output with BatchNorm/LayerNorm. If proportionality is the core problem, what theoretical reason prevents normalization layers from solving it while MIP can? What about using non-piecewise-linear activations (e.g., ELU, GELU)? Would that remove the problem? If not, can the problem still be attributed to proportionality? (Questions from Reviewer 4; related point from Reviewer 1 about why classical normalizations fail.)
- **Residual formulation analysis (Reviewer 3):** Is the hypernetwork update dense? How does the performance of θ^0 + h(E_{L2}(γ); w) compare to starting from θ^0 and directly optimizing a few steps on a new task to obtain θ_t^*? Is the gap between the hypernetwork output and task-specific fine-tuning closed?
