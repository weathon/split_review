## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces SeRA, a method for Direct Alignment Algorithms (DAAs) that uses implicit reward margins (IRM) from the DAA policy for sample selection and preference bootstrapping, aiming to reduce over-optimization and spurious correlations from static off-policy data. SeRA is compatible with multiple DAAs (DPO, SLiC, IPO) and requires no extra human or AI annotation. Experiments on instruction-following tasks (UltraFeedback dataset) show improved alignment performance. The paper provides a theoretical bound for DPO and empirical analysis across various models and datasets.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, well-structured, and easy to follow.
- The proposed method is simple and cost-efficient: it uses the DAA policy’s implicit reward without needing additional human feedback, AI judgment, or external reward models.
- SeRA is general and can be combined with different DAAs (DPO, SLiC, IPO), making it broadly applicable.
- The experimental results are extensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of SeRA, with significant improvements on the UltraFeedback alignment task.
- The paper includes both theoretical and empirical validation, covering diverse models, datasets, and alignment frameworks.
- The component analysis is thorough, evaluating individual elements of the algorithm for a nuanced understanding.
- The IRM method enables iterative SAA training without relying on external reward signals, simplifying implementation and reducing computational resource requirements.
- The motivation is clearly presented from both theoretical and empirical perspectives.
- Figures 4 and 7 show that SeRA reduces dependency on response length, suggesting a mitigation of spurious correlations.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior work:** A confident reviewer notes that using DPO implicit rewards for preference relabeling is a natural approach, similar to LLM-as-judge or RM-as-judge methods explored in prior work (citations [2,3] from the review). The data selection via implicit reward gap may simply mitigate the known issue of DPO unlearning high-quality chosen responses when the gap is small, which could be achieved with any reward model or reward-aware algorithms (citations [2,4]). The evidence that SeRA specifically addresses spurious correlations over other mechanisms is considered weak by this reviewer.
- **Connection to theory:** The theoretical upper bound on DPO risk (Theorem 1) is only weakly linked to the proposed SeRA method. The analysis does not account for the effect of multiple iterations on algorithm performance. Additionally, the bound in Remark 1 (Line 254) seems adapted from standard ERM analysis; if there are novel analytical techniques, these should be highlighted, and it should be shown that vanilla DPO fails to achieve such a bound.
- **Insufficient study of iterations:** The paper does not thoroughly investigate how the number of iterations affects performance. While Figures 4 and 7 show iter 3 outperforms iter 2, it is unclear whether further iterations (e.g., iter 4, 5) would yield additional gains or lead to over-optimization.
- **Missing comparisons to strong baselines:**
  - Compare SeRA to a direct DAA that uses on-policy preference data (e.g., generated by GPT-4 or a trained reward model).
  - Compare to iterative DPO that uses DPO implicit rewards to rank preference pairs instead of SeRA’s selection process.
  - Compare to SPPO (Wu et al., 2024) which uses all self-generated response pairs (N(N-1)/2) rather than SeRA’s selected pair per prompt.
  - Compare to using any reward model to filter out similar-quality preference pairs or to reward-aware algorithms from [2,4] (reviewer references).
- **Missing academic benchmarks:** The evaluation lacks results on standard academic benchmarks for coding, reasoning, and math (e.g., HumanEval, GSM8K, MATH), which are common in alignment papers (citations [5,6] from review). Such benchmarks would strengthen the claims of general effectiveness.
- **Computational cost:** SeRA requires computing implicit rewards for each data point, which is more expensive than iterative DPO that does not require per-point reward calculation. It is also unclear whether baseline methods and SeRA use the same computation budget, given SeRA samples multiple responses per prompt.
- **Lack of explanation for over-optimization mitigation:** More intuition is needed on why SeRA reduces dependence on spurious features like response length (Fig. 2). The paper shows the effect but does not explain why the implicit reward margin selection removes this dependency.
- **Theoretical justification for DPO’s equal treatment of samples:** The paper claims (Line 246) that DPO treats all preference samples equally even when true preferences are incorrect. However, under the Bradley-Terry model, preference annotations are stochastic, so this criticism may not be valid from a theoretical perspective; a more detailed explanation is needed.
- **Design choices for the reward scale:** It is unclear if other designs for \( r_t \) in Eq. (7), Section 3.4 are possible or beneficial.
- **Extension to other SAA methods:** It is unclear whether the theoretical results (e.g., uniform covering number estimation) can be extended to other SAA methods beyond DPO.
- **Minor issues:**
  - Algorithm 1, Line 14 should refer to “implicit reward” instead of just “reward”.
  - More descriptions of the benchmarks used should be given (even in the appendix).
- **Questions for clarity:**
  - The paper uses “Ultrafeedback” as a benchmark: is this the test split of the UltraFeedback dataset?
  - Is the derivation of the theoretical bound novel, or does it follow standard ERM analysis? The paper should clarify the novel aspects.
