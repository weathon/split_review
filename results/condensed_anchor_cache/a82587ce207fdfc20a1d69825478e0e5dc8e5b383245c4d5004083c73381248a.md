## Merged Review

### Summary

The reviewers acknowledge the paper’s timely question—whether LLMs can replace components of molecular active learning—and praise the modular framework (COLT) and clear writing. However, the reviews are mixed (scores: 3,3,5,5,6), with significant concerns about experimental design, missing baselines, insufficient novelty relative to prior work (Ramos et al. 2023; Kristiadi et al. 2024), lack of statistical rigor, and inadequate evaluation of the DPO strategy. While one reviewer (R2, score 6) appreciates the scientific honesty of reporting a null result, most reviewers find the conclusions premature or the contributions limited. The paper is seen as a useful ablation but with critical gaps in task realism, comparisons, and documentation.

### Strengths

- **Scientific honesty**: The paper asks an important question, finds that LLMs do not outperform a simple Bayesian baseline, and still reports this null result. (R2)
- **Timely and important problem** in drug discovery and molecular optimization. (R2, R3, R5)
- **Clear organization and writing**: The introduction and background make the paper accessible, even to readers less familiar with active learning for drug discovery. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Systematic experimental framework** decomposing the BO loop into representation, uncertainty quantification, and acquisition, and testing LLM substitution at each stage. (R1, R5)
- **Well-designed LLM modules** for molecular representations, property prediction, and decision-making. (R3)
- **Comprehensive experiments** and reasonable conclusions, with a proposed DPO-tuning recipe. (R1)
- **Code and software (COLT)** provided, though documentation and installability are disputed (see Weaknesses). (R1, R4)
- **Validity of experimental methodology**, including investigation of domain-specific models and synthetic tasks. (R2)
- **Upfront about limitations** of the study. (R2)
- **Important message**: LLMs may not be suitable replacements for domain-specific models with strong inductive biases (e.g., GNNs) in fields where data are largely non-textual. (R3)

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty and prior work**  
   - The contributions are unclear given that Ramos et al. (2023) and Kristiadi et al. (2024) have already demonstrated LLM-based molecular representations and uncertainty quantification in active learning. (R5)  
   - COLT is similar in capabilities to the package from Kristiadi et al. (2024); the only extensions (graph representation, variational inference) are not sufficient to differentiate the two packages. (R5)  
   - If active learning for drug discovery is not a widely researched topic, the significance of the paper is diminished; the community would need a baseline first. (R1)

2. **Experimental task selection does not reflect real-world drug discovery**  
   - The studied properties (solubility, lipophilicity) have well-known structure–activity relationships that are likely reflected in LLM training data, contrasting with real drug discovery where SAR is much harder to predict. (R3)  
   - The candidate pools are small and biased; real-world libraries (e.g., Enamine) exceed millions of compounds, and pharmaceutical companies routinely screen such libraries using DNA-encoded libraries. (R3, R4)  
   - Using MoleculeNet labels is not realistic; a docking score (or a million-scale HTS dataset) would better reflect the goal of identifying novel active compounds. (R4)  
   - The experimental setup does not address the noisy, unreliable nature of real experimental data. (R4)

3. **Missing baselines and acquisition functions**  
   - No comparison with simpler domain-specific baselines (e.g., fixed molecular fingerprints with a GP surrogate, as in ref. 48) to assess the value of deep learning in the small-data regime. (R3)  
   - The GCN/GAT baseline is trained on the full dataset, which is unrealistic; Morgan fingerprints would be a more appropriate output-agnostic baseline. (R5)  
   - The acquisition function set is limited: PoI is insensitive to the magnitude of improvement (UCB recommended instead). Greedy, UCB, and entropy-based acquisition functions are missing. (R2, R4)  
   - The paper does not compare directly with the methods of Ramos et al. (2023) and Kristiadi et al. (2024) on the same tasks under same conditions. (R5)

4. **Statistical rigor and significance**  
   - Confidence intervals are omitted from figures in Table 2; the large standard deviations in Table 1 make it unclear which trends are significant. A generic limitation sentence is insufficient. (R3)  
   - In Table 1, all LLM methods are merged into one line, making it unclear which LLM performs best; the lines strongly overlap, and differences appear insignificant. (R5)  
   - Table 1 contains incorrectly bolded numbers (e.g., “1.61” bolded instead of “1.29” for GAT+VI+EI on ESOL; also the best ESOL result is actually GAT+VI+EI, but the bolding is wrong). (R1, R5)

5. **DPO strategy evaluation is insufficient**  
   - How are the GNN–GP reference trials for DPO generated? If on the same optimization problem, direct comparison with other strategies is flawed. (R3)  
   - DPO requires an initial GNN–GP campaign followed by a second LLM campaign; all experiments across both campaigns should be counted. It is unclear whether the second campaign finds any good molecules not already found in the first. (R3)  
   - Why was Qwen chosen for DPO but not used in any other benchmarks? It is impossible to tell whether gains come from Qwen or from DPO. (R5)  
   - The DPO experimental setup and result generation are unclear; the motivation for creating the artificial target and the chemical interpretation beyond “helping the LLM think like a GP” are not explained. (R5)

6. **LLM choices and scope are limited**  
   - Only three LLMs are sampled (LLaMA, Galactica, ChemLLM) on only three tasks, which is premature for a negative conclusion. (R5)  
   - LLaMA and Galactica are known to perform poorly on scientific tasks (citations: https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13414, https://openreview.net/pdf?id=hSmn7BQZ2v, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18365); fine-tuning helps (https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2409.06080) but was not performed. (R5)  
   - GPT-4, ChemT5, and BioT5 (which can outperform GPT-4 in some cases) are not compared. (R5)  
   - It is counterintuitive that ChemLLM is not better than LLaMA for chemistry tasks; an explanation is needed. (R1)

7. **Incorrect or unclear claims about Bayesian optimization**  
   - Learning a representation is optional in BO (a GP with a graph kernel bypasses vector conversion); there are many such kernels (e.g., in Grakel). (R2)  
   - DKL is not an approximation; its exact posterior is analytically tractable. (R2)  
   - The paper states that pharmaceutical companies do not screen libraries > million-scale, but they do (e.g., Enamine). (R4)  
   - The code chunk at the header of Section 4 claims LLM representations can combine with Bayesian UQ/acquisition, which is misleading. (R5)

8. **Missing detail and reproducibility**  
   - How are the GCN and GP models trained/hyperparameters set starting from no data? (R2, R5)  
   - Number of prompts used for UQ estimates is missing; how are samples selected given token-length limits? (R5)  
   - Exact prompts for “description” subscript models are not shown; they should be in an appendix. (R5)  
   - Format of LLM output when used as a feature extractor (vector size?) is not elaborated. (R4)  
   - Y-axis label and unit of the figure above Table 1 are missing. (R4)  
   - COLT is not well-documented and not installable (code availability). (R5)

9. **Overfitting and regularization**  
   - The paper does not address overfitting for deep learning models (GCN, DKL) trained on very small datasets in a sequential setting; performance is sensitive to regularization. (R2)

10. **LLM uncertainty quantification concerns**  
    - The comparison between a Bayesian baseline and the “stochasticity” of LLM output is not sound if Ramos et al.’s methods are not Bayesian. (R5)  
    - An ablation study is needed to confirm that the real-valued numbers fed into the LLM prompts are correctly harnessed for property prediction. (R5)

11. **Dataset leakage**  
    - The paper does not quantitatively demonstrate the dataset leakage problem raised by Yu et al. (2024); this would justify the synthetic data generation. (R5)

12. **Typographical and presentation issues**  
    - Line 370: “require by magnitude more time” should likely be “require an order of magnitude more time”. (R2)  
    - Table 1: what are the “±” values? Standard error? Standard deviation? (R2)  
    - The synthetic dataset results are described as “more obvious” without analyzing the source of the difference. (R5)
