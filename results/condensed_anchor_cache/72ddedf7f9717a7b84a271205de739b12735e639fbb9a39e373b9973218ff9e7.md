## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AnyDoor, a test-time backdoor attack against multimodal large language models (MLLMs) that uses universal adversarial perturbations on images combined with a static text trigger to elicit harmful responses. The method is evaluated on LLaVA-1.5, MiniGPT-4, InstructBLIP, and BLIP-2. The four reviewers (scores 5, 3, 5, 5; decision Reject) generally find the scenario interesting and the writing clear, but they raise major concerns about technical novelty, threat model clarity, distinction from adversarial attacks, white-box assumptions, limited transferability, and robustness to preprocessing defenses. One reviewer (R2, score 3) is substantially more negative, arguing the attack is misclassified as backdoor and lacks innovation.

### Strengths
- The test-time backdoor setting for MLLMs is novel and interesting, opening a new adversarial threat (R1, R3, R4). R2 also notes the exploration of backdoor attacks on MLLMs is a novel research area.
- The writing and presentation are clear, with good visual explanations (e.g., Figure 1 illustrating the threat model) (R1, R4). R2 finds the research motivation clearly articulated.
- The experimental evaluation is comprehensive, covering multiple MLLMs (LLaVA-1.5, MiniGPT-4, InstructBLIP, BLIP-2) and including extensive ablation studies that demonstrate effectiveness (R1, R3, R4).
- The threat model—backdoor trigger in the text domain activated only when both the image perturbation and text trigger are present—is unique and well motivated (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Technical novelty is limited.** Several reviewers (R1, R2, R4) note that the core technique (universal adversarial perturbations + token-level text triggers) has been extensively studied in prior work (e.g., UAPs, "sudo"-style triggers). R4 specifically points out that Dong et al. (2023b) already applied visual UAPs to MLLMs. R2 adds that jointly optimizing image perturbations and text triggers would be a more interesting contribution.
- **Method explanation is vague.** R1 states that after reading the method section, the principle of backdoor injection during test time remains unclear. The paper dedicates too much space to scenario description rather than technical detail. R3 also requests clarification of the loss function L in Eq (2) and the "Contain" metric (whether it counts all target strings present in the response).
- **No clearly defined threat model.** Both R1 and R2 point out the paper does not specify who the attacker is, who the victim is, the attacker's capabilities, or the concrete scenarios/platforms where the attack would apply. This makes it hard to assess real-world relevance.
- **The attack is more accurately classified as an adversarial attack, not a backdoor attack.** R2 (the most negative reviewer) argues that the proposed method lacks the key backdoor component of injection (via poisoning or weight manipulation) and is instead merely adversarial noise plus a trigger. R2 provides references (USENIX Security 2024, MM 2022, ACSAC 2021) to support that the techniques are already known. This distinction is critical for evaluating the paper's framing.
- **White-box assumption limits practicality.** R3 notes that AnyDoor appears to require gradient access for perturbation optimization (white-box), which is unrealistic for deployed MLLMs accessed as services. The paper does not discuss this limitation.
- **Limited black-box transferability.** The results in Table 9 (R3) show poor cross-model transfer. R4 asks for explicit results on transferability across architectures (e.g., from LLaVA-1.5 to InstructBLIP or BLIP2) and whether the attack can generate semantically similar responses. R4 finds the use of caption evaluation metrics for transfer comparison "hardly meaningful" and requests proper discussion of the low success rate.
- **Attack is easily countered by preprocessing defenses.** R2 raises concerns that diffusion-based purification (e.g., ICML 2022) can remove image perturbations and that special character filtering can neutralize text triggers (e.g., STRIP, ACSAC 2019). R3 also notes that current perturbations (Border, Corner) are visually apparent and could be neutralized by simple cropping.
- **High perturbation budget and lack of stealthiness.** R3 observes that the L∞ bound of 32/255 is noticeable and recommends ablation with smaller budgets (4/255, 8/255, 16/255). R3 suggests randomizing perturbation locations to avoid detection.
- **Missing evaluations on adversarially trained models.** R3 requests testing on MLLMs with adversarially trained vision encoders (e.g., RobustCLIP by Schlarmann et al., ICML 2024) to assess resilience.
- **Experimental details under-specified.** R4 points out that Table 5 (attack under common corruptions) lacks details on how corruptions are applied (e.g., cropping for border attack) and what effect other attack types have. R4 also questions the rationale behind selecting certain adversarial attacks and mitigation methods.
- **Contradiction or lack of clarity in the timeliness argument.** R3 is confused by the explanation in lines 176–183 about trade-offs between capacity and timeliness, noting that data poisoning does not incur overhead whereas AnyDoor requires optimization, so the claimed advantage is unclear. R4 questions the statement on page 2 that adversarial attacks require tset = tact, asking why that is "not desirable."
- **Specific missing ablations and clarifications (overlap with weaknesses):**  
  – R3 asks: Is AnyDoor white-box? Without the image perturbation, can the trigger text alone activate the harmful response?  
  – R3 asks: How is the loss function L in Eq (2) defined?  
  – R3 asks: Does the "Contain" metric count all target strings present in the response?  
  – R4 asks: For cross-model transfer, what does "unfeasible" mean—low success rate? Please provide original results.  
  – R4 asks: What is the technical contribution beyond existing approaches (e.g., Dong et al. 2023b)?  
  – R4 asks: How transferable is the attack across models with different architecture or different finetuning?
