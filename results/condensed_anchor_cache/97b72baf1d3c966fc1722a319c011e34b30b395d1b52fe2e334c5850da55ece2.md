## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces PatchSAE, a sparse autoencoder (SAE) trained on the 11th-layer (second-last) patch tokens of the CLIP ViT image encoder, to extract interpretable visual concepts and their patch-wise spatial attributions. The authors validate that SAE features correspond to meaningful concepts (Section 2.2), show that replacing CLIP features with SAE features preserves class-discriminative information (Section 3.2), and investigate how prompt-based adaptation (MaPLe) changes concept activations. The main finding is that adaptation largely reuses existing concepts from the base model rather than learning new ones. Reviewers’ scores range from 6 to 8, with one reviewer (score 8) more positive about the methodology’s potential, while others (scores 6) are more critical regarding quantitative rigor, clarity, and novelty.

### Strengths

- The paper provides a detailed, fine-grained exploration of CLIP visual features by processing the entire token sequence with SAEs, enabling patch-level spatial attributions.
- It tackles the under-explored topic of adaptation dynamics in foundation models like CLIP.
- The use of both feature-based and example-based summary statistics (Section 2.2) to demonstrate concepts is more comprehensive than prior work relying on singular feature space statistics.
- The replacement experiment (Section 3.2) shows that SAE features capture class-discriminative information well, opening avenues for mechanistic interpretability research (e.g., concept importance per class, training dynamics of concept learning).
- The method transfers SAE methodology from NLP to vision, and visualizations show that SAE features correspond to meaningful semantic concepts at both image and patch levels.
- The approach is simple and leverages well-understood sparse autoencoder principles for interpretability.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of rigorous quantitative evaluation of SAE reliability.** The paper relies heavily on qualitative examples (e.g., cherry-picked cases for “sophisticated concepts” in L249), which is insufficient to support strong claims. Large-scale evaluation using datasets with fine-grained attribute annotations is needed, with quantitative metrics such as feature sparsity, reconstruction error, and correlation with known attributes.
- **Ad-hoc design choices without justification.** Key hyperparameters (e.g., SAE hidden dimension 49,152, expansion factor, use of the second-to-last layer) are not well-motivated. No ablation studies are performed to show sensitivity or robustness of results to these choices. Examples: why the second-to-last layer? Why 49,152 dimensions? Why the specific set of SAE feature statistics (sparsity, label entropy, mean activation, etc.)? Prior work shows ViTs learn different concepts at different depths; generalizability across layers is not explored.
- **Potential distribution shift when applying SAE to adapted models.** The SAE is trained only on base CLIP features (ImageNet) and then directly used to interpret fine-tuned CLIP models (MaPLe). Since MaPLe modifies both image and text encoders, the feature distribution may shift, making conclusions in Section 3.3 potentially inaccurate. The paper does not investigate whether a new SAE should be trained per fine-tuned model, or whether the SAE remains valid.
- **Unclear or poorly defined terminology.** The concepts of “re-mapping” and “re-using” visual concepts (title, Section 3.3) are not properly defined. The distinction between reusing old concepts and acquiring new concepts is ambiguous (e.g., “suddenly capture new concepts” is not operationalized). The mechanism demonstrated in Fig. 6c is vague and lacks concrete explanation.
- **Limited novelty and scope.** Several results are not surprising: CLIP attention maps focusing on features at different scales (Section 3.1) is a sanity check; presence of concepts in CLIP features was already shown in principle by Fry et al. and Daujotas et al. (NLP blogs). The adaptation analysis extends LLM SAE findings (Templeton et al.) to multimodal settings but without substantial new insights. The paper does not clearly relate or differentiate SAE from existing interpretability methods in vision (e.g., concept activation vectors, feature visualization).
- **Poor organization and clarity.** The paper is hard to follow for readers not deeply familiar with SAE mechanistic interpretability. Experimental details are omitted in Section 2 and deferred to Section 4; related work is not presented until Section 5. Section 3.3 (ADAPTATION METHODS AND SAE) is especially difficult to follow, and the main body frequently refers to poorly annotated appendix figures. A more concise and coherent explanation with essential figures is needed.
- **Insufficient discussion of broader implications.** The paper does not discuss whether any results are surprising or challenge conventional views, nor does it elaborate on practical value or applications beyond demonstration.
- **Lack of evaluation on other vision-language models or encoder architectures.** The study is limited to CLIP ViT; convolutional encoders are not covered. It is unclear how hyperparameters or results would transfer.
- **Missing comparison with fine-tuning (not just prompt-based adaptation).** One reviewer requested results with fine-tuned CLIP for comparison; the paper only studies prompt-based adaptation methods like MaPLe.
- **Typographical error:** L146-147: “Then we compute how frequently these features are activated” [typo: “how”].
