## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes the Consistent Iterative Denoising Model (CIDM) for robot manipulation, addressing two perceived inconsistencies in diffusion models: (1) noise supervision varies across different successful action labels, and (2) denoising targets change across timesteps. CIDM introduces a deterministic denoising field based on a signed distance function (SDF) to provide consistent gradients toward nearest ground-truth actions, and a radial loss function that emphasizes actions with minimal noise. The method is evaluated on RLBench, achieving 82.3% average success rate on multi-view and 83.9% on single-view setups, compared to baselines including 3D Diffuser Actor and RVT2.

### Strengths

- The proposal of a deterministic denoising schedule using an SDF is an interesting alternative to traditional diffusion methods, potentially enhancing temporal consistency and guiding the denoising process more directly toward ground-truth actions (Reviewer 1).
- The paper clearly illustrates the problem, motivation, and contributions, and provides theoretical analysis to formalize the issue (Reviewer 3).
- Empirical rigor is demonstrated through extensive experiments on RLBench with clear performance gains over baselines; ablation studies validate the contribution of each CIDM component (Reviewer 2).
- CIDM addresses a practical challenge in robot manipulation: diffusion models often underconverge in unbounded action spaces (e.g., gripper pose), and CIDM offers some mitigation for this important issue (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of probabilistic justification and introduced bias** – The authors claim the score function of conventional diffusion models is biased because it vanishes at local optima, but CIDM itself introduces a strong bias by always denoising toward the nearest target action. This makes the output highly sensitive to initial conditions and does not guarantee that generated samples follow the true policy \(p_{data}(y|x)\). The paper does not address these theoretical concerns or connect to unbiased generative frameworks (e.g., Annealed Langevin MCMC, Song & Ermon 2019). A comparison with theoretical analyses like rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) is missing (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).

2. **Claimed benefits not well-supported** – The underconvergence shown in Figure 4 may be due to the inference-time denoising scheduler rather than inconsistent training objectives; increasing iterations or using DDIM could resolve it. The proposed method does not show improved data efficiency or lower variance across seeds, and RVT2 outperforms CIDM in precision tasks such as block stacking. The bias introduced by CIDM is not clearly justified by the performance gain (Reviewer 4).

3. **Insignificant experimental results** – Only 25 test episodes per task (Reviewer 4) or 4 trials per task (Reviewer 1) were used, leading to wide confidence intervals. For the 18-task PerAct setting, CIDM’s 82.3% success rate has a 90% confidence interval of 0.7899–0.8513, overlapping with RVT2’s 81.4% and 3D Diffuser Actor’s 80.5%. The improvement is not statistically significant. Additionally, only means are reported without standard deviations (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).

4. **Limited scope of evaluation** – The method is only tested on RLBench. Its applicability to other robotic benchmarks or real-world scenarios is unverified. Scaling to long-horizon tasks (beyond 100 timesteps) remains uncertain (Reviewer 2). RLBench demonstrations do not exhibit obvious multi-modality; a benchmark specifically designed for diverse behaviors (e.g., Jia et al., 2024) would better test CIDM’s claimed advantage (Reviewer 3).

5. **Applicability concerns** – The method appears limited to 2D end-effector tasks (tabletop manipulation) and does not demonstrate extension to gripper control or other actuation types (Reviewer 1). The use of 2-norm in action space (containing position, angle, velocities, torques) is not justified; the assumption that distances between actions are well-defined is questionable (Reviewer 2).

6. **Errors in mathematical formulation and unclear presentation** – Equations 12 and 13 state \(\|y - \hat{y}\| < c\) with \(c<0\), which is impossible; it should be \(c>0\). The denoising field direction in Equations 12–14 seems counterintuitive: the gradient should point toward the ground truth, not away from it (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). The paper lacks clarity on several points: what “eliminating the effects of specific successful action” means (line 175); why CIDM converges to only one ground truth in Figure 4 while learning bimodal distributions in Figure 2; how robot state information is processed and multi-view embeddings are fused; and what architecture and parameter counts are used for fair comparison across models (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).

7. **Related work poorly connected** – The related work section includes references (e.g., Inversion by Direct Iteration) without clearly explaining their relation to the inconsistency problem. Speed-focused diffusion methods are listed but not directly relevant to CIDM’s motivation (Reviewer 3).

8. **Missing details and qualitative evidence** – The success probability metric is not clearly defined; values like 82.7% seem inconsistent with only 4 trials per task. Table 1 caption is unclear (“suboptimal performance” undefined). Qualitative results only show the stack blocks task; failure cases shown (Appendix A.4) involve non-multi-modal actions. No rollouts or denoising visualizations for tasks with multiple successful actions are provided, undermining the paper’s core claim (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).

9. **Radial loss lacks theoretical grounding** – The radial loss function, while conceptually sound, is not referenced to similar existing losses or theoretically justified. Its robustness and scalability remain unexamined (Reviewer 2).
