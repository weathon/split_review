## Merged Review

### Summary
This empirical paper identifies a phenomenon termed “the curse of diversity” in ensemble-based deep RL: individual members of a data-sharing ensemble (Bootstrapped DQN, naïve ensemble SAC) perform significantly worse than a single-agent baseline, even though the aggregated ensemble policy often outperforms the baseline. The degradation is attributed to the high proportion of off-policy (other-generated) data in each member’s replay buffer and the agents’ inability to learn effectively from such data. The authors test intuitive fixes (larger replay buffer, smaller ensemble, layer sharing) and find they either fail or undermine ensemble benefits. They propose Cross-Ensemble Representation Learning (CERL), where each ensemble member predicts the value targets of all other members via additional auxiliary heads (N² Q-functions total), and show it mitigates the curse in both discrete (Atari, DQN) and continuous (MuJoCo, SAC) domains.

### Strengths
- **Clear motivation and writing.** The paper is extremely well written, with a logical flow from empirical observation to causal analysis to proposed solution. Figures are clear and appendix details are helpful.
- **Novel observation and careful analysis.** The “curse of diversity” is a surprising and interesting finding. Experiments across multiple environments (Atari, MuJoCo) substantiate the main hypothesis, and the authors introspectively reason about what can and cannot be concluded.
- **Thorough experimentation.** Results are reported with statistical identifiers (confidence intervals, etc.). The paper covers a wide range of baselines and ablations (e.g., passive data ratio, replay buffer size, ensemble size, layer sharing).
- **Contributions.** The work challenges the common assumption that ensemble-based exploration improves performance primarily through better exploration, instead showing that majority voting/aggregation plays a crucial role. It systematically evaluates natural mitigations and provides a first step (CERL) toward solving the problem.
- **Novel method.** CERL is a sound representation‑learning technique that is new (to the reviewers’ knowledge) and outperforms alternatives. The discussion of related representation‑learning methods is appreciated.

### Weaknesses
- **Causal interpretation and alternative explanations.** Several statements (e.g., “it challenges some previous work’s claims that … improved performance comes from improved exploration,” “these results confirm our hypothesis”) are too strong. The experiments cannot fully distinguish between two confounded effects: (1) off-policy data hurting individual learning, and (2) different exploration distributions due to ensemble behavior. Figure 3 shows that BootDQN (indiv.) outperforms the Passive (10%) condition, yet both see the same fraction of off-policy samples—suggesting another factor (likely exploration) is at work. This ambiguity must be explicitly discussed. [Reviewer 2, high confidence]
- **Abductive reasoning.** “These results confirm our hypothesis” (p. 5) is logically flawed; the evidence is consistent with but does not prove the hypothesis. The paper should soften such conclusions and acknowledge alternative explanations. [Reviewer 2]
- **Choice of replay buffer size (Figure 5).** Using a 1 M replay buffer with a small buffer (e.g., 1 M) makes the aggregated ensemble perform no better than the Double DQN baseline, obscuring the potential for larger buffers to enable ensemble outperformance. The effect of layer sharing on this potential is also unclear with this choice. [Reviewer 2]
- **CERL’s mechanism vs. the main hypothesis.** CERL improves individual member performance while retaining high diversity—seemingly at odds with the claim that “diversity” is the root cause. Representation learning does not directly address the core issue of off-policy data (the data remains off-policy); the paper should more clearly explain *why* learning shared representations mitigates the problem. [Reviewer 2]
- **Motivation for multiple policies in continuous control.** In SAC, having multiple *policies* as sample generators (rather than value ensembles) is questionable. Typically, ensemble learning targets the value function (e.g., REDQ, EDAC). The paper should justify why policy ensembles are used here and how they differ from value ensembles. [Reviewer 3]
- **Bootstrapped DQN analysis is incomplete.** Individual BootDQN members performing worse than DDQN is not surprising because bootstrap weighting effectively changes the data for each member. To support the claim that performance improvement is purely from voting, comparisons between bootstrap vs. normal sampling and between majority voting and other aggregation methods are needed. [Reviewer 3]
- **Performance concerns.** (a) SAC results in Figure 2 do not match open‑source implementations; the authors should explain the discrepancy (e.g., different buffer size of 200 k). [Reviewer 3] (b) CERL’s performance gains appear marginal in Figure 7, and BootDQN itself does not seem helpful in many environments. [Reviewer 3]
- **Missing related work.** The paper should discuss [arXiv:2209.07288] (ensemble learning trading off exploration and exploitation via different reward values) and works on ensemble Q‑learning such as REDQ and [OpenReview NOApNZTiTNU]. [Reviewer 3]
- **Scalability of CERL.** CERL requires N² Q‑function heads, which may scale poorly with large ensemble sizes. This is a practical concern (though minor in the tested settings). [Reviewer 1]
- **Lack of distributional RL investigation.** The paper focuses on standard Q‑learning; prior work (e.g., A. Martin et al., *Keep It Simple: Using DQfD with Distributional RL*) suggests distributional RL might handle off‑policy data better. Exploring this direction would strengthen generality. [Reviewer 1]
- **Unanswered questions.** [Reviewer 2] If ensembles are primarily for exploration, poor individual performance might be acceptable as long as the aggregated policy is strong. Why do the authors consider it a problem? Similarly, if the baseline (Double DQN, vanilla SAC) already works well, does that imply exploration is unimportant in these tasks? The paper would benefit from testing harder exploration environments. [Reviewer 2] Also, why does CERL preserve diversity while improving individual performance? [Reviewer 2]
