## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper evaluates cognitive flexibility of three VLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5 Pro, Claude-3.5 Sonnet) using the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST), with four conditions (SaT-VI, SaT-TI, CoT-VI, CoT-TI) and six scoring functions. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting with text inputs yields human-level or superior performance. The authors also explore role-playing to simulate neuropsychological impairments. All reviewers note the use of a well-established test and multiple SOTA models, but raise strong concerns about overclaiming, lack of validation, narrow task scope, missing statistical analysis, and insufficient theoretical motivation. One reviewer rated the paper 5 (others 3) and found the work original and clearly presented, yet still identified the same core weaknesses.

### Strengths
- **Use of a validated neuropsychological task:** The WCST is a classic, reliable measure of set-shifting ability, enabling a potential bridge to human cognition literature (Rev1, Rev2, Rev3).
- **Broad model coverage:** Experiments include three SOTA VLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5 Pro, Claude-3.5 Sonnet), and the design covers four input/modality conditions and six scoring functions, lending some robustness (Rev2, Rev3).
- **Inclusion of human baseline:** Human participants ground the comparisons in a relatable context (Rev3).
- **Originality in exploring cognitive impairment simulations:** The use of role-playing to simulate functional deficits is a novel approach (Rev1, Rev3). Additionally, the work is clearly organized with detailed tables and figures that aid understanding (Rev3).  
- *Note on reviewer disagreement:* While one reviewer (rating 5) praised the thoroughness and originality, the other two reviewers (rating 3 each) emphasized stronger reservations—those are reflected in Weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Overstated claims of human-like cognitive flexibility:** The paper asserts VLLMs exhibit human-level cognitive flexibility, but the WCST alone does not capture the full spectrum of real-world flexibility. Performance on one structured test cannot be equated with the broader, more dynamic construct studied in human cognition (Rev1, Rev3). Both Rev2 and Rev3 stress that a more cautious interpretation is needed, and Rev2 notes human performance was at ceiling, making the comparison non-diagnostic—a version of the task with greater difficulty (e.g., more categories or complex switching) or a different task is required.
- **Simulated impairments lack validation:** The role-playing experiments have no direct comparison with human patient data, no cognitive experiments with real subjects, and no correlation to clinical populations. This makes the claims of “aligning with deficits” speculative. The paper provides few references and no statistical or behavioral comparison with human impairment patterns (Rev1, Rev2, Rev3). Even the more positive reviewer (Rev3) calls for validation against clinical data.
- **Limited task scope and generalizability:** Only the WCST is used. Other established tests of cognitive flexibility (e.g., Dimensional Change Card Sort, Intra-dimensional/Extra-dimensional Set Shift) and more dynamic, less structured tasks are needed to support broader conclusions about cognitive flexibility (Rev2, Rev3). The paper also does not address how findings generalize to other VLLMs or non-visual LLMs (Rev3).
- **Missing theoretical motivation:** No clear theoretical framework justifies why cognitive flexibility should be studied in VLLMs/LLMs, nor what insights the results offer about human cognition. Additionally, the choice of studying VLLMs versus text-only LLMs is not motivated; the visual modality may confound the results and the paper does not discuss this (Rev2, Rev3).
- **No statistical tests or effect sizes:** Numerous comparisons between conditions are made without any inferential statistics (e.g., ANOVAs, t-tests) to determine significance or effect magnitude (Rev2).
- **Potential training data contamination:** The WCST is a well-known task that may appear in the models’ training data. The paper does not test with novel stimuli or manipulated surface features to rule out memorization/pseudo-memorization (Rev2).
- **Insufficient validation of prompt designs:** The heavy reliance on CoT prompting is not ablated across different prompt wordings or conditions. It remains unclear how much performance is due to the model architecture versus the external prompting aid, and the impact of prompt sensitivity is not explored (Rev3). This overlaps with the lack of analysis of prompt wording effects and the need for controls for memorization (Rev2).
- **Need for more visualizations:** The paper could include additional visual aids to clarify results (Rev1).
