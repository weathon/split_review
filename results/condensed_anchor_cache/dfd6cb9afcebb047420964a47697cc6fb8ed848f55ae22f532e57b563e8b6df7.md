## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a systematic framework to study position bias in LLM-as-judge pairwise comparisons, introducing three metrics: Repetition Stability (RS), Position Consistency (PC), and Preference Fairness (PF). Experiments with 12 LLM judges on MTBench and DevBench (22 tasks, ~40 candidate models, 100k+ instances) find that position bias varies by judge and task, is strongly correlated with answer-quality gap, and weakly correlated with prompt component length. The authors claim that capable judges do not exhibit random position bias and offer recommendations for benchmark design. Reviewer opinions diverge sharply: two reviewers rate 5 (positive but note limited actionability), and two rate 3 (criticizing novelty and depth). The positive reviewers highlight the framework and comprehensiveness; the critical reviewers argue contributions overlap prior work, definitions are unclear, and the work lacks sufficient novelty for ICLR.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: The issue of biases in LLM-as-a-judge evaluations is significant given the broad use of LLMs for pairwise assessments (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Systematic framework**: Decoupling Repetition Stability, Position Consistency, and Preference Fairness provides a robust structure for future research on LLM judge reliability (Reviewer 1). Preference fairness is noted as a potentially new concept (Reviewer 2).
- **Comprehensive empirical results**: Experiments cover 12 judge models across two benchmarks, over 100k instances, with detailed evaluations in appendices (Reviewers 1, 3). Some claims are verified by sane experiments (Reviewer 4).
- **Intuitive insights**: The finding that position bias is strongest when answer quality gaps are small is reasonable (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 also finds the recommendations interesting, though less actionable.
- **Nice illustrations**: Explanatory figures and illustrations help convey concepts (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of accuracy evaluation**: The framework studies position bias in isolation from the judge’s accuracy (e.g., selecting the objectively better answer or consensus answer). This leads to unsupported claims, such as suggesting GPT-3.5-Turbo as a cost-effective alternative based solely on preference fairness and position consistency (Reviewer 1). A holistic view of judge quality is missing.
- **Overly general or non-actionable conclusions**: The key insight (position bias when quality gap is small) is interesting but not surprising and gives limited actionable guidance for redesigning evaluations (Reviewers 1, 3). Reviewers expected analysis of prompt template variations, option modes, or system prompt phrasing. Reviewer 4 similarly asks whether prompt engineering (e.g., making the LLM position-bias-aware) could mitigate bias.
- **Unclear definitions and exposition**: Key terms (e.g., “preference fairness,” “primacy preferred,” direction of preference) are not formalized (Reviewer 2). Section 2.2 on factors is unclear. Parts explaining Preference Fairness are not intuitive (Reviewer 4). The paper is difficult to follow in places (Reviewer 3). Many figures (Fig. 1, Fig. 3) have unreadably small text (Reviewers 2, 3). Labelling in Figure 3 is insufficient (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited novelty relative to prior work**: Position bias in LLM evaluations has been extensively studied. Specifically, Zheng et al. (ICLR) on “Large Language Models Are Not Robust Multiple Choice Selectors” cover similar biases and mitigations (Reviewer 2). The analysis also resembles prior work on position bias in summarization (Dey et al. 2020, Kryściński et al. 2019, Jung et al. 2019) (Reviewer 4). Two reviewers (2, 4) argue the contribution does not merit publication at a venue like ICLR, with one suggesting it suits a technical blog or NLP conference.
- **Suspicious error rates**: The reported 100% error rate for LLaMA on DevBench contradicts published results (e.g., “Aligning with Human Judgement: The Role of Pairwise Preference in Large Language Model Evaluators” and “Fairer Preferences Elicit Improved Human-Aligned LLM Judgments”). Similarly, Gemini-1.5-flash shows near 1 error rate on DevBench but 0 on MTBench without explanation (Reviewers 1, 2). Reviewer 1 also asks about discrepancies between two-mode vs. three-mode settings (e.g., no ties in DevBench) and whether that affects repetition stability.
- **Content imbalanced/appendices heavy**: Much important material is relegated to appendices, limiting the main paper’s value. For example, actionable guidelines could be moved from Appendix A to conclusions; results from Appendix G could appear in the main text (Reviewer 3).
- **Metric design concerns**: Reviewer 1 asks why normalized primacy/recency counts are “overly sensitive to dataset size” and how S_min^- and S_max^+ are computed. If the three metrics (RS, PC, PF) are comparable across datasets, is there a significant difference between MTBench and DevBench? Reviewer 4 questions whether Preference Fairness should assign equal value to all completely position-inconsistent situations.
- **Minor errors**: Typo in title (“Judging” misspelled? — reviewer 4). Typos in text: Sec. 3.2 line 353 “arean → arena”; Sec. 4 line 374 “Table 4 → Table 2”; Sec. 4 line 473 “Fig. 1(b) → Fig. 4(b)”. Reviewer 3 notes that appendices should be referred to as “Appendix D” rather than “Appendix. Sec. D.”—and recommends adding a short introduction to DevBench at line 307.
