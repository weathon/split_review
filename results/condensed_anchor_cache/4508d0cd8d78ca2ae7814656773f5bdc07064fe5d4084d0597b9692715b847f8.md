## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a two-step framework (MoSH) for multi-objective optimization (MOO) that incorporates user-defined soft and hard bounds on each objective via a novel soft-hard function (SHF). Step 1 (MoSH-Dense) densely samples the Pareto frontier using random scalarizations and Bayesian optimization. Step 2 (MoSH-Sparse) sparsifies the resulting set by selecting a compact, diverse subset using robust submodular function optimization (RSOS). The authors provide theoretical guarantees for the sparsification step and empirical validation on synthetic problems and real-world applications (brachytherapy, engineering design, LLM personalization). The approach aims to produce a small, user-friendly set of Pareto-optimal solutions that respect the soft/hard bounds and are robust to unknown user preferences.

### Strengths

- **Problem motivation and formulation**: The problem is of considerable practical interest (Reviewer 2). The authors creatively incorporate hard constraints and soft preferences into BO, providing a more nuanced way to express decision-maker preferences (Reviewer 4). The soft-hard function (SHF) and max-min framework for robustness against user preference weights are novel, offering a new perspective on diversity in Pareto frontier search (Reviewer 3).
- **Practical two-step framework**: The two-step process (dense sampling + sparsification) is user-friendly; sparsification makes it easier for human DMs to pick solutions. The practical approximation using submodular optimization is sound (Reviewers 3, 4). The algorithm is motivated by real-world applications with careful illustration of objective design (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical analysis**: MoSH-Sparse comes with provable guarantees on near-optimality of the selected sparse set (Reviewers 1, 4). Sample efficiency and sparsification efficiency are justified with theoretical analysis (Reviewer 1).
- **Empirical validation**: Extensive empirical results on diverse tasks (brachytherapy, engineering design, LLM personalization) show that the approach consistently leads in utility, e.g., over 3% greater SHF-defined utility than the next best in brachytherapy (summarized from abstract, noted by Reviewers 1, 4). The method demonstrates practical applicability (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited algorithmic novelty**:  
   - The dense sampling procedure largely follows existing techniques (Paria et al. 2019), with the primary modification being the incorporation of SHFs into the acquisition function. The sparsification follows standard active learning (RSOS from Krause et al. 2008). Beyond the introduced notions (SHF, utility ratio), components are mostly off-the-shelf (Reviewers 1, 4).  
   - Use of MOBO by Paria et al. (2019) and GPC by Krause et al. (2008) is not clearly stated in the main paper but deferred to the appendix (Reviewer 1).

2. **Lack of theoretical analysis for key approximations**:  
   - The transformation from the max-min to the max-average problem is presented as a practical approximation, but the paper lacks analysis of its impact on solution quality. There is no discussion of how the expectation over scalarization weights affects the quality compared to the original max-min problem. The computational cost of calculating this expectation, especially with many objectives, is not discussed (Reviewer 3).  
   - The relaxation to a decomposable form to apply RSOS is presented without sufficient justification; the paper does not rigorously demonstrate that this decomposition maintains the desired properties of the original problem. The impact of this approximation on final solution quality is not analyzed (Reviewer 3).  
   - No grid or Monte Carlo baseline is provided to approximate the original min in the max-min framework, which would give a reference for ground-truth behavior (Reviewer 3).  
   - Both reviewers note that the method overall does not come with guarantees (Reviewer 2: no guarantees for non-convex problems from random scalarizations; Reviewer 3: heavily built on relaxations/approximations without adequate theoretical insights).

3. **Insufficient empirical validation**:  
   - The comparison does not utilize standard benchmarks, such as CEC 2009 (Zhang et al. 2008) (Reviewer 2).  
   - The comparison does not extend to methods with overall guarantees, e.g., those by Papadimitriou and Yannakakis (Reviewer 2).  
   - For the brachytherapy application, there is no discussion of clinical expert usage/feedback or comparison against interactive MOO methods mentioned in the related work (Reviewer 4).  
   - Experiments compare MoSH-Sparse (step 2) only against step 1 alone (other acquisition functions). This compares max-average with other MOBO acquisition functions, not the max-min framework that the two steps together approximate (Reviewer 3).  
   - The empirical results are not convincing overall (Reviewer 2).

4. **Clarity and presentation issues**:  
   - The paper is rather confusingly written; the fact that the method does not come with overall guarantees may be lost on the casual reader (Reviewer 2).  
   - Key components (Algorithm 3, acquisition function) are deferred to the appendix, hindering understanding (Reviewer 1).  
   - Figures, especially those in Section 5, have poor quality; legends, labels, and title fonts need to be larger. Avoid snake case naming (e.g., MOBO_RS_UCB_Lin) (Reviewers 1, 3).  
   - The utility function definition (Eq. 1) is more complicated than necessary; the saturation points are not well-motivated. Are they needed for the submodular saturation algorithm? This should be clearly stated (Reviewer 3).

5. **Debatable necessity of SHF utility ratio**:  
   - The necessity of introducing the SHF utility ratio is debatable. The algorithm essentially aims for sparse coverage of the Pareto frontier; existing work (Zuluaga et al. 2016) on ε-accurate Pareto sets provides a more principled and interpretable objective. The authors' claim that it resembles hypervolume-based heuristics is not agreed upon (Reviewer 1).

6. **Incomplete discussion of SHF design choices**:  
   - The paper lacks discussion of whether decision-makers can always meaningfully specify soft and hard bounds, what happens if bounds are uncertain or change during exploration, and whether alternative utility function classes exist. The robustness of the method to the choice of SHFs is not assessed (Reviewer 4).  
   - The piecewise linear SHF seems somewhat arbitrary; the choice needs justification (Reviewer 4).

7. **Related work omissions and classification**:  
   - The related work discussion is biased: methods with overall guarantees (e.g., Papadimitriou and Yannakakis) are not even cited (Reviewer 2).  
   - Malkomes et al. (2021) is arguably classified as a Level Set Estimation (LSE) method, but it also addresses identifying sparse, diversified coverage of constrained optimal candidates; this connection is not made (Reviewer 1).  
   - Objective constraints and scalarization weights are handled independently, but it is natural they should share some dependence; why is this not considered? (Reviewer 3)

**Reviewer disagreement on novelty and contribution**: Reviewers 1 (score 5) calls the algorithm novel enough to warrant a 5, while Reviewers 2 and 4 (score 3) and Reviewer 3 (score 5) have mixed views. Reviewers 2 and 4 find the algorithmic novelty limited and the empirical evaluation unconvincing; Reviewer 3 finds the max-min framework interesting but notes the lack of theoretical analysis. Reviewer 1 sees value in the real-world motivation and theory but agrees novelty is limited.

**Questions that overlap with weaknesses** (already incorporated above):  
- How would performance compare to methods with guarantees (Papadimitriou and Yannakakis)? (Reviewer 2)  
- Why not incorporate Zhang and Golovin (2020) for more consistent optimization performance analysis? (Reviewer 1)  
- In experiments, why compare step 2 against step 1 only instead of the max-min baseline? (Reviewer 3)  
- Why handle constraints and weights independently? (Reviewer 3)  
- Why the particular SHF form, and are there alternatives? (Reviewer 4)
