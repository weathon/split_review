## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces SEED, an image tokenizer that discretizes images into a series of discrete tokens. A Causal Q-Former transforms these into pseudo-causal tokens that can serve as both input and target for Large Language Models (LLMs), enabling unified visual understanding and generation. The approach is motivated by aligning visual token representation with the autoregressive mechanism of LLMs. After tokenizer training, SEED-LLaMA is produced via large-scale pretraining and instruction tuning on interleaved image-text data, showing competitive performance on multimodal comprehension and generation tasks, and some emergent compositional abilities.

### Strengths
- Unifying vision and text representation within an autoregressive LLM is a timely and important research direction. The core motivation – learning a 1D token representation that aligns with the LLM’s left-to-right generative process – is well founded.
- The paper provides extensive ablation studies validating key architectural choices: text vs. visual embedding reconstruction, embedding vs. discrete code, causal vs. bilateral visual codes, full fine-tuning after LoRA, and instruction fine-tuning.
- Quantitative results are competitive or surpass existing methods on image-text retrieval, video-text tasks, and generative text-to-image and image-to-image tasks.
- Qualitative results demonstrate interesting multimodal capabilities including compositional and in-context image generation.
- The authors release code and model checkpoints, supporting future research and the open-source community.
- The paper is generally well-written and the assumption behind the proposal is reasonable.

### Weaknesses
- **Methodological clarity and completeness.** The methodology is not self-contained; Section 3.1 lacks equations and it is hard to track which components are trained with which objective. The description of the Causal Q-Former, VQ codebook, and de-tokenizer is insufficient. One reviewer notes that the training procedure and loss functions for each component are unclear, including how the image encoder, codebook, and text encoder interact and which parts are frozen or updated. Another reviewer stresses that all loss functions should be explicitly written out. The paper also does not sufficiently discuss the importance of individual components (image encoder, codebook, text encoder, generation module) and how specific design choices affect performance.
- **Causal dependency claim is unconvincing and causal vs. bilateral ablation is incomplete.** Multiple reviewers question the claim that the Q-Former establishes a causal dependency. The visual encoder (ViT) processes the entire image with bidirectional attention, so each patch representation already contains information from all other patches, leading to potential information leakage even if the Q-Former queries are causally masked. The argument in Sec. 4.3 for preferring causal codes is also weak: previous VQ models have shown that transformers can learn complex dependencies of non-causal codes, and the reported difficulty of the LLM in generating exactly 32 tokens is surprising given that a start token could signal the expected length. The paper does not compare the two models when the number of image tokens is enforced to be exactly 32. Additionally, the analysis of causal vs. bilateral codes focuses on generation tasks (mode collapse) but does not consider the impact on understanding tasks, leaving a gap in the evaluation.
- **Scalability and performance analysis is lacking.** Results in Table 3 show that the larger SEED-LLaMA-I (14B) performs no better (or nearly the same) as the 8B model on some image-text tasks. There is no explanation for this saturation or whether it is a limitation of the method. The paper also fails to analyze cases where BLIP-2 outperforms SEED in image-text retrieval (Sec. 4.1) and the trends in Table 3; a deeper discussion of trade-offs and limitations is missing.
- **Evaluation metrics for generation are insufficient.** Only CLIP similarity is reported in Table 2, which measures semantic alignment but not visual fidelity or realism. The widely accepted FID is not provided, making it difficult to compare generation quality with other generative models.
- **Discrete representation justification is not clear.** The paper motivates learning a good 1D discrete representation but does not adequately discuss why discrete tokens are preferred over continuous ones, especially since continuous representations appear to perform better in Table 1. One reviewer also notes that the original VQ codebook loss was replaced by a simple reconstruction loss with cosine similarity, raising concerns about codebook collapse and whether stop-gradient operations are used.
- **Unclear design choices and missing ablations.** The paper uses two different image encoders (BLIP-2 ViT for encoding to the Q-Former and an unCLIP-SD vision encoder for generation conditioning) without justification; loading both increases memory, and it is unclear if the unCLIP-SD encoder could be used for both. The contrastive loss between vision and text embeddings is applied only on the last token of the Causal Q-Former, but this is surprising because the model could potentially ignore the first 31 tokens and embed all information into the last token (unlike BLIP-2 which applies the loss to all tokens). No ablation justifies its inclusion.
- **Video processing is not explained.** It is unclear how videos are processed: whether individual frames are used to train the tokenizer or multiple frames are passed to the Causal Q-Former. If multiple frames, how is the reconstruction loss adjusted (since unCLIP-SD produces one embedding per frame vs. one per video from the tokenizer)? Also, when passing videos to the LLM, are the frame encodings simply appended?
- **Figure 7 prompts are not clearly defined.** The prompts used to generate the examples in Figure 7 are not specified, making it hard to understand the model’s capabilities and limitations.
- **Unvalidated claim about VQ-VAE.** The introduction states that VQ-VAE captures too low-level information, hindering multimodal comprehension, but no empirical evidence or comparison is provided to support this claim.
- **Missing details on hyperparameters and image token structure.** The paper does not explain why 32 tokens per image and 8192 codes were chosen. It is also not confirmed whether start and end tokens are used for images (as suggested in Fig. 4) and whether the same tokens apply to videos.
