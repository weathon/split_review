## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses the computational cost of retraining a model from scratch when new data arrives, under the assumption that both old and new data are accessible. It identifies four optimization aspects (initialization, regularization, data selection, hyper-parameters) and proposes first-step methods combining standard deep-learning tricks to accelerate convergence while maintaining accuracy. Experiments on CIFAR-100, CIFAR-10, and a subset of ImageNet using ResNet-18 show up to 2.7× speed-ups. The minority reviewer (rating 6) finds the results good and the paper well-written, while the majority (ratings 3,3,3,5) consider the contribution minimal, the setting ill-defined relative to continual learning, the experiments too small-scale, and the claimed “first-step” methods not novel.

### Strengths

- **Problem importance.** The goal of avoiding expensive retraining from scratch when a partially trained model already exists is well-motivated and practically important (R5, R4).
- **Easy to follow.** The paper is generally well-written and accessible (R2, R3, R4).
- **Thorough optimization analysis.** The analysis of SGD optimization in a continual-learning-like setup, focusing on initialization, learning-rate scheduling, batch composition, and loss, provides useful insights (R1, R3). R1 notes this analysis, though not exhaustive, opens a new direction.
- **Practical verification.** A bag of known tricks is empirically verified to work together, which can help practitioners achieve faster training while retaining accuracy (R2, R3). The strategies perform well even in multi-step training scenarios (R4).
- **Simple combination yields good improvements** on some standard datasets (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Setting misaligned with “continual learning” and core contributions minimal.** The assumption that old data is always available contradicts the standard continual-learning premise of no access to past data, and the paper does not address catastrophic forgetting – the key problem in CL (R1, R5). Warm-starting (Ash & Adams 2020) already solves a similar problem without being considered CL; the claimed new setting is therefore questionable (R5). The majority view is that the contribution is merely adapting existing standard optimization tricks (e.g., initialization from Ash & Adams, batch composition from Hacohen & Tuytelaars, 2024) to this setting, which yields minimal novelty (R1, R5). R4 explicitly recommends renaming “continuous training” to avoid confusion.
- **Experimental scope is too narrow.** Experiments are limited to small models (ResNet-18) and small datasets (CIFAR-100, CIFAR-10, a subset of ImageNet). No validation on ImageNet-1k, ViT backbones, downstream tasks (object detection, segmentation), or wide domain shifts (e.g., WiLDS). Efficiency gains are most critical for large models/datasets, yet these are not tested (R1, R3, R4, R5). Reviewer 1 and 4 request results on larger models and datasets, with comparisons to pretrain-based continual-learning baselines (e.g., L2P [Wang et al., CVPR]).
- **Missing comparisons with existing CL methods.** Neither standard CL methods (e.g., those in Sec. 1.2) nor prompt-based methods are compared on performance or compute efficiency. The \(L_r\) metric could be computed for them; without such comparisons, assessing the contribution is difficult (R3). R1 specifically asks whether prompt-based CL methods (L2P) or ViT backbones could be benchmarked.
- **Unclear or inconsistent details.**  
  - Fig. 3: the legend “L2” is ambiguous – does it mean “scratch + L2” or “naive + L2”? A “scratch + L2” curve is missing (R2).  
  - Eq. (6): typo – \(x\) and \(y\) should be \(x_j, y_j\) (R2).  
  - Fig. 6: the curve drawn between \(T_{i-1}\) and \(T_i\) and the computation of costs (lines 415–419) are not clearly explained (R2).  
  - Table 1 vs Table 2(b): cost reduction ratios are inconsistent (e.g., CIFAR-100 70+30 full data: Table 2(b) reports ×1.61/×1.45, Table 1 reports ×1.96/×1.69; 25% data: ×4.74/×4.34 vs ×5.73/×5.32). These discrepancies need clarification (R4).  
  - Line 315: the definition of learning speed uses \(e\) but \(e\) does not appear in Eq. (6) (R4).  
  - Why a 70+30 split on CIFAR-100 rather than a uniform task split? (R1)  
  - There may be a typo in Fig. 6 caption/analysis compared to the main text (Sec. 4.2 uses CIFAR-100 70+30) (R1).
- **Batch composition trick requires explanation.** The batch-composition trick is shared between fine-tuning and scratch, yet Table 1 shows it yields comparable speed-ups for both approaches (almost identical \(L_{100}\) and \(L_{99}\)). Furthermore, comparing “init+reg” and “init+reg+data” (continuous) shows no additional gain. The claim that batch composition gives larger gains when combined with the continuous model is not explained (R2).
- **Generalizability and robustness concerns.** The analysis is not tested under rapid domain shifts (R1) or with optimizers like Adam/AdamW (R1). Section 4.3 suggests the claim may not hold under domain shifts (R3). It is unclear whether the same bag of tricks would benefit existing CL methods or whether they are model-invariant (R1, R3).
- **Accuracy reported only on combined old+new data.** It would be more informative to report accuracy on old and new data separately (R3).
- **Call for broader validation.** Multiple reviewers request larger-scale experiments covering CIL/TIL settings, varied backbones, and more challenging scenarios (R1, R3, R4).
