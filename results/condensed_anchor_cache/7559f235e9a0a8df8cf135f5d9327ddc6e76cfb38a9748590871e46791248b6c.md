## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how different sampling strategies affect the convergence of online Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Theoretically, it proves that uniform sampling achieves linear convergence while two proposed mixed samplers (DPO-Mix-R, DPO-Mix-P) achieve quadratic convergence under exact gradients. Practical variants incorporating posterior distributions and logit mixing are tested on Safe-RLHF and Iterative-Prompt, showing improvements (e.g., +4.5% over vanilla DPO, +3.0% over on-policy DPO on Safe-RLHF; >4.2% over baselines on Iterative-Prompt). Reviewers agree the problem is important, the theory is interesting and non-trivial, and the framework unifies some existing methods. However, concerns center on the highly stylized bandit setting (finite response space, no exploration), gaps between theoretical assumptions (uniform sampling, exact gradients) and practical implementations (posterior-based samplers, ad‑hoc mixing coefficients), experimental validity (same reward used for training and evaluation, risk of reward overfitting), modest empirical gains that appear only at large KL divergence, missing error bars, and unclear presentation (especially the definition of the stopping‑gradient operator).

### Strengths
- **Theoretical rigor and novelty**: Provides a comprehensive convergence analysis of DPO under different sampling strategies, revealing an unexplored separation—uniform sampling yields linear convergence while the proposed mixed samplers achieve quadratic convergence (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 4 finds the quadratic convergence claim significant and impressive.
- **Unifying framework**: The paper develops a general framework of mixtures of heterogeneous sampling strategies that can reinterpret and unify some existing DPO methods (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical enhancements with concrete gains**: The proposed samplers outperform vanilla DPO, on‑policy DPO, and Hybrid GSHF on Safe-RLHF (+4.5% vs vanilla, +3.0% vs on‑policy) and Iterative‑Prompt (>4.2% over baselines). Empirical evaluations align with theory (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Novel perspective**: Sampling strategies for DPO are a novel angle, and the work offers insightful implications for algorithm design (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Timely and important problem**: The problem of alignment and online DPO is highly relevant, and the theoretical results serve as a useful starting point for understanding trade‑offs in sampling (Reviewer 3). The paper is described as well‑written and easy to follow (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental validity and overfitting concerns**:
  - Table 2 uses the same reward function for scoring as was used for training; improvement may come from reward overfitting (Reviewer 1).
  - Gains in Tables 2 and 3 are modest, and Figure 2 shows the proposed method outperforms baselines only at large KL divergence (high‑KL regime), where vanilla DPO does not even reach that KL, raising fairness and overfitting questions (Reviewer 2).
  - No error bars or confidence bounds are reported in Tables 2 and 3, leaving statistical significance unclear (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 1 suggests evaluating with GPT-4o instead of the training reward model.
- **Gaps between theoretical setup and practical implementation**:
  - Theory assumes a finite response space so that uniform sampling is feasible; this neglects the exploration problem critical for exponentially large real‑world action spaces (Reviewer 3).
  - The analysis uses exact gradients; the practical stochastic gradient setting is not covered, limiting direct applicability of the convergence rates (Reviewer 2).
  - Theoretical samplers (Definitions 3,4,5) use uniform distributions over responses, but practical samplers replace these with posterior distributions \(\pi_\theta^{2\beta}\) and ad‑hoc mixing coefficients \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\), without theoretical justification (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 also questions the intuition behind the specific forms \(\pi^{s1} = \exp(r), \pi^{s2} = \exp(-r)\) and whether the mixed sampler is optimal.
  - The paper lacks ablation isolating the contributions of (1) heterogeneous (enhancer) sampling and (2) mixing with the standard homogeneous scheme (Reviewer 2). This leaves the source of improvement unclear.
- **Limited scope and generality**:
  - The bandit setting (no context, small finite response space) does not address issues of generalization or function approximation across prompts or responses; it is unclear whether conclusions extend to more realistic settings (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear presentation, especially the stopping‑gradient operator**:
  - The definition \(\pi^s(y,y') = \mathrm{sg}[\pi^{\mathrm{s1}}(y)\pi^{\mathrm{s2}}(y') + \pi^{\mathrm{s1}}(y')\pi^{\mathrm{s2}}(y)]\) in Definition 1 is ambiguous; the most reasonable guess is the symmetrized form \((\pi^{\mathrm{s1}}(y)\pi^{\mathrm{s2}}(y') + \pi^{\mathrm{s1}}(y')\pi^{\mathrm{s2}}(y))/2\), but this is never clarified. The term “stopping‑gradient operator” is not standard and is not explicitly defined, which is fundamental to the paper (Reviewer 4). Other unclear points include: whether \(\theta\) and \(\pi_\theta\) should depend on \(x\); the distribution of the gradient noise in Definition 2 (sub‑Gaussian vs Gaussian); the intuitive reasoning for the choices of \(\pi^{s1},\pi^{s2}\) in Definitions 4 and 5; the derivation of Eq. (6); the meaning of “parameter difference” in Figure 1; the range of \(\xi_R\) in the Taylor expansion below Theorem 2; the values of \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\eta\) used in experiments; and the derivation of the posterior setting and Eq. (8) (Reviewer 4, also supported by Reviewer 2’s questions about mixing coefficients).
