## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes "decongestion by representation," where a platform learns to display selective item information to reduce congestion and improve social welfare in online marketplaces. The authors develop a differentiable welfare proxy that enables end-to-end learning from consumer choice data. They provide sufficient conditions for decongestion to promote welfare and present experiments on synthetic and real data.

### Strengths

- The paper studies an important, practical problem: reducing market congestion (e.g., vacation rental platforms where consumers chase the same few listings). (R1, R2, R4)
- The idea of using item representations (the subset of features shown by default) to decongest markets is novel and addresses real-world online marketplace concerns. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The differentiable proxy for welfare provides a sound, technically rigorous foundation, enabling the use of machine learning tools to optimize representations end-to-end. (R1, R2, R4)
- The theoretical analysis (sufficient conditions for decongestion to improve welfare) is sound and supplemented by extensive experiments on both synthetic and real data, yielding insightful results. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The modeling framework offers an interesting perspective for designing online platforms and has potential for practical applicability. (R3, R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Setup argumentation.** The claim in the abstract that “the power of a platform is limited to controlling representations” is too strong; platforms also control ranking, recommendations, auctions, and other mechanisms. (R3) The paper does not adequately justify why the congestion problem cannot be resolved by auctions (e.g., second-price, position auctions) or by ranking—these are standard tools that also address demand–supply imbalance with decentralized pricing. (R3) In the introduction, the phrase “under perceived values remain both valuable and diverse” is unclear, and the following example does not clarify why auctions or ranking cannot resolve the issue. (R3) The paper should position representation as one viable tool, not the only one. (R3)
- **Ethical concerns / misuse.** The ethics statement does not alleviate the concern that manipulating representations could be exploited to optimize platform revenue instead of user welfare, potentially hurting user satisfaction. (R1)
- **Technical concerns about the proxy and smoothing.**
    - How tight is the lower bound proxy in Equation (5)? Discussion would be helpful. (R1)
    - The problem requires smoothing a discrete object (representations). One approach penalizes the “no-choice” option; is this reasonable? The outside option can have large market share in demand studies, and the penalty could bias toward selecting items even when the outside option is preferred. The magnitude and sensitivity of this penalty to different market conditions should be further explored. (R4)
    - Need to characterize the loss introduced by smoothing the discrete problem. (R4)
- **Theoretical analysis relevance and assumptions.**
    - The theoretical analysis focuses on the perfect-information equilibrium, which may not be relevant in the online marketplace scenario framed earlier in the paper. (R4)
    - The paper proxies social welfare with congestion reduction, but decongestion itself seems a practical objective; it is unclear why platforms would want to optimize social welfare through this proxy. (R4)
    - The assumption that all item heterogeneity is captured in revealed features is strong and likely violated in practice (unobserved or latent factors influence choices); the analysis should discuss implications for method performance under such violations. (R4)
- **Experiments.**
    - Figure 4: larger mask size \(k\) leads to higher welfare gain when \(d\) is large, which is counterintuitive—one would expect a trade-off between perceived value and congestion (inverted-U curve) because larger \(k\) induces loss in perceived value. An explanation is needed. (R1)
    - How are \(V_{\text{het}}\) and \(V_{\text{hom}}\) formally defined? (R3)
    - The real-data results may depend on how choices are simulated via prices; if there are additional dependencies (e.g., network effects, brand preferences) not captured by the price-based simulation, results may not generalize. (R4)
    - Direct formal links/references to the Appendix for experimental details (both synthetic and real) are needed for reproducibility. (R3)
    - In Section 5.2, “Adam” should be accompanied by a proper citation or more descriptive naming. (R3)
- **Presentation and clarity.**
    - The introduction is unclear about what “representation” means (item-dependent? ranking?); it is only clarified later in the Setup that a single binary mask per market is used. (R3)
    - Welfare scope is ambiguous: the introduction mentions “detriment to users, suppliers, and the platform,” but the Setup defines welfare for users only. (R3)
    - Section 4 would benefit from practical examples where the sufficient conditions are satisfied. (R3)
    - Section 2 mixes ML discussion with problem setup; the learning objective, what is learned (\(\mu\), \(\beta\), etc.), and optimization details are introduced abruptly. It would be better to separate the optimization problem (welfare without expectation) before discussing ML. (R3)
    - Is the use of machine learning a separate contribution or an integral part of the setup? A clear list of contributions is needed. (R3)
- **Training data and method guarantees.**
    - In Equations (6) and (7), it appears that the same set \(S\) is used to train \(f\) and to compute \(\hat{W}_S\). Should the data be split? No guarantees on the viability of the proposed ML approach are given; a discussion of standard practices and potential issues is needed. (R3)
- **Minor issues.**
    - Footnote 3: dynamic pricing (common in ad auctions) can cause sellers to adapt quickly; what assumptions are broken in that case? (R3)
    - Equation (7): writing \(M \in S\) is formally incorrect because \(S\) consists of pairs \((M, y)\); it should be a sum over \((M, y) \in S\) or over \(l=1,\dots,L\). (R3)
    - Section 6: “study of more elaborate user…” – “of” should be “to”. (R3)

**Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (R2, score 8) found no major weaknesses, while another (R1, score 8) raised concerns about tightness, the counterintuitive result in Figure 4, and ethics. Two reviewers (R3, R4, scores 6) identified multiple significant weaknesses in setup argumentation, presentation, ML training, theoretical assumptions, and experimental details.
