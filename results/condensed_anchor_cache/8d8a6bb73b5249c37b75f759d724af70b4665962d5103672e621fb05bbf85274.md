## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes KA-GAT, a graph neural network that integrates Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) with Graph Attention Networks (GATs) for node classification. It applies a KAN layer for feature transformation followed by multi-head attention and neighborhood aggregation (GCN and GAT layers). Evaluated on Cora and Citeseer, KA-GAT outperforms GCN and GAT baselines. Reviewers found several significant weaknesses concerning experimental rigor, novelty, theoretical grounding, and presentation. One reviewer (rating 5, confidence 5) found the work novel and well-written; the other three (all rating 3, confidence 4–5) raised major concerns.

### Strengths
- The idea of combining KAN with GAT is interesting and demonstrates potential for broader applications of KAN in graph neural networks (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The model is flexible and can be integrated with other GNN layers (Reviewer 1).
- KA-GAT outperforms GCN and GAT on the two tested datasets; the KAN layer appears to be an important component based on Table 2 (Reviewer 3).
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow, making it accessible to a wide audience (Reviewer 4).
- It pioneers the introduction of KANs into graph neural networks, which is a novel approach (Reviewer 4).
- Some experiments validate the model’s performance (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental scope and baselines:** Evaluation is limited to two small datasets (Cora, Citeseer) and only two weak baselines (GCN, GAT). Comparisons against state-of-the-art GNNs (e.g., GATv2, APPNP, ADSF GNN, ARMA GNN, and other recent top-venue methods) are missing. Tasks are restricted to node classification; graph classification is not tested. The claim about handling high-dimensional features is not supported because Cora and Citeseer do not have particularly high feature dimensions. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Lack of rigorous experimental details and reproducibility:** Data splits, hyperparameter optimization, number of runs, and standard deviations are not reported. No ablation studies isolate the contribution of individual components (KAN layer, GAT vs. GCN layers, multi-head attention). No statistical significance tests are provided. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Weak novelty and motivation:** The approach is a straightforward combination of KAN and GAT without strong justification for why this specific integration is beneficial. The paper does not explore alternative feature transformation methods or compare against them. Architectural choices (single KAN layer, mixing GCN and GAT layers) are not well-motivated; the rationale for combining multiple GNN layers is unclear. The paper lacks theoretical analysis of why KANs, which approximate arbitrary functions, would uniquely enhance GATs. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4; Reviewer 3 also notes missing motivation for architecture)
- **Missing theoretical guarantees:** No analysis of expressive power, learning capabilities, or theoretical grounding for the proposed method is provided. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** Method descriptions are vague and insufficient for reproducibility. Figures and tables are not well-explained. The Multi-Head Attention GNN Layer is not properly defined or differentiated from standard GAT. A large portion of the paper discusses well-known concepts (e.g., evaluation metrics). The limitations section is generic (suggests model compression/pruning) without specific insights. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Incorrect related work claim:** The statement that KANs have not been widely applied to graph-structured data is false; existing works (citations [1]–[4]) propose KAN-based GNN models. (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of computational cost analysis:** The paper does not quantify the computational complexity or parameter count of KA-GAT relative to baselines, despite introducing a more complex architecture that may contradict KAN’s goal of reducing MLP complexity. (Reviewer 4)
- **Superficial result analysis:** Section 4.4 simply re-expresses table results in text without detailed interpretation, error analysis, or discussion of statistical significance. (Reviewer 4)
