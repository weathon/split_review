## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper unifies existing neural subgraph matching methods into a common design space with axes such as relevance distance, interaction stage, interaction structure, non-linearity, and granularity. Through extensive experiments on all combinations, the authors identify a new configuration that outperforms prior state-of-the-art models (e.g., IsoNet). The work provides insights and design guidelines, but reviewers diverge on the novelty, depth of analysis, dataset scope, and presentation clarity.

### Strengths
- The design-space formulation is convincing, well-articulated, and thoroughly grounded in existing literature (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- Extensive empirical studies systematically evaluate all possible combinations, lending credibility and rigor to the findings (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). Error bars are shown (e.g., Figure 2) (Reviewer 3).
- The results uncover a new, unexplored combination of subcomponents that outperforms the previous state-of-the-art (IsoNet) (Reviewer 2). Empirical studies convincingly demonstrate the design space’s efficacy for characterizing prior methods and devising superior new ones (Reviewer 1).
- The problem formulation is timely and interesting given the plethora of design choices in the subgraph matching literature (Reviewer 3).
- Presentation is neat and clear overall (Reviewer 1). In contrast, Reviewer 2 finds the presentation dense and Figure 1 hard to interpret, and Reviewer 3 notes partial writing clarity issues.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset scale and diversity**: All ten datasets are relatively small (up to 50 nodes) and mostly consist of small molecules, despite the broader applications mentioned (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). There is no evaluation with much larger target graphs (e.g., up to 1M nodes) while keeping queries small (Reviewer 3). This restricts understanding of performance on highly regular graphs, varying graph sizes, different feature distributions, or out-of-distribution settings – such analysis would benefit from synthetic datasets (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of principled explanation**: The paper does not provide a principled (empirical or theoretical) explanation of *why* certain configurations or subcomponents yield performance improvements (Reviewer 2). This limits the contribution and future guidance (Reviewer 2). The best model’s novelty is limited: the only differences from IsoNet are the interaction stage and non-linearity, both previously proposed (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1, however, considers the empirical findings to be effective and revealing.
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Notation inconsistency: The symbols ω and η are used inconsistently between L124 and Figure 1 (Reviewer 1).
  - Figure 1 is hard to interpret; a more human-readable visualization and margin at the bottom are needed (Reviewer 2).
  - Writing clarity: The introductory sentence about “IsoNet’s late interaction approach outperforms GMN, even when GMN’s final score computation is made asymmetric” is unclear to an unfamiliar audience (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 also suggests outlining the summarized findings at the end of the introduction for better orientation.
- **Methodological questions**:
  - Does the use of a hinge distance make sense for set alignment and aggregated-hinge (L216-236)? The authors justify hinge distance for binary adjacency matrices, but node/aggregated node embeddings are real-valued (Reviewer 1).
  - How does this work relate to neural architecture search (NAS)? The authors could better position their work by discussing relevant NAS papers (e.g., arXiv:2403.05064, OpenReview: GcM7qfl5zY) (Reviewer 3).
