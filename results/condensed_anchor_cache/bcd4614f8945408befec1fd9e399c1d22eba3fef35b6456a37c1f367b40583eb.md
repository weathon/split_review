## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents TAGG, a topology-aware graph diffusion model that integrates persistent homology from topological data analysis. Two main contributions are proposed: (1) a Persistence Diagram Matching (PDM) loss using the 1-Wasserstein distance to align generated graphs’ topological features with those of the target; (2) a topology-aware attention mechanism that incorporates homological features into the denoising network’s self-attention. The method is evaluated on several graph generation benchmarks and applied to real brain network data. Reviewers note solid technical execution and comprehensive experiments, but express concerns about incremental novelty, unclear differentiation from prior work (especially DiGress), insufficient theoretical depth, computational overhead, and missing analysis of conditioning on topological descriptors.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important gap: existing graph generation methods fail to preserve topological information; the proposed PDM loss and topology-aware attention directly target this issue.
- The idea of integrating persistent homology into diffusion-based graph generation is interesting and timely.
- Solid and thorough technical execution, with extensive comparisons against multiple baselines and detailed ablation studies.
- Demonstrates effectiveness on real-world brain network data, showcasing adaptability to complex, topologically rich datasets (e.g., ADHD-200).
- The use of the 1-Wasserstein distance within the PDM loss is versatile and effectively enhances structural awareness.
- The proposed attention mechanism differs from standard self-attention by encoding persistent homology and using the Q matrix in the forward process, addressing graph structural characteristics.
- Comprehensive empirical evaluation: results are reported across multiple metrics (MMD degree, clustering, orbit, etc.) and datasets; visualizations support the claims.

### Weaknesses
- **Methodology clarity**: The paper inadequately connects the Preliminaries (Section 3, many topological concepts) to the Methodology (Section 4). The application of these concepts is not clearly explained, making Section 3 feel unnecessary for understanding the core method.
- **Motivation**: The motivation for preserving topological features is not convincingly grounded. The authors do not analyze how existing methods fail to capture topological invariance, nor do they provide theoretical justification for why topology-awareness improves generation.
- **Incremental novelty and theoretical depth**: The core diffusion framework is heavily based on existing work (Vignac et al., 2023; DiGress). The integration of persistent homology, while useful, appears incremental. The topology-aware attention is a straightforward modification of standard attention by incorporating topological features. The paper lacks theoretical guarantees about topological preservation and does not analyze why the proposed modifications are theoretically beneficial.
- **Differentiation from prior work**: Clearer distinction from DiGress is needed; the conceptual advancements in preserving global topological features via persistent homology are not emphasized. Similarly, the paper does not sufficiently contrast with other homology-based approaches in TDA for graphs (e.g., Hofer et al., 2020).
- **Conditioning on topological descriptors (mu, Q)**: The generative model takes mu and Q as inputs during generation. It is unclear how these are obtained for new graphs. If test graph mu/Q are used, the generated result is artificially close to the original; if training mu/Q are used, this introduces bias. This undermines the claim of unconditional generation.
- **Loss emphasis on degree distribution**: The PDM loss primarily improves degree metrics, while improvements on other metrics (clustering, orbit) are less pronounced. This raises questions about the method’s ability to preserve other topological properties.
- **Computational complexity and scalability**: Thorough complexity analysis of the graph generation process—especially the persistent homology computation—is missing. No comparison with baselines in terms of time or resource requirements is provided. Scalability to larger graphs is not addressed, and performance on very large graphs is unknown.
- **Evaluation on small graphs**: Performance gains on small graphs (e.g., Ego-small) are not obvious, suggesting limited generalization ability. The significance of metric differences on small-scale datasets is not discussed.
- **Noise sensitivity and robustness**: The model’s sensitivity to different noise types and intensities in the denoising process is not detailed.
- **Differentiability / end-to-end training**: The paper does not clarify whether the topology-aware attention and PDM loss are differentiable and can be trained end-to-end. The claim of differentiability is not justified.
- **Sensitivity to filtration function**: The choice of filtration function for computing homological features may affect performance; no analysis is provided.
- **Lack of interpretability**: What new features does the method learn? The paper does not illustrate hidden representations or provide insight into what the topology-aware attention captures.
- **Missing diverse metrics**: The evaluation could be strengthened by additional graph property metrics (e.g., spectrum, diameter) to provide a more comprehensive assessment.
- **Extension to dynamic graphs**: It is unclear whether the approach can be extended to dynamic graphs where topology evolves over time.
- **Reviewer disagreement on novelty**: One reviewer (score 3) is notably more critical, emphasizing the incremental nature and lack of theoretical depth, while another (score 6) finds the idea interesting and the brain application valuable. This contrast is preserved.
