## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DiffAR, a diffusion probabilistic autoregressive model for end-to-end raw speech waveform generation. It processes overlapping frames (500 ms, 250 ms overlap) sequentially, conditioning each frame on a portion of the previous one to enable unlimited duration with constant per-step memory. The model can be conditioned on phoneme sequences, energy, pitch, and duration. Evaluated on the single‑speaker LJ Speech dataset using MUSHRA listening tests (45 raters) and ASR metrics (Whisper). Claims superior quality with natural acoustic details (e.g., vocal fry) and variations from stochastic sampling.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and technical soundness** – The autoregressive diffusion formulation is novel and fundamentally supports unlimited speech duration; memory does not scale with sequence length (R1, R3).
- **Quality and naturalness** – Generates more natural speech with finer details (e.g., vocal fry). MUSHRA scores: 3.9 percentage points higher than WaveGrad 2 and 2.0 percentage points higher than FastSpeech 2 improved. Lower CER/WER than WaveGrad 2 on Whisper ASR (R1, R2, R4).
- **Conditioning and variation** – Explicit control of prosodic features (energy, pitch, duration) plus inherent stochasticity of diffusion enables variation compared to deterministic baselines like FastSpeech 2 (R1).
- **Ablation study** – Comprehensive ablation of model components demonstrates their efficacy (R1).
- **Reproducibility** – Code and samples provided on anonymous GitHub (R3).
- **Presentation** – Well written, well organized, with an excellent literature review and clear mathematical exposition; listening tests are well conducted and described (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Weak baselines and missing comparisons** – Only compared against FastSpeech 2 + HiFi‑GAN and WaveGrad 2, both released in 2021 or earlier and no longer SOTA. Missing comparisons or discussion with other diffusion‑based TTS models: Diff‑TTS, Grad‑TTS, FastDiff, PriorGrad, ProDiff (R1, R3). Also missing VITS, YourTTS, VALL‑E, Voicebox (R1). The paper does not adequately differentiate DiffAR from these related variants; the claim of better representation than Mel‑spectrograms is not sufficiently supported (R1). The selection criteria for “most high‑performing and relevant” baselines is unclear (R1).
- **Confounded comparison** – The evaluation mixes differences in the TTS front‑end (duration/pitch/energy predictors) and back‑end (vocoder). It is unknown whether quality gains come from the proposed diffusion vocoder or the particular front‑end (which appears less sophisticated than Tacotron 2) (R2).
- **Lack of synthesis speed measurement** – Despite a section titled “COMPUTATIONAL LIMITATIONS AND SYNTHESIS TIME”, only memory usage is reported; no real‑time factor (RTF) or absolute inference speed is given (R1, R3). As an AR + DDPM system, DiffAR is expected to be substantially slower than non‑autoregressive baselines like FastSpeech 2. Quality comparisons without accounting for the efficiency trade‑off are unfair (R3). Reviewers request exact numbers or a speed‑versus‑quality plot.
- **Unacknowledged assumption change** – The paper breaks full joint diffusion modeling into conditionally independent frames but does not acknowledge this strong change in assumptions. The key difference from other diffusion‑based vocoders is not elaborated (R2).
- **Limited experimental scope** – Evaluation is on a single‑speaker dataset (LJ Speech) only. No multi‑speaker or out‑of‑domain experiments are conducted (R1, R4). Extension to multi‑speaker is only mentioned briefly as future work (R1).
- **Memory consumption criticism (minority reviewer contrast)** – Two reviewers note memory efficiency for unlimited length, but one reviewer (R4) points out that for normal‑length sentences (≤ 512 words) DiffAR consumes *more* GPU memory than FastSpeech 2 and WaveGrad 2. Simple baselines (splitting sentences into chunks, synthesizing separately, then concatenating) could also generate unlimited waveforms with lower computation. It is not shown whether DiffAR outperforms such chunking baselines when computation is matched. R4’s rating (5) is lower than the other reviewers (6 each) on these grounds.
- **Long frame length in conditional setting** – In the unconditional setting, a long frame (500 ms) provides global context, but in the conditional setting global information could be provided by the linguistic representation. No ablation study on frame length in the conditional setting; shortening the frame might reduce memory but would make the model closer to a vocoder than a full TTS system (R4).
- **Unclear conditioning details** – It is not clarified whether the linguistic representation is aware of the whole sentence text or only the current frame, nor whether a length regulator aligns text to sample points (R4).
- **Practical relevance of unlimited length** – Extremely long sentences (> 512 words) are rare in natural language. The claimed advantage for unlimited duration may not be critical for typical use cases, while the cost is higher memory and slower speed for normal sentences (R4).
- **Unconditional generation quality** – One reviewer (R1) asks about the proportion of intelligible versus random words in unconditional generation, implying evaluation gaps.
