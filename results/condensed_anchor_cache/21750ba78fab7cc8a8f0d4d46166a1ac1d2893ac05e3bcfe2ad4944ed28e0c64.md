## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Physics-encoded Message Passing Graph Network (PhyMPGN) to model spatiotemporal PDE systems on coarse unstructured meshes with limited training data. It integrates a GNN with a numerical integrator, a learnable Laplace block encoding the discrete Laplace–Beltrami operator to enforce physical feasibility, and a boundary condition padding strategy. Extensive experiments show accurate predictions, state-of-the-art results, strong generalization (e.g., over Reynolds number, initial/boundary conditions), and significant gains over baselines like MGN (DeepMind). Some reviewers note the contributions are clear and innovative; others question novelty relative to prior work.

### Strengths
- Integration of physics-encoded components (learnable Laplace–Beltrami operator) within a message-passing GNN is seen as novel and innovative by some reviewers (3,4), and ensures solutions remain physically consistent and accurate on irregular meshes (2,4,5).
- The method is simple, easy to understand, and likely compatible with other GNN-based approaches (1).
- Combination of GNN with a second-order Runge–Kutta scheme for temporal dynamics is effective and allows accurate spatiotemporal modeling (2).
- Boundary condition padding strategy improves convergence and accuracy (1,2,4,5).
- Extensive experiments across diverse PDEs and geometries; strong generalization over initial conditions, boundary conditions, and Reynolds numbers (1,2,4,5). The model outperforms the strong DeepMind MGN baseline by over 50% in some cases (4).
- Ablation studies substantiate the contributions of the key components (2,5).
- Strong generalization despite training on small datasets (5).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: Several reviewers noted that the connection between GNNs and the Laplace (–Beltrami) operator is not new, e.g., Li et al. (AAAI 2018) and baselines in Section 4.1 (1). The boundary condition padding strategy also resembles prior work – Horie et al. (NeurIPS 2022) – which should have been cited and compared (1). One reviewer (3) found the approach fairly original, indicating disagreement on this point.
- **Unclear necessity of learning the operator F**: The paper aims to learn the nonlinear operator $F$ in Eq. (1), but does not explain why this is needed when $F$ is known and given (3). Moreover, the approximation in Eq. (6) has limitations (e.g., it would not handle $F = (\Delta u)^2$ well); the form of $F$ in Eq. (1) should be specified more carefully (3).
- **Missing computational efficiency analysis**: No comparative analysis of training/inference speed or computational cost relative to other methods (neural or numerical) (2,5). The influence of important parameters (number of time steps, dataset size, segment size $M$) on computational requirements is not investigated (2).
- **Insufficient justification for coarse meshes**: The rationale for focusing solely on coarse meshes is not explained; discretization errors and the inability to capture fine-scale features are not discussed (5). The claim that traditional numerical methods require fine meshes and small time steps overlooks implicit schemes and the CFL condition trade-offs (5).
- **Time-series training loss (Eq. 7)**: Using only the first and last time step in the loss function contradicts the pushforward trick (Brandstetter et al. 2022), which suggests computing loss only on the last step (and adding noise yields better performance). The authors should clarify their motivation and demonstrate that their approach is superior (1,2). Why not compare full rollouts? (2)
- **Unaddressed limitations**: Applicability to PDE systems without a Laplace operator is not discussed (2). For inviscid first-order PDEs (wave, advection, inviscid Burger, Euler), adding a graph Laplacian could blur traveling features (4). Simulation over very many time steps (e.g., weather) is not considered (2). The padding strategy for boundary conditions on non-straight/curved boundaries is unclear (2). Generalization to 3D spatial data is not addressed; obstacles and needed architectural changes are not discussed (3,5).
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: The paper is hard to read; the introduction could be shortened, and more detail is needed on model training, handling of boundary conditions on curved/irregular boundaries (2). Section 4 is difficult to follow as the actual PDEs and their coefficients are not specified (3). The justification for why padding is needed to incorporate boundary conditions is insufficient (3).
- **Choice of temporal integrator**: The use of second-order Runge–Kutta (RK2) over RK4 or forward Euler is not justified (4). RK2/RK4 are only applicable to non-stiff problems due to stability limitations; the authors do not discuss which scheme would be suitable for stiff PDEs (3).
- **Missing experiments and clarifications**: 
  - The mathematical/physical interpretation of $z_i$ in Eq. (5) and the activation functions used are not explained (3).
  - The distinction between coarse and fine meshes is not formally defined (3).
  - The segment size $M$ in Section 3.4 should be analyzed for its influence on results (2). It is also unclear how the $T/M$ loss functions are combined and what constitutes ground truth data (3).
  - Experiments with a varying diffusion coefficient (e.g., $F = \text{div}(D\nabla u)$ with $D=1$ or $D=1+4x+5y$) would help understand approximation properties (3).
  - It is not clear how boundary conditions are used as "a priori physics knowledge" (2).
  - For convection-dominant problems, further clarification is needed (4).
