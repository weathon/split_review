## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces two novel matrix stepsize sketch Compressed Gradient Descent (CGD) algorithms for minimizing matrix-smooth non-convex functions. Theoretical convergence guarantees are provided in both single-node and distributed settings. The matrix stepsize captures the objective’s structure, enabling faster convergence compared to scalar stepsizes. The layer-wise structure of neural networks is exploited to design efficient compression mechanisms. Using a block-diagonal smoothness assumption, the proposed Det-CGD algorithm reduces communication bits without degrading total communication complexity (free compression). An optimal matrix stepsize expression is derived as a guideline for rapid convergence.

### Strengths
- **Novel algorithms and convergence theory.** Two matrix stepsize sketch CGD algorithms are introduced with convergence guarantees for both single-node and distributed non-convex settings (Reviewer 2, S1; Reviewer 3 also notes the investigation of matrix stepsizes and convergence analysis).
- **Use of matrix smoothness to reduce communication complexity.** The matrix smoothness structure is leveraged to lower distribution communication complexity (Reviewer 1). The block-diagonal structure of neural networks is exploited in compressor design (Reviewers 1 and 2, S2).
- **Free compression.** The proposed compressors reduce the number of bits broadcast per iteration without incurring additional communication complexity (Reviewer 1).
- **Optimal stepsize derivation.** The authors derive the expression for the optimal matrix stepsize for CGD, providing a guideline for achieving fast convergence (Reviewer 2, S3).
- **Exploitation of model structure.** The layer-wise design of compressors takes advantage of the layered architecture common in neural networks (Reviewers 1 and 2).

**Note on disagreement:** Reviewer 3 acknowledges only the generic strength of investigating matrix stepsizes and convergence analysis, but otherwise finds the paper’s weaknesses significant (rating 3 vs. 6 from the other two reviewers).

### Weaknesses
- **Stringent and unrealistic assumption (Assumption 2).** Inequality (4) is assumed to hold for the entire dataset rather than mini-batches, which is misaligned with typical neural network problems. Neural networks are high-order polynomial fitting (non-convex) and require third- and higher-order terms; a varying matrix \(D^t\) would capture curvature better than a constant stepsize \(D\). Even if the assumption holds, the global convex majorization in (4) may be loose, leading to slow convergence (Reviewer 2, W1).
- **Limited experimental validation.** The experiments only cover logistic regression with a non-convex regularizer, not the claimed neural network architectures. The neural network architecture used is not defined, so the layer-wise structure benefit is not empirically demonstrated (Reviewer 2, W2).
- **Lack of error feedback.** The compressed gradient in (3) is a direct compression of the full gradient without error feedback. Error feedback techniques are standard for handling compression errors in distributed optimization. Without error feedback, compression errors can accumulate, potentially causing a significant performance gap compared to methods that incorporate error feedback. It is not clear whether the proposed matrix stepsize can be extended to include error feedback (Reviewer 3; this is a major limitation).
- **Absence of standard distributed techniques.** The paper does not consider stochastic gradient descent (SGD), local updates, or partial participation – techniques widely used in distributed optimization for scalability and reduced communication. This limits the applicability of the proposed method in practical distributed learning scenarios (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear derivation of the distributed algorithm.** It is unclear how the single-client det-CGD1 algorithm translates to Algorithm 1 in the distributed setting. The connection is not clearly established, making it difficult to understand the theoretical basis of the distributed algorithm (Reviewer 3).
- **Neighborhood term in Theorem 3.** The convergence bound includes a neighborhood term that becomes significant when \(\Delta^{\text{inf}}\) is large (i.e., when the compressed gradient deviates strongly from the true gradient). This could cause instability or slow convergence, especially in heterogeneous distributed settings. The analysis does not provide clear guidance on how to select \(\lambda_D\) to balance convergence rate and neighborhood size (Reviewer 3).
- **Suboptimality of the “optimal” stepsize.** The claimed optimal stepsize is only the minimizer of the right-hand side of the normalization upper bound in (10) under certain conditions. It is not justified why this yields a globally optimal stepsize (Reviewer 2, W3).
- **Impractical log-det minimization.** Solving the log-det minimization problem with constraints in (21) is challenging and computationally prohibitive, especially when the dimension \(d\) is large (Reviewer 2, W4).
- **Lack of justification for determinant normalization.** The determinant normalization is not a common approach; further references and reasoning for using the normalized determinant are needed (Reviewer 1).
- **Unaddressed questions on algorithm design and clarity.** 
  - It is unclear why the compressed gradient is presented as a replacement for the stochastic gradient; is the gradient stochastic? (Reviewer 1)
  - No analysis of the computational complexity of the operations underlying equation (15); are there further algebraic tricks? (Reviewer 1)
  - The comparison between CGD1 and CGD2 in Figure 1 is not explained; what is the take-home message? The authors did not provide an experiment where the two methods perform differently (Reviewer 1).
