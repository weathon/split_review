- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes TIMAR, a self-supervised representation learning framework for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). TIMAR trains a joint transition model (surrogate world model) to predict future joint observations, using a BYOL-style loss to learn encodings that implicitly reconstruct the global state and capture agent interactions. The auxiliary loss is added on top of existing MARL algorithms (applied to MAT and QMIX). Experiments are conducted in SMAC, MA-MuJoCo, and MAQC. Reviewers’ overall assessment is mixed (scores: 5, 3, 3, 3). One reviewer is more positive about the significance and potential of the approach, while the other three find limited novelty, insufficient experimental results, and clarity issues.

### Strengths
- **Generalization experiments** highlight a useful property for MARL practitioners (Reviewer 1).
- **Potential significance** – Self-supervised representation learning methods are still scarce in MARL, and TIMAR could spur further research on this topic (Reviewer 1). One reviewer notes that using a world model to predict future information has not been extensively investigated in MARL (Reviewer 4).
- **Generality and applicability** – TIMAR is designed to be integrated into different existing MARL methods and is tested with two base algorithms (MAT and QMIX) across three cooperative benchmarks (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 4 notes the experimental section is wide and well-structured).
- **Straightforward approach** – The method is described as easy to follow by one reviewer (Reviewer 3; contrast: other reviewers find clarity poor).
- **Performance improvements** – TIMAR outperforms strong baselines (MAPPO, HAPPO, QMIX, MAT, MA2CL) in several tasks, especially in MA-MuJoCo (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
**Limited novelty and similarity to prior work**
- The approach is incremental: it combines BYOL-style self-supervised loss (previously used in single-agent RL) and agent‑modeling ideas from [Papoudakis et al., 2021] (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3 notes dynamic transition SSL is already effective in single-agent and results on MA‑MuJoCo are expected).
- TIMAR is highly similar to **MA2CL** [Song et al., 2023]. Equations (1), (3), and (6) in the paper correspond to Equations (3), (6), and (9) in MA2CL. The paper does not highlight this similarity or clearly explain the key differences (Reviewer 2). The only stated difference (“joint‑embedding predictive architecture”) is insufficiently justified (Reviewer 2).
- The paper does not address a MARL problem from a novel angle but rather transfers SSL techniques from single-agent settings (Reviewer 3).

**Clarity and presentation**
- The method description is hard to parse (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). **Key issues:**
  - Roles of the multiple encoder networks are unclear; one paragraph per encoder would help (Reviewer 1).
  - Symbols in Figure 1 (e.g., \(\hat{s}_t\)) do not appear in the text, making the figure hard to connect to the description (Reviewer 1).
  - Essential design choices lack motivation: why BYOL’s self‑supervised loss rather than alternatives, and why TIMAR implicitly reconstructs the global state (Reviewer 1).
  - Section 3.1 should be in Background or Related Work, not the Method section (Reviewer 2).
  - The attention mechanism and encoding are explained with too much jargon and long sentences; an intuitive explanation of what the model does and how it benefits agents is missing (Reviewer 4).
- The paper would benefit from careful rewriting and polishing (Reviewer 4).

**Insufficient and unconvincing experimental results**
- **SMAC results** – TIMAR combined with QMIX shows no significant final performance improvement over QMIX alone across the four tested maps (Figure 5; Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Results overlap with baselines, failing to highlight the algorithm’s effectiveness (Reviewer 3). Some tasks appear not to have converged (Reviewer 3).
- **Value loss and Q‑value analysis (Section 4.2)** – Plotting value loss and Q‑value estimates does not provide an intuitive explanation of why TIMAR works (Reviewers 1, 2). In Figure 7, the tasks for value loss and Q‑values do not align; TIMAR shows faster initial value loss reduction but intersects baselines later; QMIX achieves higher Q‑values in the corridor task, raising questions about TIMAR’s effectiveness (Reviewer 3).
- **Generalization and robustness results (Section 4.3)** – The results are hard to understand and do not clearly demonstrate improved generalization (Reviewer 2). In Figure 8, it is unclear which curve corresponds to TIMAR (no grey curve visible as per the label) (Reviewer 2). TIMAR also produces worse generalization in the “front_shin” scenario in MuJoCo (Reviewer 1).
- **Inconsistent performance** – TIMAR is outperformed by baselines in several environments: the first task in quadcopter control and the corridor environment in SMAC (Reviewer 1). Improvements are predominantly shown in MA‑MuJoCo; elsewhere they are small (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines** – Lack of results for MAT and MAPPO on SMAC tasks (Reviewer 3). No comparison with FACMAC, which is potentially more sample‑efficient in MA‑MuJoCo than MAPPO (Reviewer 3). The reasoning behind the baseline selection (MAPPO, HAPPO, QMIX, MAT, MA2CL) is not provided (Reviewer 1).
- The analysis would be stronger if it examined the content of TIMAR’s learned representations rather than value-loss magnitude (Reviewer 1).

**Computational overhead**
- TIMAR introduces a complex network architecture (joint transition model, multiple encoders) that likely incurs significant computational overhead (Reviewers 3, 4). It is questioned whether such complex machinery is justified for the observed small improvements (Reviewer 4).

**Unanswered questions from reviewers**
- Why does TIMAR use different integration approaches for MAT (loss applied to intermediate layer) and QMIX (additional layer before RNN)? Does this affect results? (Reviewer 3)
- Have you tried applying TIMAR to algorithms other than QMIX in SMAC (e.g., on scenarios where QMIX struggles)? (Reviewer 4)
- In MA‑MuJoCo, how are agents configured to perceive information about other agents and the global state? (Reviewer 3)
- What is the exact computational overhead of TIMAR? (Reviewer 3)
- Why does TIMAR implicitly reconstruct the global state? (Reviewer 1)
- What is the motivation for using BYOL’s loss? (Reviewer 1)
- Why does a lower value loss imply better performance when using TIMAR’s representations? (Reviewer 1)
- Compare TIMAR and MA2CL applied to the same base MARL algorithms to ensure fair comparison and explain why TIMAR outperforms MA2CL. (Reviewer 2)
