## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MILCA, a Multiple Instance Learning method that uses counting and attention. It identifies informative features via statistical tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney, t-tests) and encodes each bag as a count of these features, then applies classifiers with varying numbers of learnable parameters. The method is evaluated on classical MIL datasets, a simulated dataset, a new Wiki dataset, and a TCR-based disease classification task. The authors report improved performance over state-of-the-art baselines and provide source code.

### Strengths
- **Performance and efficiency** (R1, R2, R3): MILCA achieves better or competitive accuracy on several benchmarks, with up to 3% higher average accuracy than SOTA, and is computationally efficient due to few or no learnable parameters.  (R1, R2, R3)
- **Simplicity and fresh perspective** (R2, R3): The core idea of counting features across bags is simple yet effective; the approach is noted as a fresh perspective compared to complex embedding methods.  (R2)
- **Intuitive explanation** (R3): The analogy of explaining Instance Space, Bag Space, and Embedding Space using a book and its chapters is highly intuitive.  (R3)
- **Code and reproducibility** (R3, R4): The authors provide source code and detailed experimental procedures, aiding reproducibility.  (R3, R4)
- **New dataset** (R2): The paper introduces a new Wiki dataset for MIL.  (R2)
- **Practical significance** (R4): Applying feature selection in MIL may be meaningful in specific scenarios where features are well-normalized and highly indicative.  (R4)

*Note:* Reviewer 3 is notably more positive, rating the paper 5 and praising its clarity and efficiency, while other reviewers rate 3 and raise significant concerns about novelty, writing, and reliability.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty** (R1, R2, R3, R4): The counting-based approach is a straightforward extension within MIL; the method reuses existing modules (Mann-Whitney test) and does not introduce new theoretical contributions.  (R1, R2, R3, R4)  The concept of counting in MIL is not new (R2).  The contribution is not highly relevant to MIL and does not essentially advance the field (R4).  Reviewer 3 explicitly states “in my view, this paper lacks sufficient novelty.”
- **Writing and structure issues** (R1, R2): The paper is poorly written, with an unclear structure and lack of coherent motivation.  (R1, R2)  R1 says “writing is of a poor standard, difficult to understand,” and “structure is not professional.”  R2 says “paper clarity should be improved; lacks a clear and intuitive structure.”  R3, however, finds the paper well-written (score 3/3 for Presentation).  This is a notable disagreement.
- **Incomplete and inconsistent experiments** (R2, R3):  
  - Missing comparisons with baselines mentioned in Section 4.4 (e.g., BDR and MI-SDB) in Tables 1 and 2.  MI-SDB reportedly outperforms MILCA; without these comparisons the second novelty claim (L77) is not valid.  (R2)  
  - Performance differences between model variants (MILCA1, 2, 3, 4) are not explained; the best variant depends on the dataset.  (R2)  
  - Different model variants are used for different datasets (classical vs. simulated/Wiki/TCR) without rationale.  (R2)  
  - Datasets are simple; the method should be evaluated on more complex, high-dimensional datasets (e.g., video anomaly detection datasets like UCF-Crime, ShanghaiTech, Avenue, XD-Violence) and compared with recent methods (e.g., [1,4]).  (R3)  
  - The paper focuses only on bag label prediction; current trends in MIL emphasize instance label prediction (e.g., for video anomaly detection).  Whether MILCA can handle instance prediction is unclear.  (R3)
- **Reliability concern in implementation** (R4):  
  In `C1.py` (line 22), the code uses `np.sign(stat_train[i, 1] - stat_train[i, 0])` to determine whether a significant feature is positive (PF) or negative (NF).  According to SciPy documentation, `stat_train[:,0]` is the test statistic and `stat_train[:,1]` is the p-value.  Subtracting the statistic from the p-value is statistically unsupported and likely a mistake; the intended operation would be `np.sign(stat_train[i, 2])` (mean difference).  This error propagates to C2 and C3, potentially invalidating the feature selection process and the derived conclusions.  (R4)
- **Assumptions and limitations** (R1, R4):  
  - The method is a naïve Bayesian estimator that assumes feature independence, which may conflict with real-world applications.  (R1)  
  - Features must be highly indicative and preprocessed to have clear statistical significance (e.g., bag-of-words).  In many real scenarios (cancer diagnosis, anomaly detection) features may have inconsistent scales, cannot independently indicate class, or are correlated (e.g., deep features).  The paper does not address how to handle these issues.  (R4)  
  - The “tabular data” term is confusing and overly restrictive; the method should be applicable beyond tabular data (e.g., images).  (R2)
- **Conceptual and methodological questions** (R1, R2):  
  - How to optimize the parameter *M* (number of top features)?  Provide a figure showing *M* optimization.  (R1)  
  - Why are three classification models (C1, C2, C3) designed?  What problem is each suited for?  (R1)  
  - In Table 2, C1 has 0 learnable parameters; how is the decision made without parameters?  (R2)  
  - When is the average vs. median used in feature selection (L139)?  (R2)  
  - Why is two standard deviation shown instead of one in Table 1?  (R2)
- **Cross-validation misunderstanding** (R1): The paper states “results are the average of 10-fold cross validation” but then describes training/validation/test splits (64%/16%/20%), which does not match standard k-fold cross-validation.  This is a serious issue.  (R1)
- **Lack of theoretical analysis and motivation** (R1, R2): The motivation for designing the method is not specific; there is no theoretical analysis explaining why the method works better than existing approaches.  (R1)  The introduction lacks deep analysis of problems in existing MIL methods and how MILCA addresses them.  (R1)
- **Missing definitions** (R3): Some annotations are used without formal definitions (e.g., *v_i* in the “Novelty” section, point 4).  (R3)
