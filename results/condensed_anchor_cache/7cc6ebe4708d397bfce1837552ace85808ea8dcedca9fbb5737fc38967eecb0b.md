## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates an overlooked problem in time series contrastive learning: “bad” positive pairs that violate the assumption of shared semantic information between views. Two types are identified: noisy positive pairs (where the model learns noise patterns) and faulty positive pairs (where augmentation destroys important temporal patterns). The authors propose a Dynamic Bad Pair Mining (DBPM) algorithm—a lightweight, plug-in module without trainable parameters—that tracks the historical training loss of each pair via a memory module and down-weights identified bad pairs. Evaluations on four large-scale real-world time series datasets and several SOTA contrastive methods show that DBPM improves linear evaluation performance and provides robustness against injected bad pairs. Reviewers generally see the problem identification as novel and the solution as simple and effective, but raise concerns about ablation completeness, threshold heuristics, early-training accuracy, and the lack of marked improvement over baselines in some views.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem identification & analysis**: First study of the bad positive pair problem in time series contrastive learning. The paper convincingly illustrates two variants (noisy and faulty), provides theoretical analysis, and demonstrates detrimental effects on representation quality. One reviewer (score 8) praises the connection between this issue and label errors in supervised learning.
- **Empirical observations**: On a simulated dataset, noisy positive pairs show very low training loss, faulty positive pairs show high loss, and both exhibit consistently low variance—making them outliers on a mean-variance plane. This finding is described as “intriguing, insightful, interpretable, and plausible.”
- **Algorithm design**: DBPM is a simple, model-agnostic plug-in with no trainable parameters, introducing only minimal memory overhead. The dynamic identification technique based on historical loss statistics is well aligned with the empirical observations. Four reviewers agree the method is clearly described.
- **Presentation and clarity**: The paper is well organized, with clear motivation, illustrative figures and tables, and an informative appendix. Code and a simulated dataset are provided for reproducibility.
- **Evaluation**: Extensive experiments on multiple datasets and frameworks demonstrate consistent improvements. Additional controls show DBPM confers greater robustness against injected bad pairs compared to baseline contrastive learning. One reviewer (score 8) finds the evaluation “comprehensive, impressive, and of high quality.”

### Weaknesses
- **Ablation study limitations**: One reviewer (score 6) notes that the roles of the memory module and transformation module are not independently isolated. Missing experiments include: (a) removing the memory module entirely and identifying bad pairs based solely on current-epoch loss statistics; (b) comparing simply dropping bad pairs versus smooth weight reduction. Another reviewer (score 6, conflicting opinion) claims the ablation studies are thorough. The specific missing isolations should still be addressed.
- **Heuristic threshold selection**: The identification of bad pairs relies on two β parameters chosen heuristically. Several reviewers ask whether more principled statistical methods could automatically determine optimal thresholds. A systematic sensitivity analysis across datasets is needed, along with justification for the chosen values and discussion of generalizability.
- **Algorithmic complexity**: The discussion of computational costs is superficial. Reviewers request a detailed time and space complexity analysis, including how the memory module scales with number of samples and epochs, a breakdown of cost per step (mean/variance calculation, condition check, weight calculation), and comparison to baseline methods.
- **Early-training inaccuracy & error propagation**: In the early stages when the model is not well trained, identification may be inaccurate, and errors could propagate (reviewer 4). Hard samples that are beneficial but have large losses could be misidentified as faulty pairs; the authors should explain how to avoid this.
- **Assumption stringency**: The noisy alignment assumption that ξ_i ≫ z_i may be too stringent for real-world scenarios where noise is a smaller fraction of the signal (reviewer 2, score 5).
- **Uniqueness to time series**: The paper could better clarify why noisy/faulty positive pairs pose a unique challenge for time series data, distinguishing them from other data types (reviewer 2).
- **Limited technical innovation and marginal improvement**: One reviewer (score 5) finds the methodology—monitoring historical losses and down-weighting—not substantially novel, and the empirical results do not show a marked improvement over existing baselines. This contrasts with the more positive reviewer (score 8) who finds the novelty and improvements “impressive.”
- **Evaluation limited to InfoNCE loss**: All experiments use InfoNCE; it is unclear whether DBPM works with other losses such as supervised contrastive (SupCon) loss (Khosla et al., NeurIPS 2020). This missing comparison is requested.
- **Missing distinction between noisy vs. faulty pairs**: An ablation to isolate the individual impacts of noisy versus faulty pairs is missing. It would be insightful to know whether specific datasets are predominantly influenced by one type.
- **Equation 3 design rationale**: In Eq. 3, the mean loss is calculated using e−1 epochs instead of e (the current epoch). The rationale behind this design choice is not explained (reviewer 4).
- **Other applications**: Evaluation is limited to classification; it would be beneficial to assess DBPM on forecasting and other tasks.
- **Extension to negative pairs**: The paper does not consider negative samples. Could the method be extended to handle false negatives (e.g., similar time series incorrectly treated as negatives)?
