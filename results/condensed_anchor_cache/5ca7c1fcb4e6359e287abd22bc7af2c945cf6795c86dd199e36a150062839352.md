## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FedDualMatch, a federated learning (FL) algorithm that communicates distilled datasets instead of model parameters to eliminate error accumulation from local training. Each client generates a synthetic dataset via Local Distribution Matching (LDM) based on Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), then the server applies Global Gradient Matching (GGM) to align gradients across clients and reduce the heterogeneity exacerbated by distillation. The method is supported by theoretical proofs of convergence and differential privacy, and experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, Digital-Five, and Office-Home show accuracy and convergence improvements over baselines including FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedDM, and MFL. However, several reviewers expressed concerns about computational overhead, communication cost, privacy guarantees, limited evaluation, unclear notation and algorithm details, and unrealistic assumptions (e.g., no client sampling, Assumption 4). Two reviewers (score 5) were more positive, praising the motivation and theoretical foundation, while the other three (score 3) were more critical, citing major weaknesses in clarity, scope, and practicality.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and tackles the important problem of data heterogeneity in FL by communicating distilled data rather than model updates. The motivation is strong, with impressive findings in Figure 1 showing how distillation can exacerbate heterogeneity.
- The combination of Local Distribution Matching (LDM) at clients and Global Gradient Matching (GGM) at the server is a novel and well-explained approach. The intuition behind using LDM for feature extraction and GGM for reducing client drift is clearly presented.
- The paper provides detailed theoretical justifications: a bound on the difference in testing loss between models trained on distilled vs. original data, a convergence guarantee (though the exact rate is unclear), and a differential privacy analysis.
- Experiments on both controlled heterogeneous datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10) and naturally heterogeneous datasets (Digital-Five, Office-Home) demonstrate consistent improvements in accuracy and convergence speed over existing methods. An ablation study (Figure 5) verifies the effectiveness of each component.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear notation and algorithm description**: The mathematical notation is confusing; variables such as \(S_k\), \(w^{t+E}\), and \(W_t^{S_k}\) are not clearly defined before use (Reviewer 2). The algorithm description is incomplete: how is the synthetic dataset \(S_k\) minimized (the optimization method is unspecified)? How is the fine-tuning step in line 283 performed? The caption of Figure 2 and Figure 3 is too sparse, making them hard to interpret (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 5). Figure 4 legend contains a typo (“FedBi-Match” instead of “FedDualMatch”) (Reviewer 1).
- **Overly strong assumptions**: Assumption 4 (gradients on distilled data closely approximate those on full data) may be unrealistic, as gradient errors from dataset distillation can be large in practice (Reviewer 2). Assumptions 3 and 4 appear restrictive; citations to support their prevalence are missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Computational and communication overhead**: The method imposes high computational cost on both clients (due to layer-wise feature alignment and repeated distillation) and the server (due to M iterations of gradient matching) (Reviewers 3,4,5). Communication of synthetic datasets can be expensive and grows with dataset size (Reviewers 1,4,5). A breakdown of computation and communication complexity compared to baselines is missing.
- **Privacy concerns**: Although differential privacy (DP) is claimed, the privacy guarantee is hand-wavy. The parameters \(\sigma_r, \sigma_s, \sigma_d\) are not defined, and the algorithm for adding noise (to gradients and radius \(R_t\)) is unclear (Reviewer 1). DP feels like an add‑on rather than an integral part of the solution (Reviewer 4). Additional analysis (e.g., membership inference or reconstruction attacks on distilled data) and a clear statement of how noise parameters balance privacy and utility are needed (Reviewer 5).
- **Limited experimental validation**: Experiments use only MNIST and CIFAR-10 for controlled heterogeneity (plus Digital-Five and Office-Home for natural heterogeneity). For a 2024 publication, larger datasets (CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet, ImageNet-1K) are expected (Reviewer 2). The number of clients is fixed at 10, which is too small to demonstrate robustness and generalizability (Reviewer 5). Client sampling (a realistic FL scenario) is not considered; results may not hold with a larger number of devices per round (Reviewer 3).
- **Inconsistent results**: Table 1 and Figure 4 show a discrepancy for SCAFFOLD on CIFAR-10: accuracy for \(\alpha=0.01\) (17.14) is lower than for \(\alpha=0.005\) (33.83), which is counterintuitive. Similar inconsistencies appear for other methods (Reviewer 5).
- **Limited communication rounds in experiments**: Figure 4 shows results up to only 20 communication rounds, which may not capture full convergence for some baselines (Reviewer 5). The x-axis should be communication size (in bytes) rather than rounds for fair comparison (Reviewer 1).
- **Performance stagnation in low heterogeneity**: FedDualMatch shows little improvement when client data distributions are similar (Reviewer 5). The authors should explain why LDM becomes less effective in that regime and whether GGM can overcome it. Relatedly, Figure 5 shows that accuracy drops when IPC exceeds 10; the reason is not discussed (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient baselines and contextualization**: The comparison includes only one comparable data-sharing baseline (FedDM). Other data-sharing or privacy-preserving methods (e.g., [1][2]) should be included to better contextualize performance (Reviewer 3). Adaptive FL methods (e.g., [3]) are not compared (Reviewer 3).
- **Contribution and differentiation from FedDM**: The novelty beyond FedDM (Section 4.2 on GGM and the error ball in Section 4.1) should be more clearly highlighted in the introduction/contributions list (Reviewer 1). Theorem 2 does not give an exact convergence rate because the term \(C\) contains \(\mathbb{E}[L(w_{t-1};\mathcal{D})]\); the authors should work out the actual rate (Reviewer 1).
