## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper extends Nichani et al. (2024) by introducing a synthetic framework of interleaved Markov chains with varying lags, where a token at position \(t\) depends on a token at a context-dependent lag \(t-k\) rather than a fixed \(t-1\). Transformers must both estimate transition probabilities and select the correct causal lag in-context. The authors theoretically construct a 3-layer disentangled attention-only transformer that implements *selective induction heads* to solve this task, proving asymptotic convergence to the maximum likelihood solution. They train the same architecture with gradient descent and show that learned attention maps closely match the constructed circuit across settings (contiguous lags, non-contiguous lags, many lags, single-head). Appendices E, G, H provide detailed constructions for special cases.

### Strengths

- Well-written and clearly motivated; Figure 1 and the visualizations in Section 4 and the appendices (E, G, H) are particularly helpful in following the notation and construction. (R1, R3)
- Introduces a novel synthetic task (interleaved Markov chains) that is a reasonable, non-trivial extension of prior work (Nichani et al. 2024) and deepens insight into in-context learning (ICL) capabilities. (R1, R4)
- The 3-layer transformer construction is well motivated: each layer is designed for a specific purpose (capturing transition probabilities, aggregating probabilities, selecting the lag). (R3)
- Strong empirical evidence that the constructed circuit matches the solution found by gradient descent: attention maps from the trained model are highly similar to the theoretical construction. (R1, R2, R5)
- Discusses more general cases: single-head transformers (superposition), non-contiguous lags, and many lags. (R5)  
- The task could serve as a sanity check for alternative architectures to test whether they match transformers' ICL abilities. (R4)
- Great care taken in presenting technical details of the theoretical solution. (R2)

### Weaknesses

- **Task generality and real-world relevance**: The interleaved Markov chain task, while a step forward, remains an idiosyncratic toy setting. Multiple reviewers question how much closer it brings us to the complexity of natural language, where token interactions are semantic, non-stationary, and involve multiple interacting factors. (R1, R2, R3, R5)  
  - Reviewer 1 (R1) notes that the induction head work was compelling because it was observed in actual LLMs; it is unclear whether selective induction heads would be found in models trained on real textual data.  
  - Reviewer 5 (R5) specifically asks for verification on open-source LLMs (out-of-distribution) and points out that demonstrating causal selection on data unrelated to Markov chains would be more convincing than training directly on the same task.  
  - Reviewer 3 (R3) asks whether a fixed "correct lag" ever genuinely appears in real-world data and suggests scaling to more complex, non-linear causal structures.  

- **Architecture specificity and generalizability**: The analysis uses a *disentangled* transformer architecture (separate Q, K, V matrices per head), which makes the analysis tractable but raises concerns about generalizability to standard transformers with coupled QKV interactions. (R2)  
  - R2 speculates that moving to a standard architecture would "make things a lot less pretty."  

- **Missing experiments and ablations** (often phrased as questions):  
  - **Number of heads**: When training with fewer than \(K\) heads per layer (where \(K\) is the number of lags), the model still solves the task; the authors suggest head-level superposition but do not provide attention-map analysis to confirm this. (R1)  
  - **More than \(K\) heads**: Not tested; would duplicate heads emerge? (R1)  
  - **Number of layers**: Is 3 layers strictly necessary for selective induction heads? What happens with more layers—do some become identity functions? (R1)  
  - **Empirical support for Claim 1**: The connection between the experimental results and Claim 1 (which is stated as not yet formally proven) is not clearly explained. (R3)  
  - **Performance comparison of 1-head vs. multi-head (3H)**: The paper does not thoroughly explore why the single-head model performs differently from the multi-head construction, nor how performance changes when the number of heads deviates from the number of lags. (R3)  

- **Title and framing**: The title ("Selective Induction Heads: How Transformers Select Causal Structures in Context") overclaims generality; the work is largely theoretical and addresses a specific synthetic setup. The framing should more transparently communicate that the contribution is theoretical. (R2)  

- **Causality**: The attention-map similarity is correlational; the paper would benefit from causal analysis methods to strengthen the claim that the trained model truly implements the same mechanism. R2 provides relevant references: (https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01416, https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04709, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09087). (R2)  

- **Scope of impact**: While the paper deepens insights into ICL, it does not provide a fundamental shift in understanding nor clearly inform practical decisions. (R4, R5)  
  - *Note: R4 considers this subjective and does not factor it strongly into evaluation; R5 is more strongly critical of the narrow scope.*  

- **Scalability**: The empirical evaluation only covers synthetic data with modest lag counts; real-world causal structures are often more complex, noisy, and non-stationary. Efficiency considerations (e.g., number of heads scaling with lags) are discussed in appendices but not systematically analyzed. (R3)
