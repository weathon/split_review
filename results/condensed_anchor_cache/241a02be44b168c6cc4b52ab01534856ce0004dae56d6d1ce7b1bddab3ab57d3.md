## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies demonstration order sensitivity in in-context learning (ICL). It uncovers a clustering property: prompts sharing the same first and last demonstrations have closer embeddings, with first-demonstration clustering usually stronger. The property is explained via causal attention mask and positional encoding. Based on this, the authors propose Cluster-based Search, which reduces selection/ordering complexity from factorial to quadratic, saving 92–100% execution time while maintaining comparable performance to exhaustive search across classification and reasoning tasks.

### Strengths
- The proposed cluster-based search is simple, practical, and greatly reduces search time, making a clear practical contribution (R1, R3).
- The partial derivative analysis in the theoretical derivation is original and clearly argues for the clustering property (R1).
- The cluster-based search method is well explained (R1).
- The paper is well-structured with clear explanations, making objectives and contributions easy to follow (R2).
- The theoretical analysis is thorough, with robust proofs supporting the proposed mechanisms (R2). *Note: R3 questions the correctness of some proofs; see weaknesses.*
- Experiments are detailed and varied, demonstrating the method’s efficacy across multiple tasks. R2 finds them comprehensive; R1 and R3 consider the experimental scope limited (see weaknesses).
- The performance, especially efficiency improvement, is very promising (R3).

### Weaknesses
**Clustering evidence and analysis**
- The 2D projections (Figure 1, Figure 4) show unconvincing visual separation, especially for GPT-2 (Figure 1) and classification tasks. Quantitative clustering quality metrics (e.g., silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index) are needed to support the visual claims (R1).
- In Figure 3(a), clusters also share the same second demonstration with high percentage; for two bottom panels the second-demonstration overlap exceeds that of the last demonstration, potentially conflicting with the paper’s main claim about first/last dominance (R3).
- The analysis of the last demonstration’s role is less convincing, particularly lines 340–346 (R3).
- More thorough correlation analysis is needed: e.g., do prompts with close representations yield similar scores? What is the standard deviation? (R1, also raised as a question).
- A variant of Figure 3b using full demonstrations instead of chunks would help clarify the clustering (R1).

**Theoretical concerns**
- Proposition 4.1 is counter-intuitive: it claims embeddings of two prompts become identical if they share the same first token, but the proof’s assumption (“if causal attention mask is applied, then x_1(t) = x'_1(t) for all t ≥ 0”) is not justified. If the proposition holds, it would imply only the first demonstration matters, contradicting the paper’s emphasis on both first and last (R3).

**Experimental scope and missing comparisons**
- Too few experiments vary the number of demonstrations or pool size; scaling behavior with larger pools and more demonstrations is unclear (R1, R3).
- Only k=4 demonstrations are used; larger values (e.g., 10, 20) are needed to test the claim that middle demonstrations are unimportant (R3).
- Missing a random baseline for demonstration selection in Table 1; random ordering should be included as in Table 2 (R3).
- Insufficient comparison with other selection methods (e.g., diversity‑ or uncertainty‑based) (R1).
- No table showing actual time taken per method alongside performance scores; a clear quantitative time comparison is missing (R1).
- Models used (GPT‑2, LLaMA 1/2) are somewhat outdated; newer architectures (LLaMA 3, Phi) should be tested to assess generalizability of the clustering property (R2).
- Datasets and tasks are limited; no mathematical reasoning task (e.g., GSM8K) is included, which could reveal different order sensitivities (R2).

**Ablation and relative importance of first vs. last**
- No ablation study that uses only the first or only the last demonstration for clustering, making it hard to assess their relative contributions and whether the method could be simplified to consider only the first (R2).
- The impact of varying the number of intermediate demonstrations on performance is not analyzed in depth; statistical significance tests are missing (R1).
