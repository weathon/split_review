## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper is the first to study the adversarial robustness of Graph Transformers (GTs), proposing adaptive gradient-based structure attacks for five representative GT architectures (including those with specialized attention mechanisms and Positional Encodings based on random walks, pair-wise shortest paths, and the Laplacian spectrum). The attacks introduce relaxation on attention scores to enable gradient computation. Evaluations across node and graph classification tasks and multiple threat models (structure perturbations, node injection) show that GTs can be catastrophically fragile, even under small perturbation budgets. The authors then leverage their adaptive attacks for adversarial training, substantially improving robustness. Reviewers agree this is an important first study but raise concerns about the attack’s generalizability, effectiveness compared to simpler baselines, insufficient comparisons with established attacks/defenses, and unclear presentation of key formulas.

### Strengths
- **First study on adversarial robustness of Graph Transformers** – establishes a foundation for a previously unexplored area (all reviewers).
- **Adaptive gradient-based attacks designed for five GT architectures** – covers diverse attention mechanisms and positional encodings (all reviewers).
- **Multiple experiments across tasks and threat models** – includes node classification, graph classification, structure perturbations, and node injection (R1, R2, R3).
- **Adversarial training improves robustness** – demonstrates that using the adaptive attacks for adversarial training effectively reduces vulnerability (R1, R3, R4).
- **Solves the gradient problem for structure attacks on GTs** – introduces relaxation on attention scores to make non-differentiable operators differentiable (R2; noted as a design principle, though effectiveness is disputed).
- **Good organization and clear presentation** – some reviewers found the paper well-organized and convincing (R1, R4). Note: other reviewers found the writing confusing (see Weaknesses), so there is disagreement.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of generalizability** – the attack requires specific design adjustments for each GT model, making it an empirical study rather than a universal, systematic attack method (R1, R4). R4 further notes the approach is “specifically designed for a particular defense.”
- **Adaptive attack not consistently stronger than simpler baselines** – in many experiments (Figures 3–6), the adaptive attack is weaker than transfer attacks (R4). For Graphormer, GRIT, and SAN under a small budget (5%), accuracy drops are minimal compared to other architectures (R3, Fig. 1b). The ablation study (Tables 1–3) shows relaxing non-differentiable operators often provides no improvement or only minimal gains (R4). The paper does not compare with non-adaptive gradient-based attacks without relaxation (R1, R4), and R1 questions whether the adaptive attack significantly outperforms grey-box attacks.
- **Insufficient attack baselines** – comparisons are limited to random and transfer attacks; established methods such as Metattack and Nettack are missing (R1, R3, R4). R2 asks whether the method can be adapted to meta-attacks.
- **Insufficient defense baselines** – the adversarial training evaluation lacks comparison with other robust training techniques, graph cleaning methods, or detection approaches (R1, R3).
- **Unclear mathematical explanations** – the paper does not justify why the log function is used in the attention score relaxation (Eq. 6) or provide theoretical intuition (R2, R3). Similar lack of explanation for Eqs. (7)–(10) (R3). R2 also notes typos: the last adjacency matrix in Eq. (6) should be *\tilde{A}* not *A*, the symbol ‘H’ in Eq. (3) is undefined, and it is unclear whether the adjacency matrix in Eq. (2) already includes self-loops. R3 points out that *deg(v_i)* in Eq. (7) is used without definition.
- **Experiment setting confusion** – it is unclear whether the attack targets training data, test data, or both; the formal attack loss function is not defined (R2). R3 cannot tell which of the three listed attack methods is proposed by this paper.
- **Limited contribution** – given the weak effectiveness and lack of universality, the work appears highly incremental (R4). R1 similarly calls the attack “overly empirical” and lacking a systematic methodology.
- **Minority reviewer doubts about the discontinuity claim** – R2 questions whether positional encodings and attention mechanisms are truly discontinuous because the adjacency matrix is discrete; they ask if making the adjacency matrix continuous naturally solves the problem without needing relaxation on attention scores. R2 also asks whether a continuous but non-differentiable function can still support gradient-based attacks (Principle II).
