## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Dynamic Discounted CFR (DDCFR), a framework that uses RL (formulated as an MDP) to learn a dynamic discounting scheme for CFR iterates, replacing fixed hand-designed schedules. The policy is trained via evolution strategies on small games and generalizes to larger unseen games. Convergence guarantees are provided, and the method is modular, applicable to DCFR and PCFR⁺ variants. Experiments on multiple imperfect-information games show faster convergence and improved performance, with ablations and learned models included.

### Strengths
- Simple and clear core idea: dynamically weighting CFR iterations using a learned policy.
- Clear, well-motivated presentation; the paper is easy to follow and answers most questions.
- Comprehensive experimental evaluation with ablations, showing generalization to unseen games and superiority over fixed discounting schemes.
- Modular design: the framework is applied to DCFR, CFR⁺, and PCFR⁺, demonstrating broad utility.
- Convergence analysis is provided (Theorem 1), maintaining CFR’s regret guarantees up to constants.
- Previous reviewer concerns are addressed (including comparisons to PCFR⁺ and Greedy Weights).
- Learned models are provided in supplementary material for reproducibility.
- A comprehensive set of games is considered in experiments (Kuhn, Leduc, Liar’s Dice, Goofspiel, etc.).

### Weaknesses
- **Scalability / computational cost**: Computing exact exploitability at each decision step is a hard constraint; the paper’s claim about parallelizing this computation is questionable because the parameters depend on the exploitability after the latest iteration (R1). Wall-clock time comparisons with DCFR and baselines are missing, so the computational overhead is not directly validated (R3, R4). The training time for the neural policy is never explicitly stated (R2).
- **Limited early-iteration visibility**: Performance is only shown from iteration 500 onward; earlier behavior is hidden, making it hard to assess initial convergence speed (R1, R2). A log-scale x-axis starting at iteration 1 would better compare algorithms (R2).
- **Experimental robustness**: Experiments appear to be run only once; multiple runs are needed to convincingly demonstrate that DDCFR consistently outperforms DCFR and is not due to luck (R3).
- **RL design choices not fully explored**: The multi-task nature of the learning problem is not clearly motivated – since the task is identical across games, one would expect to condition the policy on the game as an observation (R1). PPO is dismissed as a poor choice due to long horizons and sparse rewards, but no empirical comparison or justification is given (R1). The paper jumps to RL without considering simpler bandit-based approaches for optimizing discount rates (R3).
- **Notational density and organization**: The paper is heavy on notation; some definitions (e.g., information set reach probability, interval history reach probability) could be deferred to the appendix to improve readability (R3). Overloaded notation (a for both game actions and RL actions) causes confusion (R3).
- **Missing assumptions in Theorem 1**: Theorem 1 requires the action space to be discretized, but this is not explicitly stated (R3). No explicit assumption on the boundedness of τ_t (e.g., what happens if the policy chooses τ → ∞) is given, which could affect convergence in degenerate cases (R3).
- **Ablation explanations are vague**: The ablation study results are presented but the explanations for why certain components help are handwavy; more substantiated analysis of key results is needed (R4).
- **Limited technical novelty**: The framework effectively stitches together well-established ideas (CFR, MDP, evolution strategies); though this is a minor weakness and does not greatly diminish the results (R4).
- **Missing comparisons**: No comparison to deep methods like Deep CFR (R4), and scaling to games with more players is not explored (R4).
- **Minor formatting/consistency issues**: Equation 4 has questionable parentheses and asterisk symbols (R1). Adding default DCFR parameters as a constant line in Figure 4 would aid interpretation (R1). The ordering of items (i), (ii), (iii) in Line 264 should match the stated order (R2).
