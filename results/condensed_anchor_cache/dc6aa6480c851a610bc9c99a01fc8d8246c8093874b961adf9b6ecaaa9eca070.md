## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an approach for visual pre-training for robotic manipulation. Given two video frames, it estimates hand poses using an off-the-shelf estimator, computes a transformation between them, and trains a vision encoder with a contrastive loss to predict that transformation. The encoder operates on images with hand pixels removed/inpainted. Pre-training is done on the EPIC Kitchens dataset, and evaluation is performed in simulation (MetaWorld, RoboSuite, Franka Kitchen) across 21 object manipulation tasks via behavioral cloning (BC). The authors claim an improvement in success rate over state-of-the-art methods (MoCo, VICReg, R3M, VIP). The paper is seen as addressing an important problem (appearance–motion misalignment) but reviewers raise serious concerns about motivation, technical rigor, experimental sufficiency, missing baselines, and the validity of claims.

### Strengths
- Using motion cues to pre-train vision encoders for robotic manipulation is a promising direction (Reviewer 1).
- The paper identifies an important problem: current pre-trained visual models rely on appearance-based representations that are misaligned with manipulation tasks, which are inherently motion-centric (Reviewer 2).
- The proposed contrastive loss formulation makes sense because it learns to emphasize motion over appearance, making it potentially well-suited for robotic applications (Reviewer 2).
- Pre-training on EPIC Kitchens (egocentric video) offers an effective strategy for capturing hand–object interactions, a good prior for robotic manipulation (Reviewer 2).
- Research on learning inverse dynamics as an unsupervised learning objective for visual representation remains underexplored (Reviewer 4).
- The key contribution of the paper, in the view of Reviewer 4, is leveraging human hand motion combined with video pre-training for robotics applications, rather than solely focusing on learning from motion itself.
- The authors conducted extensive experiments on three benchmarks (MetaWorld, RoboSuite, Franka Kitchen), though the results are unsatisfactory in the view of Reviewer 3 (who otherwise acknowledges the breadth of evaluation).

### Weaknesses
- **Core motivation questioned and missing context:** Reviewer 3 strongly disputes the claim that “manipulation is fundamentally defined by motion rather than ‘appearance’,” arguing that precise localization of objects is crucial and that this misunderstanding leads to a counter-intuitive design (manipulators are removed). The minority reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 1, high confidence) is much more negative on this point; other reviewers (Reviewer 2, 4) find the motivation sensible but note that the related work needed to substantiate the appearance–motion misalignment claim is lacking (Reviewer 2) and that prior work on inverse dynamics (e.g., Agrawal et al. 2016, Brandfonbrener et al. 2024) is not discussed (Reviewer 4). The paper also does not discuss forward dynamics pre-training (world models) and why inverse dynamics would be better (Reviewer 4).
- **Technical details are hand-wavy and unclear:** The contrastive loss is not defined mathematically; the paper lacks pseudo-code and rigorous notation, making it look incomplete and rushed (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 4 is unsure what is “contrastive” in the setup, because no positive/negative samples are explicitly described. The procedure for computing the hand-pose transformation is not fully specified (e.g., whether poses are in view space or world space) (Reviewer 1).
- **Insufficient and incomplete experiments:**
  - Empirical results are limited to relatively simple simulation environments; no real-robot evaluation is provided (Reviewers 1, 4).
  - The paper does not report confidence intervals or number of trials, making it impossible to tell if the claimed improvement over baselines is statistically significant (Reviewer 4).
  - Many important baselines are missing: stronger appearance-based encoders like MVP (Radosavovic et al.) and other visual pre-training methods (Voltron, VC-1, MPI) are not compared (Reviewers 1, 3). The approach is not compared to other self-supervised methods that also use contrastive or inverse dynamics objectives (VICReg, R3M) in a way that clarifies what is novel (Reviewer 2). Comparisons to sample-efficient BC methods (Implicit BC, Diffusion Policy) that work with <50 demonstrations are absent; it is not obvious whether large-scale visual pre-training helps in these settings (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablations and analyses:**
  - The effect of hand inpainting on downstream performance is not ablated (Reviewer 1).
  - The amount and curation of data from EPIC Kitchens is not described (Reviewer 1) – i.e., whether all or a subset was used.
  - The advantage of the method when removing the robot body from demonstration videos (as claimed by the authors) may be an artifact of pre-training on in-painted human hands (Reviewer 3); this is a key design choice that needs controlled ablation.
- **Dependence on off-the-shelf models:** The approach relies on hand-pose estimators and inpainting models, which adds complexity and may hinder extension to other video collections (Reviewer 1).
