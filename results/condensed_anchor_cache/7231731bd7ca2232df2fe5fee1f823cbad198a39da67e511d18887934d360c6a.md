## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces ALLIE, a chess-playing AI trained exclusively on human games from Lichess. ALLIE uses a transformer architecture (355M parameters) to model move prediction, pondering time, resignation, and game outcome, conditioned on player Elo via soft tokens. During inference, a time-adaptive Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) procedure scales the number of rollouts in proportion to the predicted human think time, aiming to close the skill gap between the AI and human opponents. In offline evaluations, ALLIE achieves ~4% higher move-matching accuracy than Maia and ~2% higher than GPT-3.5. In a large-scale online user study (7,483 games) against players rated 1000–2600 Elo, the adaptive search method yields an average skill gap of only 49 Elo, substantially outperforming search-free and standard MCTS baselines. ALLIE also models resignation with an 86.4% true positive rate.

### Strengths

- ALLIE models human chess more holistically by also predicting pondering time and resignation. These non‑move outputs are then used to parameterize a time‑adaptive MCTS, which is shown to be crucial for closing the skill gap across a wide range of Elo ratings.
- The time‑adaptive search enables remarkable skill calibration: against players from 1000 to 2600 Elo, ALLIE’s average skill gap is only 49 Elo, outperforming both search‑free and non‑adaptive MCTS baselines.
- The paper demonstrates that ALLIE can play at a grandmaster level (2500 Elo) while retaining human‑like qualities, a notable achievement given that pure behavior cloning typically yields sub‑expert policies.
- Move‑matching accuracy is strong across the full Elo spectrum (Fig. 2), and the model conditions on player strength via soft tokens for Elo, allowing continuous skill interpolation.
- The architecture is sensibly designed: board positions are represented as a sequence of move tokens (avoiding a visual representation), with separate linear heads for move, think time, and value predictions.
- The paper includes GPT‑3.5 as a challenging baseline (showing competitive move‑matching on higher‑Elo games) and conducts a user study with both quantitative performance metrics and qualitative survey feedback on enjoyment and human‑likeness.
- The evaluation methods are thorough, covering move prediction accuracy, think‑time correlation, value‑head reliability, resignation prediction, and a large‑scale online human study.
- The work is significant for the chess‑AI community and suggests a path for modeling human‑like behavior in other turn‑based games.
- The objectives are clearly defined and the paper sticks closely to them; the writing is generally clear and well‑structured.

### Weaknesses

- **Framing and terminology:** The term “human‑aligned” is misleading. AI alignment typically means acting in the best interest of humans, not behaving like one. The paper’s claims about “relying exclusively on humans” are overstated because MCTS search is a non‑human element—there is insufficient evidence that humans search a game tree in an MCTS‑like manner. A different term should be used.
- **Limited novelty and architectural comparison:** The core approach is a direct application of a transformer architecture to chess with standard heads for move/value, adding only pondering‑time and resignation outputs. No explanation is given as to why the transformer outperforms the residual networks used in Maia; the parameter size (355M vs. Maia’s much smaller models) and inference‑time differences are not discussed or controlled for. Fairness of comparisons is questionable without controlling for compute.
- **Search and skill calibration interpretation:** Failure to match grandmaster level could simply reflect a lack of strength, which adding search fixes; it is unclear whether adaptive search is truly necessary for skill calibration or whether the same improvement could come from simply increasing search depth. The paper does not explain why adaptive search also improves calibration against mid‑level opponents, nor does it compare against an AlphaZero‑like agent whose skill is adjusted via a temperature parameter. The choice of the constant `c` in the rollouts function (\(N_{sim}\)) is not justified, and there is no sensitivity analysis for `c`.
- **Move‑matching accuracy as a metric:** Move‑matching accuracy may not fully capture human‑likeness, especially for weak players who blunder in many different ways. Low move‑matching could still feel human‑like. It would be useful to report human‑alignment survey results grouped by Elo rating.
- **User study limitations:** Participants knew they were playing an AI before filling out the survey, potentially biasing their perception of human‑likeness. A Turing‑test style blind evaluation would be more objective.
- **Data and training limitations:**
  - ALLIE is trained only on Blitz games (with a specific downsampling of low‑Elo games and removal of moves made under 30 s time pressure). This raises questions about generality to standard‑time games, and the removal of time‑pressure moves discards valuable human‑intuition data. Incorporating remaining time as input might avoid this exclusion and improve human‑likeness.
  - The method is entirely offline; its applicability to other games or domains is not demonstrated.
- **Value‑head claims:** The claim of a “reliable board value estimate” is overstated. Figure 4 shows a Pearson correlation of only \(r=0.697\) overall, and for positions 30+ moves from terminal, the correlation drops below \(r=0.3\). Reporting mean absolute error would be more informative. Moreover, comparing ALLIE’s value head to Stockfish is questionable—the value head predicts the human outcome, not the optimal engine evaluation; it should be compared to actual game outcomes.
- **Resignation token in practice:** Even with a 0.1% false positive rate, a single bad resignation can ruin a game (cumulative risk over many moves). It is unclear whether the resignation prediction was actually enabled in the user study games, and how it performs in a realistic gameplay scenario (not just on isolated positions).
- **Missing related work and comparisons:**
  - The related work discussion of Maia is insufficient; Maia also aimed at human alignment and produced individual‑level models (NeurIPS ’21, KDD ’22) that achieve similar move‑matching improvements. The recently accepted Maia‑2 (NeurIPS ’24) conditions on Elo and achieves better calibration across skill levels—this should be discussed.
  - A comparison with a fine‑tuned GPT‑3.5 on the same dataset would be informative.
  - No mention of offline RL approaches (e.g., Decision Transformers) that could potentially improve out‑of‑distribution generalization over pure behavior cloning.
- **Specific technical questions (overlapping with criticisms):**
  - Why does adaptive search outperform non‑adaptive (AlphaZero‑like) MCTS, especially against 2500 Elo opponents? Is the non‑adaptive search time bounded?
  - How sensitive are move‑matching accuracy and calibration to the constant `c` in the rollout function?
  - Does move‑matching accuracy decrease as search effort increases? (Report accuracy as a function of search time.)
  - Soft tokens for Elo may still suffer from sparsity given the limited examples at each value; what is the internal representation?
  - The loss function does not mention normalization or weighting for the different terms (move log‑likelihood, think‑time MS A, etc.). Is this a potential issue?
- **Presentation issues:** Figure 3 is hard to read (the median is almost invisible); the squares in Figure 1b are not explained in the caption. Abstract has a typo: “resignations In” → “resignations in”. The main text does not explicitly state how the time spent for each move is recorded.
- **Code not available at review:** Reviewer 2 noted that their rating is contingent on the authors fulfilling their code‑release promises.

*Minority contrast:* Two reviewers rated 8 (high enthusiasm) and three rated 6 (more critical); the critical reviewers raised substantial concerns about limited novelty, insufficient comparisons, and the value of adaptive search, while the more positive reviewers emphasized the strong empirical results and the successful use of pondering time to drive search.
