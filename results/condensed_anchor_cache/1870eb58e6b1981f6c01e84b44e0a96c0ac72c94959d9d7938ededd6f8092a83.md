## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the continuous Gromov-Wasserstein Optimal Transport (GWOT) problem, aiming to benchmark existing solvers and identify their limitations. The authors argue that current continuous GWOT solvers rely heavily on discrete techniques and perform poorly when source and target training samples are statistically uncorrelated—a realistic scenario they highlight. To address these issues, they propose NeuralGW, a neural-network-based solver formulated as a min-max-min optimization problem that does not use discrete approximations. Experiments on GloVe and biological datasets show that NeuralGW can outperform competitors in uncorrelated settings but requires large training data (200K samples vs. 3K for others) and is sensitive to optimization parameters. The paper concludes that a reliable continuous GWOT solver is still missing, and NeuralGW is only a partial step.

### Strengths
- The paper fills an important gap by systematically analyzing existing continuous GWOT solvers and questioning the assumptions (e.g., correlated training pairs) under which they succeed.
- It clearly identifies a key deficiency: many solvers assume paired/correlated training samples and fail when this assumption is violated.
- The proposed NeuralGW is a novel continuous-GWOT method that does not rely on discrete techniques and explicitly handles the uncorrelated-data scenario.
- Empirical results show NeuralGW’s performance is largely unaffected by the correlatedness of source and target samples—a significant advantage that reduces data preprocessing burden.
- The method scales to large datasets via neural network parametrization and stochastic gradient updates.
- The paper includes thorough experiments comparing several existing GW computational methods.
- One reviewer (score 8) particularly emphasized that the work is one of the first to address the continuous GWOT problem directly, and that the insight into the drawbacks of existing solvers is valuable.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical gaps**: The main theorems (5.1, 5.2) rely on standard duality and reformulations involving the optimal Monge map, but existence of such a map is never justified. It is well-known that the GW or inner-product GW may not achieve a minimum, and the existence of optimal potentials is also not guaranteed (citations needed, e.g., [1], [2]). Thus the claims serve only as heuristics. No convergence guarantees are provided for the neural solver; at least a guarantee under certain conditions is expected.
- **Missing comparisons and related work**:  
  - A key related method—the neural entropic Gromov-Wasserstein solver (T. Wang & Z. Goldfeld, 2023)—is not compared, even though it also does not assume structure on data generation.  
  - Other neural-network-based GW solvers ([5], [6]) claiming similar large-scale applicability are omitted from discussion.  
  - Earlier theoretical work on the existence and regularity of the Gromov-Monge map ([1],[3],[4]) and on statistical aspects of continuous GW ([2]) is not adequately referenced.
- **Experimental fairness and scope**:  
  - The comparison is unfair: competitors are trained on 3K samples while NeuralGW uses 200K. The authors acknowledge this but do not perform experiments at a smaller scale to enable a fairer comparison.  
  - Only two datasets (GloVe, biological) are used; experiments should cover additional domains.  
  - Half of the compared methods are merely labelled “other methods” in graphs, which is confusing and should be rectified.  
  - The toy 3D→2D experiment is poorly explained (e.g., what the mixed Gaussians represent, what colors mean); moving it to the appendix would help.  
  - The reasons for choosing evaluation metrics (Top k-accuracy, cosine similarity, FOSCTTM) are not justified.
- **Algorithm limitations**:  
  - NeuralGW requires large datasets for training and is unstable/sensitive to optimization parameters and training data variation.  
  - The method is designed exclusively for continuous GWOT and would not be helpful when a discrete-measure formulation is more appropriate.  
- **Presentation and clarity**:  
  - Section 4 makes several claims about discrete GW problems (statistical assumptions, computational difficulties) but does not clearly delineate whether the core issue is generalization, sample complexity, or optimization hardness. The argument would benefit from connecting to classical learning concepts.  
  - A specific error: line 311 uses incorrect target indices.  
  - The interpretation of findings is not thorough: it remains unclear whether the observed limitation of existing solvers stems solely from their reliance on discrete techniques or from other factors.
- Minor disagreement among reviewers: While one reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive about the significance of the work, the majority (scores 5,6,6,6) expressed concerns about theoretical soundness, experimental fairness, and the conclusiveness of the results.
