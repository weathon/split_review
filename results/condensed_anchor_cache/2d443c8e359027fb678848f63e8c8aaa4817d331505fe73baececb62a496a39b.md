## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper distinguishes intrinsic self-correction (no external feedback) from self-correction that uses external signals, and investigates whether LLMs can improve their reasoning via intrinsic self-correction. Through experiments on GSM8K, Commonsense QA, and HotpotQA with ChatGPT and GPT‑4, the authors argue that current LLMs struggle to self-correct without external feedback and that prior work claiming self-correction often conflates the use of oracle labels or other forms of feedback with genuine self-correction. The paper also critiques multi‑agent debate approaches, showing they perform no better than self‑consistency.

### Strengths

- The topic is highly significant and timely; the community will benefit from wider visibility.
- The paper makes a crucial distinction between intrinsic self-correction and self-correction that relies on external feedback, and it clearly highlights the role of oracle labels in prior work (e.g., Kim et al., Shinn et al.).
- Good literature review section and use of well‑known, trusted datasets.
- The experimental design is sound; the random guessing baseline for Commonsense QA is particularly appreciated.
- The paper shows that multi‑agent debate methods do not outperform self‑consistency when the same number of responses is considered, revealing an important limitation.
- Writing quality received mixed but overall positive praise: one reviewer found the organization and writing uniformly high and a pleasure to read; another found the flow natural and enjoyable; a third reviewer, however, found the presentation confusing and contradictory (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Misleading title.** “Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet” is unsupported by the limited experiments and overly broad. Two reviewers strongly recommend a more measured title (e.g., “Reexamining the Ability of Large Language Models to Self-Correct Reasoning”). The authors have agreed to this change in their rebuttal.
- **Heavy reliance on a single, loaded prompt.** The core experiments use only “Review your previous answer and find problems with your answer”. This leading prompt can cause false‑positive error detection. The study does not extensively explore unbiased prompts (e.g., “Assume that this answer could be either correct or incorrect. Review the answer carefully and report any serious problems you find.”) or settings like zero temperature. The paper’s dismissal of prompt search as “external feedback” conflates per‑problem oracle feedback with cross‑dataset prompt engineering, which is a methodological concern.
- **Limited scale.** Only 200 questions are used for GPT‑4; the majority of results rely on ChatGPT. More challenging reasoning datasets exist.
- **Presentation issues (noted by one reviewer).** The methodology description is unclear (e.g., whether all self‑correction prompts followed Figure 2 or varied). Contradictory statements appear, such as initially focusing on intrinsic self‑correction but later investigating self‑correction more broadly. Section 3.1.3 claims that evidence does not support self‑reasoning, yet Table 1 appears to show positive results—creating confusion. The wording is vague at times (e.g., “nascent”).
- **Unsatisfactory explanation for performance degradation.** Section 3.2.1 does not provide sufficient intuition for why post‑hoc prompting leads correct initial answers to become incorrect. One reviewer also challenges the claim that a well‑designed pre‑hoc prompt should already be optimal, noting that similar reasoning would undermine chain‑of‑thought prompting.
- **Lack of model generality.** Only ChatGPT and GPT‑4 are tested; results may not generalize to open‑source LLMs such as LLaMA.
- **Unclear definition of intrinsic self‑correction.** It is not specified whether recalling examples from parameter knowledge (e.g., the “Analogical Reasoners” approach) counts as intrinsic self‑correction.
- **No systematic summary of prior problems.** The paper does not provide a table or detailed list identifying which prior methods rely on oracle labels, have poorly constructed pre‑prompts, or report incorrect benchmark results (e.g., specifics for Shinn et al. are missing).
- **Missing experiments and robustness checks (raised as questions that overlap with criticisms):**
  - How does the random guessing baseline in Section 3.1.3 work for non‑multiple‑choice datasets such as GSM8K? The footnote explanation is unclear.
  - Have the authors tried combining multi‑agent debating with self‑consistency?
  - How much effort was invested in finding the single self‑correction prompt? Would different prompt variations yield different results?
  - How should step‑wise verification paired with self‑consistency be categorized relative to self‑correction?
- **One reviewer notes that the paper reads more as a call for critical examination than as a full experimental study that supports the strong headline.**
