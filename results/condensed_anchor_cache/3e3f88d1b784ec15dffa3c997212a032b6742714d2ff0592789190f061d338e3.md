## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers describe a paper that analyzes an fMRI dataset (Mitchell 2008) of 60 noun stimuli. The authors identify 22 spatially clustered groups of voxels that are well-predicted by a linear encoding model fit to GloVe embeddings. They then perform "pruning" (binary feature subselection within GloVe) to improve representational similarity (RSA) for each cluster, achieving modest gains (max cross-validated Spearman ρ = 0.24, several clusters below 0.1). The selected features are interpreted via linear probing against human‑annotated semantic properties. The paper claims that brain regions encoding social/cognitive aspects also represent sensory‑motor features, but not vice versa.

### Strengths
- The topic (understanding feature dimensions underlying brain predictivity) is well‑motivated and timely.
- The approach is fundamentally data‑driven, avoids hand‑crafted feature spaces, and reduces potential biases.
- Writing is clear, with good notation that aids tracking across analyses; the introduction effectively reviews key challenges.
- Focusing on GloVe as a simpler baseline is useful, given the trend toward larger LLMs without assessing simpler model explainability.
- The three‑stage pipeline (encoding, pruning, probing) is sensible and could support new insights with richer datasets or more predictive models.
- The idea of grouping nearby voxels and then analyzing feature‑distinctiveness across clusters is promising and interesting.
- Multiple datasets (though limited) are used to drive understanding.
- One reviewer notes the method is well‑reasoned and potentially useful for generating semantic insights.

### Weaknesses
- **Low prediction levels**: maximum cross‑validated Spearman ρ = 0.24; several clusters fall below 0.1. Without noise ceilings, it is impossible to assess whether even these low scores are meaningful. The authors do not report noise ceilings on RDMs for each cluster.
- **No novel findings**: the main neurobiological claim (social/cognitive regions also encode sensory‑motor, but not reverse) is not strongly supported. The results largely confirm known broad organizing principles (visual, auditory, social specialization) without revealing additional insights. Most clusters heavily predict visual semantics, but this observation is not interpreted.
- **Pruning method is not novel**: the binary feature subselection is essentially a special case of feature‑reweighted or voxel‑encoding RSA (veRSA / FR‑RSA; Kaniuth & Hebert 2022, NeuroImage). The paper does not adequately distinguish itself from this existing technique.
- **Insufficient literature engagement**: only ~20 references; key recent work from groups (Fedorenko, Tuckute, Toneva, Wehbe, Huth) is not cited. Relevant papers (e.g., Conwell CCN paper on GloVe predicting neural data; work on occipital areas in Table 1) are missing.
- **Convoluted analysis pipeline**: using two different feature spaces (Mitchell’s 25‑feature model for voxel encoding, then GloVe with/without pruning for cluster interpretation) is potentially circular and undermines clarity. Why not perform the entire encoding analysis with GloVe directly?
- **Poor figures and missing brain visualizations**: no cortical surface renderings; clusters are referred to only by arbitrary numerical indices. Anatomical labels are needed. The quality is below the ICLR standard for a paper aiming to advance understanding of semantic organization.
- **Waste of space on training‑data fits**: Table 1 reports representational similarity on the “complete dataset” after feature selection that optimizes that same metric; only cross‑validated results are meaningful.
- **Averaging voxels across subjects**: this likely reduces granularity because fine‑grained conceptual information is not expected to align voxel‑wise across subjects. Clusters should be constructed in a group‑aligned space without averaging.
- **Dataset limitations**: the Mitchell 2008 dataset is small and old; more recent datasets (Pereira 2018, Nastase 2021, Tuckute 2024) would provide richer stimuli and better SNR. No justification for using only GloVe; more performant models could yield better predictions and stronger interpretations.
- **Representational analyses are insufficient for functional claims**: the paper claims to identify “functional brain modules,” but representational geometry alone (without causal perturbation) cannot establish function (e.g., chairs can be decoded from the fusiform face area).
- **Missing methodological details**: Table 1 legend does not specify that the metric is Spearman ρ; it is unclear what “f = features” means. Brain area abbreviations are not spelled out. The criteria for defining “non‑null data” and the R² cutoff for selecting the top 50% of voxels are not reported. fMRI preprocessing and whether participants read or heard words are not described.
- **Focus on “best” clusters rather than differences**: the paper emphasizes which clusters are most predictive but does not analyze how representational dimensions differ between clusters, which would be more informative.
- **Conclusion too brief**: the paper would benefit from moving methodological details to an appendix and expanding discussion of results, limitations, and implications.
- **Minor issues**: a grammatical error on line 244 (“in ways stages”); the similarity metric for S_c differs from S_GloVe without explanation.

**Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers rated 3, while one rated 1, expressing substantially less enthusiasm about the results and the paper’s contribution to understanding brain function.
