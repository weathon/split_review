## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the algorithm that Transformers implement during in-context learning (ICL) for linear regression. Authors empirically compare Transformer predictions to Iterative Newton’s Method and Gradient Descent (GD) using cosine similarity of errors and induced weights. They find that Transformer layers more closely match Newton iterations (e.g., each middle layer roughly corresponds to 3 Newton steps) and that Transformers can learn on ill-conditioned data where GD struggles. A theoretical result proves that a Transformer with O(k) layers can implement k iterations of Newton’s method.

### Strengths
- The insight that Transformers may implement higher-order optimization methods (Iterative Newton) rather than first-order methods (GD) is interesting and worth further investigation (R1).
- Visualization of algorithmic similarity (e.g., Figure 3, Figure 7) is clear, and the theoretical construction proving O(k) layers can implement k Newton steps is a good contribution (R1, R3).
- Comprehensive experiments compare Transformers, Newton, GD, Online GD (OGD), and LSTMs, including an ill-conditioned linear regression setting (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Two distinct similarity metrics (errors and induced weights) are introduced, providing tools for future research (R1, R3, R4).
- The comparison with LSTMs shows that LSTMs align more closely with OGD than with Newton/GD, adding support to the contrast (R1, R3). *Note: R1 disputes the interpretation of Table 1 and Figure 5 (see Weaknesses).*
- The paper is generally well written and easy to follow, with a good literature review (R1, R2). *Note: R3 reports many typographical errors (see Weaknesses).*
- The theoretical existence result supports the empirical findings and provides a foundation for the claim (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Gap between theory and experiments.** The proof shows O(k) layers are sufficient for k Newton steps, but experiments indicate one layer approximates ~3 Newton steps. It is unclear what algorithm the trained Transformer actually implements and why it is more efficient than the construction. An explanation or intuition for how one layer can perform multiple Newton iterations is missing (R1).
2. **Selection of highlighted cells in Figure 7 appears arbitrary.** In column 8 there are four entries with value .988 but only one is highlighted. Similarly, in column 7 multiple .884 entries exist. The authors claim a linear trend between layer index and number of Newton iterations, but highlighting different cells would break that trend. The closeness of many values (e.g., column 9: .992, .991, .990, .993) raises questions about statistical significance. It is not reported whether experiments were run with multiple random seeds to assess stability (R1).
3. **Step‑size tuning for GD and Newton is not reported.** The same step size may not be optimal for all methods, and the similarity comparison could be sensitive to this choice. No sensitivity analysis is provided (R1, R2, R3).
4. **Maximal similarity between Transformer and Newton is larger than between Transformer and GD** (e.g., cosine 0.974 vs. 0.907 in Figure 7). Since both GD and Newton should converge to the OLS solution, their similarity with the Transformer should be comparable when they converge. The reason for the discrepancy is not explained. Further, Figure 9 shows that the cosine similarity between GD weights and OLS weights is small when the number of in‑context examples is less than 20, which contradicts the theoretical convergence of GD from zero initialization to the min‑norm OLS solution (R1, R2).
5. **Inconsistent LSTM claims and Table 1 interpretation.** Figure 5 (left) shows LSTM predictions are similar to “5 steps of Newton,” while the center panel suggests similarity to OGD. The paper claims LSTM is more similar to OGD than to Newton/GD, but Table 1 (right column) gives OGD similarity 0.920, GD 0.808, Newton 0.831. R1 points out that these numbers actually show LSTM is most similar to OGD, but the figure and the text seem contradictory. The authors should reconcile these and clarify which claim is supported (R1).
6. **Theorem 1 statement is too strong.** It claims “there is a TF such that for any set of in‑context samples…” Newton’s method requires a step‑size α that depends on the eigenvalues of X^T X for convergence; therefore the constructed transformer would also depend on the dataset. The universality claim is not valid without additional conditions on α (R1).
7. **Experimental setup ambiguity.** Section 3 describes sampling a covariance matrix Σ from a distribution and then sampling feature vectors per task, but most experiments fix the covariance. The fixed‑covariance case should be clearly separated, as training on fixed vs. random covariance can yield very different results (R1).
8. **Use of cosine similarity instead of L2 norm.** The paper uses cosine similarity of errors and induced weights, but a small cosine only measures directional agreement, not magnitude. For claiming that transformers mimic a specific algorithm, L2 norm would be more appropriate. The choice and the rationale for normalizing errors are not justified (R1, R3, R4).
9. **Limited comparison of optimization methods.** Only two optimizers (Newton and GD) are compared. Other first‑order methods (e.g., accelerated GD, approximate message passing) could be equally or more similar to the Transformer’s behavior. The claim that transformers implement Newton is not exclusive (R2).
10. **Prior work overlap.** The construction that Transformers can implement Newton’s method already exists in [1] Section 8 (R2). The novelty of the theoretical result should be clarified.
11. **Missing experimental details.** The paper does not report: the learning rates used for Newton and GD; the criteria for selecting the number of steps T relative to d when computing induced weights; the methodology for obtaining the “best‑matched” hyperparameters (Definition 4); the weight initialization scheme for the Transformer; the optimizer (e.g., Adam, SGD) and its hyperparameters (batch size, weight decay). These omissions hinder reproducibility (R3).
12. **Figure 3 convergence concern.** In a noise‑free, sufficiently sampled setting, all methods (Newton, GD, Transformer) should converge to the correct prediction and produce similarity ~0.99 at the final layer. Figure 3 shows lower similarity for GD, raising doubt about whether all methods were run to convergence or with comparable criteria (R3, also echoed by R2).
13. **Metric validity questioned.** R4 asks why similarity of errors is a reasonable metric instead of direct L2 distance on predictions. The paper normalizes errors without explanation. Additionally, the definition of “induced weights” for a Transformer may not be valid: if x_query is scaled, will the output scale proportionally? The softmax non‑convexity makes this nontrivial; numerical verification is needed (R4).
14. **Freedom in “best‑matched” hyperparameters.** Using the best hyperparameter for each method gives excessive degrees of freedom. For example, a convex combination of Newton and GD with an optimized coefficient could trivially fit better, making the comparison to pure Newton or GD meaningless. The authors should discuss why this flexibility is appropriate in their setting (R4).
15. **Causal attention alignment.** The theoretical construction uses bidirectional attention, but the experimental Transformer likely uses causal attention. A discussion of whether Newton’s method can be approximated with causal attention would better align theory and experiments (R4).
16. **Typographical and grammatical errors.** The manuscript contains numerous errors that detract from readability (R3).
