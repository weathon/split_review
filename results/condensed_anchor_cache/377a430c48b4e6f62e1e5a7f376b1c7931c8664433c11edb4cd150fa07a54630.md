## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Power Scheduler, a learning rate scheduler for LLM pretraining designed to be independent of the number of training tokens and batch size. It extends the WSD scheduler by identifying a power-law relationship between learning rate, batch size, and total tokens via small-scale experiments (12M–121M models). The scheduler is combined with µP and evaluated on 3B dense and MoE models, showing comparable or improved perplexity over cosine and WSD schedulers. The paper claims the scheduler transfers across model sizes, architectures, and training budgets.

### Strengths
1. **Timely and practical motivation** – reducing the number of hyperparameters for large-scale LM training is valuable given the high cost of full-scale tuning (R2, R3). The Power Scheduler eliminates the need to pre-specify total tokens or batch size, a clear improvement over cosine and WSD schedulers (R1, R2, R4).
2. **Novel empirical finding** – the power-law relationship between LR, batch size, and token count is an interesting discovery (R1, R2). The authors conduct a large number of small-scale experiments to derive it (R2).
3. **Strong theoretical foundation** – the scheduler builds on the µP framework, which provides a rationale for hyperparameter transfer across model sizes (R3, R4). The integration with µP for stable training dynamics across model sizes is a strength (R4).
4. **Comprehensive empirical validation at 3B scale** – experiments on dense and MoE models of 3B parameters show the scheduler works at non-trivial sizes, with results comparable to state-of-the-art small LM checkpoints (R3, R4). R3 finds this convincing, but R4 notes that the proxy models are limited to 121M, causing disagreement about the strength of the scaling evidence (see Weaknesses).
5. **Clear exposition** – the paper is well-written and easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing prior work on LR–batch size scaling** – prior theoretical and empirical work (You et al., ICLR 2020; Malladi et al., NeurIPS 2022; Goyal et al., 2017) already shows that optimal LR depends on batch size, often with a square-root rule. This makes Hypothesis 1 and Fig. 2b unnecessary and suggests the Power Scheduler cannot truly be agnostic to batch size (R1). The main experiments use a fixed batch size, so the claim of batch-size invariance is unsubstantiated (R1). R1 also notes that if β₁, β₂ in Adam were adjusted, the results would likely match the square-root rule exactly.
2. **Marginal improvement over WSD** – in Table 5, the Power Scheduler improves over WSD by only +0.1 PPL (dense) and +0.2 PPL (MoE) (R3). R2 further notes that the dense model outperforms other methods in only 3 of 7 downstream tasks. R4, however, views the performance as strong – this disagreement is noted.
3. **Insufficient proxy-scale evidence** – the hyperparameter search uses only 12M, 36M, and 121M models, which R4 considers too small to reliably transfer to large scales (citing e.g. “Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities”). R3 conversely finds the 3B results convincing, but the lack of intermediate proxy scales (hundreds of millions) weakens the liability of the transfer claim (R4).
4. **Lack of theoretical justification for Eq. (7)** – the design choice that modifies the power law to avoid small LR at high token counts is introduced without rigorous justification or ablation. It is unclear whether better alternatives exist or how it aligns with µP theory (R3).
5. **Hyperparameter (a,b) sensitivity and selection** – Fig. 5 shows that a change in a can drive up to a 10-point test perplexity variation, yet the text and caption claim the result is not sensitive to a (R1). No joint plot of (a,b) against perplexity is shown, so the chosen values appear unmotivated (R1). R2 and R4 note insufficient details on the search methodology (grid/random search, budget, number of trials), making it hard to reproduce or apply to new models.
6. **Missing baselines** – no comparison to simpler schedulers like warmup + stable LR (+ optional final cooldown), which would also reduce hyperparameters and work well in continual learning (R2). The paper only compares to cosine and WSD.
7. **No learning curves** – training loss vs. tokens curves are not shown, making it impossible to assess training stability, convergence speed, or the effect of the scheduler on the dynamics (R2, R1 also requests them for 1B runs).
8. **Limited transferability demonstration** – the power-law coefficients were only validated on models from the same architecture family and the same tokenizer/dataset. Transfer to other architectures (e.g., different transformer variants), other optimizers, or other datasets is not shown (R2, R3, R4). R3 also questions whether the numbers a=4, b=0.51 require re-search for new tokenizers or optimizers.
9. **Missing related work** – the paper does not cite “Scaling Optimal LR Across Token Horizons”, which found that larger token budgets have lower optimal LRs, directly relevant to the LR–token relationship studied here (R4). R4 also notes the discrepancy that Fig. 2(a) shows longer training requires larger LR, while the cited work found the opposite at larger scales.
10. **Issues with figures and presentation** – Fig. 3 connects points with non-monotonic curves (e.g., batch size 32–64 shows non-zero second derivative), misleading; should be presented as a scatter plot (R1, R4). Fig. 6’s caption says it is for parameter a but the plotted values are negative, suggesting it is actually b (R1, R2). Large tables of hyperparameters (Tab 3, 4) disrupt the flow and belong in the appendix (R4).
11. **Missing ablation and questions as criticisms** – the effect of β₁, β₂ in Adam is not studied (R1). The design of Eq. (7) lacks ablation (R3). R2 asks for evidence of transferability, details on the search budget, and guidelines for practitioners. R3 asks how to interpret Tables 6 and 7 given many differences beyond the scheduler. R4 asks why Fig. 3 is non-monotonic and why the LR scaling direction differs from the cited work. These unanswered questions undermine confidence in the claimed universality.
12. **Misstatements about µP** – the paper incorrectly claims µP implies optimal LR is invariant to training steps and batch sizes; µP only guarantees invariance over a fixed dataset (R1). This misstatement undermines the theoretical foundation for hyperparameter transfer across token counts and batch sizes.
