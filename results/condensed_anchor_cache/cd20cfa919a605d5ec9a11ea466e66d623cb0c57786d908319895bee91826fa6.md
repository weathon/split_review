## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the problem of decomposing a continuous piecewise linear (CPWL) function into the difference of two convex CPWL functions (a DC representation), with the goal of minimizing the number of linear pieces. The authors propose to fix an underlying polyhedral complex that determines the possible locus of nonlinearity. Under this assumption, they prove that the set of all compatible decompositions forms a polyhedron (the “decomposition polyhedron”) arising as the intersection of two translated cones, and that minimal (irreducible) decompositions correspond to bounded faces and must be vertices of this polyhedron (Theorem 3.13). They identify cases where a unique minimal decomposition exists, and apply their framework to submodular functions and to constructing ReLU neural networks for a given CPWL function. They also disprove a recent approach by Tran & Wang (2024) for higher dimensions. The paper is primarily theoretical; no practical implementation or algorithm is provided.

### Strengths
- The problem of decomposing a CPWL function into a difference of convex CPWL functions is fundamental, with applications in nonconvex optimization, neural network theory, and submodular optimization.
- The paper offers a novel perspective by linking decomposition to polyhedral geometry, introducing the concept of decomposition polyhedra. This is an innovative and promising approach that may inspire future work.
- The theoretical analysis is solid and provides deep structural understanding: minimal decompositions must be vertices (Theorem 3.13), and under a fixed polyhedral complex the decomposition set forms a polyhedron with a clean characterization.
- The paper is generally well-written and well-organized, with clear statements of contributions and applications.
- Applications to ReLU activation, submodular functions, and neural network design are interesting and widen the impact.
- The work refutes a recently proposed algorithm (Tran & Wang 2024) for minimal decomposition, providing a concrete counterexample.

### Weaknesses
- **Restrictive assumption:** The entire analysis requires that the polyhedral complex (the pieces) be fixed in advance. This is a strong and potentially unmotivated limitation. One reviewer (R1, score 5) argues that the assumption seems restrictive and its naturalness is unclear, especially as existing decomposition results apply to any CPWL function. The authors themselves note this limitation, but the paper does not discuss how to choose or learn the complex.
- **Lack of bounds and complexity:** No bounds are given for the number of pieces in a minimal decomposition or for the number of vertices of the decomposition polyhedron. A discussion relating to monomial complexity is requested (R4). The claim that “simply enumerating” vertices works is not supported by complexity analysis; it is unclear whether enumeration is feasible (R1).
- **Limited applicability of main NN result:** Corollary 6.4 (smooth tradeoff between size and depth) applies only to CPWL functions compatible with a *regular* polyhedral complex. It is not discussed whether this assumption is natural, easy to satisfy, or how it compares to existing constructions that work for any CPWL function (R1). The irregular case is not addressed; it would be helpful to provide examples and discuss how structural results adapt (R2).
- **Accessibility and clarity:** The paper is hard to read for non-experts. Many technical definitions are introduced with few accompanying figures in the main text (most figures are in the appendix). Several concrete examples are missing or insufficient: a bivariate example of two different compatible polyhedral complexes (R1); an example of a polyhedral complex illustrating refinement, balancing condition, and the median function (R1); an example of a convex CPWL function with two different compatible partitions (R1); a figure showing how Lemma 3.2’s parameterization works (R1).
- **Missing details and incorrect statements:**
  - In the definition of $\mathcal{P}_f^n$ (L200), the set $\{x : g_i(x)=\max_j g_j(x)\}$ may not be full-dimensional, and $k=q$ may not hold if some affine functions are redundant (R2).
  - The definition of CPWL functions (L184) requires continuity; without it the definition would allow discontinuities at boundaries (R2). The paper should explicitly state continuity.
  - Notation used before definition: $w_f$ in Proposition 3.3 is not defined until the proof of Proposition 3.2 (R2). Similarly, $\phi$ in L1283 is undefined (R2).
  - In Proposition 3.3, the equivalence between local convexity around facets and global convexity (line 1360) needs a more rigorous justification (R2).
  - In L752, the function $h$ may not be convex as claimed; the summation of terms may lead to non-convexity if coefficients are not handled carefully (R2).
  - Typographical errors: L180 missing period, L146 “wit” → “with”, L302 typo, L468 “certing” (R1, R2).
- **Unclear or incomplete arguments:**
  - Theorem 3.13: It is not argued whether the decomposition polyhedron always has an extreme point (R1).
  - Proposition 4.1 has “function” twice (R1).
  - Reference to Proposition B.3 is not explained (R1).
  - Theorems 6.1 and 6.2: It is unclear whether they are constructive and whether one must know parameters $q$ and $k$ and their specific representations (R1).
  - Theorem 6.3: Writing $q=k$ appears as an assumption in the statement (R1).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms:**
  - Are there problems where fixing the supporting polyhedral partition is a natural constraint? (R1)
  - Definition 3.12 (“pieces” ~ minimum number of facets) – is this the same as Pareto optimality? (R3) A better term might be needed (R1).
  - Does the result for hyperplane functions (Def. 3.16) extend to deeper ReLU networks? (R3)
  - Theorem 3.18: What is the exact meaning of “minimizer”? Include an explicit linear programming formulation (R3).
- **Practical implementation:** The paper focuses solely on theory; no practical algorithm or implementation is provided, which is a weakness noted by R3 (and acknowledged by authors in the Limitations section). The decomposition polyhedron is defined but no method to actually find a minimal decomposition beyond enumeration is given.
