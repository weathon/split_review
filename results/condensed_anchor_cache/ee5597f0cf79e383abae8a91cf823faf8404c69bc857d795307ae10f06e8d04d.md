## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SePT, a fine-tuning framework that uses the self-influence score to identify bias-conflicting samples, creating a pivotal subset with reduced spurious correlations. It introduces Bias-Customized Self-Influence (BCSI) to improve detection and fine-tunes a biased model on this subset to enable quick recovery. The method also adds a cross-entropy loss for low-bias settings. Experiments on biased datasets evaluate performance across a range of bias strengths.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a critical and significant problem: spurious correlation and data bias in machine learning (R1, R2, R3).
- The use of influence functions (self-influence) to filter bias-conflicting samples is original and novel (R1, R2). This line of thought may inspire refined standards for sample selection (R2).
- The introduction provides a clear explanation of biased datasets and influence functions, making the paper easy to follow (R1).
- The fine-tuning approach avoids time-consuming retraining, which aligns with current research trends (R3). Reviewer 3 is more positive overall, finding the method timely.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear presentation and motivation**: Figure 1 appears too early, and the legends (bias-aligned, bias-conflicting) are not explained. The figure is confusing—Section 3.1 is supposed to explain why self-influence fails in biased data but does not clarify how it is applied or why it needs modification; the figure only shows BCSI outperformance without explaining the fundamental reason (R1).
- **Lack of justification for the metric**: The utility of the influence function for bias detection is questionable. There is no clear connection to biases or fairness, and no mathematical or conceptual justification for choosing self-influence over other metrics (R2; echoed by R1: novelty not well-explained).
- **No theoretical backing or intuitive explanation**: The method lacks theoretical support. Why training for five epochs with GCE (the acronym is not defined) and then applying SI works, whereas a fully trained model does not, is unexplained (R1).
- **Definition issues in Section 2.1**: The meanings of \(s_y\) and \(b_y\) are unclear. Does each class have exactly one relevant signal \(s_y\) and one irrelevant signal \(b_y\)? That would imply the most extreme case of spurious correlation (correlation=1 for all classes). Also, can one class correlate with multiple spurious attributes? (R3)
- **Figure 1 details and concerns**:
    - It is unclear whether the reported accuracies are validation accuracies (R3).
    - In Figure 1(a), precision is not monotonic with the bias-conflicting sample ratio (0.5% is top, but 5% is above 1% and 2%), suggesting noise; results should be averaged over multiple runs (R3).
    - In Figure 1(b), the claim of “consistent detection precision” seems contradicted by large variation in range across bias ratios (R3).
- **Limited practical advantage**: From Figures 1e–1h, BCSI consistently outperforms others except in the extreme 0.5% case, raising questions about its benefit in realistic settings (R3).
- **Questionable novelty of the cross-entropy loss addition**: The paper claims existing methods cannot handle low-bias settings, but previous methods could also add a CE loss. Moreover, when the pivotal set is small, adding random CE samples may have an effect close to ERM, making it unconvincing that this addition helps (R3).
- **Open questions from reviewers (overlapping with criticisms)**:
    - How is self-influence used in biased data? Why does SI underperform in biased data? (R1)
    - Need more justifications for the metric choice (R2).
    - Clarifications needed on definitions, Figure 1 accuracy, the non-monotonic pattern in (a), the variation in (b), and the CE loss addition (R3).

**Disagreement note**: Reviewer 3 (score 5) finds the fine-tuning approach timely and the problem significant, but still raises several clarity and novelty concerns. Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2 (both score 3) more strongly criticize the lack of motivation, theoretical backing, and insufficient justification for the proposed method.
