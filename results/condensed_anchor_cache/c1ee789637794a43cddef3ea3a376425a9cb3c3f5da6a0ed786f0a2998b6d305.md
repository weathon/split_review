## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CrysToGraph, a transformer-based geometric graph network for predicting crystal material properties. It combines edge-engaged transformer graph convolution (eTGC) blocks (for short-range dependencies) with graph-wise transformer (GwT) blocks (for long-range dependencies). The model is evaluated on MatBench benchmark datasets. Reviewers disagree strongly: one reviewer rates it 1 (absolute certainty of fundamental flaws), two rate it 3, and one rates it 6 (fairly confident). The positive reviewer finds the results promising and the analysis thorough; the negative reviewer deems the method not E(3) invariant and thus unsuitable; the middle reviewers question novelty, justification, and significance.

### Strengths

-   **Important problem**: Developing machine learning methods for crystal structures is relevant to society and material discovery (R1, R2). The authors are knowledgeable about GNNs/Transformers in crystallography (R3).
-   **Clear exposition and thorough analysis**: The paper delineates and explains the task well, breaking it into smaller modules. The analysis on model depth and hyperparameters across 8 datasets with 6 baselines is thorough, and the results look promising and convincing (R4). Experiments are fairly conducted on a common benchmark (R2).
-   **Moderately impressive numerical results**: The results are moderately impressive (R3). The positive reviewer (R4) considers the results promising and convincing.

### Weaknesses

-   **Positional encoding is not E(3) invariant (R1, high confidence)**: The proposed positional encoding uses extrinsic coordinates (Cartesian and fractional) that change under rotations/translations, making the model not invariant or equivariant to E(3) transformations. This is a fundamental flaw for crystal property prediction, as properties should not change under such transformations. Laplacian embedding and random walk encoding are intrinsic but redundant (closely related) and do not capture full 3D geometry. The paper does not clarify which encodings are intrinsic vs extrinsic (R1). R4 also asks for more insight into why multiple encodings are needed and suggests ablation studies to assess redundancy and contribution of each encoding.
-   **Limited novelty and incremental contribution**:
    -   Multi Head Neighbor Attention appears to be standard multi-head attention on the line graph, not presented as a new method with clear modifications (R1). The method overall seems like a linear combination of existing methods with only moderate numerical improvement (R3). The computational overhead of the transformer architecture is not justified by the performance gains (R2).
    -   The paper does not adequately explain why modeling long-range interactions is specifically key for periodic molecules rather than being a general GNN limitation (R2).
-   **Marginal empirical gains from the core long-range component**: Figures 5 and 6 show that adding graph-wise transformer (GwT) gives marginal performance improvement, especially when the number of eTGC layers is increased. This suggests local information may be sufficient, or the transformer is not effectively capturing long-range interactions (R2). Related unanswered questions (R2, R4): Why not deeper GNN layers instead of transformers? How does GwT capture long-range if it only uses neighboring edges? Have you tried interleaving eTGC and GwT blocks to avoid losing local information (e.g., GwT(eTGC(G, L(G))) x N)? Did you explore interlacing the blocks (R2)?
-   **Missing analyses and justifications**:
    -   **Computational cost**: No analysis of speed, memory usage, or training time is provided to justify the transformer's overhead (R2, R4). R4 asks if all sub-modules are trained together and for speed analysis.
    -   **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The choice of k in k-nearest neighbor graph construction is not ablated (e.g., k=6 vs k=8) (R4). R3 also asks whether the k-nn graph is an "or" k-nn, "and" k-nn, or a directed graph. The definition of "coordination number" (used in the paper) is not explained (R3).
    -   **Ablation on positional encodings**: No ablation studies to justify the use of four different positional encodings or determine which ones are most important (R4).
    -   **Model design choices not justified**: Why is softmax attention used in eTGC? Why is a simple transformer (not a graph transformer) used for GwT? (R2). Does eTGC involve actual convolution operations? (R4).
-   **Incomplete notation and missing definitions**:
    -   In Section 3.1.3, notation \(u'\) and \(v'\) is unclear: do they represent edges or angles? The relationship to \(t_{ijk}\) from Figure 1 is not drawn, making the mathematical formulation hard to follow (R4). Also, \(t_{ijk}\) is not visible in Figure 4 – is it used in encoding? (R4).
    -   Task abbreviations in tables/figures are not explained, making it difficult for AI researchers unfamiliar with materials science to understand what properties are predicted (R2).
-   **Missing related work**: The paper omits recent works on crystal property prediction using GNNs and contrastive learning (e.g., for density of states, prototypical classifiers). The related work section should be expanded (R4).
-   **Presentation issues**: Numerous typos and grammar/spelling errors (e.g., "enxtends" in the introduction) (R1, R3). Terminology like "holistic representation of spatial information" is awkward; the key property is E(3) equivariance, not "holistic" (R1). The paper would benefit from a step-by-step walkthrough of a simple crystal structure to show how modules are connected (R4).
