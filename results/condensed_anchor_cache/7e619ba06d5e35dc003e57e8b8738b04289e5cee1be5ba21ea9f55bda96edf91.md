## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Adapprox, a memory-efficient optimizer that reduces memory consumption in Adam by using adaptive randomized low-rank matrix approximation for the second moment. Unlike fixed rank‑1 methods (Adafactor, CAME), Adapprox dynamically adjusts the rank across iterations and weight matrices using the Streamlined Randomized Subspace Iteration (S-RSI) and Adaptive Rank Selection (AS-RSI) mechanisms. Experiments on GPT‑2 and BERT show memory savings compared to AdamW and improved performance over Adafactor and CAME, with modest latency overhead (~6%).

### Strengths
- **Relevance and significance:** The problem of reducing optimizer memory for large‑scale models is timely and important, and the paper is likely to appeal to a broad audience (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- **Novelty of the approach:** The use of adaptive higher‑rank (rather than fixed rank‑1) factorization of the second moment, combined with the observation about singular values of the second moment matrix, is insightful and distinguishes Adapprox from Adafactor and CAME (R1, R3, R5). The integration of S-RSI and AS-RSI is clearly described and conceptually simple (R2, R3).
- **Clarity and presentation:** The paper is well‑written, logically structured, and easy to follow. Equations and pseudocode aid reproducibility. The motivation for adaptive rank is clear (R2, R3, R4, R5).
- **Flexible performance trade‑off:** Adapprox offers a tunable balance between memory usage and model performance, which is a practical contribution (R4).
- **Promising empirical results:** The method shows strong convergence and downstream task performance relative to memory‑efficient baselines, with only a modest increase in latency (R2, R3, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope and baselines:**
  - The comparison does not include other recent memory‑efficient optimizers such as GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024) and Adam‑mini (Zhang et al., 2024), nor SGD baselines to highlight the practical impact of memory reduction (R1, R3, R5).
  - The evaluation is restricted to GPT‑2 and BERT; results on larger models (≥1B parameters) or other domains (e.g., vision tasks, different architectures) are absent, limiting claims of generalizability (R3, R4).
  - The fine‑tuning experiments (Section 4.3) start from different pre‑trained checkpoints, making comparisons unfair; PEFT methods, a natural baseline for memory efficiency, are omitted (R1).
- **Experimental setup and reporting issues:**
  - Figure 4 lacks specification of sequence length and total batch size (in tokens). Hyperparameter selection is unclear: the phrase “uniform training parameters” does not specify whether all optimizers use identical hyperparameters, and the search space and tuning process for AdamW vs. Adapprox are not detailed. The simultaneous reporting of perplexity and evaluation loss is redundant (R1).
  - The zero‑shot evaluation of non‑instruct models (Table 3) may be unreliable without a justified prompting protocol or citation of established setups (R1).
  - Figure 2(b) is hard to read (Adafactor and S‑RSI curves are indistinguishable) and the units for mean computation time are missing. The comparison of a full optimizer step (Adafactor) to a matrix decomposition algorithm (S‑RSI) in Figure 2(b) is conceptually flawed and needs clarification (R1).
  - In Table 3, memory utilization and training time for each optimizer are not reported, making it difficult to assess the memory/accuracy trade‑off (R2).
- **Lack of ablation and analysis:**
  - No ablation study investigates the necessity of dynamic rank adaptation versus using the maximum rank throughout training (R1).
  - The effect of rank on decomposition time during actual training (when GPU has high SM occupancy) is not examined; the flat timing in Figure 2(b) may not hold under heavy load (R2).
  - The impact of rank adaptation on convergence stability under different error thresholds is unexplored; an ablation varying thresholds would strengthen the paper (R3).
  - A breakdown of latency overhead into S‑RSI and AS‑RSI components is missing, and the scaling of latency with larger ranks or deeper architectures is not provided (R3).
- **Theoretical justification:** Several design choices (k₀, k_min, k_max, l, power iteration stages) are empirical and lack theoretical grounding or validation on larger‑scale cases (R4).
- **Code and reproducibility:** The absence of released experimental code limits reproducibility, especially for the S‑RSI implementation (R1, R3).
- **Algorithmic concerns:** In Algorithm 3, the approximation via QUᵀ may produce negative entries in V_t (square root is taken); the authors do not address this edge case or prove that negative entries cannot arise despite the approximate factorization (R1).
- **Minor clarity issues:** Inconsistencies between Table 5 and Section 4.3 (GPT‑345M vs BERT‑345M); matrix U dimensions mismatch between line 161 and line 195; Algorithm 3 input lists the full matrix V₀ even though only Q and U are stored; typos exist (R1).
- **Reviewer disagreement:** While several reviewers (R3, R5) rate the novelty and impact highly (8), others (R1, R2, R4) express concerns about experimental rigor, missing baselines, and lack of theoretical support, leading to lower scores (5–6). This contrast highlights that the current experimental evidence and analysis are not uniformly convincing.
