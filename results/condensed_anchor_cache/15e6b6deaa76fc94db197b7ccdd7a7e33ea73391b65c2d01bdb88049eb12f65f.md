## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Swift Hydra, a framework for tabular anomaly detection that uses reinforcement learning (RL) with a conditional VAE to generate diverse synthetic anomalous samples, and a Mixture of Experts (MoE) of Mamba models for scalable detection. Experiments are conducted on the ADBench benchmark. Reviewers are generally positive about the clarity and novelty of combining RL, CVAE, and MoE, but raise concerns about experimental rigor, missing ablations, insufficient baselines, theoretical assumptions, and unaddressed questions about generalization, data generation, and architectural choices. One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive regarding novelty, while the other three gave 6.

### Strengths
1. **Clarity and structure**: The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and clearly organized (Reviewers 1, 2).
2. **Novel integration**: The idea of using RL to drive anomaly data augmentation by rewarding diversity and difficulty is novel and well-justified (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The coupling of conditional VAE, RL, and Mixture-of-Experts of Mamba is considered a novel contribution.
3. **Empirical performance**: Thorough experiments on ADBench show strong performance, including high AUC-ROC and short inference time (Reviewers 1, 4). Table 1 shows Swift Hydra (MoME) has lower inference time than the single-Mamba variant.
4. **Visualization**: Figure 2 clearly demonstrates that the RL-based generation produces highly diverse anomalies, and the generated samples cleanly separate from normal data (Reviewers 2, 4).
5. **Reward function validation**: The paper shows consistency between the RL reward and AUC-ROC, effectively proving the reward function’s utility (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient baselines and ablation**: Only 3 baselines are compared on ADBench; many state-of-the-art tabular anomaly detection methods are missing (Reviewer 2). No ablation study is provided to isolate the contributions of the RL-based data generation and the MoE detection module (Reviewers 2). Limited to one benchmark; analysis on additional popular datasets (including multi-class or cross-dataset scenarios) would be beneficial (Reviewer 1).
2. **Unrealistic assumption in mathematical proofs**: Theorems 2 and 3 assume that a single anomaly detector cannot distinguish anomalies from normality in overlapping regions—an assumption that holds only for linear classifiers (e.g., SVM), not for non-linear deep neural networks, making the deductions invalid (Reviewer 2).
3. **Generalization to unseen anomalies not convincingly demonstrated**: No experiment in the paper explicitly proves that the method can generalize to unseen anomalies (Reviewer 3). Even though diverse samples are generated, generative models can only produce data similar to the training distribution; how entropy alone allows generation of unseen anomalies far from known anomalies is not explained (Reviewer 4). The binary decision boundary between normal and known anomalies may not handle unseen anomalies distant from the training set (Reviewer 4).
4. **Choice of CVAE and detection space**: VAE generally has lower generation quality than other generative models (e.g., diffusion models); it is unclear why CVAE is chosen and whether a diffusion backbone would improve results (Reviewer 4). Anomaly detection is performed in the original data space rather than in the lower-dimensional latent space of the CVAE, which could be more effective; the rationale is not given (Reviewer 4).
5. **Ambiguities in algorithm and method details**: 
   - It is unclear whether both real and fake data or only real data are used during training; the description in Lines 94–105 is insufficient (Reviewer 3).
   - The balanced dataset update procedure is not described; Algorithm 1 in the appendix omits how it is updated (Reviewer 3).
   - The interaction between the “gating network” and the “Tackling winner-take-all” mechanism is ambiguous—both assign inputs to experts, but it is not explained how they are used together (Reviewer 2).
   - Why generate only anomalous samples and not also normal data? (Reviewer 3)
6. **Reinforcement learning justification**: If the action (latent variable) is directly backpropagatable from the reward/loss, why is the method framed as RL? (Reviewer 3)
7. **Computational cost**: The paper does not provide analysis of training computational cost, such as number of parameters or training time per batch (Reviewer 1).
8. **Generalizability to other domains**: The method is tested only on tabular data; its applicability to image data or time-series data is not explored (Reviewer 1).
9. **Missing toy example**: A 2D toy visualization showing generated samples and decision boundaries (e.g., normal data shaped like a sine curve with anomalies surrounding it) would help illustrate how the method handles both seen and unseen anomalies (Reviewer 4).
