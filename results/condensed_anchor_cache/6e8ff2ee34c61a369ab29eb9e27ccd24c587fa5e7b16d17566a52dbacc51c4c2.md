## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes “particle guidance” to improve diversity when drawing multiple samples from a diffusion model. Instead of sampling independently (IID), a repulsion term is added to the backward SDE so that a set of particles being generated jointly are pushed apart. Reviewers agree that the problem – increasing sample efficiency and covering modes – is important, and the presentation is clear and easy to follow. Scores range from 5 to 8, reflecting a split: one reviewer (score 8) found the empirical comparisons comprehensive and convincing, while the other three (scores 5, 5, 6) raised several concerns about theoretical novelty, the altered marginal distribution, scalability, choice of the potential function, and the significance of mode collapse in diffusion models.

### Strengths
- The problem is original and important: improving diversity and sample efficiency beyond IID sampling (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The paper is very well-written, clear, and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The idea of using a repulsive potential in the backward process is interesting and the explicit formula for the joint density is a nice theoretical contribution (R1).
- The theoretical analysis (main paper and appendix) is non-trivial and interesting (R3).
- Empirical comparisons are comprehensive and convincing (R2). Experiments are well explained, sound, and show visual improvements (R1, R3).
- The approach is succinct and clear (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Altered marginal distribution**: The joint sampling changes the marginal distribution of individual samples; the method does not produce samples that are marginally distributed according to the original diffusion model. This is not sufficiently acknowledged – the paper can be misleading (e.g., page 3 suggests “matching our distribution”) – and may be undesirable for applications that require faithful representation of the true data distribution (R2, R3, R4).
- **Limited technical novelty**: The core mechanism (adding a gradient term to the diffusion SDE) is well‑established in works such as classifier guidance, reconstruction guidance, DPS, Pseudo‑inverse, and universal guidance. The novelty lies in applying this to a set of particles and analyzing the joint distribution, but the underlying technique is not fundamentally new (R2).
- **Choice of the potential function**: There is no principled, general approach to designing the potential besides the learning method in Sec. 6, which itself requires non‑trivial training. The choice of potential and its parameters (e.g., guidance weight) is not discussed in detail; a badly chosen potential could perform worse than the original diffusion model (R1, R3). Different guidance weights were used for IID and particle guidance without justification (R3).
- **Scalability and computational cost**: Simulating multiple particles and computing their gradients introduces significant memory and time overhead. A detailed analysis of memory requirements, communication overhead between GPUs, and scalability to high‑dimensional data and large batch sizes (e.g., 64, 128) is missing (R2, R3).
- **Choice of number of particles \(n\)**: How to determine \(n\) is not discussed. For \(n_1 > n_2\), marginalizing the joint distribution of \(n_1\) does **not** recover the distribution of \(n_2\). When \(n\) far exceeds the number of modes and the guidance weight is large, particles may land in very low‑density regions (R2). The impact of \(n\) on the final generation should be studied (R4).
- **Theoretical justification is weak**: The derived joint density (Theorem 1) is not very interpretable, and there is no direct explanation why particle guidance increases diversity (R4). No toy experiment compares the actual sampled law to the intended target, e.g., with unequal‑weight modes, which would reveal the distortion of statistical weights (R1).
- **Assumption about mode collapse**: The paper implies that diffusion models suffer from mode collapse, but previous experience suggests mode collapse is not severe (R4). The method is most relevant when the number of samples is close to the number of modes – which is not the case for most data. Figure 4(a) shows diversity is already high, and Figure 4(d) appears to be an artificial overfitting setting. Practical situations where IID fails should be better highlighted (R4).
- **Missing experiments and ablations**:
  - No systematic study of the effect of batch size/\(n\) on diversity and quality (R2, R4).
  - No failure cases are discussed (R1).
  - For the text‑to‑image experiments, the reference to the method is missing in the preamble of Sec. 5 (R2). In Figure 3, how guidance scale varying from 6 to 10 is used is unclear (R2).
  - Table 1 lacks references and descriptions of competing methods; the method was not tested on the Geodiff model (R2).
  - The synthetic Gaussian mixture experiment used a very small variance; why? (R1).
  - No theoretical justification why particle guidance is better than IID (R4). Would results change if an SDE solver were used instead of an ODE solver? (R4).
  - For fair evaluation, un‑cherry‑picked images (e.g., first 50 prompts of MS COCO or PartiPrompts) should be posted with identical hyperparameters and random seed (R3).
- **Figure 1 misleading**: The initial points are depicted as tightly clustered in one mode, whereas in high‑dimensions random Gaussian noise is unlikely to concentrate like this; this oversimplifies the method’s behavior (R3).
- **Missing discussion of practical challenges for learning potentials**: Sec. 6 is interesting but practical issues in instantiating that paradigm are not addressed (R2).
