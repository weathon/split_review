## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces MetaFood3D, a dataset of 3D scans of real-world food items (637 objects across 108 categories) with multi-view images, depth maps, instance masks, textured meshes, size calibration, and nutrition information linked to a comprehensive nutrition database. The dataset is designed to support dietary assessment and computer vision tasks by bridging the gap between general 3D vision and food computing. The paper benchmarks four downstream tasks: 3D food perception, novel view synthesis/3D reconstruction, 3D food generation/rendering, and food portion size estimation.

### Strengths
- The dataset covers all important aspects for nutrition and portion size estimation: multi-view images, instance masks, size and nutrition information (R1, R4). The most important distinction from prior work is the high diversity and relatively large scale while including 3D information, which is necessary for applications requiring absolute measurements (R1). It fills a gap by providing 3D food data with detailed nutritional annotations (R2) and includes more types of annotations than any prior dataset (R4).
- The evaluations are thorough, covering 3D recognition, 3D reconstruction, 3D generation, food image/scene rendering, and portion size estimation (R1, R2, R3, R4). Empirical evidence shows that access to 3D information improves portion size estimation performance (R1).
- The paper is well written and easy to follow, with decisions behind data collection explained and justified (R1, R4).
- The dataset provides rich modalities: textured mesh files, RGB-D videos, and segmentation masks (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Scale and generalizability**: The dataset includes only 637 objects across 108 categories. Compared to OmniObject3D (190 categories, ~6000 objects), this is fewer than 1/4 the number of samples despite roughly 20 more categories (R4). This limits generalizability, especially for categories with high visual variability (R2), and may restrict the dataset to evaluation-only use rather than training (R4). The design does not sufficiently highlight characteristics of the food category (R3). Novelty is limited—only a dataset and benchmarks using existing methods, with no new method proposed (R2, R4). This is considered insufficient for a top conference (R2).
- **Dietary bias**: Food categories reflect only American dietary patterns, limiting applicability for global diets (R2, R3). The dataset does not address seasonal variations in food items, which could affect accuracy of dietary assessments over different periods (R3). There is no discussion of long-term maintenance and updating to reflect changes in food types and nutritional values (R3).
- **Annotation accuracy and trustworthiness**: Depth maps are obtained via Blender from the reconstructed 3D model, not from a real depth sensor (R4). Segmentation masks appear to be inferred from off-the-shelf models rather than manually labeled (R4). There are concerns about whether the annotated calorie information can be trusted (R2). The “volume” in Table 4 lacks a detailed description of how it is calculated (R3).
- **Missing benchmark and justification**: There is no well-designed benchmark with in-the-wild testing data collected with various smartphones in various contexts, which would tie the dataset more closely to real applications (R1). The “food generation” application seems arbitrary without justification of its utility and impact (R1).
- **Specific missing experiments, comparisons, and clarifications**: No comparison with Food-101 in Table 1 (R3). Figure 2’s 3D Food Data Distribution has images with no correspondence to food names on the abscissa (R3). Differences among objects within the same category (e.g., “Apple” point clouds) are unclear; there is no standardization for selection (R3). For can-be-flipped objects captured twice, it is unclear whether two meshes are provided or how data are integrated (R3). In Table 3, the experiment with masks shows poorer performance; the authors should compute metrics with rendered results using masks to focus on the foreground to support the claim that masking plays a crucial role for Nerf-based methods (R3). There are no visual results for 3DGS; more Nerfacto results are needed (R3). No evaluation is performed on 2DGS, though it is mentioned in Related Work (R3). Additional reconstruction baselines are suggested: nerf2mesh for textured mesh recovery from NeRF, and mesh reconstruction from Gaussian splatting via 2DGS or SuGaR (R3). There is no comparative experiment between the previously used food code and the new detailed food code to support the claim of more precise dietary analysis (R3). Table 4 is included without discussion in the main text; the meaning of its numbers is unclear (R1). The Appendix section “challenges in data collection” should provide visual examples with textual description (R3). The potential of MetaFood3D for applications beyond dietary assessment (e.g., food industry, culinary arts) is not elaborated (R3).
- **Citation typos** on L279 and L280 (R1).
