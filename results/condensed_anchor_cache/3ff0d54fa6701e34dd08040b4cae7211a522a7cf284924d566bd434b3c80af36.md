## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FedADM, an adaptive variant of FedProx that uses dissimilarity measures (parameter dissimilarity and loss dissimilarity) to adjust the proximal coefficients for each client and to control the aggregation frequency across rounds. The goal is to handle data heterogeneity and resource constraints simultaneously. The paper provides convergence analysis under convex assumptions and presents experiments on SVM and some neural network datasets, showing improvements over FedAvg and FedProx in accuracy and communication cost.

### Strengths
- Novel combination of data heterogeneity and resource constraints, adaptively adjusting both the proximal term and aggregation frequency (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4).
- Non-trivial theoretical analysis that combines FedProx with changing regularization parameters and variable number of local steps (Reviewer 1).
- The problem of tuning hyper-parameters in FedProx is interesting and meaningful (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
- The idea of using dissimilarity measures to address heterogeneity is novel (Reviewer 3).
- Results offer interesting insights into the trends of optimal hyper-parameters, which can inspire future research (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Literature and Baselines**:
    - The paper omits important baselines that handle heterogeneity and resource constraints: FedLin (Mitra et al. 2021, different local steps), Luo et al. (2022, client selection), Reisizadeh et al. (2022, delays), Cho et al. (2022, biased client selection). Without comparison, it is unclear if selecting the number of local steps is the best technique (Reviewer 1).
    - Only FedProx and FedAvg are compared; even Wang et al. (2019), which this paper extends, is not used as a baseline (Reviewer 1). More recent baselines are needed (Reviewer 2).
    - The method is incremental—essentially a refined estimation of FedProx parameters (Reviewer 2).
    - Lacks direct comparison of its assumptions and convergence bounds with state-of-the-art results and with FedProx itself (Reviewer 3).

- **Theory**:
    - The analysis is based on Wang et al. (2019), which has a highly suboptimal analysis of FedAvg: the error grows exponentially with the number of local steps τ; Lemma 4.2 requires a lower bound on the objective error to obtain an upper bound; the convergence rate depends on uncontrollable terms like φ (Reviewer 1).
    - Several papers (Khaled et al. 2020, Li et al. 2020, Stich 2019, Karimireddy et al. 2020) have fixed the FedAvg analysis with minimal assumptions and polynomial dependence on τ, but these are not used. It is unclear whether the theoretical framework can yield better rates in this resource-constrained setting (Reviewer 1).
    - Very strong assumptions (Definitions 1, 2, 3) adapted from the suboptimal analysis of Wang et al. Some require conditions on iterates that are hard to ensure; existing works use much weaker conditions even for simpler FedAvg. Definition 2 is confusing because it applies ℓ₂ norm to scalars (Reviewer 1).
    - Assumption 1.2 (Lipschitz) is unrealistic and does not hold for neural networks, especially with the proximal term. Assumption 1.3 should be based on μ_{i,k} rather than the aggregate (Reviewer 3).
    - Theoretical results are poorly explained: the surrogate function is defined in Eq 23, but results appear earlier (Eq 20). Notations like F^p are not defined (Reviewer 4).
    - The convergence rate is derived before the algorithm is presented; it is unclear whether FedADM achieves the same rate and what the relationship is between Theorem 1 and the proposed algorithm (Reviewer 2, question).

- **Algorithm and Practicality**:
    - The algorithm requires estimating several quantities (heterogeneity parameter ξ in Definition 1, terms in Definitions 2 and 3) with no details on how to compute them in practice. These estimates are only valid for convex, Lipschitz, smooth problems with ℓ₂ distance, which is a poor metric for neural networks (permutation invariance). The denominator in B_k (Eq 10) can become zero if a constant is subtracted from the loss (Reviewer 1).
    - The parameter μ_{i,k} is derived from the algorithm itself; how can the learning rate in Lemma 4.2 be set based on its maximum values? (Reviewer 3)
    - The method requires substantial additional computation (line search); it is not shown to be more effective than a standard grid search (Reviewer 2).
    - The motivation for estimating B_k, H_k is unclear; they seem not to affect model training (Reviewer 2).
    - Updating the global model requires passing local datasets, which is costly for many clients; performance without this step should be reported (Reviewer 4).
    - The algorithm appears to require communication after each gradient computation, contradicting the standard FL practice of multiple local steps; this would incur substantial communication costs (Reviewer 3 – note: the paper claims communication reduction, so this is a criticism of the implementation or description).

- **Experiments**:
    - CIFAR-10 accuracy is extremely low (30–60% in Figure 8 left), even under iid splits, where FedAvg typically achieves ≥80% (McMahan et al. 2017). No baselines are plotted for CIFAR-10 or MNIST with CNNs (Reviewer 1).
    - Experiments are very limited: only SVMs are used for main comparisons; neural networks appear only in ablation studies (Reviewer 3).
    - No testing on real federated datasets with real heterogeneity (e.g., LEAF benchmark, Caldas et al. 2019) (Reviewer 1).
    - Resource constraints are limited to total time; the method’s flexibility for energy or communication constraints is not demonstrated (Reviewer 1).
    - The algorithm recomputes the optimal number of local steps every round, but the derivation assumes a fixed K rounds (Reviewer 1).
    - No standard deviations or error bars are reported; no description of random seeds (Reviewer 1).
    - Ablation study shows only parameter fine-tuning, not the effectiveness of the proposed dissimilarity method itself (Reviewer 2).
    - Simulation settings are confusing (e.g., “number of local learners to vary between 5 and 100”—does the number of clients change over rounds?) (Reviewer 2).
    - Why does FedADM outperform centralized learning, which is usually considered an upper bound? (Reviewer 2)
    - Additional experiments needed: FedProx with varying hyper-parameters; impact of different update frequencies for FedAvg and FedProx; integration with more advanced FL algorithms (Reviewer 4).
    - The method does not incorporate client sampling, limiting its scalability for large numbers of clients (Reviewer 3).

- **Writing and Presentation**:
    - The paper is difficult to follow: notation is not explained (e.g., F^p, surrogate function). The loss function in Eq 1 is not indexed by client, and parameters w, K are not related to clients (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
    - Figures 1, 3, 4, 7, 8 plot multiple heterogeneity settings and datasets together (e.g., MNIST and CIFAR-10 in the same plot), making per-setting comparisons impossible (Reviewer 1).
    - Many typos: “iteration” instead of “iteration” in figures; “dynamic” should be “dynamically” (line 064); Eq (2) should be a function of both local and global model; Eq (4) minimization is incorrect; Eq (8) should have \barμ_k; Eq (14) should not have \barμ_k (Reviewer 1).
    - The function of dissimilarity is not explicitly modeled; it is difficult to understand how it affects convergence and model training (Reviewer 2).
