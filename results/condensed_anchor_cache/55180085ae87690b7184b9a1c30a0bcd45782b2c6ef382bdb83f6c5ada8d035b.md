## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Semantics-Adaptive Dynamic Intervention (SADI), a method that constructs a dynamic steering vector from contrastive pairs to intervene on LLM activations at inference time. SADI uses activation differences across contrastive pairs to identify critical units (attention heads, hidden states, neurons) and scales activations based on input semantics. Experiments on LLAMA2-7B-CHAT, BLOOMZ-7B, MISTRAL-7B, and FALCON-7B-INSTRUCT across multiple tasks show performance gains over fixed-vector baselines without additional training. All five reviewers found the work well-written and the experiments extensive. One reviewer (score 8) particularly highlighted the novelty, easy implementation, and data efficiency (~150 examples), while the other four (score 6) noted significant concerns about theoretical grounding, contrastive pair construction details, hyperparameter sensitivity, scalability to larger models, computational efficiency claims, and lack of analysis explaining why the method works and why design choices are effective.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clearly organized, and provides clear figures, tables, mathematical formulations, pseudocode, and a visual representation of the method.
- SADI introduces a novel dynamic intervention mechanism that adapts to input semantics, unlike prior fixed-steering-vector approaches.
- The method is easy to implement and requires no additional training.
- Extensive experimental evaluation covers four model backbones, eleven diverse tasks, multiple languages, and thorough ablation studies. Ablations show that approximately 150 examples are sufficient to achieve good performance.
- SADI consistently outperforms established baselines by substantial margins, especially when using attention heads.
- The authors promise to release the code, aiding reproducibility.
- The method is general and applicable across different LLM architectures.

### Weaknesses
- **Contrastive pair construction is under-specified.** The paper does not detail the number of contrastive examples used, the criteria for selecting positive/negative pairs (e.g., semantic similarity vs. randomness), or how to handle real-world imbalance where negative examples far outnumber positives. Using a "blank space" as an incorrect answer and a "randomly chosen incorrect answer" for multiple-choice tasks raises concerns about representativeness and variance. No ablation studies examine how the number or quality of contrastive pairs affects performance, leaving questions about scalability and overfitting.
- **No theoretical justification for why the method works.** The linear representation hypothesis is mentioned but not formally linked to the element-wise intervention or the mask \(M\). The paper lacks formal analysis, guarantees, or in-depth exploration of why design choices (e.g., attention heads > hidden states) are beneficial beyond hyperparameter sweeps. Why SADI-HIDDEN performs lower on certain tasks is not explained.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity without practical guidance.** Performance is highly sensitive to intervention strength \(\delta\) and number of key elements \(K\) (Figure 2), yet the paper offers no principled approach to select these hyperparameters for new datasets. This limits practical usability, as users would need costly grid searches.
- **Computational efficiency is not properly evaluated.** No concrete metrics (inference time, memory usage, computational overhead) compare SADI against baselines. The claim that the adaptive steering step has \(O(1)\) time complexity is misleading because computing mask \(M\) and performing element-wise multiplication are not accounted for. The method’s "cost-effectiveness" is asserted without quantitative evidence.
- **Experiments limited to 7B models.** All tested models have 7B parameters; effectiveness on larger models (13B, 30B, or more) is untested. Scalability and potential emergent behaviors at larger scales remain unknown.
- **Lack of distinction from fixed steering vectors.** Algorithm 1 computes the steering vector as the mean activation difference across all positive/negative samples in the test set, which is also "fixed" per task. The essential difference from methods like CAA is unclear, and the claimed semantic adaptability appears marginal.
- **Relationship between datasets used for Difference Extraction and Adaptive Steering is unclear.** It is not specified whether these come from the same task, different tasks, or the same dataset, nor is the rationale for the choice explained.
- **SADI+SFT yields negligible improvement.** In Table 1, SADI+SFT improves over SFT by only 0.06 points. No analysis explains why the gain is so small in the supervised fine-tuning setting.
- **Minority reviewer (score 8) found the method novel and data-efficient**, but the above weaknesses were raised consistently by all other reviewers and are not addressed in the paper.
