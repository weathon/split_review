- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes that weak correlations between first and higher-order derivatives of the hypothesis function (with respect to parameters, around initialization) are the underlying principle explaining linearization of gradient-based learning systems (e.g., the Neural Tangent Kernel regime). It introduces a formalism for asymptotic behaviour of random tensors to bound deviations from linearity during SGD training, and claims that wide neural networks satisfy these weak-correlation conditions. Reviewers agree the problem is important and ambitious, but find the presentation, novelty, and empirical validation severely lacking. Major concerns include missing related work, unclear definitions and notation, lack of experiments, and no convincing demonstration that the results go beyond existing analyses.

### Strengths
- The paper pursues a highly relevant and ambitious question: understanding when and why overparameterized neural networks become linear in their parameters (e.g., NTK regime). Any substantial contribution here would be valuable. [R1, R2, R3]
- The introduction of a framework for random tensor asymptotic behaviour may facilitate other applications in the field. [R3]
- The overall local structure of the paper is reasonable, with definitions provided. [R2]

### Weaknesses
**Missing Related Work / Novelty**
- The paper does not discuss the most directly related work: “On the linearity of large non-linear models: when and why the tangent kernel is constant” (Liu, Zhu, Belkin, NeurIPS 2020), which studies why neural networks can be linearised in relation to model derivatives. Several follow‑up papers are also omitted. [R1, R2]
- The work of Dyer & Gur-Ari (2020) is not mentioned, despite being very relevant. [R2]
- It remains unclear how the presented results improve over existing ones (e.g., Lee et al., 2019). Both results are asymptotic; when is this analysis stronger? [R2]
- The claim to prove that wide NNs satisfy the weak-correlation criteria is not properly stated in the main text; the only concrete example (“neural networks with two distinct activation functions”) is relegated to the appendix, with just one sentence in the main. [R1, R2]

**Presentation / Clarity**
- The paper is very hard to follow; logical reasoning and evidence are incoherent and confusing, even with the supplement. [R1, R3]
- Several crucial definitions are missing or appear only later without explanation:
  * The “limiting parameter” introduced on page 5 is never explained in the main text. [R1]
  * Definition 3.2 uses “O” for limiting order, but the meaning of “O” is unclear. [R1]
  * In equations (16)-(17), n0 is not defined, and there should be no Delta in the second expression of (17). [R1]
  * The function m(n) is used in the main theorems but never defined; the only description is “the typical parameter of the linearization/correlation decay where m(n)→∞”. [R3]
  * “Typical parameter of the linearization/correlation decay” is not defined. [R1]
- Section 2 is entirely unmotivated; its relevance remains unclear until much later. [R1]
- The connection between the random tensor asymptotic framework and specific neural network architectures is not clearly established; it is unclear how properties of random tensors translate to gradient behaviour in practical scaling limits. [R3]
- Many statements are unsupported or imprecise:
  * “Nearly all realistic scalable systems satisfy” Inequality 19 is not justified. [R1]
  * “This scenario can be dealt with” is given without explanation. [R1]
  * Equation 9 is labelled as applying to gradient descent, but it only holds for gradient flow. [R1]
  * The NTK does not “converge to the target function” as written somewhere. [R2]
  * A “nonlinear dynamical systems” statement lacks a reference. [R1]
- The paper contains no experiments, numerical examples, or empirical validation. At minimum, toy examples demonstrating linearization under weak correlations (and the opposite) would support the claims; real‑world applicability is unsubstantiated. [R2, R3]

**Notation / Terminology Issues**
- Notation is cumbersome and unnecessarily different from that in related papers. [R1]
- The Jacobian is called “derivative matrix” and transposed relative to common usage. [R1]
- The gradient with a subscript “T” (to denote transpose) is non‑standard. [R1]
- Equation 12 reinvents notation for high‑order partial derivatives. [R1]
- Several symbols (e.g., φ, Δ, ∇^{×d}, big‑O subscripts) are not properly introduced. [R3]
- (x, y) are erroneously called “label and images” (should be the other way around). [R1]
- The claim that parameter indices can be viewed as random variables drawn uniformly is incorrect (they are summed over all parameters). [R1]

**Scope / Limitations / Core Claims**
- The paper does not delimit its scope or discuss limitations. [R3]
- The significance of the results is not convincing: when can this analysis prove linearized training in cases where existing methods (Lee et al., 2019) do not? [R2]
- No intuition is given for the quantity introduced in equation 12. [R1]
- The loss function is assumed (without statement) to depend on F−y (e.g., square loss), which is not general. [R1]
- The definition for asymptotic behaviour of random tensors (via operator norm) is not clearly distinguished from the usual “stochastic Big O”. [R2]

**Minority Reviewers’ Divergence**
- While two reviewers rated contribution low (1 and 2) and overall scores low (3 and 1), one reviewer (R3) rated contribution as excellent (4), noting the potential for “groundbreaking insights” and that the random tensor framework could be useful. However, that same reviewer found the paper’s soundness and presentation to be very poor (1 each), ultimately recommending rejection (score 1).
