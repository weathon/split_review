## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses pretraining data detection from LLMs. It proposes Fine-tuned Score Deviation (FSD), which fine-tunes the LLM on a small set of unseen (non-member) data from the same domain, then measures the deviation of scores (e.g., perplexity, Min-k%) between the original and fine-tuned model. This increases the gap between members and non-members. Experiments on four cut-off-based benchmarks (WikiMIA, BookMIA, ArXivTection, BookTection) show significant AUC improvements over baselines. Ablations study number of fine-tuning samples, model sizes, member fine-tuning, and fine-tuning methods.

### Strengths
- Simple and intriguing idea: enlarging the difference between members and non-members can boost any MIA method. (R1)
- Works for any existing scoring function (e.g., perplexity, Min-k%). (R1)
- Very interesting observation and notable improvement in pretraining data detection accuracy. (R2)
- Clear objective and strong empirical results: substantial improvements in AUC and tpr at low fpr across datasets and models. (R2)
- Clear introduction and motivation; method well explained with intuitive examples (Figure 2). (R3)
- Comparisons with relevant baselines showing significant improvement. (R3)
- Thorough experiments including ablation studies addressing additional research questions. (R3)
- Creative use of non-member data. (R3)
- Improvement is very significant. (R4)
- Experiments and analysis are solid; writing is good and easy to follow. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation methodology flaw (major concern, R1):** All main experiments use benchmarks that separate members/non-members based on cut-off dates (WikiMIA, BookMIA, ArXivTection, BookTection). As Duan et al. (2024), Das et al. (2024), and Maini et al. (2024) show, this introduces a temporal shift that biases the benchmark. The authors acknowledge this but only remove timestamps, which is insufficient because n-gram distributions still differ; the method may classify temporal data rather than membership. The paper lacks IID experiments (e.g., using Pythia with data from the Pile) to verify that improvements are not due to temporal bias. Figure 4 results might be explained by temporal bias (training more on new data may identify n-gram distribution of post-cut-off documents). The core experiments rely on flawed methodology, undermining validity of claims.
- **Lack of IID evaluation (R1, R2):** Request to conduct experiments on an IID setup, e.g., using the MIMIR dataset (R2) or Pythia with training/dev/test splits (R1). Without such evaluation, it is unclear if FSD detects membership or temporal shifts.
- **Missing theoretical explanation/intuition (R2):** No explanation or intuition about why fine-tuning increases the gap between member and non-member distributions. Understanding this could lead to less expensive methods. (R2 also asks: what is the role of the fine-tuning technique, e.g., dependency on LoRA?)
- **High computational cost and impracticality (R2, R4, R3):** The method requires fine-tuning the LLM, which is expensive and often impractical, especially for models that do not support white-box fine-tuning or are accessed via APIs (R4, R3). Many real-world scenarios involve models where only the final output is available (R3). Additionally, fine-tuning every model you want to detect is costly (R4).
- **Difficulty of obtaining unseen/non-member data (R3, R4):** The method assumes access to non-member data from the same domain, but practical strategies for collecting such data are not elaborated. Identifying truly unseen data is difficult for models trained on vast internet datasets. The method’s reliance on this potentially hard-to-obtain data is a significant limitation. The paper does not clearly define ‘domain’ and how to handle differences across domains (e.g., if pretraining data includes scientific articles and news, how to handle fine-tuning data from only one?) (R3). If non-member data in the specific domain is not available, is there an alternative? (R3)
- **Sensitivity to fine-tuning parameters (R3):** It is not explored how sensitive FSD is to fine-tuning parameters (e.g., number of epochs, LoRA rank). Fine-tuning is delicate and can easily go past the optimal point.
- **Fine-tuning per example or per domain? (R3):** Should fine-tuning happen for each example x (or each group of examples within the same domain)? If so, that would be a big drawback.
- **Method similar to meta-evaluation (R4):** Meta-evaluation typically fine-tunes a model on data to be detected to assess detection effectiveness; FSD is similar.
- **Related work terms (R3, minor):** Terms like “Data Contamination” and “Membership Inference Attack” should be introduced earlier (Introduction or Background) if central; if not, question their inclusion in Related Work.
- **Missing ethics statement (R3, minor):** Adding an ethics statement could underscore the method’s role in detecting sensitive information used in LLM training.
- **Presentation suggestions (R3, minor):** Rename “Method” section or subsection for clarity; start a distinct “Results” section.

*One reviewer (Rating 8) was particularly positive about the method’s creativity and thorough experiments, while others (Ratings 5, 6, 6) expressed serious concerns about evaluation methodology and practicality.*
