## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the theoretical and practical foundations of the Bradley-Terry (BT) model in reward modeling for LLM alignment. It establishes an asymptotic theory for BT regression, providing the first risk bound for BT models in this context. The paper introduces the concept of order consistency as the central goal of reward modeling, arguing that any monotonic transformation of true reward suffices for downstream optimization rather than exact preference probabilities. Based on this, it proposes a classification-based alternative (CLF) using off-the-shelf binary classifiers, and also explores cross-prompt comparisons to expand training data. Extensive experiments across 6 base LLMs (2B–8B), 2 datasets, and diverse annotation designs validate the claims.

### Strengths
- Provides a thorough theoretical analysis of the BT model in LLM alignment, including establishing convergence rates and asymptotic theory for BT regression, which had been lacking.
- Introduces order consistency as a core principle, offering a theoretical framework that justifies both the BT model and the simpler classification-based approach (CLF).
- Proposes the use of cross-prompt comparisons, which significantly improve performance by expanding available comparisons.
- The paper is well-written and clear, with a logical structure that explains the purpose of each analysis step.
- Extensive experiments are thoughtfully designed, covering 6 base LLMs (2B–8B), 2 datasets, multiple annotation quality/quantity levels, and pairing choices, providing rich ablation studies that support the claims.

### Weaknesses
- The labeling scheme for the classification objective (assigning (1,0) to chosen/rejected) is rough: both responses may be helpful but one is merely more so. The paper should discuss this limitation, its potential impact on results, and consider alternative labeling schemes that better capture nuanced differences.
- Experimental model sizes are limited to 2B–8B; it remains unknown whether the CLF method's advantages scale to larger models. The paper should hypothesize about scaling behavior and propose future work to address this.
- The classification objective (CLF) surprisingly shows almost no performance degradation as annotation error rate increases (Fig. 1). This unexpected robustness requires further explanation, and implications for practical applications should be discussed.
- The paper does not compare the proposed methods on existing benchmarks (e.g., standard RLHF benchmarks). Such comparisons would help contextualize the results.
- It is counterintuitive that the classification objective (learning marginal probability) outperforms BT (learning joint probability) since the former uses less information. The paper should elaborate on why this occurs.
- The idea of cross-prompt comparisons is promising but lacks discussion of restrictions and scenarios where it is particularly effective. Future work should address this.
- Figures 2 and 3 should include dataset names in each row for added clarity.
- The evaluation of reward models relies primarily on best-of-N (BoN) sampling improvements, which do not directly reflect prediction accuracy or error—central to Theorem 2.2. Including accuracy or error metrics would provide more comprehensive validation of the theoretical bounds.
- For RL training (e.g., PPO), reward model calibration is crucial, but Theorem 2.2 focuses on minimizing prediction error and may not ensure adequate calibration. An analysis of calibration or additional experiments with PPO (or similar algorithms) are needed to assess the model's suitability for RL.
- The handling of non-transitive preferences in human judgments was raised: could such preferences be considered annotation errors, or do they reflect genuine complexities? The paper should address this issue and its implications for reward modeling.
