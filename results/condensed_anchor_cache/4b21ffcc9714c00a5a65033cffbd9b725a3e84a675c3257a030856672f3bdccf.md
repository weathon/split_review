## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies online contextual bilateral trade, where at each round a seller and buyer with private valuations arrive. Valuations are unknown linear functions of a shared context plus optional i.i.d. noise. The learner posts prices for both parties under two feedback models: two-bit (observes binary signals from both buyer and seller about whether price exceeds valuation) and one-bit (only the trade outcome). Under strong budget balance (prices for seller and buyer are identical), the deterministic case obtains \(O(\log T)\) regret via an EllipsoidPricing adaptation. The noisy case uses a combination of Scouting and Explore-or-Commit to achieve \(\widetilde O(T^{2/3})\) regret, matching a lower bound. For one-bit feedback, a reduction to the two-bit setting under global budget balance (average budget balance over rounds) is given, yielding sublinear regret. The paper initiates the feature-based version of the problem and characterizes a fundamental trade-off between feedback quality and budget balance strictness.

### Strengths
- The contextual model is well motivated, merging bilateral trade with online contextual regret minimization. [R1,R2,R4]
- Tight regret bounds: \(O(\log T)\) under strong budget balance and two-bit deterministic feedback; \(O(T^{2/3})\) (with matching lower bound) for the noisy two-bit setting. [R3,R4]
- The reduction from one-bit to two-bit feedback by relaxing per-round budget balance to global budget balance is elegant and establishes an interesting trade-off between feedback and constraint. [R1,R2,R4]
- The theoretical analysis is sound and provides a complete treatment of the two-bit feedback case. [R2,R4]
- The paper is the first to study feature-based online bilateral trade. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- The novelty relative to prior work (Cohen et al. 2020, Contextual Search, etc.) is not clearly delineated. The adaptation of feature-based pricing, Explore-or-Commit, and Scouting uses known tools; specific innovations for handling the interdependence of buyer/seller valuations, overlapping uncertainty sets, and price selection within the feasible intersection are not sufficiently explained. [R1,R3]
- The two-bit feedback assumption is strong: it is unclear how the learner obtains truthful binary signals from both agents about whether the posted price exceeds their private valuation, especially when agents may be strategic. [R3]
- The strong budget balance constraint (identical prices for seller and buyer) is hard to justify in many real-world applications; the \(O(\log T)\) guarantee relies on this restrictive condition. [R3]
- No numerical experiments or discussion of computational complexity (per-round time, memory) are provided, limiting assessment of practical applicability. [R3]
- The one-bit setting lacks a matching lower bound. [R2]
- The intuition behind combining Explore-or-Commit and Scouting to achieve \(O(T^{2/3})\) regret is underdeveloped; e.g., how exploration regret reduces the range of \(\Delta_t\) leading to the final bound is not explained. [R4]
- In the one-bit reduction, the dependence on the unknown parameter \(\alpha\) is not justified; it may be a side effect of the specific budget collection strategy, and it is unclear whether adaptive exploration could remove or improve it. [R4]
- The paper does not discuss whether approximately linear reward functions would still work. [R4]
- The relationship to the closely related work “A contextual online learning theory of brokerage” (2024) – in which both traders share the same expected valuation – is not discussed in enough detail. [R2]
- The problem of learnability under one-bit feedback with per-round (instead of global) budget balance is not addressed. [R2]
- Reviewers disagree on the contribution’s significance: two reviewers (scores 8) find the results strong and the reduction elegant, while two (scores 6) note that the technical novelty is incremental and key assumptions are strong. [R1,R4 vs. R2,R3]
