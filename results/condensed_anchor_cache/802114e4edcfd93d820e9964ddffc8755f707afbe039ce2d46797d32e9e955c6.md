## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a kernel selection method for score-based causal discovery within the framework of Huang et al. (2018). It introduces a joint marginal likelihood score (instead of conditional likelihood) and optimizes kernel hyperparameters (specifically, the Gaussian kernel bandwidth) by maximizing this marginal likelihood. The goal is to avoid manual heuristic kernel parameter selection. The method is evaluated on synthetic data and two real-world benchmarks (including Sachs), and is claimed to outperform the original fixed-parameter approach and the PC algorithm.

### Strengths
- The problem of hyper-parameter selection in kernel-based causal discovery is important, and the marginal likelihood approach provides a principled criterion for this selection [Reviewer 1].
- The proposed method is convincingly motivated from an information-theoretic perspective: minimizing the mutual information between parents of a variable and the noise term [Reviewer 3].
- The method outperforms the approach of Huang et al. (2018) and the PC algorithm on synthetic and two real-world data sets [Reviewer 3]. (Note: This claim is made by the paper and accepted at face value by some reviewers, while others question the adequacy of the comparisons.)
- The paper includes a proof of local consistency for the proposed score, adding theoretical foundation [Reviewer 4].
- The intuition behind the method is presented in an accessible manner [Reviewers 3, 4].
- The initial empirical experiments support the claims, although they would benefit from expansion [Reviewer 4].

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty and strong similarity to prior work**: The paper is extremely close to Huang et al. (2018) in both content and methodology, and the differences are not clearly articulated [Reviewers 1, 2, 3]. The core idea of optimizing kernel hyperparameters in Bayesian network structure learning using a Gaussian-process-based marginal likelihood score was already developed more than 20 years ago (Friedman & Nachman, UAI2000), yet this paper is not cited and the score function (Eq. 13) may be very similar to that prior work [Reviewer 5]. The incremental introduction of trainable kernel parameters (instead of heuristic estimation) is considered too minor for a conference contribution [Reviewer 3].
- **Misrepresentation of baseline method (Huang et al. 2018)**: The paper states that in Huang et al. (2018) the kernel width is chosen in a manual and pre-defined way. However, the original paper actually learns the noise variance hyperparameter by maximizing the marginal likelihood with gradient methods (see page 1555 of the Huang et al. paper). The authors appear to have reproduced the baseline with a fixed width, which misrepresents the method and casts doubt on the validity of the empirical comparisons [Reviewer 2].
- **Incomplete experimental evaluation and comparisons**:
  - The evaluation lacks comparison against other kernel-based causal discovery methods [Reviewer 1]. Numerous score-based methods published after Huang et al. (2018) are not compared (e.g., several that have been evaluated on the Sachs dataset) [Reviewer 3].
  - The experimental section is thin; it only compares against the original Huang method (with a fixed/incorrect reproduction) and the PC algorithm [Reviewers 2, 3, 4].
  - The practical impact of optimizing the kernel parameters is unclear, especially compared to using fixed parameters or simple heuristics [Reviewer 1].
  - No ablation study is performed to contrast the proposed joint marginal likelihood score against a version where kernel hyperparameters are optimized using conditional likelihood (Eq. 11). Such a comparison would help explain why joint marginal likelihood avoids overfitting [Reviewer 4].
- **Limited scope of kernel choices**: Despite emphasizing the criticality of kernel selection, the paper only uses the Gaussian kernel throughout. The impact of other kernel types (polynomial, Laplacian, deep kernels) is not discussed or tested, undermining the completeness of the discussion [Reviewer 2, 4].
- **Missing key references** in the field, including Friedman & Nachman (UAI2000) and several other recent score-based methods [Reviewers 3, 5].
- **Unclear theoretical details and assumptions**:
  - The local consistency proof may be limited to Gaussian noise and a Gaussian kernel; this is not clarified [Reviewer 4].
  - Lemma 1 does not clearly state whether a Gaussian kernel is assumed and how the condition in Eq. 15 is used [Reviewer 4].
  - The derivation of Eq. 13 under non-Gaussian noise is not addressed [Reviewer 4].
  - The term “mixture of independent noise variables” is introduced but not sufficiently explained [Reviewer 4].
  - The claim that joint likelihood avoids overfitting in structure learning is not elaborated upon (e.g., because of marginalization over f) [Reviewer 4].
- **Notation and presentation issues**:
  - Notation is unclear and inconsistent: the noise ε is introduced without definition; the function \(k_x\) and its relation to feature mapping \(\phi_X\) is ambiguous; parent sets are not consistently denoted (e.g., \(PA\) vs. \(PA_X\)); the connection between Eqs. (3) and (4) is confusing; parameter \(\theta\) in Eq. (6) is undefined [Reviewer 5].
  - A typo is present at the beginning of Section 2 [Reviewer 5].
- **Unaddressed questions that overlap with criticisms**:
  - The algorithm maximizes \(\log p(X=x, PA=pa \mid f, \sigma_x)\) while Huang et al. (2018) uses \(\log p(X=x \mid PA, f, \sigma_x)\). The difference in dynamics and potential performance implications are not discussed [Reviewer 2].
  - Is Eq. 11 the same as the conditional likelihood? The authors should clarify [Reviewer 4].
  - The motivation examples in the paper have issues: (a) The RBF kernel’s RKHS is dense in \(L_p\) spaces, so the first example’s claim of inadequate capacity may be due to finite samples rather than kernel choice; the term “correlated” is used instead of “dependent” [Reviewer 2]. (b) The second example’s regression direction does not follow the standard conditional independence test procedure (regressing X1 on X2 and X3 on X2, not X2 on X1), making the spurious edge explanation questionable [Reviewer 2].
- **Computational cost**: The use of Gaussian processes implies \(O(n^3)\) complexity, but this is not discussed. The additional cost of optimizing a potentially non-convex objective over the simpler fixed approach is not analyzed [Reviewers 2, 3, 4].
- **Minority positive comment**: One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 6) found the hyper-parameter selection important and the marginal likelihood criterion reasonable, but even that reviewer noted weak differentiation and insufficient comparison. Other reviewers (scores 3, 3, 5, 5) were more critical, with two (Reviewers 2 and 5) finding the contribution poor and the misrepresentation or missing prior work severe.
