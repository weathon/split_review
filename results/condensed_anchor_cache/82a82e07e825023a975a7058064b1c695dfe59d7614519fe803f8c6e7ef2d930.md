## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MaestroMotif, which extends the Motif framework (Kilssarov, 2024) by using an LLM to translate natural language skill descriptions into reward functions, generate code for skill initiation/termination, and produce a code-based high-level policy over learned skills. Skills are trained via PPO on the NetHack Learning Environment (NLE) using rewards synthesized from human-annotated offline data. Evaluations on long-horizon tasks (navigate, interact, composite) show that MaestroMotif outperforms LLM-only policies, reward-based RL baselines, and prior hierarchical methods like Motif. Ablations highlight the importance of joint skill learning and LLM scale. Reviewers praised the experimental breadth and significance, but raised concerns about reliance on human expertise, generalizability beyond NetHack, baseline fairness, and missing details in the main text.

### Strengths
- MaestroMotif effectively incorporates human prior knowledge into skill design, yielding high-performing agents on complex tasks in NLE. The zero-shot composition of learned skills for arbitrary natural language commands is a notable achievement.
- The experiments are comprehensive: comparisons against LLM-only, PPO, behavior cloning, and Motif baselines; ablations of simultaneous vs. isolated skill learning, LLM scale (Figure 9), and architecture variants; and results clearly demonstrate MaestroMotif’s superiority on the tested tasks.
- The approach fits cleanly within prior work on option frameworks and “code-as-policies,” and is a logical extension that combines large labeled datasets, human expertise, and LLM code synthesis/planning – exemplifying human-AI collaboration.
- The paper provides full reproducibility details in the appendix (unit tests, specific prompts, re-prompting strategies), which is a valuable resource for future work.
- Writing is clear and well motivated; connections to the option framework make the paper easy to parse.
- The simultaneous learning of skills enables curriculum effects – an interesting and well-demonstrated insight.

### Weaknesses
- **Source of performance gains**: Reviewers questioned whether gains come from the method itself or from the heavy human effort in selecting and engineering skill descriptions and prompts. Baseline algorithms may not have received comparable task decomposition or human priors, making the comparison unfair. Reviewer 1 (score 5) emphasized this concern, while Reviewer 3 (score 10) viewed the technique as generally applicable. The delta over baselines is less impressive if baselines are “severely hamstrung” (e.g., score-maximizing approaches cannot accept any sub-goals; Reviewer 2).
- **Generalizability**: Evaluation is limited to a single environment (NetHack). Skills like *Descender* and *Ascender* are highly environment-specific. Reviewers suggested demonstrations in robotics or other domains, and questioned whether the five chosen skills are a targeted set rather than reusable. Reviewer 1 argued that foundational skills on a robot platform with zero-shot transfer would be stronger evidence. Reviewer 2 noted blurry lines between core contribution and NetHack-specific tweaks.
- **Reliance on offline data and human annotation**: Reward functions are trained only on pre-collected expert/exploratory trajectories, which may be out-of-distribution for the final RL policy. This distribution shift may be more severe here because the RL baselines used to collect the data were poor. Reviewers requested verification that the reward model generalizes (e.g., measure annotator agreement on final policy trajectories). The need for a pre-existing dataset of trajectories is a limitation that is not discussed, nor are the quality/diversity requirements or the number of trajectories needed.
- **Human effort for labeling and feedback**: Learning the option networks requires intense manual effort: humans must label different states with preferences/task-alignment. The system also depends on the quality of human feedback; no robustness analysis is provided. It is unclear how much time and expertise went into designing the skill descriptions and prompts – would non-experts produce the same results? Reviewer 4 raised this as a potential brittleness.
- **Cost and computational expense**: The framework relies on repeated prompting of a 405B model, which could become prohibitively expensive. No metrics on time, memory, or monetary cost are reported for running MaestroMotif on a single task or for generalization to new tasks.
- **Missing pseudocode and clarity in main text**: Several important architectural decisions are unclear without reading the appendix:
    - How does the LLM prioritize among skills when multiple instantiation conditions are met?
    - What happens when no skill’s instantiation condition is satisfied?
    - Does the training-time policy over skills adapt/evolve as skills change during training? (Reviewer 1)
    - The exact relationship between MaestroMotif and Motif (Kilssarov, 2024) is not fully specified.
- **LLM code generation reliability**: MaestroMotif relies heavily on LLM code-generation quality. Figure 9 shows that many LLMs struggle even with unit tests and code refinement. It is unclear whether the 405B model achieves near‑perfect code or still has key failures. Further failure‑mode analysis is needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Assumption about skill decomposition**: The method assumes that a fixed set of pre‑defined skills can cover all testing tasks, which is convenient but may not hold in general. If a test task requires a missing skill, the current architecture cannot recover. Reviewers asked whether the LLM could propose new candidate skills or whether a failure detection mechanism could trigger re‑training.
- **Evaluation details missing**: 
    - The performance reported for task‑specific training – is it during training (with exploration noise) or after training (no noise)? If with noise, it may underestimate performance on mistake‑sensitive tasks (e.g., crossing a bridge) (Reviewer 4).
    - Baseline comparisons: Reviewer 4 noted that MaestroMotif’s skill training also consumes GPU time; for fair comparison, “RL from scratch” approaches should be given the same total training time and environment interactions.
    - Why does the Reward Information baseline perform worse than Motif task‑specific training, if it has access to the true task reward? (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing connections and discussion**: 
    - No mention of planning literature (PDDL, TAMP) where LLMs generate operator descriptions similar to MaestroMotif’s skills (Reviewer 4).
    - The issue of skill chaining (mismatched termination and initiation distributions) is not addressed – how would the approach handle this? Would more constrained termination functions be needed?
    - It is not specified whether the code will be released publicly.
- **Minor issues** (Reviewer 1):
    - Typos: “aploicy” → “a policy” (L196); “Llama 3.1 70b” → “70B” (L239); “implemenetation” (L508).
    - Figure 8(c): varying performance is hard to compare because the two approaches are visualized in separate plots.
    - Appendix A.2 and A.3 have identical subsection titles.
    - Line 269: “What negative impact?” – needs clarification.
- **Contrast between reviewers**: Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 2) was most critical, questioning the method’s novelty and generalizability. Reviewer 3 (score 10, confidence 4) was very positive, calling the results a “big improvement in hierarchical RL” and the technique “quite general.” Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 4 (both score 8) were generally positive but raised specific concerns about baseline fairness, cost, and missing analyses. This disagreement underscores the need for clearer attribution of performance gains and broader validation.
