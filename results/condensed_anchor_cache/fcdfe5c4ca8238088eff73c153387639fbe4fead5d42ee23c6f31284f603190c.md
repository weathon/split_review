## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a concept discovery and learning (CDL) framework to align semantic representations of category labels with relevant attribute labels in vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP. It observes inconsistencies between class labels and attributes. CDL queries an LLM for useful visual features per category, builds a concept-class correlation matrix, uses mutual information to select discriminative class-agnostic concepts, and fine-tunes CLIP’s last projection layer. Experiments on ImageNet, Food-101, CIFAR-100, and smaller datasets are conducted, along with a human study on interpretability. The paper claims improved interpretability and recognition accuracy.

### Strengths
- The idea is reasonable and contributes to interpretability; it is technically and intuitively correct.
- The framework is easy to follow and implement, with clear figures.
- Extensive experiments on multiple image datasets (ImageNet, Food-101, CIFAR-100, etc.) and a human study are conducted.
- Experimental results show that the proposed approach outperforms baselines.

### Weaknesses
- **Technical novelty and contributions (minority reviewer more negative):** The overall framework—decomposing a class name into basic visual concepts and composing them for supervision—is seen as trivial by the minority reviewer (score 3). The mutual-information-based ranking (Eq. 3) is not new; the minority reviewer doubts overall novelty and finds contributions weak. The majority reviewers (scores 5) found the framework reasonable but also noted limitations.
- **Marginal performance gains:** Table 1 shows only little improvement from using concepts; gains are marginal and do not justify the complexity. In Table 2, boosting concepts from 1000 to 2000 yields only 0.1% improvement on ImageNet, suggesting the concept space is not effectively utilized or selected concepts are not sufficiently discriminative.
- **Alignment evidence missing:** Table 1 demonstrates misalignment in CLIP but there is no similar table for CLIP+CDL showing that it improves the alignment between classes and concepts. Current experiments only show CDL becomes a good concept detector, not that it effectively relates concepts to classes.
- **Heuristic prompting method:** The method for alleviating the class-name dependence (e.g., “What are useful visual features for distinguishing a {category name} in a photo?”) is heuristic; alternatives should be considered and compared.
- **Missing comparisons:** Experiments should follow settings of prior work. The paper should compare with VDES (ICLR 2023) and show results with different backbones.
- **Outdated references:** Only two papers from 2023 are cited. Notably, VDES is cited as an arxiv paper despite being published at ICLR 2023.
- **Justification of mutual information:** The necessity of MI over simpler criteria (e.g., normalized co-occurrence frequency between categories and classes) is not explained. No ablation study or example concepts selected for different classes are provided.
- **Missing similarity threshold details:** The threshold `$\texttt{th}$` (page 5) is not discussed beyond Section 3.2. No typical value, no statement on whether it is fixed across datasets, and no sensitivity analysis.
- **Human study missing details:** Figure 4 lacks error bars despite each data point annotated by three humans (as per appendix). No information about the annotator interface (e.g., whether samples were displayed fairly to avoid order/position bias).
- **Clarity issues:** The meaning of “CDL+CDL” in Table 2 is unclear. A typo (“directlyed”) appears on page 2. The paper is not always easy to follow the key idea; many insights behind the simple language concepts are not clearly explored.
