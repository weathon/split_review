## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers acknowledge EMMA’s goal of efficient cross-modal fusion via a lightweight early-fusion module that leverages pre-aligned CLIP encoders. Strengths include intuitive design, interpretability analysis, hallucination robustness, and a clear focus on efficiency. However, all reviewers raise significant concerns about novelty (strong similarity to LLaVA), the effectiveness of the CLIP text encoder for general instruction-following, unfair or incomplete experimental comparisons, insufficient ablation, presentation issues, and limited benchmark coverage.

### Strengths
- **Intuitive and simple design**: Using the inherent alignment of pre-trained CLIP vision and text encoders for early fusion is natural, and the overall structural design is clear and simple.  
- **Interpretability analysis**: The paper provides novel and detailed analytical insights into the modality adaptation process through L1, L2, and mutual information comparisons, enhancing the understanding of how visual and textual tokens are integrated.  
- **Robustness against hallucinations**: Empirical results on AMBER and FOIL demonstrate that EMMA reduces hallucinations, an important practical advantage.  
- **Motivation**: Improving efficiency with minimal added parameters (under 0.2% increase) is a well-motivated and relevant goal for MLLMs.  
- **Writing and presentation** (majority view): The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with substantial experiments and discussion. *(Note: one reviewer found the writing disorganized – see Weaknesses.)*

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty / strong similarity to LLaVA**: The model is very similar to LLaVA; the only additions are a CLIP text encoder and an early fusion module. The paper does not provide a theoretical comparison with LLaVA or Qwen‑VL, which already use straightforward projection layers with minimal parameters.
- **CLIP text encoder effectiveness unsubstantiated**:  
  - CLIP is trained for classification, not general instruction-following. Its effectiveness for long or complex instructions is doubtful; no performance guarantees exist when instructions are verbose or correlate poorly with visual tokens.  
  - Benchmarks are skewed toward VQA tasks (high instruction–vision correlation). The method should be evaluated on fine-grained perception benchmarks such as AI2D, OCRbench, TextVQA, and InfoVQA – especially given slight performance declines on ChartQA and DocVQA, which suggest limitations in spatial reasoning and text recognition.  
  - Visualizations of CLIP text encoder activation on long/non‑classification instructions are needed.  
- **Unfair or incomplete experimental comparisons**:  
  - Table 1 omits LLaVA; Figure 3 only compares with LLaVA. Both need to include a broader set of methods (e.g., mPLUG‑Owl2, Qwen‑VL) in a consistent table/figure.  
  - No comparison with other vision‑language early‑fusion works (e.g., QA‑ViT) or other adapter-based methods (e.g., MMA, Tip‑Adapter). Experiments keeping the vision and language models fixed across adapters are required.  
  - Training data quantities are mismatched: EMMA uses 1.8M samples, while baselines differ. Controlled experiments are needed (e.g., EMMA on LLaVA 1.5’s 1.2M set, and LLaVA 1.5 on EMMA’s 1.8M set).  
  - The visual models used by baselines in Tables 2 and 4 are not reported; their parameter counts should be provided.  
- **Unwarranted claim about CLIP alignment**: The claim that CLIP’s pre-alignment obviates complex cross-modal modules is unsupported because EMMA still uses a projection layer (similar to LLaVA) and adopts pre-alignment training. Ablation experiments removing or replacing this projection layer (e.g., with a simple linear layer) are necessary.  
- **Interpretability concerns**:  
  - The conclusion that the alignment module identifies the “most” informative textual tokens is not convincing: a fixed linear layer cannot adapt to the random positions of valuable tokens, and the method’s behavior on complex instructions (e.g., long hints in SQA) is unclear.  
  - The mutual information analysis (Figure 6) may be unreliable for datasets requiring YES/NO responses, where mutual information between visual representations and response encodings lacks meaningful relevance.  
  - Figure 5’s legends are unclear (purple, light green, dark green bars); more descriptive captions are needed.  
  - The analysis in Section 3.2 should include more datasets beyond MMVP.  
- **Presentation and writing inconsistencies** (one reviewer): The paper is disorganized in places – Section 3.1 ends with experimental results, Section 3.2 begins with model details, Figure 1 is referenced long after its appearance, and Figures 2, 5, 6 lack axis labels and detailed captions.  
- **Figure scaling issue**: Figure 3’s scale exaggerates small improvements (e.g., 64.3→66.19 on MMB‑en); a histogram bar would be more appropriate.  
- **Overly absolute claim**: The statement in lines 247–250 that “a persistent challenge remains in effectively aligning these two modalities” is too absolute, as prior work solves task-specific adaptability via the LLM itself.  
- **Marginal gains**: Performance improvements are small on many datasets.  
- **Insufficient hallucination discussion**: If hallucination is highlighted, the paper should cite more relevant work (e.g., R‑Bench, MMRel, MME).  
- **POPE subsets**: Detailed per‑subset performance on POPE should be reported.  
- **Missing figure reference**: The conclusion at line 338 does not clearly indicate which figure it refers to.
