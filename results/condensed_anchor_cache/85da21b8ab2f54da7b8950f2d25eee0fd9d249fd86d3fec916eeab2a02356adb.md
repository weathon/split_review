## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces JOOD, a jailbreak attack against safety-aligned LLMs and MLLMs. The core insight is that out-of-distribution (OOD) transformations of harmful prompts—such as mixing text (e.g., interleaved, concatenated, word-level permutations like "baopmpble" for "bomb") and images (Mixup, CutMix, RandAug)—increase model uncertainty and bypass safety alignment. Experiments on GPT-4, GPT-4V, and LLaVA show high attack success rates (ASR), with JOOD outperforming prior baselines. The paper is well-written and the OOD perspective is considered novel by most reviewers. However, reviewers are divided: one (score 6) finds the work interesting and clear, while another (score 3) criticizes the lack of technical innovation and practical defensibility. The two other reviewers (scores 5) acknowledge the importance of the vulnerability but raise concerns about experimental completeness and method clarity.

### Strengths

- **Novel OOD perspective**: The paper shifts focus from in-distribution adversarial prompts to OOD-ified inputs, revealing a critical vulnerability in safety alignment (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The presentation is clear, with helpful diagrams (Figure 1) and examples (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- **High attack effectiveness**: JOOD achieves strong ASR against proprietary models (GPT-4, GPT-4V) compared to existing attack methods (Reviewers 2,3,4).
- **Simple and practical approach**: The proposed transformations (text mixing, image mixing) are straightforward yet effective, underscoring how even basic OOD transformations can bypass safety guardrails (Reviewers 3,4).
- **Comprehensive empirical validation**: The paper includes multiple attack scenarios, clear metrics (ASR, harmfulness scores), and ablation studies on transformation techniques and hyperparameters (Reviewer 4).  
  *Note: Reviewer 3 considers the experimental design not comprehensive enough (see Weaknesses).*  
- **Significant implications**: The findings highlight an urgent need for safety mechanisms robust to OOD inputs, especially given real-world deployment of LLMs/MLLMs (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of technical and theoretical innovation**: The core idea (data mixing) is not novel beyond the OOD observation; no new techniques or theory are introduced. The paper does not clearly differentiate JOOD from prior work like [[R1]](Jailbreak in pieces) and [[R2]](FigStep) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3 question 1).
- **Practical evaluation concerns**: The harmfulness evaluation relies on external LLMs (e.g., GPT-4 as judge), which may introduce bias or false positives (e.g., a benign response about bomb survival could be flagged as harmful). The reported ASR may stem from evaluator limitations rather than genuine jailbreak effectiveness (Reviewer 1).
- **Easily detectable/filterable**: OOD inputs like word-mixing are likely to be caught by fluency checks or perplexity filters. A low-fluency prompt can be trivially rejected (Reviewer 2).
- **Ambiguity and unreliability of text-mixing**: Blended words (e.g., "baopmpble") may not be consistently interpreted as intended by different models or tokenizers. The mechanism by which models reconstruct the original harmful concept is unclear—e.g., OOD words should map to UNKNOWN tokens, yet the model still answers as if it understood "bomb" (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
- **No systematic method for word mixing**: For a new target word or victim model, the attacker must re-design the mixing strategy; there is no principled approach to generate effective OOD prompts efficiently (Reviewer 2).
- **Tokenizer dependence not addressed**: Different LLMs use different tokenizers (e.g., BPE vs. WordPiece). The same mixed word may not work across models, but the paper does not analyze this (Reviewer 2).
- **Unclear candidate generation and optimization**: The paper does not specify how many candidate inputs are needed per attack, nor is there a strategy to guide optimized input generation (Reviewer 1). The attack setup is ambiguous: e.g., are all five text-mixing and all four image-mixing techniques applied independently, and if so, how are the best ones selected? (Reviewer 3 question 3).
- **Insufficient model coverage**: Comparisons are mostly limited to GPT-4V for MLLM attacks; missing benchmarks on other MLLMs (e.g., Gemini) and especially on LLMs—only GPT-4 is tested. Open-source LLMs (Llama, Mistral) are not included. For GPT-4, the attack fails entirely on the Social Violence category (ASR 0) without explanation (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- **Missing comparisons with state-of-the-art**: GPT-4o and other recent models are not evaluated. Replicating JOOD on GPT-4o and GPT-4V was not consistently successful (Reviewer 4 question 3).
- **Lack of analysis on individual text-mixing variants**: The paper does not compare the effectiveness of different text-mixing techniques (e.g., H-Interleave vs. V-Interleave) nor propose which are optimal (Reviewer 4).
- **Description of attack methods is unclear**: The formulas (1),(2),(3) omit variables for text-mixing and image-mixing techniques; the exact transformation pipeline is confusing (Reviewer 3).
- **No defensive measures discussed**: Given the simplicity of JOOD, the paper does not explore potential defenses or countermeasures (Reviewer 3 question 2).
- **How to handle multi-word malicious concepts?** The method is only demonstrated on single-word harmful concepts (e.g., "bomb"). For multi-word phrases, no mixing strategy is provided (Reviewer 2 question 3).
- **Minor typo**: "For OOD-ifying T^h into a novel text instruction, …" (Reviewer 2).
