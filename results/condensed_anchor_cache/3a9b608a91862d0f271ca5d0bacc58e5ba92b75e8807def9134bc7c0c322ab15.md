## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SWSA (Selection With Synthetic Anomalies), a framework that generates synthetic anomalies via diffusion-model interpolation in latent space (using a small normal-image support set and no training) to enable model selection for anomaly detectors when no labeled validation data exists. The method is tested on three datasets (Flowers, CUB, MVTec AD) for two tasks: selecting image-based anomaly detectors and selecting prompts for CLIP-based zero-shot anomaly detection. The authors report that SWSA often matches selections made with a ground-truth validation set and outperforms baseline selection strategies.

### Strengths
- **Important question**: The work addresses a practical and under-studied problem—how to evaluate/select anomaly detectors without labeled validation data.
- **Clear and simple idea**: Generating synthetic outliers via latent interpolation and diffusion decoding is conceptually straightforward.
- **Novel method**: Using synthetically generated anomalies as a validation proxy for model selection is a novel contribution.
- **Strong empirical performance on natural-image datasets**: On Flowers and CUB, SWSA often matches ground-truth selection (though it does not hold on MVTec AD; see Weaknesses).
- **Thorough evaluation and good baseline set**: The study includes multiple anomaly detectors, three types of synthetic anomalies, and comparison against several baselines.
- **Extensive experiments**: Workload is significant, covering both natural images and industrial anomaly detection (MVTec AD) and prompt selection for CLIP.
- **Good literature review**: Related work is nicely reviewed and the overall presentation is considered excellent by Reviewer 2 and good by Reviewer 1.
- **CLIP prompt selection validation**: The framework is also shown to be effective for selecting prompts in zero-shot CLIP-based anomaly detection.

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaiming in abstract/conclusion**: The abstract states the method “selects the same models and hyper-parameters as selection with a ground‑truth validation set” in an extensive study, but this fails on the more realistic MVTec AD dataset (see Tables 1, 2 and Figure 2; Reviewer 1: “on MVTec it does not hold, see their appendix”). This makes the conclusion misleading.
- **Limited evaluation of anomaly detectors**: Only one distance‑based anomaly detection method is evaluated (Reviewer 1). The authors should test reconstruction‑based methods (e.g., Autoencoder) or other families (Reviewer 1’s question).
- **Simplistic anomaly generation**: Mixup in a single latent space provides no control over the type of anomaly created. Calling it “style and content mixture” is dubious because there is no explicit separation of content and style (Reviewer 1). The authors should consider alternatives: stochastic mixup in latent space, inpainting, or perturbing features in different latent dimensions (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Poor performance on industrial anomalies**: On MVTec AD (where anomalies are subtle, e.g., small defects), the synthetic anomalies are not effective (Reviewer 3). The method works for high‑level semantic anomalies (CUB) but fails for low‑level semantic anomalies (MVTec AD) (Reviewer 3). See Table 1 and Figure 2.
- **Lack of control over diversity and realism**: The diffusion model may generate anomalies that do not resemble the true anomaly distribution (e.g., unrealistic images that would never be observed) (Reviewer 2). The diversity of generated anomalies is not guaranteed, potentially leading to a narrow evaluation (Reviewer 2).
- **Bias from diffusion model**: The method relies on diffusion models trained on specific datasets, which may not generalize to all anomaly types (Reviewer 2).
- **No control for the encoding‑decoding step**: Real images are never run through the encoder‑decoder pipeline, so the method may learn to discriminate real vs. diffusion‑generated images rather than real vs. anomalous (Reviewer 1). An experiment is needed to check whether the synthetic anomalies are recognized as generated rather than anomalous (Reviewer 1’s question).
- **Limited exploration of dataset differences**: The paper does not discuss why synthetic anomalies work on Flowers/CUB but not on MVTec AD, and whether different synthesis strategies are needed for different anomaly types (Reviewer 3).
- **Figure 3 clarity**: Figure 3 lacks explanation of what is plotted (e.g., the five repetitions in the figure; what they are and whether they can be compared). The legend is incomplete (e.g., squares not included in the legend) (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Applicability of zero‑shot task**: The zero‑shot prompt selection result is interesting scientifically, but practically it is unlikely that practitioners would use such synthetic data for serious anomaly detection (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing experiments on smaller defects**: For MVTec, it is unclear whether the method would work for smaller defects or anomalies caused by imaging‑setting differences rather than semantics (Reviewer 1’s question).
- **Definition of anomaly in image domain**: The paper does not clarify how anomalies are defined—are they out‑of‑distribution data, unrealistic images, or something else? If the latter, why would one expect to observe such images? (Reviewer 2’s question).
- **Complex/multimodal normal data**: It is unclear how the method would perform when the normal data distribution is complex or multimodal, as the diffusion model might struggle to generate meaningful anomalies (Reviewer 2).
- **Redesign of generation process**: The paper is heavily experimental; the authors do not provide insights or ideas for redesigning the diffusion generation process based on the experimental results (Reviewer 3).
