## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CAMEL, a meta-learning method that learns a shared neural network feature extractor with task-specific affine linear heads (equation 3.2). At test time, the linear head for a new task is obtained via least‑squares regression (or gradient descent) on a few labelled examples. The authors motivate the affine structure by showing that many physical systems admit a linearly parameterized form, and prove (Proposition 1) that under certain conditions the learned task weights can recover the true physical parameters, hence enabling interpretability. Experiments compare CAMEL against MAML, ANIL, and CoDA on several physical modelling tasks and a robotic control problem, reporting lower error and lower computational cost.

### Strengths

- The paper is well‑motivated and well‑written. The motivation for interpretable data‑driven modelling of physical systems and for simplifying gradient‑based meta‑learning is clearly justified.
- CAMEL is a simple but effective method; the use of task‑specific vectors \(w_t\) that can be learned via least squares is natural for physical applications.
- Table 1 and the discussion in Section 2.2 provide a useful overview of how CAMEL differs from MAML, ANIL, and CoDA.
- Experimental results convincingly demonstrate low identification error (e.g., Figure 2) and competitive performance across multiple physical systems. The provided PyTorch code is simple and easy to understand.
- The method is a reasonable model when the true dynamics follow the formulation of equation 3.2.
- The focus on interpretability and physical‑parameter identification is important and often overlooked by existing meta‑learning methods.

### Weaknesses

- **Experimental setting and baseline comparisons.**  
  - Every experimental meta‑learning setting in the paper appears to be new; the method is not evaluated on existing benchmarks from CoDA or from Wang et al. (NeurIPS 2022), making it hard to assess whether improvements come from a fundamental modelling advance or from suboptimal implementations of the baselines.  
  - The hyperparameters used for MAML/ANIL (e.g., training step size 30, adaptation step size 10 in Table 2) are arbitrary and unlikely to be optimal; more systematic tuning is needed.  
  - The number of trials, standard deviations, units, and measurement details for the average adaptation MSE and computational time reported in Table 2 are not given. Statistical tests on the reported values would be helpful.  
  - The number of gradient steps used for each method and whether Adam hyperparameters were kept identical are not clarified.

- **Lack of novelty and misplaced context.**  
  - CAMEL is not truly “model‑agnostic” (it requires fixing the last‑layer width \(r\)) and is essentially an instance of multi‑task representation learning followed by linear fine‑tuning. A more general version was theoretically studied by Maurer et al. (JMLR 2016), and Wang et al. (ICML 2021) proposed almost exactly the same approach (shared features + linear head adaptation via least squares).  
  - The paper situates CAMEL among gradient‑based meta‑learning methods, but because it does not use gradient adaptation it should be compared against other last‑layer meta‑learning methods. Missing comparisons include MetaOptNet (Lee et al., CVPR 2019) and R2D2 (Bertinetto et al., ICLR 2019), which solve the last linear layer exactly (e.g., closed‑form for regression).  
  - Important related work on meta‑reinforcement learning that adapts controlled dynamics and policies (e.g., Nagabandi et al., ICLR 2019) is omitted.

- **Interpretability claims are overstated and insufficiently demonstrated.**  
  - Proposition 1 shows that task weights can recover true parameters only under the assumption that the training loss reaches zero and that \(c = c_* = 0\) (which implies \(h(x;\theta_0)=0\) and is a restrictive simplification). A complete statement with all assumptions relating \(r, n, T, N\) is missing, and the implications of relaxing \(c=c_*=0\) are not discussed.  
  - Even if Proposition 1 holds, the model still contains two neural networks (equation 3.2), leaving a large part of the system uninterpretable.  
  - The interpretability argument is not demonstrated beyond showing that CAMEL learns the dynamics; the authors do not show how the learned physical parameters enable interpretation of the model or system. The inclusion of physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs) in this discussion would strengthen the argument.

- **Notation and presentation issues.**  
  - Notation is inconsistent (e.g., \(f_t(x; \pi)\) vs. \(f_t(x)\), ground truth \(v_*\) has \(n\) outputs while \(v\) has \(r\) outputs).  
  - The justification for using certain methods occasionally relies on popularity (e.g., “Given the popularity and expressiveness of neural networks…”).  
  - Some citations are placed in‑text where parenthetical citations are expected.

- **Technical omissions and unclear statements.**  
  - The statement that “adaptation at test time … is guaranteed to converge if the number of samples is greater than \(r\)” is confusing because ordinary least squares is solved analytically, not iteratively. The case when the least‑squares problem is underspecified (fewer samples than \(r\)) is not discussed.  
  - For Example 1 and Example 4, the learning goal (why predict \(u\) as a function of \(x = (q,\dot q,\ddot q)\)) is not explained.  
  - It is unclear whether common physical parameters could be learned in the task‑agnostic part \(v_*(x)\), and what guarantees that this part remains truly task‑agnostic.  
  - The authors only study the case \(c=c_*=0\); this simplification needs stronger justification or exploration of the non‑zero case.
