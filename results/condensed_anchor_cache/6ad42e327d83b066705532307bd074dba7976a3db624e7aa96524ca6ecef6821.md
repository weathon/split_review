## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the least disagree metric (LDM) for active learning, an asymptotically consistent estimator, and a batch active learning algorithm (LDM‑S) that combines low LDM values with diversity via a modified k‑means++ seeding. Reviewers generally view the approach as novel and well‑motivated, with extensive experiments across diverse datasets and architectures. However, opinions vary sharply: one reviewer rated the paper 1, citing significant issues with theoretical clarity and the disconnect between theory and practice, while the other three gave scores of 8, 6, and 6, praising the exposition and empirical rigor but noting several limitations.

### Strengths
* High‑quality exposition: concepts are defined precisely with intuitive explanations (e.g., example after Definition 1, 2D binary classification illustrations).
* Novel and principled approach: LDM is theoretically motivated as a measure of distance to the decision boundary, and the estimator is shown to be asymptotically consistent under mild assumptions; Proposition 1 and 2 in the appendix confirm the intuition in the 2D case.
* Comprehensive empirical evaluation: tested on 3 OpenML datasets and 6 benchmark image datasets, with a wide range of baselines, multiple architectures, statistical tests (t‑test, Dolan‑Morè performance profiles), and 5 repeats.
* Diversity mechanism without extra hyperparameters: the modified k‑means++ seeding makes the batch selection diverse, and its importance is supported by ablation studies (Appendix E.3).
* Abundance of ablations and hyperparameter studies: number of MC samples, batch size, LDM stop condition, σ interval, and the diversity weighting are all analyzed.
* Extraordinary evidence for diversity in Appendix E.3: a clear first example and a real‑world MNIST demonstration give strong intuition (reviewer suggests moving this to the main paper).
* Good flow and natural organization: theorems and conclusions lead logically to the next parts.
* Rigorous inclusion of performance profiles and statistical significance tests.

### Weaknesses
* **Theoretical presentation is rushed and disconnected from practice:** Section 2.2 (estimator theory) is described too briefly; the need for a Polish space, the Lipschitz condition, and Assumption 3 are not adequately motivated or referenced. The parameter‑perturbation sampling scheme is not shown to satisfy Assumption 3, and no error analysis (bounds/rates of convergence) is performed for the 2D case where true LDM is measurable.
* **Misleading claim:** In Section 2.3, the statement “in Appendix B.2, we show that \(E_w[\rho_M(h,g)]\) is monotone increasing in \(\sigma^2\)” is presented as general, but the proof only applies to the 2D binary classification case.
* **Missing important baselines:** SAAL, Cluster‑Margin, Similar, and reference [4] are not included in the experiments.
* **Lack of computational cost analysis:** the cost of LDM estimation and LDM‑S (especially in relation to batch size, ensemble size, and number of MC samples \(M\)) is not discussed; the method appears to have complexity comparable to BADGE (which is quadratic in batch size), needing an ensemble of models (more expensive than single‑model methods like BADGE and CoreSet).
* **No explicit link between LDM and active‑learning performance:** the paper provides asymptotic consistency of the estimator but does not analyze how LDM relates to informativeness or final model accuracy; illustrative examples showing effectiveness are missing.
* **Insufficient discussion of limitations:** the need for an ensemble (higher cost) and other drawbacks are not addressed; a comparison of the seeding strategy applied to other scoring functions (e.g., [1], [2], [4]) is also absent to show that performance gains are consistent.
* **Mild performance benefits:** while often the best or second‑best, the improvements over existing state‑of‑the‑art are modest; it is unclear whether the approach scales to large datasets (e.g., ImageNet running time is omitted from Table 2).
* **Incomplete main‑text experimental details:** the full experimental setting and many ablation studies are relegated to the appendix; the evaluation metric in Section 4.2 is ambiguous (only “accuracy” is mentioned in the appendix).
* **Clarity issues in algorithms and notation:**
  * Algorithm 1 is missing the input \(x\).
  * Algorithm 2 does not clarify which parameters are passed to Algorithm 1 (hypothesis parameterized by \(v\), number of samples \(m\), small \(s\), but \(\{\sigma_k^2\}\) is unclear).
  * In Theorem 1, the symbol \(f\) is undefined (should probably be \(\alpha\)).
  * Assumption 3 is over‑complicated (\(\sup_{\epsilon\in(0,1)}\lim_N \alpha(N,\epsilon)=0\) simply means \(\lim_N \alpha(N,\epsilon)=0\) for every \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\)).
  * Minor typo: “flips‑flopped” → “flip‑flopped” (p.1, last paragraph).
* **Aleatoric uncertainty concern:** sampling close to the decision boundary may select points whose uncertainty is aleatoric (unreducible), which could explain why a diversity heuristic was necessary; this warrants more discussion.
* **Question on MC‑dropout and posterior sampling:** reviewers ask whether MC‑dropout (more efficient than ensembles) or more advanced posterior sampling methods (e.g., Zhang et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2014) could be used.
* **Hyperparameter interactions not studied:** the effect of each hyperparameter (MC samples, batch size, stop condition, σ interval) is examined independently, but potential interactions are ignored.
* **Figure 2a:** LDM crosses are hard to see near the decision boundary; color change suggested.
* **Reviewer disagreement:** one reviewer (score 1) was far more critical, especially regarding the rigor and clarity of the theoretical sections and the implementation‑theory gap; the other reviewers (8, 6, 6) shared many of the same weaknesses but viewed the overall contribution more positively.
