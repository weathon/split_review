## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses discrimination-free insurance pricing when sensitive attributes (e.g., gender, race) are privatized and hidden from the insurer. The authors propose a multi-party framework where a trusted third party (TTP) uses noised (differentially private) sensitive attributes to produce non‑discriminatory premiums without the insurer directly accessing the true attributes. Theoretical risk bounds are derived for two settings: known and unknown noise rates. The method is evaluated on US Health Insurance and Auto Insurance datasets, demonstrating robustness to noise.

### Strengths
- Addresses a practically important and underexplored problem: achieving actuarial fairness under regulatory constraints that limit the collection and use of sensitive attributes.
- The proposed framework is mathematically grounded, with rigorous theoretical guarantees (Theorems 4.3 and 4.5) for both known and unknown privatization noise rates.
- Novel application of differential privacy and a multi‑party setup to the insurance sector, distinguishing it from standard algorithmic fairness work.
- Experimental evaluation is thorough and well‑motivated, covering scenarios with known and unknown noise rates, and including sensitivity analyses (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- Exposition, especially in Section 4, is straightforward and the paper is well‑organized, making it accessible even to readers less familiar with privacy‑preserving machine learning (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- The theoretical results (risk bounds) are of interest to the privacy‑preserving ML community (Reviewer 4).
- One reviewer (Reviewer 4) initially raised strong concerns about practical implications but, after the revision, found these issues adequately addressed and supported acceptance.

### Weaknesses
- **Practical feasibility of the TTP framework**: Some reviewers questioned how realistic or widespread such a trusted‑third‑party protocol currently is in insurance practice. Would the authors comment on flexibility if a TTP is infeasible? (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Missing comparison to existing fairness methods or baseline models** for insurance pricing; the empirical analysis does not include any baselines that do not use the TTP or that use alternative fairness approaches (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited dataset scope**: Only US Health Insurance and Auto Insurance datasets are used; including other insurance types or more complex demographic distributions would strengthen the evaluation (Reviewer 3).
- **Need for explicit discussion of limitations and future directions**: The paper would benefit from a clearer statement of the method’s known limitations and possible extensions (e.g., to continuous sensitive attributes) (Reviewer 3).
- **Tightness of the upper bounds**: The relationship between the empirical risk under local differential privacy \( \hat{R}^{\text{LDP}}(f) \) and the risk of the optimal fair function \( R(f^*) \) is not analyzed for tightness; in particular, how does the gap behave as noise decreases? (Reviewer 1, question 3; Reviewer 3, weakness 2)
- **Clarification of Theorem 4.5**: What does \( \tilde{\epsilon} \) represent? How does it differ from Theorem 4.3? Is it the error from estimating the noise rates \( \pi, \bar{\pi} \)? (Reviewer 1)
- **Meaning of technical condition**: Does the condition \( M_g + \frac{C_1 + \theta}{\ln 2} > \tilde{\epsilon} > \theta \) have a practical interpretation, or is it purely a technical assumption? (Reviewer 1)
- **Problem‑setting ambiguity**: If the insurer cannot directly access sensitive attributes but can use an indirect method, why is a TTP still needed? Could the insurer implement the method directly? (Reviewer 1)
- **Why is the transformation \( T(X) \) necessary?** The paper does not clearly justify the need for this step; a diagram of the protocol (insurer / TTP interaction) would also be helpful (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of practical fairness evaluation**: The experiments claim “fair” pricing but do not report how different subgroups (e.g., men vs. women) are affected (prices paid, benefit from payouts), nor do they discuss potential adverse selection or other equilibrium effects (e.g., high‑risk drivers buying more cheap insurance, moral hazard). One reviewer (Reviewer 4) considered these omissions critical, although after revisions he judged them adequately addressed.
- **Slow convergence and its relevance**: Even a linear model with six features requires thousands of epochs. Why is convergence rate prioritized in the experiments, and is this a meaningful property for real insurance pricing (where training cost is negligible compared to overall revenue)? (Reviewer 4)
- **Extension to continuous sensitive attributes**: Can the framework handle non‑binary or continuous attributes? The paper currently focuses on discrete sensitive variables; the authors should discuss challenges or possible extensions (Reviewer 3, question 3).
