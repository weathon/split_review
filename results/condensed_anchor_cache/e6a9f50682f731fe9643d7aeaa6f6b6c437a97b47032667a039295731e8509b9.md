## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes FedEx-LoRA to address the inexact aggregation problem when applying LoRA in federated fine-tuning (the average of products BA from clients does not equal the product of averages). The method adds a residual error term to the frozen pretrained weight matrix on the server, which is sent to clients each round to correct the aggregation error. Evaluations are performed on RoBERTa and GPT-2 across NLU and NLG benchmarks, comparing against FedIT and FFA-LoRA. Reviewer scores range from 3 (strong rejection) to 6 (weak accept), with one reviewer (R3) considerably more positive and one (R4) considerably more negative; the others are moderate (5).

### Strengths

- The problem of inexact aggregation in federated LoRA is clearly identified and addressed with a straightforward method (R1, R2, R3, R4). The approach ensures exact updates while preserving LoRA’s efficiency, and is well-motivated (R3).
- Theoretical justification and extensive empirical evaluations (multiple benchmarks) demonstrate consistent gains over state-of-the-art federated fine-tuning methods (R2, R3). R2 highlights good reproducibility due to detailed experimental settings.
- The paper is well-written and organized, with clear diagrams and formulas making the methodology accessible (R3).
- Figure 2 provides interesting insights—deviations from exact aggregation decrease with model depth, which may stimulate further reflection on model architecture and fine-tuning (R4). One reviewer (R3) notes the significance for privacy-preserving applications and the method’s practical impact.
- The communication cost increase over FedIT and FFA-LoRA is described as minimal given the performance improvement (R2). However, this view is contested by other reviewers (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Communication overhead is a major concern.** The server must send the residual error term (dimensions equal to the pretrained weight matrix) to each client every round, which is equivalent to full fine-tuning communication for the layers where LoRA is applied. This is unacceptable for many federated environments (R1, R4). R4 estimates the residual could be as large as \(kT\) times the LLM parameter size (\(k\) clients, \(T\) rounds). R2 notes that the residual rank scales with the number of clients (\(k \times r\)), leading to large communication costs when many clients are used (experiments only had 3 clients). R2 and R4 both stress that the paper does not demonstrate scalability to realistic client counts (e.g., hundreds).
- **Computation overhead on the server is significant.** The server must compute \(n\) matrix products (BA for each client) plus the residual error term each round—a several-fold increase over FedIT (R1, R4). R1 points out this is heavy on CPU-based server nodes; using GPU requires careful scheduling due to PCIe bandwidth limitations.
- **Incremental contribution.** The core idea of adding back the discrepancy between average of products and product of averages is a straightforward adjustment during aggregation. The problem has been raised and studied in detail in prior works [1] Sun et al. (2024) and [2] Wang et al. (2024), which are not discussed or compared as baselines. This limits the novelty and contribution, especially for a top-tier venue like ICLR (R2, R4).
- **Limited model scale and task scope.** Experiments are only on RoBERTa and GPT-2 (both <1B parameters). It is unclear how the method scales to larger foundation models like Llama or Mistral, where memory and computation of residual matrices could become a bottleneck (R3). R4 notes that related federated LoRA works often use larger LLMs, making the experiments less convincing. The evaluation is restricted to standard NLU/NLG tasks; complex tasks requiring multi-hop reasoning, commonsense QA, mathematical reasoning, or coding are not tested (R3).
- **Missing standard FL evaluations.** The paper does not vary the number of clients or the portion of randomly selected clients per round, which are standard analyses in federated learning. The experiment with only 3 clients does not reflect realistic FL scenarios (R2).
- **No convergence analysis.** The impact of the high-rank residual matrix on convergence stability and speed, especially with many clients, is not examined (R2).
- **Quantization challenges.** Introducing residual matrices to the original weights complicates quantization (e.g., QLoRA). The residuals require re-quantization at each aggregation round, potentially increasing computational cost and causing information loss. No analysis of these practical implications is provided (R3).
- **Privacy impacts not analyzed.** The paper claims benefits for privacy-preserving FL but does not analyze how residual matrices might leak information about local client data or how the method performs under differential privacy constraints (R3).
- **Writing clarity issues.** Some equations are confusing: e.g., Eq. (5) and (7) mix global and local notations; Figure 1 uses dot product (\(B \cdot A\)) while equations use \(BA\) or \(B \times A\); notation for rounds and global vs. local weights is inconsistent (R4). Minor typos present (R4).
- **Open questions that overlap with weaknesses** (treated as missing analysis/ablation requests): (i) How does the residual term affect the update of A and B? An analysis experiment is needed (R2). (ii) Why does FedEx-LoRA outperform centralized LoRA on some benchmarks? (R3). (iii) Can the method extend to other LoRA variants (MoE-LoRA, QLoRA) and what adaptations are needed? (R3). (iv) How does the method perform with larger models (Llama, Gemma) and more complex tasks (reasoning, coding)? (R3). (v) How to address the strong communication and computation overhead problems on server and clients? (R1).
