## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PREMIUM, a tag-based, LLM-agnostic framework for personalizing LLMs by selecting user-characteristic tags per query, then incorporating them into the prompt. It introduces two variants: PREMIUM-Prompt (LLM-based tag selection) and PREMIUM-Embed (embedding-matching), the latter being lightweight and deployable on laptops. Training the tag selector relies on users ranking responses generated with different tags. A new benchmark dataset, Ranking-TAGER, is created using AI annotators to provide preference feedback. Experiments on LaMP-2 and Ranking-TAGER show improved accuracy and win rates over baselines, and analyze adaptability to dynamic preferences, scalability, and data efficiency.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a timely and important topic: LLM personalization. (R3, R4)
- The framework is LLM-agnostic (applicable to closed-source models) and does not require training the LLM itself, broadening applicability. (R2, R3)
- PREMIUM, especially PREMIUM-Embed, is efficient and lightweight, enabling local deployment with minimal compute (e.g., laptop-level). (R3, R4)
- The paper provides a well-structured tag-selection approach that aligns with user characteristics. (R1)
- The introduction of the Ranking-TAGER dataset fills a gap in standardized evaluation for tag-based LLM personalization. (R1, R3)
- Most parts of the paper are generally easy to follow. (R2)
- Extensive experiments and comprehensive analyses showcase the framework’s effectiveness, adaptability, and scalability. (R3, R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear how tags influence the LLM after selection.** The method for transforming selected tags into a prompt (or other mechanism) is not explained step-by-step. Neither a diagram/flowchart nor details on whether tags are appended, used to modify attention, etc., are provided. (R1, R2)
- **Effectiveness limited by the tag set.** Personalization is heavily constrained by the predefined tags and their granularity; tag-based methods are not dominant in recommendation systems, raising concerns about expressiveness. (R2; minority reviewer R2 is particularly critical here)  
- **Preference feedback assumption limits practical applicability.** Requiring users to rank numerous responses (the m responses per query) may hinder real-world adoption. (R2, R3)  
- **Need to learn a separate selector per user** (if tags are not globally fixed) leads to efficiency issues and poor learning with sparse data. (R2)  
- **Unclear how groundtruth user tags are generated** for evaluation in Ranking-TAGER. (R3, R4)  
- **Dataset construction uses AI annotators**, which is not persuasive as a source of groundtruth. Including tags as user characteristics is uncommon in real scenarios. (R4)  
- **Missing comparisons to key baselines:** baselines [1] and [2] (not specified in reviews, but requested), and soft-prompt tuning methods. (R2)  
- **Evaluation of dynamic user preferences is too simple** because changes are already abstracted by different tags; more difficult settings (e.g., gradual preference shifts not captured by tags) are needed. (R4)  
- **No evaluation on proprietary LLMs** (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini) to confirm generalizability. (R3)  
- **No user study** assessing readability, relevance of selected tags, perceived personalization, and cognitive load of the tag selection interface. (R1)  
- **No discussion on how to determine the tags in the tag library** (e.g., process, source, coverage). (R3)  
- **Unclear impact of the number of responses (m) used for preference ranking** on method performance. (R3)  
- **Potential negative effect on non-personalized tasks** – queries that do not need personalization (reasoning, factual QA) may be harmed by tag injection; this is not analyzed. (R3)  
- **Lack of explicit definition of “personalization”** – the paper does not provide a concise concept definition with specific examples. (R4)  
- **Embedding-based tag recommendation is well-studied** in recommendation systems; novelty relative to that literature is unclear. (R3)
