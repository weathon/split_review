## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SCAR (Style Consistency-Aware Response Ranking), a method to select instruction-response pairs for SFT based on response stylistic consistency. It defines two style elements: linguistic form (structural presentation) and semantic surprisal (predictability of content). SCAR uses a trained ranking model to prioritize style-consistent examples, sometimes as few as 0.7% of the full dataset, achieving performance comparable to or better than models trained on the entire dataset on coding and open-ended QA benchmarks.

### Strengths
- The focus on style consistency (linguistic form and semantic surprisal) as a data selection criterion for SFT is novel and meaningful.
- The paper is rich in content, with extensive analysis and a large number of experiments across datasets and model families (LLaMA2, LLaMA3).
- Ablation studies are conducted, and the authors open-source code and data.
- The core finding that higher consistency in response style leads to better LLM performance (given comparable quality) is well-supported by analysis in Figure 1 and Table 1.
- SCAR offers practical efficiency by drastically reducing training data size while maintaining or improving performance.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and positioning:** One reviewer found the motivation unclear, as the paper opposes the common emphasis on diversity in instruction-tuning data without strong justification. The position of SCAR (general-purpose model vs. specialized model) is also unclear. If intended for general models, performance on diverse benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, BBH, HumanEval in TULU evaluation) using the checkpoints from Table 5 should be reported. If for specialized models, SCAR should be compared with domain-specific high-quality data sources (e.g., evol-instruct, instruction backtranslation) rather than StackExchange.
- **Definition-metric misalignment:** Multiple reviewers noted that the definitions of “linguistic form” and “semantic surprisal” are not well aligned with the metrics used. For linguistic form, metrics like TTR, MTLD, Flesch score, sentence length, and punctuation frequency do not fully capture the stated definition (e.g., tone, formatting, variable naming). For semantic surprisal, the definition (choice of solutions/ideas) goes well beyond what perplexity alone can measure; the metric is too narrow.
- **Concerns with the evaluation methodology:**
  - The method selects responses whose style is closest to an external model (GPT-3.5) rather than detecting inherent consistency in the dataset, imposing an external style bias.
  - The referenced response includes the instruction, which may cause the model to correct the response (even when asked to ignore the instruction), introducing a confounding variable between linguistic form and instruction-following.
  - The model is only tested on AlpacaEval (evaluated by LLMs), and the training data itself uses LLM-generated responses. This raises the concern that SCAR exploits style features that happen to be favored by the LLM judge, rather than improving general capability.
  - Objective benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, BBH) were expected but not reported; one reviewer noted SCAR’s performance on such tasks is necessary to verify general improvement.
- **Missing baselines and comparisons:** A simple rule-based baseline, such as the “longest” response method from prior work, is missing. Comparisons with other data selection methods are inadequate in some scenarios (e.g., domain-specific training).
- **Experimental design issues:**
  - The experiments are limited in scale (datasets and LLM sizes) and some results (e.g., performance degradation on LLaMA2-13B) are not explained.
  - The model family shifts from LLaMA2 (line 148) to LLaMA3 in later experiments without clear rationale, making the setup hard to follow.
  - The method is overly complex (requires LLM generation of analysis and corrected responses, plus training a custom ranking module) without an illustrative figure of the overall workflow. The lack of a main workflow figure makes the paper hard to follow.
  - Font in Figures 1 and 3 is difficult to read; the organization of Section 4 (experimental setup) could be improved.
  - Calculation of perplexity PPL(y_c|x) first filters out functional words (y_p), which may destroy sentence consistency and fluency, making the resulting ppl less meaningful.
- **Specific technical questions and confusions:**
  - What is the output format of StackExchange (only code or both text and code)? Is the linguistic metric meaningful for code, given that the standard deviation of TTR is larger for code than text?
  - Why is max pooling used to calculate v_p (preserving only one token’s information)?
  - What parameters need to be trained in SCAR?
  - Why are the selection ratios for code and text different (12.5% vs 10% – is this intentional)?
  - Colors in Table 1 are not aligned with other figures.
  - In Figure 1, is the left panel showing complete responses or only extracted functional words? Is the right panel showing perplexity over complete responses or over y_c?
  - How exactly are the takeaways concluded from the analysis in Figure 1?
  - In Figure 2 (Open-ended Domain), almost all metrics are worse than the Full Data baseline on the Human dataset except for ppl, which contradicts previous findings; the authors need to explain this.
- **Disagreement noted:** One reviewer (score 3) considered the motivation weak and experiments unconvincing, while two other reviewers (score 5) found the investigation meaningful and the experiments rich. The unclear positioning and limited generality remain points of concern across all reviews.
