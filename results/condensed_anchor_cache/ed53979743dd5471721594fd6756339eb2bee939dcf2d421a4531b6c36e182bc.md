## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a framework for comparing image representations by analyzing their local geometries using the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM). It quantifies local sensitivity to stimulus distortions and uses this to find a pair of “principal distortions” that maximize variance across a set of models, enabling comparison of more than two models. The method is applied to early visual system models and deep neural networks (AlexNet, ResNet50), revealing differences invisible to global approaches like RSA. The work addresses an important problem in cognitive science and model understanding.

### Strengths
- **Well-written and clear**: The paper is easy to follow, with strong motivation and a logical structure (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
- **Novel and innovative**: The concept of comparing local geometry via the FIM and principal distortions is compelling and generalizes prior work to multiple models (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Reveals hidden differences**: The method uncovers distinctions not captured by global geometric measures (e.g., RSA), offering deeper insight into model behavior (Reviewer 4).
- **Interesting and meaningful results**: Visualizations highlight differences related to texture bias, adversarial vulnerability, and the role of architecture vs. training (AlexNet vs. ResNet50) (Reviewer 3, 5).
- **Helpful visualizations and supplementary**: Figures and qualitative results effectively convey the concept; the supplementary material is informative (Reviewer 3).
- **Sound experimental framework**: The experimental design investigates variability from both training data and architectural design (Reviewer 5).
- **Broad discussion and potential**: The discussion contextualizes findings within biological vision, DNNs, and their interplay (Reviewer 4).
- **Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Score 8) was more positive overall, praising novelty and clarity, but still noted incremental advancement and concerns about deterministic representations (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **FIM metric and connection to geometry**: The effectiveness of the FIM as a metric for local geometry is unclear. The connection between FIM and actual geometric properties of representation space remains tenuous (Reviewer 1). For deterministic DNNs, applying the FIM seems arbitrary; the paper would benefit from relating it to the pullback of the Euclidean metric onto image space (Reviewer 4). The technical advance may be seen as incremental (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of validation and quantitative rigor**:
  - Principal distortions are shown only qualitatively; there is no statistical validation (e.g., consistency across different images, per-class sensitivity distributions) (Reviewers 1, 3, 5).
  - In the early vision models experiment (Section 4.1), there is no quantitative evaluation; interpretations rely solely on visual inspection of distortion patterns (Reviewer 3).
  - No psychophysical validation is provided to confirm whether principal distortions relate to human sensitivity (Reviewer 3). The paper claims the method could compare with human perception but does not test this.
  - A per-class sensitivity distribution across the dataset would help understand which models are more sensitive to high-frequency localities (Reviewer 5).
- **Model selection limited to older architectures**: Only AlexNet and ResNet50 are used. Modern networks like EfficientNet or Vision Transformers (ViT, Swin) are missing, which would strengthen claims of universal applicability (Reviewers 2, 5). Additionally, robustness to training randomness is not tested (e.g., models trained on different seeds) (Reviewer 5).
- **Practical utility and generalizability unclear**:
  - No concrete use cases are provided (e.g., model improvement, transfer learning, interpretability) (Reviewers 2, 3).
  - It is unclear whether the method applies only to classification networks or also to detection, segmentation, or generative models (Reviewer 3).
  - The manuscript does not establish how the local metric can be aggregated to provide global insights across different stimuli or datasets (Reviewer 4). How to choose a representative stimulus or combine results from multiple stimuli is not addressed (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing details and ambiguities**:
  - There is no dedicated Dataset section, making it hard to understand the scale of experiments (Reviewer 5).
  - Several implementation details are unclear: how the stimulus-dependent function \(f(s)\) is computed (Reviewer 1); handling when \(I(s)\) is positive semi-definite and approaches zero (Reviewer 1); how two image representations are obtained from a single image (Reviewer 1); how coefficients for \(\epsilon_1\) and \(\epsilon_2\) are determined (Reviewer 1); and how relative sensitivities were scaled for early visual system models (Reviewer 4).
  - The relationship between distortion maps and attention maps (e.g., in Transformers) or saliency/explainability methods is not discussed (Reviewers 4, 5).
- **Length**: The paper exceeds nine pages (Reviewer 3).
