## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes meta-training Transformers to act as general-purpose in-context learners (GPICL) with minimal inductive bias. It characterizes transitions between generalization, memorization, and failure as a function of model size, number of tasks, and meta-optimization. It identifies accessible state/memory size as a key bottleneck for meta-learning capabilities, and proposes practical interventions like biasing the training distribution to improve meta-training and meta-generalization. Evaluations are performed on image classification datasets (CIFAR10, MNIST, FashionMNIST, SVHN). Reviewers note the paper addresses an important problem in learning-to-learn.

### Strengths
- Demonstrates that Transformers can be meta-trained for in-context learning on standard image classification datasets, with significant effort to show generalization (R1, R4).
- Figure 2 provides convincing evidence of a transition from memorization to generalization induced by model capacity and sample size (R1).
- Provides interesting insights into transitions from memorization to task identification to general learning as model size and number of tasks increase, and identifies accessible state/memory size as a more crucial bottleneck than parameter count (R1, R3).
- Proposes practical interventions (e.g., biasing training distribution) that improve meta-training (R1).
- Presents a simple baseline model (GPICL) with minimal inductive bias that shows competitive performance compared to models with stronger inductive biases (R3).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow in its presentation of methods and results (R3). (R1 notes writing clarity issues—see Weaknesses.)
- The paper addresses an important problem in the community: learning-to-learn (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Writing clarity**: The term “general-purpose in-context learning” lacks a rigorous mathematical definition, making the paper hard to read (R1). The memory/state concept in Section 4.2 is heuristic; beyond LSTM and transformers the definition of state is not concrete, and the insight that “large state is more crucial than parameter count” is not fully grounded (R1).
- **Resubmission without revision** (minority reviewer, score 1): The paper is a resubmission from another machine learning venue with zero modifications since its previous rejection. The authors have not addressed points raised in the earlier review process, which this reviewer considers crucial for improvement. The lack of revisions suggests a disregard for the peer-review process and raises concerns about the authors’ commitment to improving the work (R2).
- **Novelty and contributions**: The contribution is limited; the approach is very similar to previous work such as Meta-Transformer, OptFormer, or PFNs, with only a data augmentation step (random projections) being introduced (R4). The paper does not make clear what elements are new contributions versus what is already present in other papers. The random projection strategy and transformer coding are the main modeling novelties, but the paper would benefit from a clear contributions section (R3).
- **Experiments on simple datasets**: Experiments are mostly limited to simple datasets (CIFAR10, MNIST, FashionMNIST, SVHN). It is unclear if the findings generalize to harder and larger datasets, and whether good performance is due to inherent learning ability or the datasets being easy (R3). Experiments are poor in demonstrating the validity and superiority of the method; no strong baselines are used, and most experiments are limited to MNIST (R4).
- **Data augmentation (random projections)**: The data augmentation step is not well-founded; random projections likely introduce noise, and no ablation is performed to test its effect on generalization (R4).
- **State size vs. parameter count claim**: The claim that performance improves with state size rather than parameter count may be an artifact of using simple datasets like MNIST, where additional parameters are no longer helpful (R3). This concern overlaps with the generalizability of experiments.
- **Lack of related work discussion**: The paper omits discussion of relevant prior work such as Meta-Transformer, OptFormer, and PFNs (R4).
