- Scores: 3, 3, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Episodic Memory for Cognitive Agents (EMCA), a graph-based framework that incrementally stores and retrieves multimodal episodic experiences (speech, vision, non-verbal cues) without pre-training on specific scenarios. EMCA builds a memory graph with semantic and temporal connections, supports dynamic clustering and context-aware retrieval, and is evaluated on multiple datasets, including a custom Big Bang Theory dataset and an Agent dataset. The goal is to enable continuous learning and flexible reasoning for cognitive agents. One reviewer (R3) considers the idea novel and well-tested; three others (R1, R2, R4) rate the paper 3 and raise serious concerns about clarity, missing implementation details, and overstated claims.

### Strengths

- **Addresses an important problem**: Enabling agents to retain and flexibly retrieve past experiences from multimodal data without task-specific pre-training is a timely and relevant challenge (R1, R2, R4).
- **Novel architecture**: The graph-based episodic memory structure with temporal and semantic edges, dynamic clustering, and context-sensitive retrieval is seen as sound and innovative (R3, R4). One reviewer (R3) specifically highlights the lack of pre-training requirements and the ability to process multimodal data in real time.
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The paper tests EMCA on multiple datasets, includes comparisons to baseline methods (including graph-based approaches), and provides ablation studies (R3, R4). Reviewer R2 notes that results outperform other graph-based methods and that Table 2 (showing required modalities per question type) is interesting.
- **Useful datasets**: The Big Bang Theory and Agent datasets are considered potentially valuable contributions to the community (R2, R3). However, most reviewers (R1, R2) note that dataset construction details are missing.
- **Growing field**: The problem of scaling context for robots and agents is of increasing importance, and the graph-based approach is a meaningful step (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and presentation**:
  - The paper is fragmented, poorly structured, and difficult to follow (R1, R2). Multiple reviewers note excessive white space, odd line spacing, misplaced figures (mid-page instead of top/bottom), small font sizes, and missing spaces after punctuation (R2). One reviewer (R3) found the presentation clear, but the majority disagree.
  - Terminology is introduced without definition (e.g., “key events,” “location weight,” “subjective temporal timescales,” “master”) (R1, R4). The term “master” is considered problematic; it should be replaced (R2, R4).
  - The paper reads as a list of HOW without explaining WHY behind design choices or distinguishing core method from implementation details (R4).
  - Section 4.1 on theoretical comparison with temporal graphs is unclear and does not connect to the rest of the paper (R2).
  - The results in Section 5.1.1 are presented in a single dense paragraph; better organization and qualitative examples are needed (R2).
  - Figure 5’s line graph (x-axis = method, lines = dataset) is inappropriate; a grouped bar chart would be better (R2).
  - There is a repeated paragraph (L054–L062) and broken sentences (R3, R4).

- **Insufficient task and output definition**:
  - It is not clear what the actual task is: what commands does the “master” give? Is the output a location, a free-form text answer, or something else? (R2)
  - The response generation process (how the system converts retrieval results into answers) is not described, yet the entire experimental evaluation depends on it (R4).
  - For Section 4, the point about combining “master” memory with agent memory is unclear (R2), and the “theoretical comparison” claimed is not evident (R2).

- **Missing or vague technical details**:
  - Core implementation details are absent: model architecture, parameter settings, processing pipeline, pseudocode, embedding methods for nodes, and retrieval/clustering algorithms are missing (R1, R3, R4). This makes reproduction nearly impossible (R4).
  - The statistical methods for estimating missing timestamps are unspecified (R1).
  - Several equations are unclear: e.g., Equation 8 (similarity between event and episodes), Equation 14 (temporal parameter), Equation 17 (undefined `sim`), the relation between `S_T` and `T_voiced`, and how `T_acoustic` is generated (R1, R4).
  - The fusion of `T_acoustic` and `T_voiced` is not explained, nor is the format and extraction of visual components (`Vscene`, `Vplace`, `Vtime`) (R1).
  - How tasks are categorized from text summaries (e.g., meeting, lunch) and how “key events” are identified is not specified (R1).
  - The role of functions `w` and `l` in retrieval is left unexplained (R1).

- **Dataset construction and bias**:
  - Most datasets were created by the authors, raising potential bias in evaluation (R1). The construction process for the Big Bang Theory and Agent datasets is not described: episode selection, annotation, question types, and challenges are missing (R2, R3). No code or datasets are promised for release (R3).

- **Overstated claims and scope mismatch**:
  - The paper claims EMCA encodes data “like human memory” without evidence; this claim should be toned down and tied to psychological `what–where–when` organization (R1).
  - The title and introduction emphasize robots and learning, but the method is evaluated only in virtual/agent settings and does not involve learning (no adaptation of stored knowledge to new situations) (R4).
  - There is no discussion of how generalizable the framework is to other tasks (e.g., robot movement control) or what would need to change (R4).

- **Memory management and forgetting**:
  - The system lacks a forgetting mechanism; it is unclear how outdated or duplicate information is handled, and whether memory pruning or consolidation is implemented (R3, R4). One reviewer (R4) argues that without such management the representation is of limited value.
  - The paper does not address what should be stored in the first place; it appears everything is stored, including duplicates (R4).

- **Scalability and real-time performance**:
  - The growth of the graph (nodes and edges) with the number of episodes is not analyzed; computational complexity of graph traversal and update algorithms is missing (R3).
  - Real-time latency for retrieval and update operations is not reported, which is critical for practical deployment (R3).
  - Only ablations on modalities and search methods are provided; sensitivity to clustering thresholds and other design choices is absent (R4).

- **Related work and comparisons**:
  - The related work section is sparse; comparisons are limited to temporal/knowledge graphs and are superficial (R1). Formal memory models like Hopfield networks or psychological memory models are not discussed (R1, R4).
  - Figure 5 is not convincing except for the Arigraph baseline (R4). Memory requirement as a metric is missing (R4).

- **Excessive equations and low-value sections**:
  - Section 3.1 on signal capture is not useful and adds bulk without clarifying the core method (R2). Many equations are superfluous and make the paper harder to read (R2, R4). Space would be better used for dataset description and implementation details (R2).

- **Formatting and typographical issues**:
  - The paper uses wrong LaTeX commands for references (`\citep`, `\citet` are not used correctly, leading to repeated author names) (R4).
  - Opening and closing quotation marks are inconsistent (R4).
  - Several references are incomplete (e.g., “as shown in 4” without a figure reference) (R4).
