## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies when, as the training set size \(n\) increases, a diffusion model transitions from memorizing its training data to generalizing (sampling the underlying data distribution). The authors define this transition mathematically: generalization occurs when the generated distribution is closer to the true population distribution than to a Gaussian-smoothed version of the empirical training distribution. Using isotropic Gaussian data and a linear (Gaussian) diffusion model, they derive a sharp transition when \(n\) is proportional to the data dimension \(d\). Numerical simulations with a linear model and a small experiment with a nonlinear architecture (Fig. 4) are provided.

### Strengths

- **Important problem.** Understanding the memorization–generalization trade-off in diffusion models has substantial theoretical and practical relevance, particularly for privacy and replication risks (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Novel definition and framework.** The formal mathematical characterization of the transition (comparing distance to smoothed empirical vs. population distribution) is original and could influence future research directions (Reviewer 4).
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis under strong assumptions.** The derivation for Gaussian data with a linear diffusion model is statistically solid and includes a clear transition at \(n = d\) (Reviewers 3, 5). This provides a concrete baseline for further study.
- **Potential practical implications.** The finding that the transition point links to scale could inspire empirical work on dataset scaling in diffusion models (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Circular / redundant definition.**  
  The definition of “generalization” already requires the generated distribution to be closer to the population than to the smoothed empirical distribution. This predetermines the transition and does not provide an independent measure of generalization (Reviewer 1). Moreover, the observation that a rapid decrease in \(E_{OG}\) coincides with generalization is a direct consequence of the definition, not a surprising discovery (Reviewer 5).

- **Overly simplified assumptions limit applicability.**  
  - **Gaussian data.** The theoretical analysis assumes isotropic Gaussian data, which is far from realistic high-dimensional data (e.g., lying on low-dimensional manifolds) (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5). It is entirely unclear whether the same pattern holds for real datasets like ImageNet, where memorization is observed with \(n\) several orders of magnitude larger than the input dimension \(d\) (\(d\sim 10^5\), \(n\sim 10^7\)), contradicting the paper’s claim that the transition only depends on \(d\) (Reviewer 3).  
  - **Linear (Gaussian) diffusion model.** The score function of a Gaussian is linear, so a linear model can only generate Gaussian distributions. This model is fundamentally incapable of memorizing the training distribution’s complex score (a weighted Gaussian convolution of single-point scores), so the analysis cannot properly study memorization (Reviewer 1). The model also neglects the numerical SDE/ODE discretization that is central to practical score-based generative modeling (Reviewer 5). The empirical experiment on a nonlinear architecture still uses isotropic Gaussian data, so the linear and nonlinear sampling procedures are essentially identical; it does not demonstrate applicability to real data (Reviewer 5).  
  - **No consideration of model capacity / overparameterization.** The degree of overparameterization, which is known to affect memorization (Yoon et al., 2023), is not addressed (Reviewer 1).  
  - **Effect of data complexity ignored.** The transition may depend on intrinsic dimensionality or data complexity (e.g., facial vs. natural images), but this is not discussed (Reviewer 3).

- **Inconsistent empirical evidence.**  
  - Figure 4 does not show the claimed transition: the metrics \(E_{TG}\) and \(E_{OG}\) both decrease at a similar rate without crossing, unlike the schematic in Figure 1(c) (Reviewers 1, 4). The probability that the generated distribution is closer to the population distribution remains high (\(>0.8\)) regardless of \(n\), contradicting the paper’s own memorization–generalization split (Reviewer 1).  
  - Error bars and standard deviations are missing, and linear instead of log scales obscure trends (Reviewer 1). The Hellinger distance in Figure 2(b) changes by only 0.02, which is insufficiently significant (Reviewer 1).

- **Inconsistent theoretical regimes.**  
  The analysis in Section 2 requires \(n\) to grow exponentially with \(d\), yet later sections (from Section 3 onward) consider the proportional regime \(n \simeq d\) or \(n \ll d\). These regimes rest on different assumptions, creating a disconnect between the theory and the numerics (Reviewer 1).

- **Lack of novelty and practical message.**  
  The core comparison of errors on training vs. generated data, while sensible, does not yield deep or unexpected conclusions within the linear model. The paper lacks a key result that has meaningful implications for large-scale diffusion model training (Reviewers 2, 5). The final comment on practical applications is naive because it ignores the quality of generated samples, which is critical in practice (Reviewer 1).

- **Poor presentation and notation.**  
  - Figures lack essential labels and color codes; axes are not clearly described (Reviewers 1, 4).  
  - Many equations are presented without explanation or connection to the empirical results (e.g., Eq. 21) (Reviewer 1).  
  - Notation is confusing and undefined: \(x.\nabla\), \(\langle y \rangle_{\pi_t}\), \(\langle y_i y_j \rangle_{\pi_t}\) (Reviewer 3); the model says “Suppose we know \(\rho(0,x)\) exactly” but score sampling requires \(\rho(T,x)\) and scores (Reviewer 5); “original distribution \(\rho_O(x)\) inferred from \(N \gg n\) samples” uses undefined \(N\) and unclear distinction from \(\rho\) (Reviewer 5); samples are not stated to belong to \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (Reviewer 5).  
  - The limit \(n\to\infty\) with \(\alpha = \log n/d\) fixed is unclear because \(d\) changes; \(Z_{1^c} = e^{-d/2}/n\) seems to miss dependence on \(x\) or an \(\mathcal{O}\) symbol; the meaning of \(\approx\) in Eq. (10) and \(\mathcal{O}(\cdot, \cdot)\) in Eq. (11) is not precise (Reviewer 5).  
  - Variables \(y_i, y_j\) appear suddenly in Eq. (14) without prior definition (Reviewer 5).  
  - Lines 298–301 are unclear (Reviewer 5).  
  - The definition of memorization in lines 337–338 (“the diffusion model is considered to be memorizing … when the probability that \(\Delta > 0\) …”) is confusing because the probability is over the randomness in the training data, making it a property of the dataset, not the model (Reviewer 5).  
  - The writing has grammatical errors and poorly constructed sentences (Reviewer 1).
