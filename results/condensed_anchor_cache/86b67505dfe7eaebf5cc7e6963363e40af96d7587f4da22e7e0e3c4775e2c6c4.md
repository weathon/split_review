## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes two novel jailbreak methods for LLMs based on mismatched generalization: natural language games (e.g., Ubbi Dubbi, inserting “ub” before syllable rimes) and custom language games (hand-crafted rules like inserting “alf”). The authors argue these are easy to construct, that errors by the LLM in applying game rules still leave the prompt interpretable to humans, and that fine-tuning against one game does not generalize to others. Attacks are evaluated on GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and Claude-3.5-Sonnet using a single dataset (SALAD-Bench) and a GPT-4o-mini safety judge, reporting up to 93% success rate. The authors also fine-tune Llama-3.1-70B on one custom language game dataset and find poor generalization to unseen games, even very similar ones.

**Reviewer scores:** 1, 3, 3, 3. One reviewer (R1) is much more negative, citing severe limitations in methodology, evaluation, and novelty. The other three see merit but identify significant weaknesses.

### Strengths
- The proposed attack is simple and easy to understand; custom variations are straightforward to create (R2, R3).
- Demonstrates a new, practically concerning class of jailbreaks by leveraging linguistic transformations that can be hard to defend against (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Tests on SoTA closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Claude-3.5-Sonnet), showing real-world safety consequences (R2, R3).
- The finding that larger models (GPT-4o) are *more* susceptible than smaller ones (GPT-4o-mini, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) is interesting and challenges assumptions about alignment scaling (R3).
- The fine-tuning experiments (Table 5), demonstrating poor safety generalization even to extremely similar language games, are a valuable and novel contribution (R2, R3, R4). Previous work mostly considered very different jailbreaks (R2).
- The study of whether safety fine-tuning against one language game attack can help resist other custom language game attacks is a useful research direction (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of systematic methodology & manual crafting.** The approach relies heavily on manually creating language game rules; a principled, automatic framework for generating and adapting language patterns would strengthen the contribution (R1).
- **Insufficient empirical evaluation.**
    - Only one dataset (SALAD-Bench) is used; additional benchmarks such as AdvBench are needed to demonstrate generalizability (R1).
    - Only three models tested; evaluations on more models, including Llama-3-405B, Llama-2, or smaller open-weight models like Llama-3.1-8B, are necessary to show broader applicability and to test the “larger models are more susceptible” claim (R1, R3, R4).
    - No baseline results without any language game are reported; base model susceptibility to the chosen prompts is unknown (R3).
    - No comparison to a simple random modification baseline (e.g., random character insertion) (R3).
- **Missing comparisons to state-of-the-art attacks and defenses.**
    - The proposed method is not compared to existing jailbreak attacks (e.g., GCG, AutoDAN, PAIR, TAP, DeepInception) or to prior work using encryption/encoding or ciphers (e.g., Yuan et al., 2023; Bianchi, Deng, Zeng, Zou) (R1, R3, R4).
    - The attack is not evaluated against known defenses (e.g., paraphrasing, SmoothLLM, Backtranslation, self-reminders) (R1, R4).
- **Reproducibility and evaluation setup issues.**
    - Default sampling settings are used; results are not reproducible. Authors should either use greedy decoding with a fixed seed or report summary statistics (average ± std) over multiple runs (R2, R4). Small observed differences (e.g., 86% vs 82% for Self 4 vs Self 5) could be due to randomness (R4).
    - The reliability of the GPT-4o-mini safety judge is not measured. Human evaluation (e.g., false positive/negative rates on a sample) or cross-validation with an established judge (e.g., MD-Judge from SALAD-Bench) is needed. Using a closed-source evaluator risks non-reproducibility due to model changes over time (R2, R3, R4).
    - The judge prompt (Appendix A.1) translates to Chinese first; this is unexplained and may affect judgment. The prompt only asks for 1/0, but the paper mentions “unclear” labels; how are these determined? Also Figure reference on line 598 is incorrect (16 instead of 4) (R2, R3, R4).
- **Unreliable translation method.** The safety judge translates the LLM’s language game output back to standard English, but the process is error-prone (e.g., Figure 8: "Certainly! Here are stee" instead of correct translation). Programmatic translation (e.g., removing “alf”) would be more reliable and reduce error sources (R2).
- **Inadequate details on fine-tuning experiments (Section 4.5).** The construction of the jailbreak dataset (e.g., which prompts, how many, game rules) is vague. Also, was the language game prompt included during fine-tuning? (R4, R3) Further, the claim (line 435-436) that fine-tuning defends against “other forms of attacks” (0-3% success) needs clarification: does this mean only the exact game trained on? This is ambiguous (R1, R2, R4).
- **Need for generalization of fine-tuning defense.** Only single-game fine-tuning is tested. Would fine-tuning on multiple custom language games (e.g., Self 1-4) lead to better generalization to unseen games? This is an important missing ablation (R4, R2 optional).
- **Utility measurement missing.** The paper does not evaluate whether LLMs can still provide useful responses under language games (e.g., using MT-Bench), which is important to assess practical usability (R4).
- **Baseline on vanilla Llama-3 needed.** For the fine-tuning experiments, baseline results on the unmodified Llama-3.1-70B are necessary to understand the defense’s effectiveness (R1).
- **Clarify ambiguous statements.**
    - Lines 160-161 abruptly mention “generalized across intermediate layers”; the connection to mismatched generalization of input data is unclear (R2).
    - Lines 214 claim models have “prior knowledge” of language games; this should be stated as a conjecture unless evidence from training data is provided (R2).
    - Lines 322-327 claim GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini “often address questions while framing their answers in a positive manner” — provide specific examples and explain why this is problematic (R4).
- **Other missing analyses.**
    - Provide case studies for “unclear” responses, especially for GPT-4o/4o-mini, and include the safety judge labels for each appendix case study (R2).
    - Clarify the role of the language game prompt: what happens to success rates if it is not included? (R3)
    - Justify why “human readability” of the obfuscated prompt is important for a jailbreak attack (R4).
- **Potential overclaiming of novelty.** Given prior cipher and encryption-based jailbreaks, the contribution of language games as a distinct class needs stronger differentiation and comparison (R4, R2 question 2).

**Note from the most critical reviewer (R1, score 1):** The above weaknesses are severe; in particular the lack of systematic methodology, insufficient empirical evaluation (single dataset, few models), no comparison to prior attacks/defenses, and lack of a baseline on vanilla Llama-3 constitute fundamental shortcomings that significantly undermine the contribution.
