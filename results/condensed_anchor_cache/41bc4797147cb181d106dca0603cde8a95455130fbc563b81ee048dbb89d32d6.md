## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an automatic method to diagnose calibration problems in regression by interpreting Probability Integral Transform (PIT) histograms. It trains a mixture density network (MDN) as an interpreter on synthetic PIT histograms (generated from mixtures of two normals for true distributions and single normals for estimated distributions). Given a predictive model’s PIT histogram, the interpreter outputs a probable observation-generating distribution; comparison reveals miscalibration. Evaluation is performed on three standard regression datasets, reporting improvements in NLL and CRPS.

### Strengths
- Calibration for distributional regression is an interesting and important problem; the introduction to PIT histograms is well presented. (R1)
- The experiments are sufficient to validate the practical use of the PIT histogram and the interpreter; learning the data-generating distribution through a mixture of Gaussians provides insight into non-calibration issues. (R2)
- Investigating PIT histograms to offer an easily understandable interpretation that aids in identifying calibration issues is a valuable and interesting direction of research. (R3)
- The paper makes some effort to explain the idea of fitting the empirical CDF histogram (i.e. PIT histogram) to check calibration level. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical justification**: The method is presented without thorough (theoretical) justification; Section 3 is very concise and fails to ground the approach. (R1, R3)
- **Synthetic training data is overly limited**: The true distributions are restricted to mixtures of two Gaussians and the estimated distributions to single Gaussians. It is highly unlikely that this captures all forms of miscalibration that can occur in practice, and it is unclear how one would produce a sufficiently general synthetic dataset. (R1, R3)
- **Empirical evaluation is far too limited**: Only three datasets are used; no case with multi-modal densities is included; large-scale datasets are not examined; the effect of dataset size on calibration behavior is ignored; no applicability in the learning process is shown. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Choice of neural network interpreter is unjustified**: The authors aim to learn an “inverse function of PIT”, but this function could be computed analytically (e.g., via Quantile Recalibration [Kuleshov et al., ICML 2018]). Using an MDN without justification seems redundant. (R3)
- **Observed improvement in NLL/CRPS is not novel**: The reported improvement is attributable to the use of mixture predictions, which has been observed before (e.g., Dheur et al., ICML 2023). (R3)
- **Misleading/overreaching statements**: “Optimising the sharpness of the distribution subject to its calibration is necessary” is too strong – Gneiting et al. (2017) proposed this only as a paradigm, not a necessity. The type of calibration discussed (probabilistic calibration) should be explicitly specified. (R3)
- **Poor coverage of the field**: The paper fails to mention established definitions (quantile calibration, threshold calibration, distribution calibration) and ignores well-known tools for measuring miscalibration (calibration error, pin-ball loss, calibration test). Reliability diagrams are only referred to as “calibration plots” without formal introduction. (R4)
- **Proposed method is redundant**: Once the PIT histogram is obtained, it is already a reliability diagram directly leading to metrics like calibration error. The mixture model used to approximate the marginal distribution of the target can be replaced by simple histograms. Moreover, the paper only considers a constant predicted distribution on the target value, which can hardly be considered a regression model. (R4)
- **Missing explanations and unresolved questions** (incorporated as criticisms):
  - Should one always use MDNs as base estimators to avoid detection of miscalibration by the proposed interpreter? Have the authors encountered any scenarios where MDNs are miscalibrated, and could such cases be included? (R1)
  - In the data-generating regime, the method requires multiple observations per input (y_{ij}), but such repeated measurements are not generally available. How is the effect of x_i assessed when only one y_i is given? (R2)
  - Can the number of mixture components be tuned by some principled strategy? (R2)
  - In Figure 3, how is the negative log-likelihood calculated? The explanation is missing. (R2)
  - Why not simply use reliability diagrams and calibration error, which already quantify miscalibration and can be interpreted? (R4)
