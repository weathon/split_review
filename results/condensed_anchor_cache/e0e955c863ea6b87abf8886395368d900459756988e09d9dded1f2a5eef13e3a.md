## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses two gaps between scaling law studies and practical language model training: (1) models are often over-trained to reduce inference costs, while scaling laws assume compute-optimal regimes; (2) scaling laws typically predict perplexity, but models are evaluated on downstream task performance. The authors create a testbed of 104 models (0.011B to 6.9B parameters) trained with varying token counts on three data distributions (C4, RedPajama, RefinedWeb). They fit scaling laws that extrapolate to over-trained regimes (e.g., predicting validation loss for a 1.4B parameter, 900B token run—32× over-trained—and a 6.9B, 138B token compute-optimal run using 300× less compute). They also propose a power law relating perplexity to downstream task performance, predicting average top-1 error across tasks with 20× compute reduction. The paper is well-motivated, provides strong theoretical analysis linking to prior Chinchilla/Kaplan scaling laws, and demonstrates clear experimental logic.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation and practical significance:** Addresses the overlooked over-training regime and downstream performance prediction, directly guiding experimental setups for pre-training large models.
- **Extensive and comprehensive testbed:** Experiments span 104 models across three datasets (C4, RedPajama, RefinedWeb) and a wide range of model sizes (0.011B–6.9B) and token counts, making the evaluation diverse and appropriate.
- **Strong predictive accuracy:** Achieves relative error of 0.7% on validation loss and 0.05% on average top-1 error across 17 downstream tasks.
- **Strong theoretical relationship with prior work:** Section 2 provides stimulating analysis linking the proposed over-training scaling law to existing Chinchilla/Kaplan scaling laws, giving strong theoretical support.
- **First to investigate over-training regime and downstream evaluation:** This work is original in tracking next-token prediction performance under over-training and on downstream benchmarks.
- **Logical experimental design:** The approach of fitting scaling law hyperparameters on one set of settings and validating on separate over-training and downstream settings is sound.
- **Clear presentation:** Paper is well-written with detailed steps for law derivation, model configuration, and scaling law fitting. The motivation, derivation, experiment results, and conclusion are presented logically.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing inference cost comparison:** The motivation states over-training smaller models reduces inference costs, but the paper does not directly compare inference costs (e.g., latency, throughput) of an over-trained smaller model versus a larger compute-optimal model under a fixed compute budget. This experiment or reference to prior work is needed to validate the core claim.
- **Downstream performance scaling law lacks rigorous validation:** Table 2 shows significant gaps between predicted and actual top-1 error for individual tasks, while average gap is smaller. The paper does not analyze prediction variance across tasks or discuss which task types (e.g., code, reasoning) yield higher errors, nor potential mitigating strategies. This makes individual task predictions unreliable (reviewer 2: “predictable downstream performance” is misleading—should be “predictable average performance”).
- **Strong assumption α=β in Eq. 4 limits generality:** The proposed law assumes the exponents linking loss to model size (α) and data size (β) are equal. This may not hold across diverse datasets or model architectures, leading to inaccurate predictions when exponents diverge.
- **No exploration of architectural or training optimization effects:** The study does not address how scaling laws hold under different activation functions, attention mechanisms, normalization techniques, or efficient architectures (Mixture-of-Experts, approximate attention, attention-free models). Different architectural choices could alter training dynamics and scaling behavior.
- **Limited generalization in out-of-distribution settings:** The paper notes high relative error in out-of-distribution domains (particularly code), indicating scaling laws may not generalize well to unseen data distributions. This is critical for practical applications.
- **No consideration of post-training, fine-tuning, or inference-time compute scaling:** The paper ignores how over-training affects subsequent fine-tuning or inference-time optimizations. The optimal pre-training regime may differ if the goal is performance after fine-tuning rather than minimizing pre-training loss.
- **No discussion of multimodal models:** The method relies on a fixed token vocabulary and architecture; it does not address emerging multimodal models (images, audio, video) or token-free language modeling. Scaling laws may not generalize due to different input representations and cross-modal interactions.
- **Missing comparison with prior work on over-training:** Reviewer 4 notes that work [1] (Temporal Scaling Law for Large Language Models) predicts training outcomes for small models (<0.1B) trained on many tokens (400B tokens, M>4000), i.e., over-training. The paper should discuss differences between its proposed law and [1].
- **Presentation issues:**
  - Figure 1 and Eq. 4: Inconsistency between definition of M (line 70 says M=N/D, but Fig. 1 caption and line 127 say D=MN). At line 69-70, “M = N/D” appears to be a typo for “M = D/N”.
  - Figure colors: In Figure 1, colors for M=320 and M=640 are very similar, hampering readability.
  - Typo: Line 142 “than in” is awkward.
  - Figure 1: Is this a log-log plot? Not explicitly clarified.
- **Variability in individual task predictions undermines reliability:** Despite good average performance, high errors on specific tasks suggest the scaling law may not universally hold without data or model refinements. Reviewer 2 points out that practitioners care about specific tasks, not just averages.
- **Unaddressed questions about scaling to much larger models (30B–400B) and about whether over-training effects remain uniform across model sizes and datasets.**
