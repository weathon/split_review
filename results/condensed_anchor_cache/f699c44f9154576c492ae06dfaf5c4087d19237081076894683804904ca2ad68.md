## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes "Self-Taught Evaluators," a method to train LLM judges using only synthetic data, without human annotations. It uses an iterative self-improvement process: starting from unlabeled instructions, it generates contrasting response pairs (one constructed to be better), has the LLM produce reasoning traces and judgments, selects those agreeing with the synthetic labels, and fine-tunes the model. Repeating this yields improvements—e.g., Llama3-70B-Instruct from 75.4 to 88.3 on RewardBench, matching GPT-4 and supervised reward models. The method is tested on RewardBench, MT-Bench, and HelpSteer2. Reviewers acknowledge novelty, clear presentation, and promising results, but raise concerns about generalization (only Llama3-70B-Instruct tested), reliance on two models (Mistral 8x22B for initial generation), limited baselines, iterative instability, data quality, and missing downstream reward validation. Scores: 5,5,5,6,6, indicating support with notable criticisms.

### Strengths

- **Novel self-supervised framework**: The iterative self-improvement process for training evaluators without human annotations is original and addresses a significant challenge (costly, stale human data). The synthetic preference pair construction (generating a "worse" response via a modified instruction) is especially novel.
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well-structured, easy to follow, with clear communication of the method, experimental setup, and hyperparameters aiding reproducibility.
- **Reduces human labeling dependency**: The method is scalable and cost-effective, offering a practical alternative for model evaluation.
- **Promising empirical results**: On RewardBench, the self-taught evaluator outperforms commonly used LLM judges (e.g., GPT-4-0125) and matches top-performing reward models trained with labeled examples. Improvements are also shown on MT-Bench and HelpSteer2. The method is tested on multiple datasets and data sources, with ablations and analysis (e.g., per-category scores, iterative progression).
- **Comprehensive experiments and ablations**: The paper provides extensive empirical evidence, including scores per iteration, different data sources, and analysis of design choices (e.g., prompt selection, number of iterations).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited model generalization**: The method is only tested on Llama3-70B-Instruct. It is unclear if the approach works for other LLMs, different sizes, or architectures. (Reviewers 1, 5)
- **Reliance on two models undermines "self-taught" claim**: The pipeline uses Mistral 8x22B Instruct to categorize instructions and generate initial synthetic responses and judgments, while Llama3-70B-Instruct is used for iterations. This makes the method more a distillation of knowledge from two models rather than a pure self-taught process. The motivation for using a second model is not justified, and it is unclear what would happen if the same model were used everywhere. (Reviewer 2)
- **Quality and consistency of synthetic preference pairs**: The method for generating "worse" responses (via a modified instruction x') is simple and may produce pairs where the "worse" response is not actually inferior. There is no verification that y^l is indeed worse than y^w, and no discussion of corner cases or quality degradation. Examples of these generated pairs are missing from the paper and appendix. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5)
- **Insufficient comparison baselines**: The paper mainly compares with GPT-4-0125 and a few reward models, but omits other prominent LLM-as-a-Judge methods (e.g., Best-of-N, other self-training approaches). No comparison with Best-of-N is provided, despite its relevance. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Iterative training does not always improve performance**: Performance often decreases or fluctuates after the first iteration (e.g., Table 1 "Chat" and "Reasoning" columns, Table 2, Table 5 "Chat" column). The monotonic improvement is not consistent, and the method's iterative nature is not clearly beneficial. The paper should discuss potential reasons for declines and investigate an upper bound. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Missing evaluation on downstream reward usage**: The main purpose of a reward model is to provide a reward signal for optimizing a policy LLM. The proposed evaluator is not tested in this setting, leaving its practical utility unverified. (Reviewer 4)
- **Data curation and reproducibility details are insufficient**: The paper lacks clarity on the exact steps for curating data from WildChat (e.g., deduplication, PII detection, handling of multi-turn conversations, non-English content). The amount of synthetic data per iteration and guidelines on optimal data volume are not provided. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- **Justification for category selection is weak**: The choice to focus on the "reasoning" category as challenging is not well-justified; other categories (e.g., "Coding," "Social Studies") could also be complex. The classification using Mixtral is not validated (no accuracy metrics, confusion matrix, or ablation on using different LLMs for classification). (Reviewer 3)
- **Majority vote methodology is unclear**: At inference, N samples are generated and the majority verdict is taken, but it is unclear whether baselines (e.g., GPT-4 in Table 2) also use this majority vote. The choice of N and the impact of majority voting on performance are not discussed. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Potential model collapse and overfitting**: The method iterates on fixed instructions and responses, risking overfitting to those specific examples. The mechanism for generating better critiques without external exploration is unclear; there is no discussion of how the model avoids collapse as seen in prior iterative schemes (Shumailov et al., 2024). (Reviewer 4)
- **Lack of statistical significance**: No multiple seeds or standard error/deviation are reported, making it hard to discern whether iterative improvements are significant. (Reviewer 4)
- **Prompt selection and complexity inference are not validated**: The method uses Llama-3-70B-Instruct to infer prompt complexity (score 1–5), but this is not validated against human annotations or other metrics. Many simpler prompts remain despite filtering for challenging ones. (Reviewer 3)
- **Several minor issues**: Citation notation inconsistencies (e.g., line 137 vs 139; "West" -> "Best" line 090); Figure 1 is not referenced in main text; Table 5 "Chat" has Iteration 3 bolded but Iteration 1 is highest; typographical errors (e.g., "?" in Figure 2 and line 241). (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5)
