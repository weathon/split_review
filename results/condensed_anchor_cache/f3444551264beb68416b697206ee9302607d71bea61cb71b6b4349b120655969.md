## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LLaVA-Read, a multimodal large language model that combines dual visual encoders with a visual text encoder to improve text-rich image understanding. Three reviews are split: two (R1, R3) rate it 3 (reject) and one (R2) rates it 6 (weak accept). R1 highlights successful integration of existing techniques and valuable experimental findings (e.g., Figure 2 comparison, findings 1–3). R2 praises the analysis of text recognition limitations and the model’s performance gain over baselines. R3 notes the method is simple and reproducible, and ablations confirm effectiveness. However, all three raise concerns about novelty, methodological clarity, benchmarking, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Integration of established techniques** (R1): The model combines a dual-encoder system from LLaVA‑HR (Luo et al., 2024) for handling high/low resolutions, an off‑the‑shelf OCR tool (PaddleOCR) as in LLaVAR (Zhang et al., 2023d), and a layout recovery process inspired by LATIN‑Prompt (Wang et al., 2023a).  
- **Revealing diagnostic analysis** (R1, R2): The paper analyzes multimodal LLMs’ text recognition abilities, showing they are strong on scene text but weak on dense textual content in images. Figure 2’s comparison between a commercial OCR tool and CLIP across font sizes is especially insightful (R1).  
- **Competitive experimental results** (R2): LLaVA‑Read surpasses multiple state‑of‑the‑art baselines on text‑rich image understanding tasks.  
- **Simplicity and reproducibility** (R3): The overall architecture is straightforward, and the ablations confirm the value of the proposed components.  

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental design** (R1, R3): The method is largely a combination of known techniques (dual encoders, OCR integration, layout recovery). R3 notes similar ideas appear in Mini‑Gemini, mPLUG‑DocOwl2, Ferret UI (You et al., arXiv:2404.05719), and CogAgent (Hong et al., arXiv:2312.08914). Missing discussion of differences/advantages to these related works.  
- **Insufficient explanation of methodological changes** (R1):  
  - The Mixed Resolution Adaptor (MRA) fuses 1st, 4th, and 7th layers, while LLaVA‑HR fuses the final three layers. The notation on P4L213 differs from P5L217 – unclear how it diverges from the original MRA.  
  - Whether the layout recovery step in the OCR Tokenizer is identical to LATIN‑Prompt is not clarified.  
- **Benchmark reproducibility and performance gaps** (R1):  
  - Table 1 shows large discrepancies compared to original reports. For example, EAGLE (Shi et al., 2024) reports 86.6 (Llama3‑8B) and 85.4 (Vicuna‑13B), but the reproduced score here is 68.5. Similar gaps exist for Idefics‑2. A transparent reproduction process is needed.  
  - Model sizes in the table are not always given (e.g., Idefics‑2 at 8B, EAGLE at 8B and 13B); fair comparison requires labeling parameters.  
- **Missing related works** (R1): Key works on external OCR (CREAM, Kim et al., 2023), multiple vision encoders (SPHINX series, DeepSeek‑VL), and document understanding (UDOP, Idefics3) are omitted. Recent strong models (Llama‑3.2, Qwen2‑VL) could be referenced for future work.  
- **Ablation study gaps** (R1):  
  - The effect of layout recovery is not clearly demonstrated. A baseline with LayoutLM or pix2struct would help. If using LATIN‑Prompt directly, a citation to its verified effectiveness suffices.  
  - Table 3(b) lacks “recognition score” and “total” metrics; tasks beyond OCRBench should be included.  
  - The default resolution is 1024 (used in most ablations); it should be stated whether all LLaVA‑Read results in Table 1 use 1024 resolution.  
  - The criteria for selecting the two vision encoders (if different from LLaVA‑HR) need explanation.  
- **Role of high/low resolution and pre‑training tasks** (R2): It is unclear whether performance gains come from the resolution design or from carefully engineered pre‑training objectives. The authors’ perspective on how high vs. low resolution aids text understanding would clarify.  
- **OCR integration flexibility** (R3): Using an off‑the‑shelf OCR tool is not flexible; the model’s quality hinges on OCR accuracy and quantity. Missing OCR information can harm performance.  
- **Additional concerns from questions** (R1):  
  - Clarify “LLaVA‑Read‑H” (P7L375) – does it refer to the high‑resolution encoder? Are there two versions (768×768 vs. 1024×1024)? Consistent definitions across results are needed.  
  - Are vision tokens limited to 576 (24×24) due to the low‑resolution encoder?  
  - Would increasing resolution beyond 1024 continue to improve performance, and at what cost?  
  - Table 2 example (randomly selected?) may misrepresent GPT‑4V’s performance (GPT‑4V is best on KIE). The example “BRN” vs. “Brown” could be a phrasing difference rather than a recognition error.  
  - In the OCR tokenizer’s f_q step, if basic tokenization is used, consecutive whitespaces may be removed, reducing layout recovery value. A specialized tokenizer should be confirmed.  
  - Provide token‑length distribution of OCR tokenizer results; OCR tokens can consume large LLM context. Compare length with other methods.  

**Reviewer disagreement**: R2 (score 6) found the model’s performance gain significant and considered the paper sound overall, whereas R1 and R3 (both score 3) saw the innovation as insufficient and the evaluation as flawed. The above weaknesses capture concerns from all reviewers, with R2’s criticism being narrower (pre‑training vs. resolution design, missing comparisons to Ferret UI/CogAgent) while R1 and R3 highlighted more fundamental novelty and reproducibility issues.
