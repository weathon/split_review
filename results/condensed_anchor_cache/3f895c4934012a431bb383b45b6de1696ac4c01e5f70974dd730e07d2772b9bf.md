## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes InsTag, an open-set fine-grained tagging method that uses ChatGPT to tag instruction-following samples with 6.6K intent/topic tags. It defines instruction complexity (average number of tags per sample) and diversity (proportion of unique tags relative to total recognized tags). The authors analyze popular SFT datasets (Figure 2) and find that model performance correlates with higher diversity and complexity. They then design a data selector that chooses 6K diverse and complex samples, training TagLM which outperforms WizardLM and Vicuna (trained on 10× more data) on MT-Bench (Table 4). A distilled local tagger is briefly introduced in Section 5. Reviewers find the method simple and the results strong, but raise concerns about evaluation breadth, reliance on a single tagging model, metric definitions, and insufficient detail on the distilled tagger.

### Strengths
- **Simplicity and intuitiveness**: The method is straightforward, simple to implement both conceptually and practically (Reviewer 1, 3).
- **Strong empirical results**: TagLM is the only model achieving high MT-Bench scores with <10K SFT examples; it outperforms random selection and popular open models like Vicuna and WizardLM (Reviewer 1, 4). Table 4 shows that more data does not guarantee better performance, and InsTag selects effective examples (Reviewer 1).
- **Useful dataset analysis**: The approach measures existing datasets (Figure 2) and provides guidelines on the roles of diversity, complexity, and dataset size (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Evidence for quality over quantity**: Reinforces that data quality (diversity/complexity) is crucial for alignment, consistent with prior work (Reviewer 3, 4).
- **Novel and effective tagging method**: Using ChatGPT as an open-set tagger with normalization/aggregation rules is novel and well-validated both as an analysis tool and as a selection tool (Reviewer 4 – contrast with Reviewer 2 who considers the idea existing).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation**: Only MT-Bench (80 questions) is used across the entire paper. Lacks evaluations on AlpacaEval (800 examples), MMLU, Big Bench, HumanEval, reasoning, common sense, and factual knowledge benchmarks. This makes it hard to assess generalizability and potential bias toward specific capabilities (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). MT-Bench’s small size may be easier to cover with 6K examples (Reviewer 4).
- **Reliance on ChatGPT tagging not explored**: The method depends on GPT-4’s open-ended tagging; no experiments compare tagger quality across models (e.g., GPT-4 vs. Vicuna). The impact of tag quality on final model performance is uninvestigated; noisy tags could lead to suboptimal selection. The paper mentions a distilled tagger but provides insufficient detail (architecture, training, precision/recall, impact on SFT performance). It is unclear how much performance would drop using the local tagger (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). Questions about trade-offs between tagger model size, cost, and final model performance remain (Reviewer 4).
- **Metric definitions are incomplete or not validated**: 
  - Complexity defined as average tag count per instance is not grounded in prior work; it may not align with human intuitions. A single tag like “multi-hop reasoning” might indicate higher complexity than several simple tags (Reviewer 2, 4).
  - Diversity treats all tags as equally distinct, ignoring semantic closeness (e.g., “solve” vs. “inquiry”). Tag overlap is not accounted for (Reviewer 4).
  - The paper lacks a deeper analysis of linguistic/syntactic features that characterize high- vs. low-complexity/diversity samples (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of theoretical framework**: The work is largely empirical with no theoretical explanation for why the proposed metrics correlate with SFT effectiveness, making it hard to generalize beyond the tested datasets (Reviewer 3).
- **Novelty concern**: Selecting diverse and complex samples is an existing idea; the paper is seen as an implementation and application rather than a conceptual breakthrough (Reviewer 2 – contrast with Reviewer 4 who considers the tagging method novel).
- **Missing experiments requested by reviewers**: 
  - How does the distilled tagger perform if used for data selection? Test with different models (Reviewer 1, 4).
  - How does TagLM perform on MMLU, HumanEval, AlpacaEval? Are there specific skills where it underperforms? (Reviewer 4).
  - Does the average tag count correlate with human judgments of complexity? (Reviewer 4).
  - Further examples of the method’s applicability beyond instruction tuning (Reviewer 3 – not a criticism but a missing demonstration).
