## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes UFGTIME, a GNN-based multivariate time series forecasting model that first applies a Fourier transform to the input, then constructs a KNN graph over (time index, feature dimension, frequency) nodes (a so-called “hyperspectral graph”), and finally applies a framelet message-passing operator to this graph before inverse-transforming to the time domain. The work is motivated by limitations of the recent FourierGNN model, which uses a fully connected hypervariate graph and discards temporal ordering. Reviewers vary strongly: two rate 5, one 3, and one 1. Positive reviewers find the frequency-based graph an interesting twist and the framework well-defined, with competitive MSE and useful ablation studies. Negative reviewers question the soundness of connecting nodes from different frequencies, the narrow motivation (fixing only FourierGNN, while many prior works use principled graph representations), and weaknesses in the empirical evaluation (e.g., ETT benchmarks now considered uninformative, reporting errors in Table 3). There is also no qualitative evaluation of the learned graph, no comparison to strong recent baselines like PatchTST or TimeMixer, and no standard deviations reported.

### Strengths
- The work addresses limitations of FourierGNN by incorporating temporal order via frequency-domain embeddings and introducing sparsity into the graph structure (R1, R3).
- The framework is simple in design and the use of frequency-based graph representation is an interesting twist on handling spatiotemporal dependencies (R2, R3).
- Experimental results show competitive MSE performance against popular baselines on several datasets, and ablation studies confirm the importance of learning a sparse graph (R2).
- The technical descriptions of the hyperspectral graph and the framelet message-passing operator are well articulated, with theoretical complexity analysis (R3).
- Developing effective graph-based architectures for time series forecasting remains an important research direction (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow motivation and lack of context.** The paper focuses on fixing a specific limitation of FourierGNN, but many prior works already use principled graph representations (e.g., product graphs, visibility graphs, or learnable sparse graphs via KNN or Gumbel softmax) that avoid the discussed issues. The paper misses a thorough discussion of these alternatives and does not clearly justify why the pure graph paradigm or FourierGNN’s approach is inherently superior to state-of-the-art non-graph methods like PatchTST and TimeMixer (R2, R3, R4).
- **Soundness of the hyperspectral graph construction.** Connecting nodes that correspond to different frequencies and different entities is questionable: signals with energy in entirely different frequency bands could become neighbors, and the representation does not preserve the natural structure of the data. The construction closely mirrors the hypervariate graph it replaces, differing mainly by replacing direct sequence modeling with discrete frequency components (R1, R4). Additionally, the term “hyperspectral graph” is not clearly justified—nothing inherently “hyper” is introduced beyond indexing nodes by frequency (R1, R4).
- **Empirical evaluation issues.** (a) The ETT datasets used in Table 3 are now known to be weak benchmarks—simple linear models can match or outperform deep models (Toner et al. ICML 2024) (R4). (b) Results show performance degradation compared to FourierGNN in some settings (e.g., long-term forecasting in Table 2 is often worse), and there is a suspicious discrepancy on ETTh2 where UFGTIME has best MAE but nearly double the MSE of Autoformer (R2, W2). (c) Table 3 contains a likely reporting error: on ETTh1, the MSE at horizon 192 (0.425) is lower than at horizon 96 (0.430), while the MAE increases as expected (R4). (d) The experimental setup for Section 3 (motivation experiments) is unclear; performance degradation after permutation is shown but not compared to baselines, and Figure 2 shows minimal difference between sparse and fully connected Laplacian (R3). (e) Standard deviations and number of runs are not reported anywhere, making it impossible to assess statistical significance of the claimed improvements (R1, R2).
- **Missing comparisons and qualitative evaluation.** The paper does not compare against strong recent time series models such as PatchTST or TimeMixer, and does not provide visualizations of the learned graph dependencies (e.g., an adjacency heatmap) to validate whether the sparse connections capture meaningful inter-series or frequency relationships (R2, R3). Additionally, many baselines in Table 2 require a predefined input graph, but the appendix does not specify which similarity metric was used to construct that kNN graph (R4).
- **Limited novelty of the graph framelet operator.** The framelet message-passing operator appears to be a direct adaptation of existing framelet methods to the proposed graph representation, offering little methodological novelty (R1, R4).
- **Presentation and clarity issues.** The paper is confusing, often blending obvious claims with unsupported statements (e.g., stating that “CNN cannot model spatial information” when geospatial deep learning contradicts this). Several arguments, such as the claim that reordering frequency nodes violates structure (lines 212–217), are logically incomplete. Figures (e.g., Figure 6 contour plot for discrete variables and non-integer optimal k) are unclear (R1).
- **Missing complexity and scalability analysis.** While the method is efficient, the paper does not break down the complexity of all GNN-based baselines or analyze how window size and number of input variables affect memory/time requirements, which would be helpful for understanding scalability (R2).
