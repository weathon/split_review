## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses minority data generation (low-density regions of the data manifold) using diffusion models. It introduces a minority score based on Tweedie’s denoising formula to quantify sample uniqueness, then uses an auxiliary classifier trained on high-scoring samples to guide generation via classifier guidance. Experiments are conducted on CelebA 64×64, ImageNet 64×64, LSUN Bedrooms 256×256, and a proprietary Brain MR dataset. The authors provide theoretical justification and report quantitative improvements over several baselines.

### Strengths
- The problem of generating minority samples is interesting and has practical applications (e.g., medical imaging) (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The use of Tweedie’s denoising formula to derive a minority score is novel to the best of reviewers’ knowledge (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and has good presentation (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- Theoretical background (e.g., Proposition 1, Tweedie’s formula) is well explained and supports the claims (Reviewer 2).
- Quantitative results are intuitive and consistent across datasets; performance gains are shown on benchmark real data and in a demanding medical imaging scenario (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The appendix provides detailed explanations, extra examples, and theoretical derivations (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
*Note: One reviewer (Rating 3, Confidence 5) was substantially more negative than the other three (all Rating 6). Their specific criticisms are included below and marked where appropriate.*

**Limited novelty and incremental contributions**
- The work is a combination of existing ideas (Tweedie’s formula, classifier guidance); technical contributions are somewhat limited (Reviewer 3).
- The rationale behind the minority score and the exact motivation for generating minority data is not sufficiently elaborated (Reviewer 3).

**Insufficient baselines and literature review**
- Baselines are limited to outdated methods: StyleGAN 2020, DDPM 2020. More recent approaches such as Latent Diffusion Models (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022) are neither cited nor compared (Reviewer 2).
- The only recent diffusion baseline (Sehwag et al., 2022) is used on only one dataset (ImageNet). The paper mischaracterises Sehwag et al.’s claim (they attribute the problem to uniform sampling, not inherent majority bias). Moreover, Sehwag et al. produced 256×256 images via upscaling, while this paper shows only 64×64 results (Reviewer 1).
- Several relevant works are missing from the literature review: Qin et al. (2023, Class-Balancing Diffusion Models), Huang & Jafari (2023, Enhanced Balancing GAN), Samuel et al. (2023, Generating Images Of Rare Concepts) (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The method is not evaluated on CIFAR-LT, a dataset specifically designed for long-tail generation tasks (Reviewer 1).

**Methodological and theoretical gaps**
- The paper interchangeably uses the general term “diffusion models” and assumes Tweedie’s formula is a general form for any diffusion model, which may not hold (Reviewer 1).
- In Proposition 1, the assumption that “a given noise-conditioned score network sθ(xt, t) has enough capacity” needs clarification; the effect of violating this assumption on the proposed method is not discussed (Reviewer 1).
- In Fig. 1(a), the perturbation timestep used to generate the noise samples is not explicitly stated (0.9T or T?), which critically impacts the interpretation of Section 3.1. A more detailed justification of why the reconstructed image is a valid reconstruction is needed (Reviewer 4).
- Nomenclature error in Section 4.2: the representation of \(\tilde{l}\) appears incorrect (Reviewer 4).

**Evaluation concerns**
- The proposed method does not achieve satisfactory precision and recall on the LSUN-Bedrooms dataset. A more thorough discussion of trade-offs between precision and recall for minority generation is needed (Reviewer 4).
- On ImageNet, Sehwag et al. (2022) outperforms the proposed method in two out of four evaluation metrics (Reviewer 2).
- No sensitivity analysis to hyperparameter choices (e.g., guidance scale, threshold for minority score) is provided; this limits reproducibility and understanding of robustness (Reviewer 2).
- Overfitting risk in low-density regions is not adequately addressed: with few real minority samples, how does the model differentiate genuinely novel features from artifacts? An analysis of authenticity and diversity beyond nearest-neighbour distances is missing (Reviewer 4).

**Missing experimental details and clarity**
- In Fig. 2, the assignment of “small”, “moderate”, and “high” minority scores on real data is not explained (Reviewer 1).
- The exact size of the real-data subset used for validation in each experiment is not specified (Reviewer 4).
- The selection of minority score levels for generating data is unclear; the range or distribution of scores used should be reported (Reviewer 4).
- It is unclear how minority guidance interacts with other forms of guidance (classifier guidance, classifier-free guidance). Only Appendix E shows conditional generation examples, but no quantitative evaluation is provided, limiting assessment of broader applicability (Reviewer 3).
- The paper does not explore whether focusing on minority generation degrades performance on long-tail (i.e., majority) data (Reviewer 2).
- No systematic verification that generated samples actually correspond to minority instances beyond the minority score itself (Reviewer 2).

**Reviewer disagreement**
- One reviewer (Rating 3, Confidence 5) rated soundness, presentation, and contribution all as 2 (fair), while the other three (Rating 6, Confidence 4) rated soundness and contribution as 3 (good) and presentation as 2–3. The notable divergence stems from the minority reviewer’s concerns about mischaracterization of prior work, insufficient baseline usage, and lack of evaluation on a dedicated long-tail benchmark like CIFAR-LT.
