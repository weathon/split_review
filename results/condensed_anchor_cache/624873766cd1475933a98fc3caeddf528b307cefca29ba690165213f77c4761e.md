## Merged Review

### Summary
SPACE is a benchmark for evaluating spatial cognition in LLMs and VLMs, comprising both large-scale tasks (navigation, cognitive maps) and small-scale tasks (mental rotation, shape reasoning), each presented in multiple modalities (egocentric images/video, BEV images, BEV text; or image/text). Results show frontier models perform near chance on many classic cognitive science tests, far below humans.

### Strengths
- Timely and important topic with implications for real-world use of foundation models.
- Very extensive and diverse set of 15 tasks spanning large-scale and small-scale spatial cognition, grounded in cognitive science literature. Tests both LLMs and VLMs across three scenarios (Ego image/video, BEV images, BEV text), reflecting real-world complexity.
- Translation of spatial reasoning tasks to text-only input is a nice contribution, enabling evaluation of text-only models; the benchmark is expected to be immediately valuable to the community.
- Comprehensive evaluation across many frontier models (closed and open-source), giving a clear picture of current capabilities.
- Interactive tasks for route retracing and shortcut searching (model receives observation, decides action, receives updated observation) are a notable design feature.
- Well-written, with detailed information on evaluation methodology, prompts, and model settings. Figures and gray shading for results below 50% of human performance aid interpretability.
- Human performance reference included for all tasks, making model-human comparisons straightforward.
- Significance: reveals that frontier models lack basic spatial cognition despite advanced skills, prompting rethinking of whether causal language modeling alone can lead to human-like intelligence without embodiment; raises questions about reliability and trustworthiness.

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing citations and novelty discussion**: Several previous works have already shown that LLMs struggle with navigation/planning (Valmeekam et al. 2023, Momennejad et al. 2023) and spatial relation reasoning (Yamada et al. 2024, Ivanova et al. 2024). These are not cited, and the paper does not discuss how SPACE goes beyond these existing benchmarks or what new information it provides.
2. **Unfamiliar task formats and potential ecological validity issues**: Most tasks are directly adapted from tests designed for humans/animals, not LLMs/VLMs. For example, vision encoders like CLIP are trained on ImageNet-style object images and may not be suited for egocentric observations or BEV images. Models may have better spatial cognition internally but fail due to unfamiliar prompt formats. The paper does not demonstrate that models can understand the task formats (e.g., showing high accuracy on easier tasks using the same prompting style). This concern is shared by multiple reviewers (R1's point about image resolution possibly disadvantaging VLMs; R3's call for ecologically valid presentations; R4's request for comprehension checks). R3 specifically suggests trying egocentric text descriptions (e.g., "there is a landmark with a picture of a dog on the left wall") for both large-scale and small-scale tasks (grid inputs) as a more model-friendly representation.
3. **Insufficient detail on image/video preprocessing and its potential impact on results**: Image and video resolutions are not specified. Model performance can vary significantly with input size and aspect ratio handling, which may partially explain why LLMs (text-only) outperform VLMs on some tasks. Missing details include: exact resolutions used, resizing/aspect ratio changes for different models, video duration, number of sampled frames per video (too dense→redundancy, too sparse→missing landmarks), and BEV text encoding process (e.g., how many array dimensions were tested, effect of smaller dimensionality of text arrays vs. BEV images). Reviewer 4 recommends an ablation study with different image resolutions to quantify impact, especially for distance and direction estimation.
4. **Insufficient model coverage for VLMs**: Only two VLMs were tested for image tasks, both from the GPT family (GPT-4v, GPT-4o). More SOTA VLMs should be evaluated to strengthen conclusions.
5. **Small number of images per task**: Table 3 lists only question counts, not image counts. Some tasks appear to have fewer than 10 unique images, limiting robustness. Exact image counts per task should be provided and sufficient images ensured.
6. **CoT prompting not controlled**: "Think step by step" prompts appear in the appendix, but it is unclear which tasks used Chain-of-Thought. Tasks using CoT should be flagged and results compared to non-CoT versions to assess whether reasoning steps help.
7. **Lack of structured feature decomposition**: The paper presents many tasks independently but does not identify shared features of spatial cognition (e.g., A, B, C, D) across tasks and map tasks to those features. This would make evaluation more diagnostic (which aspects models succeed/fail at) and reduce the overwhelming amount of disparate results.
8. **Unsupported claim about spatial cognition as prerequisite for higher cognition**: The discussion states that spatial cognition is necessary for tasks like olympiad geometry, yet does not cite psychological evidence for this relation. The specific example (Trinh et al.) uses a model trained from scratch on synthetic geometry data, not a general language model, so it does not directly support the claim that frontier models skip basic cognition.
9. **Specific task correctness concern (R1 minority view)**: In the direction estimation task (Figure 6), the example angle of 78° appears inconsistent given that the two landmarks seem almost collinear. This needs clarification.
