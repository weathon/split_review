## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies offline Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) with trajectory-wise reward feedback. It proposes three algorithms: (1) MLE-based reward estimation followed by distributionally robust planning over a confidence set (known transition), (2) analogous extension with MLE of the transition kernel when dynamics are unknown, and (3) a variant for action-based comparisons using advantage function estimation and greedy policy. The theoretical analysis provides polynomial sample complexity guarantees as long as the target policy is covered by the offline data, introducing a new single-policy concentrability coefficient. The results generalize prior work (Zhu et al.) from linear to general function approximation. No empirical evaluation is included.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written.
- Provides several strong, novel theoretical results on offline PbRL, including generalizing Zhu et al. from linear to general function approximation.
- Introduces a new single-policy concentrability coefficient to measure coverage of the target policy on the dataset.
- Guarantees learning the target policy with a polynomial number of samples under partial coverage.
- Considers multiple realistic settings: known/unknown transition, action-based comparisons, and fits them within a similar algorithmic framework.
- Nice comparisons with previous work (Sec. 4.1, Remarks 2 and 4).
- Important theoretical contribution in a field dominated by empirical work; not restricted to a specific function class; considers return-based (trajectory-wise) utility, which is more general than state-action rewards.
- Adaptation of the concentrability coefficient to preference feedback is interesting and separates offline policy optimization from exploration/exploitation.

### Weaknesses
- No empirical evaluation or case studies; the paper is entirely theoretical. Multiple reviewers (R1, R2, R3）request experiments to verify scaling and practical performance.
- Lack of implementation details and intuition for constructing a practical algorithm. The algorithms are theoretically sound but no guidance on how to implement them (R2).
- Related work comparison is insufficient, especially with practical PbRL algorithms. Return-based utility approximation is not novel (e.g., “Preference-Based Policy Learning” [1] and survey [2]）; the omission obscures what falls within the framework and the distinctions from prior art, limiting perceived novelty (R4).
- The sample complexity bound in Proposition 1 depends on the log bracketing number, which can be arbitrarily large when epsilon is small or bounds B,R large, making the bound weak. The practical implications are not discussed (R2).
- The sample complexity expression N = O(…log(…1/N)) appears to have N on both sides, which is mathematically inconsistent and needs clarification (R2).
- Several terms and definitions are not clearly explained:
  * The mapping g in Definition 1 and its role in the preference model (R2, R4).
  * The epsilon-bracketing number applied to the preference set (R2).
  * The notation for π, μ, d is used interchangeably (R2).
  * The slack parameter in Sec. 4.1 has the wrong symbol (R4).
- Alg 2 has a typo: “Distributionally robust plnanning” (R4).
- Some relevant information is left to the appendix, including the feasible implementation and the comparison to “Principled reinforcement learning with human feedback from pairwise or k-wise comparisons” [3]; these details are important for practitioners (R4).
- Remark 3 is not explained (R4).
- The statement that “cannot be relaxed to the per-step concentrability without additional assumptions, such as on the reward structure” is not obvious; it is unclear why it follows from the theorems that state-action rewards do not enable this relaxation (R4). One reviewer explicitly asked for explanation.
- Missing references: for the per-step concentrability coefficient commonly used in offline RL, and for the well-known observation that the optimal policy is Markovian and deterministic when reward is state-action-wise (R4).
- Several points needing clarification:
  * In Sec. 4.1, the distance between r and r* is computed as total variation or ℓ1 norm; it is not obvious how this applies if r and r* are scalars (R2).
  * Why does the per-trajectory concentrability coefficient C_tr not depend on μ_1? (R2)
  * How does C_r(G_r, π_tar, u_1) reduce to sqrt(C_tr)? (R2)
- The link function in Eq. (1) is assumed known; it is unclear whether the algorithm would work if the link function is unknown (R3).
- For the unknown transition case, does the result extend when transition data and preference data are sampled separately? (R3)
