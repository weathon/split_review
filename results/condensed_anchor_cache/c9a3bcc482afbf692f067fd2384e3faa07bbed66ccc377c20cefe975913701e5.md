## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper targets open-world failure detection and reasoning in robotic manipulation, which is critical and valuable for downstream tasks. The authors propose FailGen, a procedural simulation pipeline for generating failure demonstrations, and fine-tune a VLM (AHA) for failure detection and reasoning. They demonstrate improved performance on the standalone failure reasoning task (surpassing six compared models) and in three downstream applications (reward design, task-and-motion planning, zero-shot trajectory generation) where AHA's feedback boosts success rates by 21.4% on average over GPT-4. Reviewers note the problem is important and the dataset valuable, but raise concerns about experiment sufficiency, baseline comparisons, generalization, and clarity.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important, safety-critical problem that is beneficial when learning-based robotic algorithms are deployed in the real world (R1, R3).
- The procedural data generation pipeline (FailGen) is useful for the community and can be extended to other simulators, encompassing a comprehensive set of failure categories (R1, R4; R4 emphasizes extendability).
- The effort in developing a large-scale failure dataset is valuable toward building robust models that understand the physical world (R2).
- It is inspiring that improved failure detection and reasoning performance yields strong benefits on multiple related downstream tasks: reward function refinement, task-plan optimization, and sub-task verification (R1, R3).
- The presentation is generally well-structured and easy to follow (R1); the website has many great demos (R3).
- Training solely on simulated data generalizes effectively to real-world failure datasets, robotic systems, and unseen tasks (from paper; not contested by reviewers).

### Weaknesses
**Baseline Comparisons and Experimental Rigor**
- Implementation details of baselines are unclear: what input format (single image, sequence, multiple views, video) was used for each model? This significantly affects performance (e.g., Gemini excels with video but would degrade with multi-view images). The paper must specify the exact input format (number of views, temporal length, individual vs. sequential processing) to ensure fair comparison (R4; echoed by R3).
- No comparison with robotic-oriented failure reasoning methods from the Introduction/Related Work (e.g., methods that incorporate state information or specific robotic datasets). Comparisons only against general VLMs, which are not designed for robotic tasks (R4).
- Reviewer 3 asks whether the REFLECT framework was used as a baseline; if not, a comparison with REFLECT (highly relevant) should be included.
- Reviewer 2 notes that the paper focuses on simple pick-and-place tasks in clean backgrounds. Results on more challenging dexterous tasks (e.g., robot hand grasping and reorientation with fine-grained failure modes) and generalization to diverse backgrounds/objects are needed. Discussion of negative results is encouraged.
- Missing real-world experiments make it hard to evaluate sim-to-real transfer loss and actual practicality (R1). (See also generalization concern above.)

**Soundness of Downstream Applications**
- It remains unclear whether incorporating failure descriptions is a reliable way to improve VLM-based reward design, planning, and subtask verification. For example, in reward design, a penalty term derived from AHA may hinder learning if too large. The authors should show iterative reward tuning (tuning strength/number of terms) that converges (R2).
- More extensive comparisons between AHA and GPT-4 are needed to understand what leads to improvements: is GPT-4 unable to generate accurate failure descriptions, or is the generated text too lengthy for downstream VLMs? What does a "good" failure description look like? Comparison to human oracles with different writing styles would help (R2).

**Methodology and Design Discussion**
- Lack of discussion of hallucination in LLMs/VLMs. The paper motivates failure recognition with the shortcoming of hallucinations, but AHA will still suffer from this. Mitigation strategies (e.g., uncertainty estimation) should be discussed; a missing citation on uncertainty-based VLM hallucination detection is relevant (R1).
- Related to prompting: in prior work, chain-of-thought analysis in the prompt template improves failure detection. Does AHA's reasoning part help detection? How do different prompting templates (with/without CoT) influence performance? (R1)
- In Section 3.1, why separate rotation failure into "incorrect" and "missing"? These may belong to the same category (R1, R3). Also, what is the difference between "rotation" and "no rotation" categories? (R3)
- Failure types and reasoning responses are somewhat rough; the framework could benefit from more detailed and finer-grained failure categories (R3). Reviewer 4, in contrast, finds the set of failure categories comprehensive and extendable.

**View Angle and Camera Setup**
- Training camera view angles are fixed per RLBench settings. How many views does the model require during testing? Must test view angles match the exact poses from RLBench training? An ablation on number of camera viewpoints would clarify real-robot applicability (R1, R4).

**Presentation Issues**
- Subsection 4.1 could be shortened with a more concise mathematical formulation (R1).
- Add a subsection in Methods to explain how improved failure detection/reasoning helps each downstream task; putting this in Experiments is less apparent (R1).
- The usage in downstream tasks 2 (TAMP planning) and 3 (sub-task verification) is essentially similar (detect/reason about sub-task failures). Grouping them with a taxonomy of high-level usage manners would be clearer (R1).
- The description "we approach failure reasoning by first predicting a binary success condition..." is less straightforward; clarify that reasoning follows detection (R1).
- Figs. 1 and 2 are hard to read; reorganize sub-figures and increase size of images and text (R1).
- Figure 1 is not referenced or discussed anywhere in the paper (R4).
