## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper theoretically proves by construction that linear single‑layer transformers can implement temporal difference (TD) learning in their forward pass – a phenomenon called in‑context TD. The authors show this both by explicitly constructing weight matrices that execute TD(0) and by demonstrating that multi‑task pre‑training of a transformer to estimate value functions naturally gives rise to such behaviour. They extend the construction to other policy evaluation algorithms: TD(λ), average‑reward TD, and residual gradient. Empirical validation is performed on a simple Boyan’s chain environment (40‑step horizons). The proofs and derivations are detailed in the appendix.

### Strengths
- The paper provides a novel theoretical exploration of the internal mechanics of linear transformers for in‑context reinforcement learning, moving beyond mere performance claims to explain *why* the architecture works for value estimation.
- The proofs are concrete and rigorous; the appendices present theorems and corollaries with clear, step‑by‑step reasoning, which enhances reproducibility.
- The analysis is extended beyond TD(0) to multiple classic reinforcement‑learning algorithms (TD(λ), average‑reward TD, residual gradient), showing the generality of the construction.
- One reviewer (score 8) found the combination of proofs and empirical evidence convincing and novel, helping to further the understanding of in‑context RL.

### Weaknesses
- **Title overscoping**: The paper studies only **linear attention**, not the commonly used softmax attention, and shows that a specific multi‑task training regime yields TD‑like updates. A more precise title would be “Transformers *Can* Learn Temporal Difference Methods …” (Reviewer 1).
- **Justification of the setting**: The relevance of studying RL with linear attention over very short horizons (e.g., 40 steps) is questioned, because real RL problems typically involve much longer trajectories where attention becomes computationally expensive and loses precision. The paper should better motivate why in‑context RL with linear attention (or any transformer) is worth studying (Reviewer 1).
- **Strict initial conditions and unrealistic assumptions** (Reviewer 3): Many proofs rely on highly constrained setups, e.g., strict diagonal/block‑diagonal weight matrices and specific embeddings. The paper does not explain how such structures would emerge or be maintained during standard training in complex environments, making it hard to generalise the theoretical results to realistic settings.
- **Insufficient empirical evaluation** (Reviewers 2 & 3):
  - The experiments are limited to a very simple Boyan’s chain / grid‑world environment. It is unclear whether the reported value‑estimation errors reflect the transformer’s in‑context TD learning or could be equally achieved by standard architectures (e.g., MLPs, RNNs) that are known to struggle with in‑context learning. A comparison with architectures that do not support in‑context learning is missing.
  - No comparisons are made against existing RL algorithms that use transformers (other than behaviour‑cloning methods), e.g., a transformer trained with DQN or PPO. Without such comparisons, the practical advantage of in‑context TD over existing approaches cannot be assessed.
  - The claims of generalisability (e.g., to larger or more complex tasks) are unsupported; working well on a small‑scale problem does not imply the same behaviour will hold for higher‑dimensional state spaces or more intricate dynamics (Reviewer 3, also citing lines 531‑532).
- **Paper readability** (Reviewer 2): The text often borrows equations from prior work without sufficient explanation. For example, the derivation of Equation (4) is unclear; the connection between the linear attention mechanism and the scalar value output is not spelled out. The presentation would benefit from a self‑contained explanation of all symbols and a step‑by‑step derivation from the transformer architecture.
- **Unclear or unsubstantiated claims** (Reviewer 3): The conclusion (lines 523‑524) is vague about whether policy iteration or policy optimisation forms the core foundation. The claim that results will extend to larger tasks (lines 531‑532) lacks evidence.
- **Missing ablations and variations** (all reviewers):
  - Why does in‑context TD only emerge when the task distribution is “sufficiently challenging” (trained across thousands of MRPs)? Would the same effect appear if the transformer were trained on a single task rather than multiple tasks? (Reviewer 2)
  - The construction for TD(λ) (Corollary 3) requires a specific mask matrix that aligns eligibility traces in a fixed manner. The paper does not address how such strict temporal alignment would be achieved or maintained in realistic environments where dependencies shift dynamically (Reviewer 3).
  - The study focuses on value‑function estimation, not control. How could the approach be extended to policy optimisation or control tasks? (Reviewer 2)
  - If the transformer were used as a policy (rather than a value estimator), would its internal operations still resemble TD learning, or would a different RL algorithm arise? (Reviewer 2)
