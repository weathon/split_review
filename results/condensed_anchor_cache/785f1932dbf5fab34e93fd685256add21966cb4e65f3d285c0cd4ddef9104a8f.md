## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes A-MoD, a parameter-free routing method for Mixture-of-Depths (MoD) models. A-MoD uses the attention scores from the previous layer to decide which tokens to compute in the current layer, eliminating the need for learnable routing layers. The method is evaluated on ImageNet classification using DeiT/ViT models of various sizes. Reviewers agree the idea is simple and interesting for reducing training overhead, but they raise major concerns about the narrow evaluation, missing comparisons, unfair baselines, lack of throughput measurements, and practical limitations for large-scale or dense-prediction tasks.

### Strengths
- The idea of eliminating learnable routing (a major source of training instabilities) by reusing existing attention maps is a simple and interesting research direction. (R1, R4)
- The method is clearly explained and easy to implement for small models on short token sequences. (R4)
- The approach aligns with other efficient ViT methods (e.g., A-ViT) that dynamically select tokens for computation. (R3)
- Some experimental results show that A-MoD outperforms standard MoD under certain settings; e.g., up to 2% higher ImageNet accuracy and 2× faster transfer learning compared to MoD and isoFLOP baselines. (R2, R3)  
  *Note:* One reviewer (R3) counters that A-MoD significantly lowers original ViT performance (ViT-Tiny ~74% vs A-MoD 69.76%–71.8%), and the abstract’s “2% improvement” is relative to an isoFLOP baseline, not the full model. This disagreement is preserved below.

### Weaknesses
- **Narrow scope and missing SoTA comparisons:** The evaluation focuses only on MoD as a baseline, but MoD is not necessarily SoTA; e.g., in Table 1 even isoFLOP baselines sometimes outperform MoD. Papers such as DynamicViT, Token Merging (TO‑ME, ICLR 2023), and A-ViT achieve token pruning with better reported accuracy/latency, yet no comparison is provided. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Unfair baseline setup:** The isoFLOP baseline in Table 1 uses a ViT with fewer layers to match FLOPs, but in 3 out of 8 configurations the baseline has <90% of the FLOPs of the A-MoD model. A fair comparison would require a Pareto curve showing performance vs compute, especially for the C=12.5% case. (R4)  
  Additionally, the paper does not compare against a vanilla ViT of the same depth; instead a reduced-layer ViT is used as baseline, which is not a proper test of efficiency. (R3)
- **Missing throughput/latency evaluation:** FLOPs are an incomplete cost metric. A-MoD requires materializing the full attention matrix, while efficient implementations like FlashAttention avoid this cost. The paper provides no inference throughput or latency measurements, making it unclear whether A-MoD yields real speedups, especially for long sequences or large models. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Limited to alternating layers (max 2× speedup):** Because each A-MoD block bases its pruning on the previous block’s attention, it can only be applied every other layer. This caps theoretical speedup at 2× over a dense model, whereas learnable routing could prune at every layer. Recent MoE architectures (e.g., Snowflake Arctic) show benefits of applying routing at every layer. (R4)
- **Attention matrix requirement impractical for large models:** Modern attention implementations do not materialize full attention matrices due to O(n²) cost. Implementing A-MoD for LLMs or large generative models would require non-trivial re-engineering. (R4)
- **Evaluation only on classification:** Token pruning is natural for classification (where many tokens can be discarded), but the paper does not test on dense-prediction tasks (segmentation, language modeling, image/video generation) where pruned tokens can produce artifacts. This is a significant limitation. (R4)
- **Questionable semantic meaning of attention for routing:** The rule “higher average attention → higher importance” is not justified. Background tokens often attend strongly among themselves; Fig. 4 shows that MOD sometimes selects boundary patches and parts of the object but skips background—does this make sense? The authors should explain the semantic interpretation. (R2, R3)
- **Shallow layers and attention reliability:** Shallow layers typically do not produce meaningful attention maps. The paper applies the same selection ratio across all layers—does this degrade performance? Fig. 5’s attention maps need more examples (not just bird and car) to support the claim, and it is not clear whether specific heads or examples were cherry-picked. (R3)
- **Training and transfer learning issues:** The paper continues training from an existing checkpoint for an additional 100 epochs (Line 234) rather than training from scratch. The transfer learning experiment does not use fixed pretrained weights, making its contribution to efficiency claims unclear. (R3)
- **Performance drop vs. full ViT:** A-MoD significantly lowers the original ViT performance (e.g., ViT-Tiny 74% → 69.76%–71.8%), contradicting the abstract’s suggestion of a “2% improvement” (which is relative to an isoFLOP baseline, not the full model). (R3)
- **Inconclusive results in Fig. 7:** In Fig. 7(a) performance is aligned with the isoFLOP baseline; in Fig. 7(b) the compute gap is too large to draw conclusions. (R4)
- **Missing details for implementation:** How are multiple attention heads within a layer handled? Would different heads choose different tokens? Are there reproducible MoD results on ImageNet? (R3)  
- **Missing comparisons with hybrid architectures:** The paper does not discuss how A-MoD compares to efficient ConvNet or Conv‑Transformer hybrids (e.g., ResNeXt, LeViT, FastViT). (R1)
