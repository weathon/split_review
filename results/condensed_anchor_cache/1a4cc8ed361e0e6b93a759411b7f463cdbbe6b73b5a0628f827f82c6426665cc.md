## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a generative model for complex vector drawings by representing them as sets of strokes (“stroke-clouds”) and applying a two-stage framework: a Set Transformer encoder compresses variable-cardinality stroke sets into latent codes, and a diffusion decoder (LSG) generates stroke sets conditioned on those latents, justified by De Finetti’s Theorem of Exchangeability for conditional independence. The method is evaluated on a new Anime line-art dataset (Anime-Vec10k). Ratings: 5, 6, 6. One reviewer (Rating 5) is notably more critical than the other two (both 6).

### Strengths

- **Problem importance and novelty**: The task of generating complex vector drawings is underexplored, and the set-based representation directly addresses limitations of sequential autoregressive models (variable stroke count, permutation sensitivity). [R1, R2]
- **Clear exposition**: The paper is well-structured, with a logical flow from motivation to method. [R1] (R2 rated presentation as “fair”, however.)
- **Simple, implementable core idea**: The method builds on standard components (Set Transformer, diffusion) and should be easy to re-implement. [R1]
- **Permutation invariance and variable cardinality**: The Set Transformer theoretically guarantees permutation-invariant encoding and can handle a varying number of strokes. [R2]
- **Flexible stroke generation**: The decoder can sample arbitrary numbers of strokes for a sketch. [R2]
- **Potential for transfer to other domains**: The approach could be adapted to other artistic styles (e.g., oil painting) by changing the stroke model and dataset. [R2]
- **Principled use of De Finetti’s Theorem**: The framework formally justifies treating strokes as conditionally independent given the latent code, which is a strong theoretical grounding. [R3]
- **Publishable contribution**: Despite weaknesses, the method takes on a difficult problem in an imaginative way and produces reasonable results. [R3] (Note: R1 and R2 found the visual quality poor, see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses

- **Fixed output stroke count vs. variable motivation** (MAJOR, R1): The model generates a fixed 1000 strokes, contradicting the claimed advantage of variable cardinality. For simpler sketches, the fate of extra strokes is unclear; the discussion in Appendix E is insufficient. R1 also asks why DDPM samples appear to have more strokes than DDIM samples (Figure 5).
- **Strong i.i.d. assumption and lack of matching** (MAJOR, R1): The diffusion denoising treats strokes as conditionally independent (i.i.d.) without any correspondence or Hungarian matching between random noise strokes and target strokes. This can lead to degenerate solutions (e.g., all noise strokes denoising to the same stroke), and it is unclear how the stroke sequence is ordered during training.
- **No quantitative experiments or baseline comparisons** (MAJOR, R1, R2, R3): The paper contains no quantitative evaluation (e.g., FID, user study, Turing test) and no comparisons to existing methods. R1 notes that methods like ATISS (Paschalidou et al. 2021) and COFS (Para et al. 2023) are directly applicable and have available code; their omission incorrectly implies no prior work scales. R2 requests comparison even with raster-based methods. R3 explicitly states “no experiments at all,” which is a major lack of rigor and undermines claims of “high quality”.
- **Simplistic stroke representation** (MAJOR, R2, R3): Only quadratic Bézier curves are used, lacking width, transparency, and higher-order control points. This limits expressiveness for complex strokes (e.g., long curly hair) and artistic applications. R3 also notes that all generated outputs are faces in the same view angle and crop, severely restricting generality.
- **Latent space interpolation issues** (MAJOR, R2): The interpolated sketches in Figure 8 appear out of domain, suggesting the latent space is not smooth or the interpolation method is flawed.
- **Lack of semantic control and generality** (MAJOR, R3): The system provides no mechanism to control noun class, style, or viewpoint; all examples are manga-style faces. R3 argues the paper’s conclusion misidentifies the primary limitation (blaming probabilistic reconstruction) when in fact the lack of semantic understanding is more critical. R3 questions generalization to other objects, views, styles, and realistic stroke complexity.
- **Unsubstantiated claims in introduction** (R3): The assertion that vector representations are inherently linked to creativity and pixel representations to perception is made without any citation to perceptual or art-historical literature, and R3 finds the argument misleading.
- **Missing related work** (R1, R3): The paper fails to cite any work prior to 2015, including a large body of non-photorealistic rendering (NPR) literature, and omits recent set-based generative methods (ATISS, COFS). This undermines the paper’s positioning.
- **Few qualitative results and potential overfitting** (MINOR, R1): The paper should show nearest training examples to generated samples (both in raster and perceptual space) and a 10×10 grid of training and generated images to assess dataset quality and overfitting.
- **Dataset not yet released** (R1 question): Unclear if the Anime-Vec10k dataset will be made public.
