## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper empirically investigates denoising pre-training and scaling behavior of geometric graph neural networks (Geom‑GNNs) for molecular representation learning. It examines model size, aspect ratio, radial cutoff, and architecture across downstream tasks including molecular dynamics (kinetics), fold classification, quantum‑chemical properties (QM9, xxMD), and conformation modeling. The authors find that Geom‑GNNs do not follow power‑law scaling and saturate early due to under‑reaching and over‑smoothing, and they suggest that addressing label uncertainty and employing active token mixing may be more critical than scaling. The possibility of zero‑shot transfer from pre‑trained Geom‑GNNs is also explored. Reviewer opinions are split: one reviewer (score 8) considers the work innovative, well‑organized, and insightful for the community, while the other three (scores 6, 6, 6) raise substantial concerns about novelty, clarity, experimental scope, and strength of claims.

### Strengths

1. **Relevant research topics**: Pre‑training for geometric GNNs, scaling behavior, and issues of over‑smoothing / under‑reaching are timely and important.
2. **Comprehensive exploration**: The study systematically investigates model width, depth, aspect ratio, cutoff radius, and architecture (ET, ViSNet) in the context of pre‑training.
3. **Kinetic modeling improvement**: Substantial VAMP score gains are demonstrated for molecular dynamics tasks using denoising pre‑training.
4. **Multiple downstream tasks**: Performance is evaluated on kinetics, fold classification, QM9, and xxMD, showing applicability of the pretrained embeddings.
5. **Dataset splits**: Experiments include not only random splits but also scaffold and temporal splits, providing more realistic generalization assessment.
6. **Zero‑shot transfer investigation**: The exploration of pre‑trained Geom‑GNNs as zero‑shot learners is noted as interesting, with initial evidence of strong latent representations.
7. **Pre‑training insights**: Observations on model width, depth, aspect ratio, and cutoff radius offer practical guidance for pre‑training geometric GNNs.
8. **Advancing unified architectures**: The work inspires reconsideration of Geom‑GNN design and training strategies for a general‑purpose geometric descriptor.

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear research goal**: The paper lacks a clearly defined research aim; it is hard to understand why specific experiments are chosen and how they collectively answer a central question.
2. **Limited novelty**: The use of denoising pre‑training for geometric GNNs was already introduced by Zaidi et al. (2022). No new methodology is proposed (it is unclear whether “token mixing” is a new contribution).
3. **Zero‑shot claim disputed**: Multiple reviewers argue that the so‑called “zero‑shot transfer” is more accurately described as downstream fine‑tuning, because a separate network is trained with the VAMP objective. Clarification is needed (e.g., why the VAMP task is considered zero‑shot).
4. **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**:
   - For molecular dynamics, comparison with Timewarp is missing.
   - For QM9 and xxMD, comparisons with standard architectures such as E(n)‑GNN, SE(3)‑Transformers, DimeNet++, MACE, SchNet, DimeNet, GemNet, Equiformer, and MAC are absent.
   - Standard QM9 results from prior work are not included for comparison.
5. **Pre‑training only uses coordinate denoising**: Other pre‑training strategies that combine node‑mask prediction with coordinate denoising have been shown effective but are not considered. The potential impact of adding a node‑mask objective on generalization across conformations and on scaling behavior is not explored.
6. **Limited evaluations to specific molecules**: Datasets (QM9, xxMD) are restricted to small sets of molecules. QM9 in particular (B3LYP) is not informative; newer benchmarks like POLARIS or subsets of the Therapeutic Data Commons are suggested.
7. **No clear recommendations for practitioners**: Given the stated aim of “pushing the limits” and inspiring new training strategies, the paper does not provide a concise, actionable summary of its empirical findings.
8. **Paper structure confusing**: Chapters 4–6 present experimental results, but the setup and reasoning for each experiment are not well motivated, making the paper hard to follow.
9. **Missing ablation on fine‑tuning vs. scratch with model depth**: Section 6.1 compares models trained from scratch vs. fine‑tuned, but only for varying model width; depth variation is omitted despite previous discussions of depth effects in pre‑training.
10. **Presentation issues**:
    - “Token mixing” is used heavily but never defined.
    - In Figure 6 (model width), the legend should indicate the width for each point; in Figures 4 and 7 (model depth), the legend should specify the exact number of layers.
    - Grammatical errors (e.g., “In silico molecular computation and simulation are indispensable tool in…”).
11. **Questions on experimental setup**:
    - Why does Appendix D show a discrepancy between datasets used for pre‑training and downstream tasks? According to Table 2, QM9 appears used for both, while Table 3 seems to involve different datasets. Are models in Table 3 not fine‑tuned?
    - Do the specific evaluations on bespoke datasets (e.g., xxMD) generalize to broader molecular domains? Additional evidence linking these evaluations to universal scaling/downstream relationships is needed.
