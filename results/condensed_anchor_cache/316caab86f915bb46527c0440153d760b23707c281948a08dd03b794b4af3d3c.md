## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper addresses the problem of augmenting Gaussian process (GP) regression training data with synthetic data from a simulator that may be misspecified. It proposes using the log marginal likelihood as a criterion to decide which simulated points to accept, and introduces a faster computation via rank‑one Cholesky updates to overcome the O(N³) cost. Experiments on synthetic and public datasets demonstrate MSE improvements relative to basic baselines.

Reviewer scores are 3, 6, 3, 3. One reviewer (R2, score 6, confidence 2) is substantially more positive, finding the paper conceptually well‑motivated and the experiments thorough. The other three reviewers (R1, confidence 4; R3, confidence 5; R4, confidence 4) rate it as a weak reject, citing major weaknesses in motivation, literature coverage, writing quality, and baseline comparisons.

### Strengths

- The problem of combining limited true data with more easily obtained simulator data is interesting and has industrial use‑cases (R1).
- Using log marginal likelihood as the selection criterion is an appropriate, theoretically grounded choice (R1, R2).
- The effort to reduce the infeasible time complexity of standard Cholesky decomposition via rank‑one updates is a valuable contribution (R1, R4: “the authors thought carefully about the computational cost of their method and how to reduce this where possible”).
- The paper is generally easy to understand and conceptually well‑motivated (R2). Experiments are methodologically thorough: more than 10 iterations would be nicer, but the characterisation of computational costs is appreciated (R2). The treatment of misspecification in experiments is clear and realistic (R2).
- The paper connects with anomaly detection and sparse GP regression (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Weak motivation and lack of literature connections.** Multiple reviewers note the absence of connections to multi‑fidelity modelling (Kennedy & O’Hagan 2000, Le Gratiet & Cannamela 2015, Peherstorfer et al. 2018), active learning (R1), multi‑task/transfer learning (Bonilla et al. 2008, Perdikaris et al. 2017), and other relevant frameworks. R3 states: “the model mixes both fidelity levels, which is not appropriate.” R4 adds: “the absence of discussion and thorough comparison with these baselines makes the paper severely flawed.” The literature review is largely pre‑2021; only the `pvlib` citation is more recent (R1). The contributions appear incremental (R1).

- **Poor writing and presentation.** The paper is very poorly written (R1, R4). The introduction feels like disjoint sentences (R4). There is no proper background section on GPs to set notation (R4). Many typos and inconsistencies exist (R1): “SoD” acronym appears after not being mentioned for several pages; “Plane GP” is used—likely “Plain GP” (R2, R3, R4); “lax assumptions” should be “weak assumptions” (R2). Methodology sections (3 and 4) are overly verbose and unclear; R4 recommends using lemmas/propositions to hide unnecessary derivations. Presentation quality does not meet the ICLR standard (R1).

- **Mathematical notation errors and inaccuracies.** In Section 4, the notation `K_{m+1*}^{-1} + sigma^2I` is used at least four times when `(K_{m+1*} + sigma^2I)^{-1}` is intended—a serious but likely notational mistake (R2). There is confusion between random variables and draws from random variables in lines 126–133 (R4). Footnote notation “(x,y)2” is confusing (R2). Some grammar issues: “Contributions of this research are 2 folds. First, we proposed…” (R2). In Appendix C, “lengthscale” misspelled (R2).

- **Sparse GP descriptions are inaccurate.** The paper states that sparse GPs “generate a small number of pseudo‑training data points”; in fact, sparse GPs use the full training data and project through inducing points (R2). Similarly, Appendix A.2 misleadingly says marginal likelihood is not computable; actually it is too expensive, so a variational lower bound is used (R2).

- **Missing and inadequate baselines.** The paper does not compare to obvious approaches such as multi‑fidelity GP, multi‑task GP, or transfer learning (R3, R4). The experiments section provides very little detail on competitors (R4). Figures lack clear captions and discussion (R4). The ablation study (Appendix E) is a good idea but Figure 5 is low quality (R2).

- **Experimental design and implementation issues.**  
  *Termination condition:* “accept until half of all simulator data” is arbitrary; no justification is given (R2).  
  *Hyperparameter re‑learning:* After Algorithm 1, hyperparameters are not re‑learned; Bayesian optimisation typically re‑learns every few acquisition steps (R2).  
  *Metric choice:* MSE is the only metric used; it may be too closely related to logML. Other metrics would be interesting (R2).  
  *Numerical error:* Iterative rank‑one updates can accumulate numerical error; the paper does not discuss or correct for this (R2, referencing Rasmussen & Williams Algorithm 3.5).  
  *Computational cost measurement:* Table 4 shows time but does not confirm it matches big‑O predictions; a log‑transformed y‑axis in Figure 4 would be clearer (R2).  
  *Model specification:* The paper assumes simulator data can be misspecified, but the GP itself may also be misspecified (kernel choice, smoothness, noise). Could the log‑marginal likelihood still assess simulator quality if the GP is misspecified? (R2)  
  *Simulator training:* No details on how the simulator is trained or whether it uses data independent of the GP (R2).  
  *Threshold tuning:* If real observed data is very scarce, should the acceptance threshold be lowered or raised? (R2).  
  *Computational cost of the simulator:* If the simulator is expensive, would the threshold be lowered to accept more points? (R2).  
  *Data dominance:* Could accepted simulated data dominate the real data and cause the inclusion criteria to deteriorate? (R2).

- **Presentation issues.** Mysterious white space at the top of page 10 (R2). Figure 5 should be improved (R2). Equations in Section 4 need restructuring and concise presentation (R4).
