## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper argues that LLMs can accurately perform arithmetic without external tools, challenging prior assumptions. The authors train a 2B-parameter model (MathGLM, fine-tuned from GLM-10B) on a new arithmetic dataset and a step-by-step version of the Ape210K Chinese math word problem dataset. MathGLM achieves ~100% accuracy on multi-digit arithmetic (vs. 4.3% for GPT-4) and comparable performance to GPT-4 on the Chinese MWP test set. Evaluation uses arithmetic tasks and the Ape210K/K12 Chinese datasets; scaling experiments and analyses of difficulty levels and error distributions are included.

### Strengths
- Investigates an important and timely research area: whether LLMs can perform accurate arithmetic without tools, addressing a core challenge of LLM math ability (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The paper is generally clear and well-written, with theory and results stated clearly (R1, R3).
- Construction of a large arithmetic dataset covering diverse operations and formats, and a refined Ape210K dataset with step-by-step solutions, are interesting contributions that would be even more useful if made publicly available (R3, R4).
- The step-by-step training strategy and curriculum learning are creative approaches that help the model learn arithmetic operations (R4, R2).
- Extensive experiments demonstrate clear accuracy gains over GPT-4, ChatGPT, and other LLMs, with ablation and scaling experiments providing useful insights (R2, R4).
- Analysis of problem difficulty (e.g., grade levels) and error distribution is a valuable addition (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and tool usage**: The paper does not adequately justify why an LLM, with its inherent precision limitations and high computational cost, is a suitable choice for tasks where standard tools achieve 100% accuracy. The claimed high accuracy (93.03% on complex computations) still falls short of exact tools, and the motivation for using LLMs over calculators is weak (R1). (Contrast: some reviewers view the specialization as novel and well-motivated (R4).)
- **Limited operation scope**: Evaluation covers only addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation. The method likely does not generalize to more complex functions (e.g., log, sin, integrals). The paper lacks discussion of these limitations for real-world math (R1). Mathematics requires exactness, making LLM-based calculation a questionable strategy (R1).
- **Language and dataset bias**: The primary word problem datasets (Ape210K, K12) are Chinese. No experiments on widely-used English datasets like MATH or GSM8K. Since GPT-4 is primarily English-trained and MathGLM is fine-tuned on Chinese, the comparison may be invalid due to language bias. The paper does not address this (R1). Cross-lingual experiments on English math datasets are needed to assess language-agnostic reasoning (R4).
- **Generalization and memorization concerns**: The advantage in reported benchmarks appears to stem from the larger training set rather than architectural innovations. Training and testing setups differ from similar work, making comparison challenging (R3). The claim of “profound understanding” is unsupported because generalization is not properly tested. Adding training samples from the testing range (e.g., 50k 12-digit records) does not prove generalization; it shows memorization from extended training (R3, citing arxiv.org/abs/2306.15400). Out-of-distribution tests on longer numbers and more operands are needed to distinguish memorization from true arithmetic ability (R3, citing arxiv.org/abs/2207.02536). Some reviewers note the model's performance on arithmetic tasks is strong, but they also request out-of-distribution evaluation (R4).
- **Impact on general capabilities**: Specializing for arithmetic may compromise performance on other tasks. Broader evaluation on non-math tasks (general language understanding) and broader math reasoning (symbolic manipulation, logical deduction, geometric reasoning) is needed to characterize tradeoffs (R2, R4).
- **Methodological clarity**:
  - The step-by-step strategy is not an architectural innovation; it is a property of the solution format. The paper should clarify how the architecture actually implements step-by-step reasoning (R3).
  - Curriculum learning benefits are not demonstrated: no comparison with a non-curriculum baseline (R3). The reasoning behind curriculum learning's benefits is not fully fleshed out; analysis of impact on learning trajectory (e.g., which problem types benefit at each stage) is missing (R4).
  - The reconstruction of Ape210K is not fully described: were step-by-step solutions generated automatically, and how was quality verified? (R3)
  - Why are separate models trained for Arithmetic and Math Word Problems? The training strategy section is unclear; the paper aims to advance both aspects simultaneously but seems to train distinct models (R3).
  - How were GPT models tested? The prompting methods (e.g., chain-of-thought) used to probe GPT models should be explained, and performance with advanced prompting strategies should be reported (R3).
  - The title is misleading: it suggests GPT-family models achieve the best accuracy, while the tuned model is from the GLM family. “LLMs” would be more appropriate (R3).
  - “GLM” is not defined in the introduction; include acronym and reference (R3).
  - The manuscript content is often redundant; duplicate or similar sentences should be removed (R3).
- **Need for more analysis**: Visualizations and examples of the step-by-step generation process (including both successful and unsuccessful cases) would help understand MathGLM’s learned skills and failure modes (R4). An analysis of errors made by MathGLM is also requested to guide future improvements (R4).
- **Reproducibility**: No Reproducibility Statement or pointer to source code is provided, making it difficult to replicate experiments (R3).
