- Decision: Reject

## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers across the board acknowledge the clarity of the proposed idea—adjusting layer-wise learning rates inversely to gradient magnitude at initialization—and its potential importance. However, the majority of reviewers (three out of four, scores 3) are critical, citing insufficient justification of algorithmic choices, missing ablations and comparisons, limited theoretical analysis, and lack of clarity in writing and related work. One reviewer (score 6) is more positive, emphasizing the method's simplicity and the importance of the problem, though they also note scaling concerns and the need for comparisons with layer-wise optimizers like LARS/LAMB.

### Strengths

- **Problem importance**: The paper addresses an important problem; layer-wise learning rate initialization is sensitive and can significantly impact training (Reviewer 1). Speeding up SGD with a simple learning rate initialization could be a cost-effective alternative to adaptive optimization algorithms (Reviewer 4).
- **Simplicity and clarity**: The method is easy to understand (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The heuristic (inverse proportionality to gradient norm) is new to the best of one reviewer's knowledge (Reviewer 3).
- **Encouraging experimental results**: The experiments on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-1k with convolutional and transformer architectures show improved accuracy and training stability (Reviewers 2, 3). The results are convincing, removing fluctuations and improving accuracy (Reviewer 2). The experimental results are very encouraging (Reviewer 3).
- **Motivation**: The introduction of Section 3 provides a good justification for why a well-justified heuristic for layer-wise learning rates computed before training is worth studying (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Unclear algorithmic choices in Algorithm 1** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4):
   - The choice of the $\mathcal{L}^1$-norm over the gradient (line 8) is not justified; an $\mathcal{L}^2$-norm would be more standard and directly relates to gradient magnitude. The choice seems arbitrary without theoretical justification (Reviewer 3).
   - The use of the inverse square root (line 10: $\eta_i = \eta \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{G_i}}$) is arbitrary; why not inverse of $G_i$ or another function? No reasoning is provided for this specific functional form (Reviewer 3).
   - The normalization computation (lines 11–13) is not explained; the rationale behind this specific normalization and its effect on performance is unclear (Reviewer 3).
   - There is a typo in line 4: "$n$" should be "$t$" (Reviewer 3).
   - The gradient estimates $G_i$ are incremented $T$ times but are not normalized by $T$ or any other quantity depending on $T$. Two questions arise: how do we choose $T$? Should we normalize the $G_i$ somewhere? (Reviewer 3).

2. **Missing ablation on hyperparameter $T$** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4):
   - The choice of $T$ (number of batches used to estimate gradient magnitude in Algorithm 1) is not ablated. Without an ablation study, it is impossible to determine sensitivity to this parameter, whether the chosen value is optimal, or if the algorithm's robustness is undermined (Reviewer 2).
   - Reviewer 4 asks: How important is the choice for $T$ and does this relate in some way to learning rate warmup?
   - Reviewer 3 questions how $T$ is chosen and whether $G_i$ should be normalized by $T$.

3. **Insufficient comparisons and baselines** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4):
   - The paper compares only to SGD and AdamW, not to existing layer-wise optimization methods such as LARS and LAMB. Since LARS/LAMB also adjust learning rates per layer and could further improve performance on top of SGD/AdamW, these comparisons are needed to isolate the contribution of the proposed method (Reviewer 1).
   - Hyperparameters (most notably the learning rate) should be tuned individually for each algorithm to provide a fair method comparison (Reviewer 4).
   - Reviewer 2 notes that ResNet-50 and SGD are no longer mainstream; the impact on different model structures (e.g., Swin-T, ConvNeXt-T) should be analyzed.

4. **Limited novelty and theoretical analysis** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4):
   - The method appears to be an incremental improvement over existing layer-wise learning rate adjustment techniques; more theoretical analysis is needed to justify the approach and demonstrate its unique contribution beyond empirical observations (Reviewer 2).
   - Algorithm 1 closely resembles running an adaptive optimizer (e.g., Adagrad) with a learning rate of zero for a number of mini-batches. This connection is completely ignored (Reviewer 4).
   - The paper fails to mention its relation to Adagrad (Duchi et al., 2011) or other adaptive methods, which is especially relevant because Adagrad can be interpreted as dividing the learning rate by a running average of the norm (Reviewer 4).
   - Historical context: layer-wise learning rates were probably addressed in the first edition of tricks of the trade (1998) and were likely the only way to make deep networks trainable; the main advantage of adaptive optimizers has always been that the painful process of finding learning rates for each layer is no longer necessary. This context is missing (Reviewer 4).
   - Reviewer 3 asks for at least a short analysis in simple cases or extreme cases (e.g., layer size tending to infinity). How do the computed learning rates compare to those implied by Neural Tangent Kernel scaling (Jacot et al., 2018)? No such analysis is provided.

5. **Clarity and writing issues** (Reviewer 2):
   - There are typos and confusing statements, e.g., "Figure 7: Train loss for 2-layer MLP trained on CIFAR-10 trained." and Section 2: "Another direction previous works indirectly modified the learning rate is through the use of scale factors..." The writing needs thorough revision for clarity and accuracy. The example for Figure 7 seems to describe a different experiment, and the Section 2 statement lacks specific examples of the 'scale factors' referenced (Reviewer 2).

6. **Scaling and generalizability concerns** (Reviewers 1, 4):
   - The method's performance on very large models (e.g., language models) is not demonstrated. It is unclear whether the method can be a general technique applicable beyond vision tasks (Reviewer 1).
   - The experimental setup is too complex (large models, learning rate schedules) to properly evaluate the merits of the proposed method. Simple experiments with error bars (cf. Kingma et al., 2015) would be beneficial to establish statistical significance (Reviewer 4).

7. **Training cost** (Reviewer 1):
   - Algorithm 1 requires extra steps to estimate gradients ($T$ steps). The computational overhead (e.g., time cost) is not discussed. Additionally, if those $T$ steps were added to baselines (SGD and AdamW), would their performance improve? (Reviewer 1)

8. **Inconsistent performance on modern architectures** (Reviewer 2):
   - The layer-wise scheme does not perform well on Swin-T and ConvNeXt-T when using AdamW. What is the impact of the proposed algorithm on different model structures and modules? (Reviewer 2)

9. **Figure 6 appears contradictory** (Reviewer 2):
   - Figure 6 is not intuitive and even suggests that the single-way method is better than the proposed method in some cases (Reviewer 2).

10. **Missing investigation of schedule effects and hyperparameter sensitivity** (Reviewers 3, 4):
    - Reviewer 3 asks: Are the results consistent when we change the learning rate? Does the proposed method perform better than the single learning rate method in any circumstance?
    - Reviewer 4 asks: How much does the learning rate schedule affect the performance of the proposed method?
    - Reviewer 4 asks: Is it possible to include a run where Adagrad iterates the data for one epoch with learning rate zero, and how does that compare to the baseline and the proposed method? (This is a missing comparison that would clarify the relationship to adaptive optimizers.)
