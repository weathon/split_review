## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SePer, a metric that quantifies retrieval utility in RAG by measuring the reduction in semantic perplexity of the LLM after providing a retrieved document. The method uses entailment-based semantic clustering and Monte Carlo sampling to estimate belief distributions. Evaluations show SePer correlates well with human annotations and can assess the impact of factors like model size, number of documents, prompt compression, and reranking. However, reviewers raise concerns about metric validation, reliance on entailment models, missing ablations, and whether SePer truly measures information gain rather than answer quality.

### Strengths
- The motivation is well-justified: using information gain/shifts in LLM knowledge distributions to evaluate retrieval utility in RAG, building on an old IR idea rarely used in recent work.
- The introduction of SePer is considered necessary and innovative, filling a gap between joint RAG evaluation and traditional IR metrics.
- The method is well-formulated and clearly written.
- Experiments demonstrate that SePer aligns closely with human preferences and provides a more precise and efficient evaluation across diverse RAG scenarios.
- The metric shows high correlation with human annotations, which is valuable for content evaluation.
- The work represents interesting new relevance estimation moving beyond classical IR notions.
- Demonstration that SePer can be a reasonable measure of document utility is interesting.
- Theoretical analysis supports SePer's accuracy, fine-grainedness, and generalizability.

### Weaknesses
- Metric validation is insufficient: the paper lacks thorough construct, content, convergent, and discriminant validity, as well as reliability and sensitivity analysis (reviewer 2 cites measurement theory literature: Jacobs & Wallach 2021, Wagner et al. 2021, Xiao et al. 2023). Section 4.1 provides some evidence but the remaining experiments in Section 5 are informal and difficult to connect.
- A minority reviewer (score 5) argues that the experimental baselines evaluate semantic similarity, not information gain, so comparing SePer to them does not demonstrate superiority in measuring knowledge gain. This reviewer notes that answers with high certainty but poor information (e.g., "I don't know") can still achieve high metric scores, suggesting SePer may reflect answer quality rather than information gain.
- The design relies heavily on an entailment model and semantic clustering with threshold τ. No analysis is provided on how different entailment models or threshold values affect SePer scores. Sensitivity analysis for τ and guidance on choosing it for different tasks/datasets is missing.
- The paper does not include concrete semantic analysis across different query types (e.g., factual, comparative, definitional) or document types (e.g., short snippets, long articles, structured data). It is unclear if SePer performs equally for complex reasoning vs. simple facts or how document length/complexity affects it.
- The assumption that retrieval utility is uniformly distributed across different reasoning steps may not hold universally, potentially oversimplifying complex retrieval interactions.
- Extensive Monte Carlo sampling and semantic space clustering may not be feasible for applications with limited resources. No cost or runtime analysis is provided despite the claim of efficiency.
- Important implementation details are missing: how reranking is performed is unclear (only a single vague sentence), and the prompt compression method needs more detail.
- Presentation issues: many figures and tables are not well explained (e.g., Table 1 numbers are unclear; no clear definition of SePer reduction is given; Equation 9 is not clearly connected to the concept). Typos exist (e.g., "a efficient" → "an efficient", "It's high correlation score" → "Its high correlation scores", "results are shown in 3" → "Figure 3").
- The paper does not design a method to directly use SePer in RAG (e.g., to select retrieved documents based on SePer), which would be a more direct evaluation of its utility. Relatedly, it is unclear whether SePer correlates with final answer quality when documents with higher SePer are used for generation.
- Missing acknowledgment of similarity to Farquhar et al. (2024).
- Related work in information retrieval is dismissed; the paper should discuss cognitive process research such as Belkin's Anomalous States of Knowledge (ASK) theory.
- Writing can be improved: Section 3.2's Properties lack justification or citation, and the research questions in Section 5 are introduced without proper motivation.
