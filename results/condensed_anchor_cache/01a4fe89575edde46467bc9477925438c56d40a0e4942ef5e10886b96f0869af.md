## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes NfgTransformer, a transformer-based architecture for representing normal-form games (NFGs) by encoding actions and payoffs with permutation equivariance. It is evaluated on tasks including equilibrium solving (minimizing NE gap), deviation gain regression, ranking, and payoff table completion. Reviews highlight the novelty of learning game representations and the use of equivariance, but also raise concerns about unclear motivation, insufficient technical novelty beyond using a standard transformer, toy experiments, lack of theoretical guarantees, and missing training details.

### Strengths
- Empirical results outperform baselines in several toy benchmarks (R1).
- Originality: no known prior work doing this; the task of learning game representations is novel (R2, R4).
- Idea of using equivariant networks to capture inherent symmetry in game theory is impressive, timely, and promising (R3).
- Using transformer architecture for this purpose is reasonable (R3).
- Acquired embeddings have potential to enhance downstream applications and are valuable in empirical game theory (R4).
- Attention mechanism offers interpretability; case studies in Figure 5 are particularly insightful (R4).
- Scalability: parameters remain independent of the number of actions (R4).
- Experiments cover a wide range of game-theoretic applications (R3).

### Weaknesses
- Presentation is poor or incomplete across multiple reviews (R1, R2, R4).
  - The main goal is hard to parse; the paper does not clearly explain why a game encoder is needed versus using classical methods directly (R1, R2). More concrete examples (e.g., ranking in Go/Chess) are needed (R1).
  - Figure 1 and Figure 2 are not self-contained; annotations (light vs. bold colors, token lengths labeled “T”) are confusing (R1). Meaning of arrows pointing to the same block is unclear (R2).
  - Internal links are broken (pointers to Fig. 5, Proposition 3.2, Sec. 5.1, Sec. 4.4, and references) (R1).
  - The first sentence of the introduction is misleading: it cites only DeepMind papers (Silver et al., Lanctot et al., Vinyals et al., Omidshafiei et al., Liu et al., Perolat et al.) and omits the most relevant non-DeepMind successes (poker: DeepStack/Libratus/Pluribus/ReBeL; No-Press Diplomacy: Diplodocus) (R2).
  - Methodology description is insufficient: it is not clear what the architecture is trained to do, what the downstream use case is, or how the training works (R2). Section 4.2 describes three attention mechanisms too briefly; more mathematical expressions are needed (R4). The name “player-to-coplayer self-attention” is misleading; “joint-action-to-player self-attention” would be better (R4).
  - In Section 5.1.1, how is the NE gap optimization performed? How is a Nash equilibrium strategy recovered from the action embeddings? These central questions are unanswered (R2). No justification is given for why one should solve NFGs with this architecture instead of a classical method (R2).
  - In Section 5.1.2, how deviation gain regression is done is not obvious, and there is no discussion of why an architecture is needed when this task simply requires matrix multiplications (R2).
  - In Section 5.2 payoff table completion, training details (e.g., how DISC games are generated, how the model is trained) are omitted (R2, R4).
  - Figure 3 results are hard to discern; a table would be clearer (R4).
- Technical novelty is limited: the permutation equivariance claimed as the main contribution is a direct consequence of the transformer architecture, not a novel design (R1, R3). The application to NFGs is interesting but does not introduce significant technical novelty (R1). It is not clear how NfgTransformer differs from a vanilla transformer, which is already permutation-equivariant (R3).
- Theoretical guarantees are lacking: the claim that permutation equivariance is “inherent” in normal-form games (in terms of both actions and players) is not obvious and needs proof (R3). The paper provides no theoretical guarantees on achieving the desired equivariance, convergence to Nash equilibrium, convergence speed, generalizability, or any other theoretical advances (R3).
- Experiments are conducted on toy games; the method should be validated on real-world games such as Go or Chess (R1). A few applications cannot justify claims for general game-theoretic tasks (R3). The experiments lack discussion of why one should prefer the learned representation over classical methods (R2).
- Reviewer disagreement: Reviewers 2 and 4 found the task novel and the approach promising (ratings 8), while Reviewers 1 and 3 considered the technical contribution low and the problem motivation unclear (ratings 3 and 5). One reviewer who rated the overall score 8 nonetheless gave soundness=2, presentation=1, contribution=1 (R2).
