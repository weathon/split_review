- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 5, 5, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper hypothesizes that adversarial perturbations contain human-identifiable features and proposes a method to extract them by averaging perturbations from many (270) models with added Gaussian noise. The authors identify two effects: a masking effect in untargeted attacks that obscures original-class features, and a generation effect in targeted attacks that creates target-class features. They also claim that these features relate to transferability, model interpretability, and other adversarial phenomena. The five reviewers are deeply split. Three found the topic interesting and the visualizations promising, with large-scale evaluation (5 attacks, 270 models) seen as a strength. One reviewer (R5) gave the lowest possible score despite praising the clarity and novelty, citing insufficient depth per experiment and missing baselines. One reviewer (R4) gave a score of 3 with high confidence and argued the findings directly contradict a well-studied line of work (perceptually aligned gradients, PAG) and that the observed phenomena are either already known or directly deducible from prior work, without providing the necessary theoretical or empirical evidence to overturn existing theories.

### Strengths

- **Interesting and timely problem.** The underlying mechanisms of adversarial perturbations are an important research question.
- **Large-scale evaluation.** 270 models, 5 attack algorithms (BIM, PGD, CW, DF, SPSA), 3 datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet). This breadth is seen by most reviewers as solid and reliable.
- **Simple yet reasonable method.** Averaging across many models and noise samples is a straightforward way to reveal features.
- **Promising qualitative results.** Denoised perturbations show clear patterns such as object contours and the masking/generation effect; visualizations are compelling.
- **Human evaluator experiment (BIM MM+G) achieved 80.7% recognition**, supporting the claim that the extracted perturbations are human-identifiable (though only one setting was tested).
- **Identification of contour features as important** and demonstration that perturbations converge to object regions.
- **Clear narrative and good presentation** (R2, R3, R5). The paper connects findings to transferability, adversarial training, and interpretability.
- **Validation across both targeted and untargeted attacks**, plus inclusion of search-based attacks (SPSA).

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient novelty and relation to prior work (R1, R4, R2 after rebuttal).**  
   - Adversarial perturbations containing meaningful features is not a new finding; related works [1,2] have studied “robust” features.  
   - The two proposed obstacles (excessive gradient noise, incomplete features) are not clearly defined. Especially “incomplete learned features” lacks evidence and concrete explanation (R1, R5).  
   - The method (noise augmentation + model ensembling) is already widely used in transfer attacks, and prior work has shown that more transferable perturbations contain more “robust” features (R1).  
   - Averaging gradients with Gaussian noise (randomized smoothing) on a single model is already known to produce PAG [4] (R4).  
   - Averaging across models is expected to enhance robustness slightly [5,6] and thus lead to PAG (R4).  
   - Variation of non-robust features across architectures [7] explains why averaging converges to robust features (R4).  
   - The paper does not provide theoretical analysis to overturn existing PAG theories [8-12] that restrict human-identifiable features to robust models (R4).  
   - The explanation of transferability (Section 7(1)) contradicts works [15,16] showing that non-robust features play a crucial role in transfer (R4).  
   - The explanation of non-trivial accuracy on the manipulated dataset (Section 7(3)) is flawed: original-class features dominate, so the model would still learn them, contradicting the paper’s interpretation (R4).  
   - The paper does not sufficiently explore the *underlying reasons* for why these features are human-identifiable; it only observes the phenomenon (R2).

2. **Definition and validation of “human-identifiable” is inadequate (R2, R3, R5).**  
   - Current experiments only demonstrate that perturbations are “model-identifiable” (classifiable by a model), not directly human-recognizable. A rigorous human subject study with diverse participants is required (R2).  
   - The human evaluator test was conducted only for BIM MM+G; no BIM SM (single-model) baseline is provided, making it difficult to interpret the 80.7% rate. Justification for choosing BIM is missing (R5).  
   - The claim that perturbations from different models contain distinct human-identifiable features lacks a quantitative measure of distinctness. The independence assumption across models is not justified (R3).

3. **Limited experimental depth despite breadth (R3, R5, R4).**  
   - Only 20 fixed classes out of 1000 are used, with only 10 samples per class (200 total). Justification for class/input selection is missing; representativeness is questionable (R3, R5).  
   - Attacked attacks used (BIM, CW, DF) are outdated (five years old). More recent gradient-based attacks (momentum-based MI-FGSM, DI-FGSM, ensemble attacker CWA) should be included to test generalizability (R3, R4).  
   - For transferability claims, SOTA transfer attacks (MI-FGSM, DI-FGSM, CWA) are needed; only BIM, CW, DF are compared (R4).  
   - Ablation studies are lacking: effect of number of models, number of noise samples, and comparison of settings MM, MM+G, SM+G are needed to understand how features emerge (R3, R4).  
   - Claim that perturbations from different attacks are similar requires quantitative measures (cosine similarity, SSIM) for full perturbation and per-region (object contour vs. background) (R3).  
   - SM settings should be included in cosine similarity experiments to provide a baseline (R5).  
   - It is unclear whether presented perturbations come from successful attacks; behavior for unsuccessful attacks should be analyzed (R3).

4. **Insufficient analysis of masking and generation effects (R1, R2, R3).**  
   - The analysis is superficial; more visualizations and quantitative analysis for targeted attacks are needed. The paper contains only a short paragraph on this scenario (R2).  
   - More results on the generation effect in targeted attacks are requested (R1).

5. **The role of Gaussian noise vs. ensembling is not disentangled (R4).**  
   - Appendix A Figure 7 shows that without Gaussian noise the masking effect is much weaker, suggesting ensembling noise is more critical than ensembling models. This undermines the claim that human-identifiable features are inherent in perturbations rather than a result of added noise.

6. **Assumptions and theoretical support are weak (R3, R4).**  
   - The assumption that noise across models is independent is not justified; gradients may correlate across similar architectures or training data (R3).  
   - The paper challenges the well-established PAG theory without sufficient theoretical evidence or addressing contradictory theories (R4).

7. **Reproducibility concerns (R4).**  
   - No statement on open sourcing; finding 270 surrogate models is difficult to reproduce.

8. **Minor issues (R5).**  
   - More citations needed for contour extraction claims (e.g., that humans use those image portions for classification).  
   - The motivation for adding noise instead of other augmentation is unclear; the “limited number of neural networks” problem is not well explained.  
   - Standard deviation of added noise differs across attack algorithms without justification.  
   - Human evaluator test: reason for discarding lowest/highest accuracy per subset should be clarified.
