## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper extends the maximal-update (μP) scaling strategy (Yang et al., 2022) to arbitrary directed acyclic graph (DAG) architectures. It provides architecture-aware initialization and layer-wise learning rate (LR) scaling rules designed to maintain a constant scale of pre-activation updates across network depth, width, convolutional kernel size, and connectivity patterns. The authors derive formulas for initialization variance (fan‑in scaling with in‑degree, final layer scaled by 1/n²) and for the maximal LR: η* ≃ c (∑_p L_p³)^(–1/2) where L_p counts ReLU layers on path p. Experiments on MLPs and CNNs with complex topologies verify the approach, and application to NAS‑Bench 1.1 shows that the proposed hyperparameter settings alter network rankings, questioning the validity of current NAS benchmark comparisons. The paper aims to create fairer evaluations by ensuring all architectures are better trained.

### Strengths
- **Compelling motivation and significance:** The paper questions the common practice of evaluating architectures without accounting for hyperparameter dependencies, and highlights a key failure of NAS benchmarks where rankings can be changed by better training (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Theoretical contribution:** The derivation of architecture-aware fan‑in initialization and LR scaling based on μP is technically correct and original to some extent (Reviewer 1: “theory appears to be correct”; Reviewer 3: “thorough and well‑supported proofs”). The paper expands μP to DAG topologies, depth, residual skips, long-range connections, kernel sizes, and convolution extensions (Reviewer 4). It establishes a direct link between LR scaling and layer depth for the first time (Reviewer 4).
- **Empirical validation:** Experiments demonstrate the method’s effectiveness on a wide range of architectures, including MLPs and CNNs (Reviewer 1: “works in experiments”; Reviewer 2: “superior performance”; Reviewer 4: “improvements in achievable accuracies across NAS‑Bench”). The method also extends to GeLU neurons (Section E2, Reviewer 4).
- **Impact on NAS benchmarking:** The observation that the proposed settings change architecture rankings is an important critique of existing NAS evaluation practices, prompting a reassessment of progress in the field (Reviewer 4: “prompts questions about the ranking … critical reassessment”). The paper addresses two literature gaps: μP not defined for DAGs and NAS benchmarks being untrustworthy if networks are not well‑trained (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation quality:** The exposition is clear and well‑organized, with effective visual aids (Reviewer 1: “explains the key points better than the original paper”; Reviewer 3: “excellent writing and organization”; Reviewer 4: “clarity of exposition … enhances comprehension”).

### Weaknesses
- **Empirical verification of update scale:** The paper aims to keep update scale O(1) during training but does not empirically measure that scale to confirm the method works as intended (Reviewer 1).
- **Reproducibility and implementation clarity:** The exact initialization and LR scaling are not easy to replicate. Missing pseudocode or reference implementation; the parameter *c* is not specified, and counting the number of paths L_p is unclear (Reviewer 1). The definition of L_p should appear in the main text, not only in the appendix (Reviewer 1).
- **Final‑layer initialization not explained:** The statement that final‑layer weights are initialized with variance 1/n² instead of 1/n is taken from μP but not justified in this paper (Reviewer 1).
- **Weak correlation in Figure 2:** Figure 2 shows a weak correlation with questionable linearity and scattered data points, making the claimed relationship less robust (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited LR range in Figure 3:** The range of learning rates tested in Figure 3 is narrow, making it difficult to assess effectiveness across a broader spectrum (Reviewer 2).
- **Modest improvement in top‑performing architectures:** The proposed method primarily boosts lower‑accuracy (“bad”) architectures, while improvements for top performers are marginal and could be due to random variation (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 4 similarly asks: if the method cannot improve top architectures, why is it relevant for practical NAS, which aims to find the best architecture?
- **Sensitivity to random seeds and statistical significance:** The observed ranking changes might be influenced by random seeds rather than the proposed method. The paper lacks a rigorous sensitivity analysis to seed variation and does not demonstrate that ranking changes are statistically significant (Reviewer 4).
- **Neglect of modern training practices:** The base maximal LR is found for one epoch, which may not be meaningful for training with complex schedules (e.g., cosine annealing, warm restarts). The criticism that NAS uses “the same hyperparameters” overlooks the sophisticated schedules commonly employed (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited engagement with prior art:** The paper insufficiently discusses adaptive optimizers (Adam, RMSprop) and advanced initialization techniques beyond fan‑in/fan‑out; this makes it hard to assess novelty and contribution (Reviewer 2).
- **Failure modes of μP not clarified:** The paper claims to outperform μP but defers those results to Section E.3. It does not adequately explain what specific failure mode of μP (e.g., violation of Desiderata L.1) is addressed by the DAG approach, nor does it clarify assumptions and comparisons across architectural configurations (Reviewer 3). The theory cannot apply to normalization layers, a fact mentioned only late in the paper (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor presentation issues:** Abstract phrase “most designs of principles”; why MSE loss is introduced in Equation (6) when experiments use cross‑entropy; “speed‑up” should be “speeds up” (Reviewer 1). The main body could be clearer by reducing repetitive criticism of architecture search (Reviewer 2).
