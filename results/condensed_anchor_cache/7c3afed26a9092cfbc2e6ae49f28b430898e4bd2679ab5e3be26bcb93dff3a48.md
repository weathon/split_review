## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a multi-level text alignment framework to adapt LLMs for time series forecasting. It decomposes time series into trend, seasonal, and residual components, then reprograms each component into text representations aligned with pre-trained word tokens via component-specific prompts and cross-attention. The aim is to improve both predictive accuracy and interpretability. Experiments across multiple datasets report state-of-the-art performance, particularly in long-term and few-shot forecasting. Two reviewers (scores 5) find the methodology clear and the results compelling; one reviewer (score 3) is more critical of the interpretability claims and potential data leakage.

### Strengths
- The mechanism to align decomposed time series components (trend, seasonal, residual) with component-specific word pools via cross-attention is unique and addresses semantic consistency between textual prototypes and temporal features.
- The method balances prediction accuracy and interpretability by decomposing the series, aligning each component with a different word pool, and predicting them separately.
- The methodology is well-structured, logically presented, and supported by clear diagrams.
- Experiments are extensive, covering multiple benchmark datasets, a range of forecasting horizons (long-term, short-term), and few-shot/zero-shot scenarios. Strong predictive accuracy is consistently demonstrated over conventional and LLM-based baselines.
- The paper tackles a promising and interesting problem: ensuring semantic consistency between LLM text prototypes and time series features (e.g., trends).

### Weaknesses
- **Potential data leakage**: The LOESS decomposition is applied to the entire input series \( X(i) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times L} \). This could allow future information to influence the decomposition of training windows, leading to artificially favorable performance. The authors should clarify how the decomposition is implemented temporally.
- **Interpretability claims are insufficiently supported**: 
  - Only the trend component is shown with highlighted maps and selected words; seasonal and residual components are not similarly visualized or analyzed (noted by two reviewers).
  - The claim of “LLM interpretability” is broad. A term like “TS-text alignment” might be more precise, as the method focuses on aligning time series with text embeddings rather than explaining the LLM’s internal reasoning.
- **Limited LLM evaluation**: Only GPT-2 is used, and only its first 6 layers are employed (not the full model). This raises questions about:
  - Generalization to other architectures (e.g., LLaMA, BERT) and whether the approach truly “adapts” an LLM vs. using a small subset of its parameters.
  - How performance and interpretability vary across different LLM backbones, training objectives, and capacities.
- **Principal ambiguity regarding alignment**: It remains unclear how unconstrained cross-attention between a trend component and a trend-specific word pool ensures that upward-trending patches naturally align with words signifying upward trends (rather than downward). The design choices appear incremental over S2IP-LLM (switching from concat to cross-attention, adding separate word pools, component-specific prompts, separate prediction heads). A more detailed explanation or anonymous code release is needed to clarify the mechanism.
- **Missing experimental rigor**:
  - No error bounds or confidence intervals are reported in Table 1; experiments should be run multiple times with ranges provided.
  - Three questions: Are the component-specific word pools the same across different datasets? Why was GPT-2 chosen (and only its first 6 layers)? How does the partial model use align with leveraging LLMs for interpretability and accuracy?
- **Computational cost not addressed**: The paper does not analyze training/inference overhead or scalability of the multi-level alignment framework.
- **Writing errors**: Missing references to Figure 1 and Table 9 in the main text.
- **Minority (score 3) reviewer emphasizes stronger doubts**: The interpretability analysis is considered too narrow (only trend highlights), the risk of data leakage is serious, and trying only one LLM backbone (GPT-2) is insufficient to validate the approach’s generalizability.
