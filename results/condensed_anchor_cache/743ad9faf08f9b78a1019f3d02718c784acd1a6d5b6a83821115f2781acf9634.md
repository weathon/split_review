## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates Theory of Mind (ToM) representations in neural networks (MLP, CNN, CNN-LSTM) trained on a simplified ToM task (Stand-off). The authors define behavioral features (no-mindreading, low-mindreading, high-mindreading) and use a decoding analysis (Intrinsic Feature Representation score, IFR) to measure whether these features are encoded in model activations. The results are mixed, with the CNN-LSTM showing some advantages. Reviewers find the topic interesting but raise major concerns about weak architectures, oversimplified task, lack of novelty in the probing approach, poor experimental detail, and unclear writing. One reviewer’s comments are about a self-awareness task in language models, which appears mismatched to this paper; those points are included as given. Overall, reviewers question whether the models learn genuine ToM or just surface correlations, and whether the analysis provides meaningful insight.

### Strengths
* The problem is interesting and well-motivated. Theory of Mind is a rich cognitive capability, and testing AI on paradigms from psychology is a sensible direction (R1, R3).
* The paper is mostly well written and easy to follow, except for some sections (R2). Methods and results are generally clear (R3).
* The decoding approach offers a straightforward way to measure different properties of model representations (R2). It allows quantifiable insight into feature dependencies (R3).
* Some originality in exploring ToM feature representation in neural networks, an underexplored area (R3).
* The work could have potential significance for advancing computational ToM and understanding social cognition in AI (R3).
* (R4) The paper tackles an interesting and underexplored area (self-awareness in language models—topic mismatch noted). The interdisciplinary approach adds theoretical dimension. Attempt to propose an explainable framework and enhance capabilities through fine-tuning is commendable. The experiments provide an initial attempt.

### Weaknesses
**Weak models and task**
* The set of models is very weak: only MLP, CNN, CNN-LSTM. The main result that CNN-LSTM encodes some features may simply be due to more parameters, not better alignment with ToM (R1). The architectures are highly simplified and trained tabula rasa on a small task, so they likely learn low-level correlations, not genuine ToM (R1, R2). Poor generalization across conditions casts doubt on whether any ToM is learned (R2).
* The task is oversimplified and details are unclear. A schematic of inputs and steps is needed (R1, R2). It is unclear how the player knows if the opponent’s vision was obscured, and whether opponent behavior is deterministic (if so, no ToM required) (R2). The “low-mindreading vs high-mindreading” distinction is introduced arbitrarily without justification (R2). The definitions of no/low/high mindreading appear in Section 4.1 but Figure 1 uses them earlier, causing inconsistency (R4). In stage one training, how can models be confused about the presence of an opponent when there is none? (R2)

**Lack of novelty and weak methodology**
* The feature-based decoding analysis is not novel; similar probing/decoder methods have been used extensively in machine learning and neuroscience for decades (R1, R3).
* The primary metric (IFR) uses MSE instead of cross-entropy for decoding discrete variables, but this choice is not justified (R3).
* The paper claims “Theory of Mind presents a significant generalization challenge in computational modeling”—this sentence is vague (R3). Claim that “LLMs continue to significantly underperform humans on ToM tasks” is incorrect given recent work (e.g., GPT-4 human-level on high-order ToM, arxiv 2405.18870) (R3).
* Statement that “linear models might struggle to find data in long vectors” reflects a misunderstanding of linear model properties (R3).

**Poorly explained and incomplete results**
* Results are described but not explained; little effort to reason about why patterns occur (R2). Significant insights are scarce (R3).
* Definition of “accurately represented” is unclear (R2). In Section 5.1, the claim that a feature not having a negative effect means it is being used is false—positive effect is needed for evidence of use. Figure results appear mixed, and chance level is not established (R2).
* The assumption that extraction model accuracy corresponds to the task model’s ability to use that feature is critical but unvalidated (R4). No experiments confirm this correspondence (R4).
* Clarity issues: Figure 1 has overlapping error bars between outlined and filled bars, making distinction ambiguous (R4). In Figures 1 and 2, Stage 3 training column lacks filled bars for novel tasks—unclear if nonexistent or excluded (R4).

**Missing details and reproducibility**
* Dataset not explicitly mentioned; key training settings (hyperparameters, baseline comparisons) are absent (R4). The “extraction models” introduced in Section 3.2 are not sufficiently described; a flowchart or diagram is needed (R4).
* No quantitative formalism or equations to make the approach reproducible (R4). No statistical analysis (confidence intervals, significance tests) provided for any results (R4).
* The background is overly lengthy and philosophical; core methodological and experimental sections need expansion (R4).

**Writing and presentation issues**
* Numerous citation formatting errors (R2). Repeated words, incomplete citations, undefined acronyms (R3). Ambiguous sentences: e.g., line 119 “if it believes the larger treat is somewhere…” without specifying conditions for the opponent not believing (R4). Transition from background to contributions is abrupt (R4).
* (R4) Several points about self-awareness in language models are mismatched to this ToM paper but included as given: the explainable framework is insufficiently described; no formal metrics; empirical motivation for self-awareness is lacking; fine-tuning benefits not theoretically justified; many questions about self-reflection tasks, generalization, statistical analysis, etc. These should be considered in context of the paper reviewed, but the mismatch is clear.

**Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (R1, R3) give score 1, two (R2, R4) give score 3. R1 and R3 are more negative, questioning the paper’s contribution and novelty strongly; R2 and R4 see some merit but still find major flaws.
