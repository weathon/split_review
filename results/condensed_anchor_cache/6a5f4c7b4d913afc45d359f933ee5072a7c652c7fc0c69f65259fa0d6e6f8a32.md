## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper defines and analyzes discretization mismatch errors (DMEs) in neural operators such as FNO, showing that training at coarse resolutions does not guarantee consistent performance at finer resolutions. It proposes the Cross-Resolution Operator-Learning Pipeline (CROP), which uses band-limited lifting and projection layers to reduce DMEs. Reviewers disagree on novelty and theoretical rigor: two reviewers rate 8 (strong originality, solid analysis, high significance) and two rate 5 (concerns about novelty, theoretical framing, numerical comparisons, and applicability). All acknowledge the importance of the problem and find the empirical results compelling.

### Strengths
- Identifies and provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of discretization mismatch errors in neural operators (particularly FNO), addressing a critical gap in the field (R2, R3).
- Gives a clear formal definition of discretization invariance (from Kovachki et al., 2021) and contrasts it with the colloquial usage (R4).
- Proposes the CROP method, which uses band-limited function spaces and 1×1 convolution lifting/projection layers to effectively mitigate DMEs while allowing flexibility in choosing the intermediate neural operator (R1, R2, R3).
- Mathematical analysis includes Lemma B.1 (bound on input function difference between resolutions) and Lemma B.2 (extension to neural operators with lifting/projection), providing insight into error accumulation through layers (R2, R3). Some reviewers find this solid; others question its strength (see Weaknesses).
- Compelling empirical validation across multiple PDEs, including high‑Reynolds‑number Navier‑Stokes, showing CROP achieves superior performance over classic architectures like FNO (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Well‑written, clearly structured, and carefully composed, with reproducible experimental setups (R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and framing**  
- Previous works (e.g., 2024 review on “discretization convergence”) and empirical demonstrations of FNO’s zero-shot super-resolution performance on smooth problems reduce the novelty of the insight. The primary contributions are the theoretical DME analysis and the CROP method (R4). In contrast, R2 and R3 view the work as highly original for clarifying a widespread misconception.

**Theoretical analysis**  
- Proposition 4.4 gives only an upper bound for DME; an upper bound does not guarantee that DME trends follow that bound. Moreover, the bound in Lemma B.2 does not depend on \(M\) (the higher resolution), making the claim that DME increases with \(M\) questionable (R1, R4). The derivation of Lemma B.2 (especially terms \(\omega^L\) and \(\prod C_\ell\)) is straightforward, making the conclusions unsurprising and likely generalizable to other operator models (R1).  
- R4 notes Proposition 4.4 is informal and potentially misleading (e.g., if the operator learns a constant function, DME would not strictly increase).  
- R2 asks for explanation of evidence from [1] (Kovachki et al., 2021) that shows consistent performance across resolutions when training and testing at the same resolution. The paper should address this discrepancy and clarify whether the observed “discretization invariance” in [1] is dataset/task‑specific or a general property.  
- The equation in Definition 4.1: what is \(L\)? (R4)

**CROP design and capabilities**  
- CROP is not inherently discretization‑invariant; it operates on fixed scales chosen a priori. How can it be adapted to handle infinitely fine resolutions? (R1)  
- It is unclear whether high frequencies are adequately captured by the pointwise neural network in the projection layer. L₂ error may not be sufficient; additional spectral evaluation (e.g., plotting frequency spectra as in [1] Figure 1) is recommended (R4). R3 also notes limited exploration of high-frequency information capture compared to super‑resolution methods.  
- High variance in some results, e.g., Figure 3b; an explanation is needed (R3).  
- Minimal discussion of hyperparameter sensitivity, especially the choice of band‑limit in lifting/projection layers. Is there a systematic way to determine optimal band‑limits? (R3)  
- Limited discussion of long‑term stability for time‑dependent problems like Navier‑Stokes (R3).  
- Computational complexity compared to standard FNO for very high‑resolution inputs should be discussed (R3).  
- How does the choice of intermediate neural operator affect performance and efficiency? Are certain architectures preferable for specific problem types? (R3, R4)  
- For high‑Reynolds tasks, using a higher number of modes or an Incremental Fourier Neural Operator would be a fairer baseline; ablation studies on this would strengthen the evaluation (R3).  
- Additional experiments on deeper FNOs with skip connections (to address convergence issues) are suggested (R3).

**Numerical evaluation**  
- Table 2 could include results from training resolutions \(127 \times 128\) or \(256 \times 256\); recent ICLR 2024 papers show more favorable results (R1).  
- Table 4 concerns: the operators (Darcy, Poisson) are linear but the tested neural operators are nonlinear. Moreover, Poisson results (Table 7) do not show a significant advantage of CROP over standard FNO (R1).  
- R4 asks: how much hyperparameter tuning was performed for the baselines, especially for Navier‑Stokes?  
- R3 suggests including pseudocode or the full architecture.

**Minor issues**  
- Claim that “FNO is not limited to rectangular domains, periodic functions, or uniform grids” may be misleading without context (R4).  
- Typo in line 234: “aN FNO” (R4).  
- R4 asks for a reference for the claim that “It is widely believed that training an FNO on one resolution allows inference on another without degrading performance.”
