## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces CTSyn, a diffusion-based foundational model for generating heterogeneous tabular data. It uses a cross-table VAE to embed tables with diverse schemas into a unified latent space, followed by a conditional latent diffusion model (DDPM with classifier-free guidance via a pre-trained LM) for generation, and type-specific decoders. Pre-training on a large collection of diverse tables is shown to improve downstream performance. Experiments on real-world datasets evaluate fidelity, ML utility, and privacy, with comparisons to multiple baselines.

### Strengths

- While prior works have employed autoencoders + latent diffusion for tabular data (e.g., LSGM [1]), prior work on heterogeneous table synthesis has been limited.
- The paper provides good comparisons against baselines across a range of datasets for fidelity, privacy, and ML utility, using strong baselines and large diverse real-world datasets.
- The approach successfully maps categorical variable embeddings back to original space with a simple and effective method, providing a useful baseline.
- The paper is well-written and the proposed technique is detailed clearly.
- Results in column-wise statistical metrics (Table 2) clearly demonstrate the prowess of CTSyn.
- Figure 2 and Table 3 demonstrate CTSyn’s ability to maintain privacy (compared to non-DP synthesizers) while still learning useful representations, balancing accuracy without replicating training data.
- The architecture design combining a cross-table VAE with a conditional diffusion model is novel and effectively maintains data quality and flexibility across different tables.

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns (minority reviewer, score 5):** One reviewer found the approach lacks substantial novelty, being similar to LSGM [1] (VAE+diffusion in latent space) with the main addition being handling of individual variables; the VAE architecture conditioned on metadata/column names does not introduce a fundamentally new approach to latent space learning, making the contribution incremental.
- **Interpretability of latent space:** The common latent space across tables limits the ability to interpret what embeddings mean; this aspect was not studied (distinct from privacy analysis).
- **Scalability:** The use of a pre-trained LM for per-row, per-column-type embeddings raises questions about scalability to enterprise tables with thousands of columns (due to joins, lineage additions). This directly impacts the dimension (Eq 1, Page 4) and may affect representative power when reduced to a low-dimensional space from tables with very different column counts. The diffusion training process also requires costly pre-training and fine-tuning, making scaling to large tables (100s of columns, millions of rows) challenging.
- **Tokenization for abbreviated/cryptic headers:** Many tables have short/abbreviated column headers where the LM tokenizer may suffer; the authors should consider adding a study on this.
- **Lack of clarity on table metadata:** The paper does not describe how meta-data was obtained, what it contains (e.g., table descriptions, source information), or how it is structured/encoded. This affects assessment of generalizability.
- **No evaluation on noisy/imperfect data:** The model is termed a foundation model but robustness to noise during fine-tuning is not investigated—including missing values, incorrect entries, or inconsistent formatting. Such analysis is necessary for real-world applicability.
- **Handling of free text and date-time columns:** CTSyn cannot handle columns with free text (e.g., medical records) or date-time data; these were dropped (line 280). The paper does not explain why date-time was excluded (e.g., inability to handle linearly increasing data).
- **Missing explanation for why synthetic data outperforms real data:** In Table 2a and Figure 2, classifiers trained on CTSyn data achieve higher F1 than those trained on real data. This surprising result lacks a detailed explanation of the experimental settings (e.g., cross-validation specifics, data split, how synthetic data is integrated into training).
- **Insufficient evaluation robustness:**
    - No discriminator test to assess whether a surrogate model can distinguish real vs. synthetic data.
    - No metric like "distance to closest record" for authenticity.
    - ML utility experimental setup lacks transparency: number of folds, data splitting method, and how synthetic data is integrated are not clearly described.
    - No comparison of computational cost (training and generation) against other methods.
- **Ablation study incomplete:**
    - No ablation with only the column name condition applied (requested by reviewer).
    - Need more comprehensive study of each component’s role (cross-table VAE vs. conditional diffusion) and impact of varying layers/hidden units in VAE and diffusion, different loss functions/optimization strategies.
    - Specific ablation request: evaluate performance on missing column values in training/fine-tuning tables.
- **Positional encoding (PE) and column embeddings:** The paper treats column embeddings and PE as substitutes (column order invariance). It is unclear how PE was generated for different tables; could they be designed to encode complementary information not captured by column embeddings/metadata?
- **Choice of quantile transformation for numerical values:** The rationale for using quantile transformation over min-max or standard normalization is not explained. Quantile transformation can improve handling of skewed distributions but may introduce artifacts.
- **Meta embedding e_m excluded from Eq. 1:** The sequence E in Eq. 1 does not include the meta embedding e_m; incorporating it could strengthen alignment between table content and contextual information.
- **Column-wise vs. joint modeling evaluation:** Table 2 shows only column-wise comparisons, which do not assess joint modeling of column relationships, higher-order dependencies, or causal structures. The paper should consider multivariate metrics or metrics for conditional distributions.
- **Reproducibility issue:** The provided code (vae.sh) did not run smoothly; updated .sh script or exact package versions are needed.
- **Limited comparison with LLM-based tabular synthesizers:** The paper’s statement that "synthesizers cannot add information not included in the original training data" does not fully capture recent methods that leverage pre-trained LLMs for transfer learning (e.g., TabTab, LaTable, GReaT). A comparison with such methods is needed to clarify CTSyn’s novelty, especially regarding the claimed limitation of LLMs for continuous values (Line 099). Recent work [1] suggests fine-tuning can improve LLM performance on continuous values.
- **Final parameter size** of the CTSyn model is not reported.
- **Minor typos:** Line 129 ("specific") and line 266 ("The").

**Reviewer confidence:** 3, 4, 4, 5 (one reviewer less confident, others moderate to high).
