## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces BrainMAE, a self-supervised masked autoencoder with static and dynamic graph attention components to learn ROI embeddings from fMRI time-series directly. It is evaluated on several datasets for classification and regression tasks, consistently outperforming existing baselines. Interpretability analysis explores functional relevance, but concerns exist about novelty, experimental rigor, and clarity.

### Strengths

- The approach is original and serves as one of the first applications of masked autoencoding to brain imaging representation learning (R1). (Contrast: R2 considers the components (Transformers, MAE, Dynamic GNNs) individually known and the combination an engineering model without methodological novelty – this disagreement is explicitly noted.)
- BrainMAE consistently and significantly outperforms established baseline models across three distinct downstream tasks, including both classification and regression (the latter often avoided in methodology papers) (R1, R3).
- The paper systematically introduces and evaluates three desirable ROI embedding characteristics (Section 2.1), providing a principled evaluation framework (R3).
- Learned ROI embeddings show good inter-dataset consistency and functional relevance, demonstrating generalizability (R1, R4).
- The interpretability analysis yields insightful findings (e.g., gender captured in the first principal component) that resonate with ongoing neuroscience research (R3). (Contrast: R2 views the PCA and attention-score analysis as post hoc and lacking scientific insight because the model remains a black box – this disagreement is explicitly noted.)
- The paper is well-written, well-structured, and easy to follow (R2, R4).
- Extensive experiments with a good balance of figures and tables (R2, R3).
- The analogy drawn between brain regions and word embeddings in NLP is interesting and novel (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and theoretical depth:** The method is a combination of known techniques (Transformer, MAE, Dynamic GNN) with no new theoretical contributions or guarantees; the approach is heuristic and engineering-driven (R2). (Note that other reviewers find it original – see Strengths. This contrast highlights disagreement.)
- **Interpretability claim is insufficient:** The model lacks scientific inductive bias, and the representation analysis (PCA, attention scores) is post hoc and does not open the black box (R2). The title claims interpretability, but no mechanism truly explains how the model arrives at decisions (R2). (Again, contrast with R3 who finds the interpretability insightful.)
- **Missing baseline comparisons:**
  - Traditional ML models (e.g., SVM, Random Forest) on the flattened upper triangle of the FC matrix are not included. Such simple models can be competitive with deep learning on FC data (R3).
  - Existing self-supervised learning models for fMRI are not compared, e.g., Shi et al. 2023 (Frontiers in Neuroscience), Malkiel et al. 2022 (MIDL), Thomas et al. 2022 (NeurIPS) (R4).
- **Missing evaluation metrics:** For the binary gender prediction task, sensitivity and specificity should be reported in addition to accuracy/AUROC (R3).
- **Window size rationale:** Using 15-second time segments is too short compared to the fMRI literature (typical window 30–60 seconds). The authors do not provide a rationale for this choice (R4, citing Savva et al. 2019, Brain and Behavior).
- **Utility and clarity of dynamic graph attention (DG-TSE/DG-BrainMAE):**
  - SG-BrainMAE consistently outperforms DG-BrainMAE in all tables, making the dynamic graph attention component seem unnecessary (R1). The reason for introducing it is not explained (R1).
  - The term “dynamic” is not clearly defined in the context of graph attention, and the static/dynamic naming overlaps confusingly with the Fix-FC/Dynamic-FC distinction in the paper (R1, R3).
  - There is a potential contradiction: DG-BrainMAE’s lower performance is attributed to inherent noise, yet the ROI embeddings are motivated as a way to mitigate that same noise (R4).
- **ROI embeddings may encode absolute position instead of functional traits:** Since ROI order is fixed during training, the permutation-invariant graph attention mechanism could learn absolute position information. Additional evidence is needed to confirm that embeddings capture ROI characteristics rather than position (R4).
- **Static graph attention after training:** The learned ROI embeddings E are fixed; with fixed queries and keys in the SG-TSE attention, the transformer block becomes redundant (R4).
- **Writing and readability issues:**
  - Notation is disorganized: “node features” (Equation (3)) are described before the input fMRI segment X is formally introduced in Section 2.2 (R1).
  - Figure 2 mentions SG-, DG-, and FDG-BrainMAE, but the text immediately below (“BrainMAE Variants”) only describes two. This visually misleading placement harms readability (R3).
  - The overall flow, while good, has some hard-to-follow parts (R1).
- **Missing details and clarifications:**
  - Why was MAE chosen over other autoencoders (e.g., VAE)? The motivation is unclear (R3).
  - Why are self-loops removed in the graph attention component? This is not standard in GNN literature and was not tested in ablation (R3).
  - How are folds created in the 5-fold cross-validation? Are they stratified separately per task, and are subjects completely separated across train/test? (R3)
  - What is the exact difference between DG- and FDG-BrainMAE? The description suggests FDG removes the static graph mechanism entirely, but then it seems similar to DG – clarification needed (R3).
  - Figure 1 (and Section 2) lacks a mention of the task-specific linear head; the pipeline is only clarified in Section 3.3.1, causing confusion about the pretraining/finetuning divide (R3).
  - What exactly is shown in Figure 3A? (e.g., t-SNE for one subject or mean across subjects?) (R1)
  - Why was task-fMRI from HCP not included, while NSD task-fMRI was included? (R3)
- **Unsupported claims:** Some statements, especially regarding the dynamic graph attention, are not well supported by the evaluations (R1).
