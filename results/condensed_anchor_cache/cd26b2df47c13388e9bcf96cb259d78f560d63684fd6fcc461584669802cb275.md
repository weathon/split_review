## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies that standard bias metrics (e.g., Bias@K) are skewed by spurious correlations between background context and gender in COCO Captions. It proposes a pipeline that uses InstructPix2Pix to generate synthetic, gender-balanced contrast sets (GenSynth, GenSwap) by editing only the gender attribute while fixing the background, followed by automatic filtering based on similarity to real images. Using these balanced sets, the authors benchmark bias in CLIP-based models and argue that existing metrics are invalid due to dataset bias. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the approach is plausible and easy to follow, but they raise major concerns about scope (limited to gender and COCO), technical novelty (simple application of generative models), insufficient handling of image artifacts and new biases introduced by the generative model, lack of comparisons with other editing methods, missing alternative metrics for evaluating bias on the debiased dataset, and limited qualitative analysis. Scores ranged from 3 to 6 (reviewer confidence: 3-4). One reviewer rated the contribution as excellent (4), while two rated it as fair (2), highlighting disagreement over the paper’s novelty and impact.

### Strengths
- Identifies a vital problem: existing model-bias metrics are skewed by dataset bias, giving new insight that could impact the community (R1).
- Shows that background context is a significant source of gender bias via a spurious-correlation classifier (R1).
- Provides a viable framework to generate a balanced dataset without spurious gender–background correlations and releases a dataset under this framework (R1, R3: novel approach for editing an attribute and observing its effect on bias).
- Addresses a crucial issue for the ML research community (R2).
- Methodology presents a plausible solution for mitigating gender bias (R2).
- Using generative models for bias reduction is a promising direction (R2).
- Paper is clear and easy to understand (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope**: Only tested on COCO Captions and a single attribute (gender). No discussion or empirical results on generalizability to other datasets or attributes (e.g., age, race). For attributes where the object is tightly integrated with the background, the editing method may fail (R1, R3).
- **No alternative bias metric proposed**: The paper shows that current metrics are flawed due to dataset bias but does not propose a concrete way to measure model bias on the debiased dataset, leaving a methodological gap (R1).
- **Weak technical novelty**: The strategy is essentially an application of generative models; the core idea of modifying gender attributes is not novel, and the paper lacks deep exploration of challenges in adapting the model for bias mitigation, including why InstructPix2Pix is uniquely suited and what its limitations are (R2).
- **Artifacts in generated images**: Qualitative examples (Appendix Fig. 1) still show obvious artifacts even after filtering. The authors do not adequately address how these artifacts may affect bias measurements (R2, R3).
- **No comparison with other image editing methods**: The paper only uses InstructPix2Pix; it does not compare with other methods that may produce higher-quality contrast sets, nor does it analyze how editing quality impacts observed gender biases (R3).
- **Potential introduction of new biases**: Generative models are themselves biased. Editing changes not only facial features but also skin color and clothing (Fig. 3), and the model seems to underrepresent Asian faces. The paper does not analyze how these new biases propagate into the dataset or attempt to mitigate them (R2).
- **Insufficient qualitative analysis**: The experimental results are predominantly numerical with modest improvements. The paper would benefit from showing typical success and failure cases of bias mitigation, and acknowledging that complete eradication of gender bias is infeasible (R2).
- **Lack of control for other variables**: The InstructPix2Pix instructions only edit gender and do not control for correlated variables such as skin tone or race, which can introduce confounding biases (R3). Similarly, mere gender manipulation may not resolve bias when other contexts (e.g., facial hair, attire) carry stronger gender signals (R2).
- **Missing discussion of differences from prior work**: The paper frequently references Wang et al. 2021a but does not discuss in detail how it differs from that work (R3).
- **Limited discussion of CLIP models**: The experiments heavily rely on CLIP models, yet the paper provides little background on them (R3).
- **Unreported data statistics**: In Section 3.2 the authors note that images may be incorrectly labeled as “undefined” but do not give the percentage of mislabeled images (R3).
- **Appendix content missing from main paper**: Relevant qualitative examples are in the appendix; reviewers would have liked to see them in the main paper (R3).
