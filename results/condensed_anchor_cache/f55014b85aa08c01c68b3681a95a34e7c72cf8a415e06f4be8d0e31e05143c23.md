## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a sensitivity loss regularizer for neural operators, applied to the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) to produce Sensitivity-Constrained FNO (SC-FNO). The regularizer penalizes the derivative of the solution with respect to physical parameters \(\mathbf{p}\) (i.e., \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\)), computed via automatic differentiation. Experiments on two ODEs and two PDEs show improvements in sensitivity accuracy, inverse problem performance, and robustness to concept drift and sparse data, compared to standard FNO and FNO with PINN regularization.

### Strengths
- The sensitivity-aware regularization is a novel and unique approach that directly addresses the critical but often overlooked need for accurate derivative information in surrogate modeling of parametric differential equations (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  
- SC-FNO demonstrably improves the accuracy of \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) and the solution \(\mathbf{u}\) under concept drift / parameter perturbation, and enhances inverse problem (parameter inversion) accuracy, even when standard FNO fails (Reviewer 1).  
- The method is applicable to a wide range of neural operators (e.g., DeepONet as shown in Appendix D.6) and has high research value (Reviewer 3).  
- The paper is clearly written with no redundant mathematical derivations, is easy to follow, and has well-defined motivation and objectives (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
- Experiments cover a diverse set of ODE/PDE problems (five problems), and improvements are robust across different equations and supervision approaches (Reviewer 4).  
- SC-FNO does not incur large computational or memory overhead compared to alternatives (Reviewer 4 – from paper claim; though some reviewers question this – see Weaknesses).  

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concern:** The idea of using a gradient loss to enforce sensitivity is not new (e.g., gradient PINN), and the proposed loss is essentially a supervised loss on \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) which requires additional labeled derivative data compared to the original FNO. If \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) is already available, adding a supervised loss is straightforward; thus the contribution is rather limited (Reviewer 4).  
- **Need for derivative information:** The regularizer requires access to the Jacobian \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) (Eq. 6), either from a differentiable numerical solver, finite differences, or direct supervision. This requirement may be impractical in real-world settings where obtaining accurate derivative information is challenging, and derivative-based techniques are vulnerable to data noise (Reviewer 2). The paper does not analyze the impact of numerical error when approximating \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) from data (Reviewer 4).  
- **Computational and memory overhead:** The additional gradient computation via automatic differentiation (AD) or finite differences (FD) increases runtime and memory. Reviewers 1, 2, and 3 all request explicit discussion of runtime, memory consumption, and model complexity (e.g., number of learnable parameters) in the main text and in comparisons with FNO and FNO-PINN (Tables 1–3). This overhead may become prohibitive as the number of parameters grows (Reviewer 2: e.g., >10 parameters or function-valued parameters) or for high-resolution PDEs (Reviewer 3).  
- **Training difficulty:** The hybrid loss (sensitivity + data + possibly PINN) introduces training challenges that are not discussed. SC-FNO should be harder to train than FNO, but the paper does not mention differences in convergence or stability (Reviewer 4). Moreover, FNO-PINN might perform better if trained with adaptive loss weighting (e.g., Wang et al., 2022), but such tuning was not shown (Reviewer 4). The SC-FNO loss uses learnable coefficients (Algorithm 3, Liebel & Körner 2018), but the training procedure should be outlined in the main text, not only the appendix (Reviewer 4).  
- **Limited experiments:** The method is tested only on relatively simple ODEs/PDEs; its effectiveness for complicated, high-resolution, chaotic systems (e.g., Lorenz system, Reviewer 1), or bifurcation scenarios (e.g., \(y'=py\) with \(p\) near zero, Reviewer 2) is unknown. The approach may struggle when parameters are functions rather than scalars, and the Jacobian \(\partial \mathbf{u}/\partial \mathbf{p}\) would be huge (Reviewer 1).  
- **Metric choice:** The paper uses \(R^2\) as the evaluation metric, not the relative \(L^2\) norm common in the FNO literature; this makes comparisons with prior work less direct (Reviewer 4).  
- **Missing in main text:** The paper should clarify whether \(P\) is a scalar, vector, or function, and how it is incorporated into the FNO model (e.g., as an additional input channel) (Reviewer 1). Concrete equations for the parametric forms of the ODEs/PDEs should be given early (Reviewer 1). The overall writing quality could be enhanced by moving key details from the appendix (architecture, algorithm) into the main text (Reviewers 1, 4).  
- **Other concerns:** Higher-order derivatives in the loss were not explored; potential benefits or limitations are unaddressed (Reviewer 2).  
- **Minority positive contrast:** Reviewer 1 was notably more positive (score 8) than Reviewers 2, 3, 4 (scores 5, 6, 6). Reviewer 1 found the rebuttal satisfactory and raised the score, while Reviewer 4 remained critical of novelty and training difficulty. This disagreement is not fully resolved.
