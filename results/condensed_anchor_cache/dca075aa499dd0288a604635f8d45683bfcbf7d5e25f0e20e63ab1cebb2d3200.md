## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes three methods to enhance Prototype-Based Federated Learning (PBFL): Class-wise Prototype Sparsification (CPS) to reduce communication costs, Privacy-Preserving Prototype Aggregation (PPA) to avoid sharing class distributions, and Class-Proportional Knowledge Distillation (CPKD) for improved personalization. These are integrated into two PBFL frameworks. Experimental results show up to 10× and 4× communication cost reductions with comparable or improved accuracy. Reviewers acknowledge the timely topic and easy integration, but most find the contributions insufficiently novel, lack theoretical support, have incomplete experiments, questionable privacy claims, and presentation issues.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles important issues in federated learning: communication efficiency, privacy, and personalization under data and model heterogeneity (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The three methods are easy to integrate with existing PBFL approaches. One reviewer (score 5) found the results comparable with baselines, achieving up to 10× and 4× communication cost reductions and sometimes outperforming original and other communication-efficient baselines (Reviewer 1).
- CPS can effectively reduce communication cost under extreme data heterogeneity (Reviewer 3).
- The topic is relevant and the paper is generally well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and overlap with prior work.**  
   - CPS is a straightforward application of structured sparsity; sparse learning strategies with binary masks have been widely studied (Li et al. 2021, Babakniya et al. 2022). The paper does not compare with existing sparsity techniques (Wen et al. 2016, Han et al. 2015) (Reviewers 1, 2).  
   - CPKD, weighting distillation by class proportions, has been shown in similar or more advanced forms (Wu et al., Dai et al.) (Reviewer 2).  
   - Most valuable insights are already published; the new methods are not a large enough part of the paper to justify acceptance (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). One reviewer (score 5) still found the performance comparable, but other reviewers considered the contribution insufficient.

2. **Lack of theoretical support and convergence guarantees.**  
   - No proof of convergence or theoretical analysis for any of the three methods (Reviewer 1).  
   - PPA lacks a formal privacy guarantee; it is unclear how the server determines the normalization factor K without knowing each client’s \(n_{i,j}\) (Reviewers 1, 4).  
   - CPKD’s mathematical rationale is absent; the weighting term \(\beta_{i,j}=p_{i,j}/\max_k p_{i,k}\) appears arbitrary—why not simply \(p_{i,j}\)? (Reviewers 1, 4).  
   - The impact of CPS sparsification on performance and convergence is not analyzed theoretically (Reviewer 3).

3. **Insufficient and incomplete experiments.**  
   - Missing ablations on feature dimensions (e.g., 64, 128, 256), different levels of data heterogeneity (e.g., \(\alpha=0.05,0.5,0.1\)), partial client participation, and varying model architectures (Reviewer 1).  
   - No comparison with non-PBFL baselines (e.g., SCAFFOLD) (Reviewer 1).  
   - No experiments demonstrating practicality in resource-constrained environments, such as devices with different bandwidths (Reviewer 2).  
   - Reported accuracy improvements are marginal (Reviewers 4, 1).

4. **Questionable privacy and personalization claims; methods conflict.**  
   - PPA’s privacy benefits lack formal guarantees and empirical support (Reviewer 3).  
   - CPKD requires direct access to the client’s class distribution \(P_{ij}\), contradicting PPA’s goal of hiding class distributions (Reviewer 3).  
   - It is unclear why PPA, designed for data protection, improves classification accuracy (Reviewer 2).  
   - The privacy and personalization contributions are minimal; a focused exploration of sparsification would be stronger (Reviewer 3).

5. **Presentation and writing issues.**  
   - The introduction and related work sections appear LLM-generated, with abrupt transitions (Reviewer 3).  
   - No summary overview diagram (Reviewer 2).  
   - Related work is superficial, missing references to key heterogeneous FL methods (regularization, bias suppression, distal terms, model splitting, decoupling, weighted aggregation) (Reviewer 3).  
   - HPFL is never defined; prototypes are not explained early enough (Reviewer 4).  
   - The three methods lack integration, reading like a loosely assembled engineering report (Reviewer 3).

6. **Computational overhead not evaluated.**  
   - No discussion or analysis (empirical or theoretical) of the computational overhead introduced by the three methods (Reviewer 1).
