## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SSLMem, a framework to define and measure memorization in self-supervised learning (SSL) by comparing alignment differences between representations of augmented views from encoders trained with vs. without a specific data point. Empirical analysis across architectures (ResNet, ViT), SSL methods (SimCLR, BYOL, MAE), and datasets (CIFAR-10/100, SVHN, STL-10, ImageNet) shows that SSL models memorize a significant fraction of training data, and this memorization correlates with downstream generalization performance. Reviewer assessments vary: one reviewer (score 8) finds the work sound and important; three reviewers (scores 6) acknowledge the contribution but raise substantive concerns about theoretical grounding, experimental breadth, and practical implications.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and underexplored problem: defining memorization in SSL, where existing supervised definitions do not apply. (Reviewers 1,2,3)
- Clear presentation and easy-to-follow argumentation. (Reviewers 1,2,4)
- Thorough empirical evaluation covering multiple architectures, SSL methods, and datasets. (Reviewers 2,3)
- Proposed metric is agnostic to SSL method and augmentation type, making it broadly applicable. (Reviewer 3)
- Provides interesting insights into the memorization-generalization relationship in SSL. (Reviewers 1,3)
- The representation-level memorization measure could be a useful tool for other applications (e.g., localizing memorization in models). (Reviewer 2)
- Novel conceptual contribution in generalizing memorization beyond supervised learning. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Why memorization benefits generalization is not explained.** The paper shows the correlation but does not clarify the mechanism. For example, when high-memorization points are removed, does performance drop primarily on similar points (suggesting long-tail generalization rather than memorization)? (Reviewer 2, 3)
- **The regularization in Eq. (3) may confound results.** Limiting alignment (cosine similarity) directly penalizes a core SSL objective, so the observed accuracy drop could be due to degraded representation quality, not reduced memorization. (Reviewer 2)
- **Training set overlap between encoders f and g is insufficiently justified.** The paper uses an 80/10/10 split, where only 10% of points are excluded from g. It is unclear why this overlap is necessary or how varying the overlap (e.g., 70%, 50%) changes the memorization score. (Reviewer 3)
- **The memorization score lacks theoretical grounding and intuitive justification.** The claim that higher alignment on f than g indicates memorization is not rigorously explained, and no theoretical backing is provided. (Reviewer 3)
- **Insufficient comparison to existing memorization metrics.** No analysis is shown comparing the proposed metric to supervised memorization definitions (e.g., influence functions, example difficulty) or to other SSL-specific measures (e.g., training dynamics, generalization gaps). (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Limited practical implications.** It is unclear how to use the memorization score in practice—e.g., what to do with samples having high vs. low scores. (Reviewer 4)
- **Experimental scope is restricted.** 
  - Architectures: Only ResNet (various depths) and ViT; no systematic comparison across different widths, depths, or parameter-matched architectures. (Reviewer 4)
  - Downstream datasets: Only coarse-grained classification (CIFAR-10/100, SVHN, STL-10, ImageNet); no fine-grained datasets (e.g., Food-101, Flower102). (Reviewer 4)
  - Only one differential privacy experiment (DP-SGD, one  algorithm) is presented; more DP algorithms and setups are needed to support the memorization-DP claim. (Reviewer 1)
- **Performance gap in removal experiments is small.** Removing up to 2000 high-memorization points from 25K training samples (or full CIFAR-10) yields only a modest accuracy drop; it is unclear whether these points are the most critical for generalization. (Reviewer 4)
- **No analysis of the role of specific augmentations.** It is not examined whether certain augmentations (e.g., masking vs. cropping) or sets of augmentations drive the memorization scores, or how the metric behaves under different augmentation families. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Clarity issues in the main text:**
  - The normalization procedure that constrains memorization scores to [-1,1] is not described (Section 4). (Reviewer 2)
  - It is unclear whether “three independent seeds” means different data splits or just different weight initializations. (Reviewer 2)
  - Table 1: “Frac. Mem.” is used in the table but “Avg. Mem.” is defined in the caption—these should be consistent. (Reviewer 2)
  - Section 4.4: It is ambiguous whether the removal of high-memorization points is from the 25K subset or the full CIFAR-10 training set. (Reviewer 2)
  - Section 4.4: Cosine similarity is used for regularization while L2 distance is used for the memorization score—why the difference? (Reviewer 2)
  - Section 4.5: The term “exploited” in the context of Deja Vu is unclear; it is also not explained whether MAE is expected to exhibit higher memorization. (Reviewer 2)
  - The definition of “atypical datapoints” (e.g., visual inspection vs. other indicators) is not specified. (Reviewer 2)
- **The contributions are considered incremental by one reviewer.** The finding that memorization benefits generalization is already known in supervised learning; applying similar ideas to SSL is seen as lacking novelty. (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of experiments connecting memorization score to other generalization metrics.** No comparison to measures from the generalization literature (e.g., Jiang et al., ICLR 2020) or to coreset selection methods (e.g., Deep Learning on a Data Diet). (Reviewer 4)
- **No experiment training on only high-memorization samples.** This would test whether those samples alone suffice for good down-stream performance, which is a natural complement to the removal experiments. (Reviewer 4)
- **The metric’s dependence on the choice of encoder g is not ablated.** It is unclear whether g must share the same architecture or can be pre-trained on a different dataset; no ablation studies on g are provided. (Reviewer 3)
- **The reasoning for why points in Sc (long-tail) have higher memorization than Ss (shared) is not well motivated.** Since Ss is used to train both f and g, the relative nature of the score may artificially reduce memorization for Ss, which is not discussed. (Reviewer 3)
