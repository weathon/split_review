- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 1, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents R-KinetiQuery, a framework that translates sign language into SQL queries using ergodic theory and information geometry. Reviewers agree the problem is difficult and of potential interest, but they find the motivation unconvincing (deaf users could use text-to-SQL instead), the theoretical contributions disconnected from the model, the architecture dated (LSTM + 2017 text-to-SQL), the related work missing recent LLM-based solutions, and the experimental evaluation unsolid (benchmark dataset not publicly available, details missing, baselines outdated).

### Strengths
- **S1.** The paper provides a theoretical foundation for sign-language to SQL translation, applying ergodic theory and information geometry (Reviewer 3). However, this is contested by Reviewer 2 who judges the theory to be mostly recasting known results (see Weaknesses).
- **S2.** The problem is difficult and of interest to the conference (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **W1 (Motivation).** The problem is esoteric: speech-impaired users can already use text-to-SQL tools without the extra complexity of sign language. It is unclear whether the studied problem has any real-world applicability (Reviewers 1, 3). *Also raised as Question 1 by Reviewer 3: “Can you describe a real-life scenario where the proposed framework can be used?”*
- **W2 (Theoretical contribution).** Most of the paper’s theoretical results are derived from definitions (Defs. 1–6) that are general for almost any ML architecture; the analysis is largely recasting known results. There is no connection between the theory and the proposed model—the results do not influence model choices. A reviewer would be able to produce Section 3 for almost any paper introducing a machine learning model (Reviewer 2). *Also raised as a question by Reviewer 3: “What is the connection between your theoretical results and the problem?”*
- **W3 (Outdated model).** The model relies on an LSTM for image vector embeddings and a text-to-SQL architecture from 2017 (Reviewer 2). This is not competitive with modern approaches.
- **W4 (Related work).** The related work is weak, especially on NL-to-SQL. Recent LLM-based approaches that can handle cross-modal translation are not reviewed nor discussed. This omission is critical because LLMs can naturally handle the sign-language → SQL problem (Reviewer 3).
- **W5 (Lack of justification for unified framework).** The challenges in sign-language-to-SQL generation are not clearly articulated, and the proposed method lacks justification. It is unclear why a two-step approach (sign language → natural language → SQL) would not work; the unified theoretical framework does not benefit the translation task (Reviewer 3). *Also raised as Question 2 by Reviewer 3: “Can you justify the advantages of the proposed framework compared to the two-step approach?”*
- **W6 (Experimental evaluation).** The evaluation is not solid: the paper introduces a new benchmark dataset for sign-language-to-SQL translation but does not provide its URL, and the details of the dataset are missing, making it impossible to assess the results. The chosen baselines are outdated; recent LLM-based solutions should have been included (Reviewer 3). *Also raised as Question 3 by Reviewer 3: “Can you provide additional details regarding the experimental setup and provide more data points to validate the effectiveness of the proposed method?”*
- **W7 (Presentation issues).** The abstract uses excessive superlatives (“groundbreaking framework”, “key innovation”, “superior performance”) typical of LLM jargon (Reviewer 1). Section titles are unconventional: “Contextual Landscape” instead of “Related Work”, “Comparative Assessment Framework” instead of “Empirical Evaluation” (Reviewer 1). The introduction has no citations (Reviewer 1).
- **W8 (Math jargon, unclear relevance of domain adaptation).** The content reads as a mishmash of math jargon combined in ways that make little sense. The introduction heavily emphasizes domain adaptation, but it is unclear why this is an important challenge for the problem at hand (Reviewer 1).
