## Merged Review

### Summary

The three reviewers assign scores 8, 6, and 5 (average 6.3). Reviewer 1 is the most positive, highlighting originality (recursive splitting for reachability abstraction), clarity, and solid experimental improvement over HRL baselines. Reviewers 2 and 3 are more critical, raising substantial concerns about statistical significance, lack of detail on the crucial reachability approximation, unclear practicality, theoretical justification, and high variance from non‑stationarity. All reviewers note that the high‑level idea is clear and that the theoretical results are a contribution, but the paper lacks thorough description of representations and training procedures.

### Strengths

- **Originality**: The idea of spatial abstraction based on recursive splitting of state partitions to ensure reachability at different hierarchy levels is novel. The three‑layer architecture combining spatial and temporal abstractions is a new contribution (R1, R2).
- **Theoretical grounding**: The paper provides a bound on sub‑optimality of policies trained with reachability‑aware abstractions and supports progressive refinement of these abstractions (R1, R2). The theoretical results are presented with intuitive descriptions despite complex notation (R3).
- **Clarity of high‑level idea**: The main concept of the STAR algorithm is explained clearly, making the paper easy to follow (R1, R3). The mathematical description is nicely coupled with intuition (R2).
- **Online learning**: STAR performs online learning of both policies and goal representations (R2).
- **Experimental evaluation**: The method is tested on three continuous control tasks of increasing dimensionality (Ant Maze, Ant Maze Fall, Ant Maze Cam) and achieves higher success rates in fewer steps compared to recent HRL baselines (HIRO, HRAC) (R1). The environment choices and baselines are reasonable (R2).
- **Supporting materials**: Proofs in the appendix are fairly clear, and the pseudo‑code aids procedural understanding (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient statistical significance**: All results are reported for only 5 runs. In AntMaze and AntMazeCam, where HIRO and HRAC are close to STAR, this small number of trials makes it difficult to conclude superiority. At least 10–20 runs are needed to assess variance and reliability (R2).
- **Lack of clarity on task complexity**: The paper does not define what “complex” continuous control means—only dimensionality, or also sparse rewards, distribution shifts, or difficulty of dynamics? A precise definition is needed to contextualize results (R2).
- **Deterministic environment assumption**: The method assumes a deterministic environment, limiting applicability to real‑world stochastic settings. No discussion on adapting to noisy transitions or stochastic rewards is provided (R2).
- **High variance from non‑stationarity**: Multiple hierarchical layers create high non‑stationarity, likely causing high variance during learning. The paper does not adequately address this or propose mitigation strategies (R2). For example, using a backward model starting from the goal set could reduce the need to explore all abstract goals (R2).
- **Reachability approximation poorly detailed and costly**: The essential step of approximating reachability is described only by reference to other papers. It is unclear how reachability is estimated for *all states* in a continuous state space (R3). The forward model \(\mathcal{F}_k\) is not analyzed: how is it trained, what architecture is used, how does its accuracy affect overall reliability? Inaccuracies are not discussed (R3). The computational cost of refining abstractions and the trade‑off between precision and overhead from AI2‑related bounding box computations are not addressed (R3). A wall‑time comparison and compute utilization discussion would help (R3).
- **Moving target problem**: The low‑level policy changes constantly during learning, potentially invalidating previous reachability estimates. The paper does not discuss how the algorithm handles this (R3).
- **Theoretical contributions need better justification and placement**: Section 4 could be restructured—Theorem 2 is the key result, while Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 could be moved to the appendix (R3). The paper claims Theorem 2 makes the method sound, but it only proves existence of *some* refinement, not necessarily the one used in the algorithm. It is not explained why constructing such an abstraction is non‑trivial (e.g., why not simply partition into \(\varepsilon\)-diameter sets) (R3). Reviewer 2 questions whether all proofs in Section 4 are novel or existed in prior work (e.g., GARA or Liu 2021) (R2).
- **Definition and clarity of goal space and actions**: The goal space \(\mathcal{G}\) and state space \(S\) are not clearly defined; the initial \(\mathcal{G}\) and \(G_t\) need clarification (R1). The action space of the Navigator is said to be in \(\mathcal{G}\), but it is unclear if this concerns history or possible actions (R1). The Navigator reward phrase “max is computed over observed exploration data” is confusing—what does “observed” mean? (R1). In Definition 2, after removing \(G_i\) and adding \(G_1'\) and \(G_2'\), why is it necessary to specify that \(G_i\) is the union of the two after removal? (R1).
- **Exploration scalability**: The reachability check requires exploring the complete partition of states, but the paper does not discuss how the exploration cost scales with the dimension of the state space (R1). Appendix B.2 discusses growing state space but not computational cost explicitly (R1).
- **Figure quality**: Figure 4 is not informative; it does not clearly show progressive exploration toward the goal. The representation is poor compared to similar visualizations in prior work (e.g., Nachum et al., ICLR 2019) (R1).
- **Missing comparisons and experiments**: The paper does not compare with Nachum et al. ICLR 2019 (R1). It is not tested on tasks other than the Ant Maze (R1). How does STAR perform on larger state spaces or different task types? (R1, R2). There is no estimate of the trade‑off between results and resources (memory, time) compared to other methods (R1).
- **Questions about algorithm design**:
  - Why is the Navigator useful? What are its theoretical and practical advantages over just having Manager and Controller, especially given that only a few abstractions are used in experiments? (R3).
  - The Manager’s reward seems arbitrary; without justification it may fail with randomly shaped barriers. Is it designed based on environment dynamics? (R2).
  - The representation of abstractions: they appear box‑shaped and disjoint in experiments—is this always the case? Are they represented explicitly? (R3).
  - Is the splitting operation the only refinement? Could join be used if abstractions are too fine, and would that be beneficial? (R3).
  - Initial stage: when the agent can barely move, is there a risk of shattering the goal space arbitrarily before becoming capable of acting? (R3).
  - How is \(\pi_{Man}\) and \(\pi_{Cont}\) composed to form \(\pi_{low}\)? (R2).
  - The notation “~” in \(G_{t+k} \sim \pi_{Nav}(s_t, g^*)\) and \(g_{t+l} \sim \pi_{Man}(s_t, G_{t+k})\): is it meant as sampling from a distribution or just conditioning? (R2, R1’s question about action space).
  - How are both goals and actions sampled from policies? (R2).
  - How are \(N\) and state set \(G\) initialized? If based on \(\pi_{Nav}\) behavior, how is high variance handled, especially as dimensionality increases? (R2).
- **Minor issues**: A few typos (e.g., “spactial” instead of “spatial” in conclusion, “we we” in Section 5.3) and imprecise usage of motifs (R1, R2).
