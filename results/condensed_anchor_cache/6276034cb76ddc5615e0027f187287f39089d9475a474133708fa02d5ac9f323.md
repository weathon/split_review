## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether OOD-generalizable representations can be learned from in-distribution data alone. Empirically, it compares linear probing performance of three pretrained models: (1) CLIP (large, diverse data), (2) supervised ImageNet pretrained, (3) supervised ImageNet + distillation to predict CLIP features (treated as an “oracle” objective). The third model underperforms CLIP on OOD, suggesting a fundamental limitation. Theoretically, it studies two-layer ReLU networks trained by SGD with ERM and regularization, identifying a new failure mode called “feature accompaniment” – asymmetric activation causes each neuron to become class-correlated, yielding non-zero gradient projections onto background features, causing the network to store per-example spurious signals even when they have no dataset-wide correlation with the label. The paper argues this failure stems from inductive biases of nonlinear networks and is distinct from spurious correlations.

### Strengths
- **(R1, R2, R3)** The paper studies OOD generalization from the novel perspective of inductive biases (training dynamics, not just global minima), which is rarely considered in existing theoretical models and more practical for understanding real failures.
- **(R2, R3)** The “feature accompaniment” failure mode is intuitive, novel, and distinct from the prevailing focus on spurious correlations. Reviewer 2 considers it a potentially fundamental phenomenon arising from activation asymmetry that could inspire further research.
- **(R1)** It is interesting to investigate OOD generalization via the “rich representation” viewpoint – representations containing diverse, redundant features that become crucial OOD.
- **(R1)** Showing that non-convex networks can learn and store irrelative per-example spurious signals even without dataset-level label correlation is an insightful empirical/theoretical finding.
- **(R3)** The theoretical analysis for 2-layer ReLU networks is principled and provides foundations proving the existence of this failure mode. The paper attempts to argue that existing theoretical models may not cover why OOD failures happen in practice.

### Weaknesses
- **Disconnect between empirical and theoretical sections (R1, R2, R3).**
    - Reviewer 1 points out that “OOD generalizable representation” is defined differently: empirically as “rich feature set” (linear probing, Figure 1), theoretically as “invariant vs. background features”. These principles do not support each other. Reviewer 2 notes the settings differ (distillation vs. single model ERM; different losses). Reviewer 3 states the empirical claim in Section 2 may not be fully justified.
- **The “oracle” comparison in Section 2 is flawed (R1, R3).**
    - R1: CLIP is pretrained on far more data than ImageNet, so it is not an achievable upper bound for an ImageNet-pretrained model. More supervision (e.g., 22k categories) could yield better OOD representations; the supervised+CLIP model is not truly an oracle in the rich-representation sense.
    - R3: Distilling CLIP on ImageNet likely loses features that are not present in ImageNet but are helpful OOD (e.g., core features of OOD birds/cars that differ from ID). Thus the underperformance may simply be due to missing out-of-distribution features, not evidence of a fundamental representation-learning failure.
- **Missing connections to related work (R1, R3).**
    - R1: The theoretical section does not discuss prior works on SGD and feature learning (e.g., [3][4][5] as cited by R1).
    - R3: The study does not relate “feature accompaniment” to prior findings on simplicity bias (Shah et al., 2020) and gradient starvation (Pezeshki et al., 2021), which also describe how simple features suppress learning of more complex features. R3 asks whether these results are contradictory or complementary.
- **Lack of actionable implications and quantification (R3).**
    - The paper does not suggest how to use the findings to improve or predict OOD generalization in practice. It remains unclear how much OOD generalization failure is actually attributable to “feature accompaniment” in empirical settings.
- **Missing analysis for multiple domains (R2).**
    - The theory only considers a single training domain. R2 asks: if there are multiple domains with different background distributions, will the training process (e.g., Equation (1)) still cause feature accompaniment? Are there scenarios where even with multiple domains OOD generalization remains impossible?
- **Presentation of empirical principle (R1).** The principle that “good OOD linear probing performance” equals “good generalization representation” (rich representation) should be clarified early.

Note: Reviewer 1 is notably more critical (score 5, soundness 2, contribution 2) than Reviewers 2 and 3 (both score 6, soundness 3, contribution 3/4). The above weaknesses reflect the stronger doubts from R1, especially regarding the flawed oracle comparison and the conceptual disconnect between empirical and theoretical parts.
