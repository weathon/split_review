## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes feeding graphs as images into multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to solve graph-structured problems (e.g., influence maximization, network dismantling, six basic graph tasks). Overall, reviewers agree the research direction is interesting and timely, but the execution is flawed. Most reviewers (R1, R4, R5; scores 3,3,3) find the paper lacks novelty, methodological rigor, and useful insights, while R2 (score 5) is moderately positive and R3 (score 6) is the most positive, praising the extensive experiments and promising results. Key concerns include: insufficient novelty (previous work GITA and VisionGraph already did this), missing experimental details (models, visualization methods), simplistic conclusions, and lack of credible proof for claims about scalability and spatial intelligence.

### Strengths

- Interesting and promising direction: exploring how MLLMs can interpret graph visualizations (R1, R4, R5).
- Extensive experiments across multiple tasks; MLLMs reportedly outperform baselines (e.g., simple heuristics, GNNs) in network dismantling and influence maximization (R3; more positive than other reviewers who question the experiment validity).
- Methodology is well-structured with helpful visual examples that illustrate how MLLMs process graph data (R3).
- Figures and tables are clear and aesthetically presented (R4).
- Experiments on two combinatorial optimization tasks (influence maximization, network dismantling) usefully supplement previously explored graph reasoning tasks, offering valuable insights for the emerging field of visual-language graph reasoning (R5).
- Writing is generally good (R2; note that R1 and R5 criticize the writing/organization, so this is a minority positive point).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of novelty and missing key references.** The claimed core contribution—feeding graphs as images into MLLMs—has been previously introduced by GITA (NeurIPS 2024) and VisionGraph (ICML 2024). Many findings (zero-shot reasoning, MLLM outperforming LLMs, layout impact, challenges with scale) overlap with those works. The paper exaggerates its originality and fails to clearly differentiate its contributions, especially for the combinatorial optimization tasks (R5, also echoed by R1 and R4).
- **Insufficient experimental details.** The specific MLLM and LLM models used are not mentioned in Section 4 (basic graph tasks) (R4). How graphs are converted into images is not clearly specified (R5). The set of models tested is too narrow, and baselines are insufficient to draw robust conclusions (R4, R5). For example, stronger heuristic methods should be included to set upper bounds for non-learning methods (R1). Model selection inconsistencies and varying prompt settings prevent a fair comparison between MLLMs and LLMs (R4).
- **Oversimplified and unconvincing conclusions.** The claim that “regardless of how large or complex the network is, the input remains a fixed-size image” is an oversimplification; MLLMs often struggle to accurately recognize nodes and edges even in small graphs, so outputs may lack interpretability and practical feasibility (R4). The claim that MLLMs can efficiently process global relationships needs more convincing evidence (R4). The so-called “large-scale” graphs in experiments have only tens of nodes, which does not justify claims about handling truly large-scale networks (millions of nodes); larger graphs quickly become indistinguishable smudges (R5). The paper reads more like an incomplete experimental report than a rigorous research paper, lacking theoretical explanations, methodological robustness, and compelling case studies (R1, R4, R5).
- **Concerns about fairness and prior knowledge in visualization.** The transformation from graph to image (e.g., choice of color, node degree, force-directed layout) may embed human prior knowledge that gives MLLMs an unfair advantage over conventional methods (R2, R5). Different visualization methods (e.g., circular, hierarchical) should be examined; relying solely on one layout limits generalization (R1, R3).
- **Limited evaluation scope and missing experiments.** The paper tests only common homogeneous graphs; dynamic/evolving graphs, weighted graphs (with edge attributes), and heterogeneous graphs (with node/edge types) are not considered (R2, R3, R5). For graphs exceeding thousands of nodes, it is unclear whether MLLMs can provide meaningful solutions for combinatorial problems (R5). The experiments also lack interactive visualization tools (e.g., zooming) that could help with dense, node-crowded regions (R3). Comparing multiple MLLMs with different GNNs and LLMs would better demonstrate performance bounds (R5).
- **Poor writing and organization.** The paper jumps from introduction directly into experiments, with related work and methodology embedded in those sections (R5). Terms such as BA, ER, WS are introduced without adequate explanation (R5). Some figures are confusing: Fig 7 and Fig 9 lack explanation of what “LS” is and what the lines (degree, betweenness, closeness) represent, making it hard to see how they demonstrate MLLMs’ intelligence (R2). There is a typo in the caption of Fig 5 (“only three agents are presented” should be four) (R2). Abbreviations like HD, HCI should be defined at first use (R1). The paper is better suited for an application-focused venue than a research conferences (R1).
