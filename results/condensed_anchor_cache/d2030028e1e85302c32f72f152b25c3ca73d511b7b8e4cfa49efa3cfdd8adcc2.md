## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces FlashRNN, a hardware-optimized library that accelerates traditional RNNs (LSTMs, GRUs) on modern GPUs. It uses kernel-level fusion (matrix multiplication and activation functions), a parallelization variant inspired by multi-head processing in Transformers, and the ConstrINT framework for hardware-aware auto-tuning under constraints like cache sizes. FlashRNN achieves up to 50× speed-ups over vanilla PyTorch implementations and allows 40× larger hidden sizes compared to their Triton implementation. The authors compare to cuDNN and FlashAttention2 and plan to open source the kernels and optimization library. Reviewers are mostly positive about the problem, clarity, and hardware-aware optimization, but note several weaknesses and missing comparisons. One reviewer (R3, score 8) is more positive than the others (scores 6,6,6), highlighting the importance of scaling RNNs and the comparison to cuDNN.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and clearly structured (R1, R3).
- Hardware-aware optimization for accelerating RNN model training/inference is promising (R2, R4). The integration of kernel fusion (matrix multiplication and activation functions) significantly reduces memory access overhead, a critical bottleneck in memory-bound computations (R4).
- The ConstrINT framework for modeling hardware constraints (cache sizes, memory) ensures adaptability to different GPU architectures (R4). The auto-tuning via integer constraint satisfaction is a novel approach (R1: "hardware aware fine tuning").
- Comprehensive benchmarking including relevant backends such as cuDNN (R1, R3). Open-source release will benefit the research community (R2, R4).
- The paper tackles an important problem: overcoming the strict sequential processing limitation of RNNs for state-tracking tasks, where Transformers are less natural (R3). It justifies why LSTM optimization remains relevant compared to Transformers (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear procedures and insufficient explanation**:  
  - The interaction between ConstrINT and other FlashRNN components is unclear; an overall architecture/workflow would be helpful (R2, Q1).  
  - Algorithm 5.1 (kernel fusion procedure) lacks a detailed explanation and would benefit from further illustration (R2, Q2).  
  - In Algorithm 5.1, line 242 loads \(R_{tbgs}, B_{tg}\) to registers and SRAM. It is not specified how many registers/how much SRAM can be used; using all available may conflict with storing intermediate results and impact performance (R2, Q3).  
  - Lines 299-303: multiple heads are computed in parallel without synchronization – could this affect accuracy? Also, custom caching strategies cannot be applied due to Triton restrictions on GPU register access – how large is the gap between the intended design and the Triton implementation? (R2, Q4-Q5).  
  - Padding zeros for small batch sizes at the cost of efficiency: how much efficiency is lost? (R2, Q6).

- **Evaluation issues and unfair or missing comparisons**:  
  - Comparing to vanilla PyTorch is unfair because PyTorch has overhead to launch its internal CUDA kernel; several fusions can be achieved through compilation (e.g., torch.compile, TVM), and not using compilation makes the comparison unfair (R1, R4). Profiling to show closeness to the roofline is missing (R1).  
  - The evaluation setup in Figures 1-4 is confusing: FlashRNN’s performance (four backends: CUDA fused, CUDA alternating, Triton fused, Vanilla PyTorch) appears lower than the baselines (FlashAttention2, nn.LSTM) in most configurations. Why compare with FlashAttention2? It is unclear how FlashRNN differs from applying FlashAttention-like methods to RNNs (R2, Q7; R1).  
  - Replacing attention blocks with FlashRNN LSTM for speed comparisons (lines 467-468): does this substitution provide a meaningful comparison? (R2, Q8).  
  - The paper mentions the HASTE library and that it overcomes its limitations, but does not benchmark against HASTE (R3, Q).  
  - None of the experiments include accuracy comparisons; this is a limitation (R2, Q9). Numerical precision differences in training compared to PyTorch’s cuDNN are noted but not investigated (R4).

- **Missing analysis and scalability concerns**:  
  - No experiments on inference performance; memory access patterns differ from training (R4).  
  - Not explored how FlashRNN handles very long sequences, where RNNs suffer from vanishing gradients and memory constraints (R4).  
  - Scalability issues remain: RNNs still require sequential processing, and FlashRNN does not fully address the parallelization advantage of Transformers (R4).  
  - Focus on GPU optimizations reduces applicability to TPUs or CPUs (R4).  
  - Has the method been tested on GPUs with less SRAM? What are the options if the weight matrix cannot be cached? (R4).  
  - A more detailed comparison of computational complexity scaling between FlashRNN and Transformers (input size vs. hidden states) is needed (R4).  
  - It is not fully convincing why FlashRNN would outperform FlashAttention or how it fundamentally differs from applying FlashAttention-like methods to RNNs (R4).
