## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Fast Gradient Leakage (FGL), a gradient inversion attack using StyleGAN as prior knowledge and a joint gradient matching loss. FGL is designed to reconstruct high-resolution face images (CelebA, 224×224) from shared gradients in federated learning, supporting larger batch sizes (up to 60) and faster convergence than prior methods. Reviewers are divided: one (score 5) finds the approach creative and effective at revealing privacy risks; two (score 3) acknowledge the problem importance and seeming reconstruction effectiveness but flag novelty and experimental issues; one (score 1) considers the work non-innovative and the reconstructed images of poor quality for downstream use.

### Strengths
- **Problem importance and motivation** are clear and relevant (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Novel combination of StyleGAN prior and joint gradient matching loss** for faster and clearer image recovery is viewed as creative and effective by Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3 notes scalability to larger batch sizes and complex datasets as a strength.
- **Comprehensive validation** of FGL’s feasibility is provided (Reviewers 1, 3); Reviewer 1’s experiments are considered clear and demonstrate advantages over prior methods.
- **Scalability and efficiency**: FGL handles larger batch sizes (up to 60) on high-resolution facial datasets, improving upon earlier constraints (Reviewer 3).
- **First validation on a facial dataset** for this type of attack (Reviewer 4, though noted as already claimed in prior work by others).
- **Revelation of privacy risks** in federated learning, especially on sensitive data, highlighting the need for stronger defenses (Reviewer 1).

*Minority positive perspective*: Reviewer 1 (score 5) is notably more enthusiastic about the creative use of StyleGAN and loss design, and the clarity of experiments.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The use of a GAN for prior knowledge in gradient inversion was already explored in GGL (Li et al., CVPR 2022) and other works (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The gradient matching loss is a combination of L1, L2 etc. similar to TAG (Deng et al., 2021); label inference follows Yin et al. (2021); multi-seed optimization is used in IG/GradInversion (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 specifically disputes the claim “for the first time … on a CNN architecture for high-resolution facial datasets”, citing prior GAN-based works. Reviewer 4 views the method as “mostly a combination of existing methods.”
- **Experimental comparisons are unconvincing**: The authors changed the attack dataset and evaluation metrics relative to baselines, making the claimed superiority not convincing (Reviewer 4). The reproduction of baselines is disputed – Reviewer 4 states “the performance of these baselines is not as poor as depicted in Fig. 5” and notes that GIA studies using GAN prior (Jeon et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022) were not compared. Reviewer 2 recommends comparisons with analytic-based attack methods (e.g., Fowl et al., ICLR 2022).
- **Lack of evaluation against privacy defenses**: No tests against gradient perturbation, differential privacy, or noise injection (Reviewers 1, 2). Several existing defenses (Soteria, InstaHide, Precode, etc.) are suggested for evaluation (Reviewer 2).
- **Reconstruction quality is poor**: In Figures 3, 5, and the Appendix, recovered facial images differ significantly from originals in face shape, hair, skin color – large pixel- and semantic-level errors; for downstream tasks like face recognition the reconstructed images have little value (Reviewer 3). This despite the method’s stated goal of attacking facial data.
- **Poincaré distance loss (Eq. 7) is unjustified and ineffective**: The rationale for using Poincaré distance on a face dataset (no hierarchical structure) is unclear (Reviewer 2). The paper states labels are already inferred, yet introduces this loss without analysis; the Appendix shows only ~60% correct label recovery for 8 labels, far inferior to Yin et al.’s method (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Metrics justification is not convincing**: The authors claim SSIM, MSE, PSNR have limited applicability, but these are standard metrics for reconstruction quality; the claim is not explained (Reviewer 2).
- **Time advantage is insufficiently analyzed**: Time comparisons are only narrative (final section) without prerequisites such as time to reach a quality threshold; given baselines appear poor, they could be terminated early (Reviewer 4).
- **Batch size limited by hardware**: Experiments use batch size 60 due to hardware constraints; scalability to larger groups typical in FL is uncertain (Reviewer 1). No theoretical analysis of performance with increasing batch size (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited generalizability**: Focus on high-resolution facial images only; not tested on medical, financial, or other data types common in FL (Reviewer 1).
- **Questions on assumptions**: The paper states “the server is prohibited from unilaterally modifying the initial model,” but the cited works (Fowl et al., Boenisch et al.) assume the server *can* modify the model; under an honest-but-curious server, why cannot it modify the initial model? (Reviewer 2)
- **Writing and presentation issues**: Confusing formula expressions (partial derivative symbol in loss at line 217, undefined ‘m’ in Eq. 6 – Reviewer 4); Eq. 5 uses derivative of model output *z*, but *z* is not a model parameter (Reviewer 4); Fig. 1 color scheme terrible, Fig. 2 lacks detailed introduction (Reviewer 4). Minor formatting: inconsistent math symbols for weights and gradients (Line 151), subtitle periods inconsistent (Line 96), missing references years and nested bracket citations (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing ablation**: No analysis of using diffusion models instead of GAN as prior, or comparison between different GAN choices (Reviewer 3).
- **Label recovery with repeated labels**: The method from Yin et al. is not applicable when a category has multiple samples; the paper does not address this (Reviewer 3).
