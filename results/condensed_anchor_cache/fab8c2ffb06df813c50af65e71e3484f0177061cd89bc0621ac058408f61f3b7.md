## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces SharedContextBench, a benchmark for evaluating long-context LLMs in shared-context scenarios requiring KV cache reuse. It comprises 12 tasks across four abilities (string retrieval, semantic retrieval, global information processing, multi-tasking) in two modes (multi-turn, multi-request). The authors evaluate five categories of long-context methods on six transformer-based LLMs, finding that sub-O(n) memory methods struggle in multi-turn, while sparse encoding with O(n) memory and sub-O(n²) computation perform well; dynamic sparse patterns are more expressive than static ones. The paper also proposes a new Tri-shape sparse attention mechanism. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 8. Reviewers 1,5,6 (score 8) enthusiastically endorse the benchmark as filling a critical gap. Reviewer 2 (score 3) is more negative, criticizing limited novelty and scope. Reviewers 3 and 4 (score 6) offer moderate praise but raise specific missing experiments and analyses.

### Strengths
1. **Fills a major evaluation gap**: Most existing long-context benchmarks test only a single query per input, allowing eviction-based methods to appear capable when they are not. SharedContextBench focuses on multi-turn shared-context scenarios, revealing true model capabilities. (Reviewer 1, confidence 4; also noted by Reviewers 2,3,5,6)
2. **Comprehensive task coverage**: 12 tasks across four abilities in two shared-context modes, including string retrieval, semantic retrieval, global information processing, and multi-tasking. Extended retrieval tasks (key-value, prefix-suffix, multi-hop) are particularly interesting. (Reviewers 2,3,4,5,6)
3. **Extensive comparative evaluation**: Tests five categories of long-context methods on six transformer-based LLMs, providing valuable comparative insights. The taxonomy in Table 1 and breakdown results in Tables 8 and 9 are helpful. Visualizations are intuitive. (Reviewers 1,2,3,5,6)
4. **Detailed analysis of method behaviours**: Identifies that sub-O(n) memory methods degrade in multi-turn scenarios, sparse encoding with O(n) memory and sub-O(n²) computation performs well, dynamic sparse patterns are more expressive than static ones, and layer-level sparsity in hybrid architectures is promising. (Reviewer 6, confidence 4; also mentioned by Reviewer 2)
5. **Clarity and organization**: The paper is easy to follow with a clear goal, contributions, and well-presented results. (Reviewers 3,4)
6. **Practical significance**: The benchmark addresses real-world applications (multi-turn conversations, self-play CoT, repository-level code debugging) and enables informed decisions for building effective long-context models. (Reviewers 3,6)

### Weaknesses
1. **Missing prefix caching methods**: Prefix caching is discussed in related work but not included in the experiments. (Reviewer 3, question; also noted by Reviewer 3 as a weakness)
2. **Unclear dataset origins**: Many tasks appear similar to those from InfiniteBench; it is unclear whether they are direct adoptions or modifications. A table listing sources for each task would improve transparency. (Reviewer 1, confidence 4; Reviewer 5, confidence 2)
3. **Lack of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis**: The paper does not analyze how hyperparameters (e.g., sparsity level, chunk size) were tuned or how performance varies with them. No error bars or heatmaps are provided, making it hard to assess robustness. (Reviewer 6, confidence 4)
4. **Limited discussion of real-world fidelity**: Retrieval tasks artificially vary target string positions without considering real-world distributions (e.g., power law). The paper does not discuss how well synthesized data reflects real use cases. (Reviewer 3, confidence 3; Reviewer 2, confidence 4, also notes tasks may not capture all domain-specific needs)
5. **Unclear contribution of Tri-shape sparse attention**: The new mechanism only significantly outperforms A-shape sparse attention in the first turn, and the paper does not show how the benchmark uniquely guided its design or highlighted its benefits. (Reviewer 3, question; confidence 3)
6. **No explicit comparison with other long-context benchmarks**: The paper does not highlight unique insights that SharedContextBench provides over existing benchmarks (e.g., HELMET, Michelangelo), nor does it show whether relative method rankings are consistent. (Reviewer 3, question; Reviewer 4, weakness)
7. **Methods are often extensions of existing models**: Many tested methods (e.g., StreamingLLM) are adaptations of models designed for multi-turn, limiting the novelty of the evaluation. (Reviewer 2, confidence 4)
8. **No exploration of internal mechanisms**: The paper does not analyze deeper dynamics such as information propagation in sparse attention, which would provide further insight. (Reviewer 2, confidence 4)
9. **Unclear table formatting**: Table 4 has confusing bolding and underscore conventions—some highest scores are not bold and the meaning of underscores is not explained. (Reviewer 6, confidence 4)
10. **Missing inter-turn dependency tasks**: Few or none of the questions require information from a previous QA turn in the shared context; this should either be added or acknowledged as a limitation. (Reviewer 1, confidence 4)
