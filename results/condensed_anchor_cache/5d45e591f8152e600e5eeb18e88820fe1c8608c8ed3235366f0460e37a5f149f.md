## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper tackles the problem of missing or scarce control data in crossover trials, where placebo data is lost after patients switch to active treatment, complicating long-term efficacy and safety assessment. The authors propose a Gaussian mixture counterfactual generator (GMCG) that generates counterfactual no-treatment outcomes without requiring control data for training, handling time-varying continuous doses and comparing treatment switchers to an extended placebo group. Two reviewers (rated 5) found the methodology insufficiently motivated and lacking key causal assumptions; two reviewers (rated 8) found the problem important, the method reasonable, and the simulation results encouraging, but still noted missing formal assumptions and limited validation.

### Strengths
- Novel approach to a real and important problem (crossover trial counterfactual generation) – mentioned by both lower- and higher-rated reviewers.
- Writing is clear, well-structured, and well-motivated; the problem setting is novel and worth thinking about.
- The Gaussian mixture counterfactual generator is presented relatively clearly.
- Charts and figures are visually appealing.
- Experiments are sufficient, extensive, and illustrative; numerical results demonstrate effectiveness.
- Simulation results in Appendix C (richer trends) are particularly convincing; higher-rated reviewer found them encouraging.
- The method is technically sound (though missing some assumptions) and likely to be applied by empirical researchers.

### Weaknesses
- **Methodology not well motivated**: Both the static state analysis (SSA) and Gaussian mixture model are introduced abruptly. Why SSA over other causal methods (doubly-robust, IPTW, marginal structural models)? No clear articulation of novelty and how the approach differs from existing synthetic control methods (propensity score, matrix completion, linear factor models such as Abadie and Garbanzal 2003). A lower-rated reviewer found the paper unconvincing in explaining why the method tackles the scarce/no control data problem.
- **Lack of formal causal assumptions**: The paper does not explicitly discuss the key assumptions for causal inference (consistency, overlap, unconfoundedness). Even a higher-rated reviewer noted that formal assumptions explaining when the method yields accurate results are missing – in particular, how well the model estimates the dose response function and extrapolates to zero dose. A failure mode is shown in Figure 5, but no condition covering it is formalized.
- **Parameter estimation unclear**: How are the Gaussian mixture parameters \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), \(\Phi_k\) found? What is \(k\) (number of components) used in the experiments? The estimation problem at line 161 defines \(\mathbb{Q} = [ W, \pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k, \Phi_k]\) but does not explain \(\Phi_k\). The kernel matrix in equation (11) is unclear.
- **Model specification issues**:
  - Equation (3) does not show a clear dependency of \(\mathbf{s}\) on \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\), yet Section 4 introduces dependence without discussion.
  - Treatment is modeled as a linear combination of factors with additive noise (equation (2)), but treatment is binary in most cases. The noise term \(\eta_a^{(t)}\) is assumed independent of \(W_a^{(t)}s\), but to ensure the outcome is binary the noise must depend on \(W_a^{(t)}s\) – a dependence not accounted for.
  - The likelihood specification is unclear; presumably noise is independent standard Gaussian, but this is not stated. Notation \(\Psi_0\) at the top of page 4 is never defined.
- **Counterfactual formulation in Section 4.1 is counter-intuitive**: The method imputes a change in the static state \(s\) that is compatible with the prior factual history and a counterfactual future treatment path. This needs more elaboration and clearer connection to the large literature on linear factor models for counterfactual imputation (e.g., https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07691). The subscripts “cf” and “f” in Section 3.3 are not explained.
- **Simulation limitations**:
  - The simulation in Section 5.1 is trivial: the average outcome path for untreated individuals is flat, so imputed no-treatment paths match ground truth trivially. The richer example in Appendix C (Figure 4) would be more convincing in the main body.
  - The simulation adheres exactly to the model assumptions. There is no test under alternative models (e.g., dynamic or non-linear models) to assess robustness.
  - Empirical results could be strengthened by including simulated but realistic placebo models (e.g., toxicity models for dose responses). The non-linear dose response function used may not be realistic; other functions could be tested.
  - Varying the number of patients in experiments would help practitioners understand when the method is appropriate.
- **Presentation**: Framed boxes in Section 4 consume excessive page space; they could be replaced by lemmas or theorems to improve readability.
- **Validation suggestion**: A placebo-test approach similar to Abadie’s synthetic control method (pretending treatment starts earlier and comparing imputed to factual outcomes) could be used to validate the method in a given empirical setting – this is currently missing.
- **Confidence in the method is mixed**: Two reviewers (rating 5) are less convinced, citing lack of motivation and missing assumptions; two reviewers (rating 8) find the method reasonable but still note significant missing aspects.
