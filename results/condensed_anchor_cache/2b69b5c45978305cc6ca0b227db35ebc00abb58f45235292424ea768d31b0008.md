## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper provides the first theoretical study of training Transformers with nonlinear attention to achieve Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generalization. Using a simplified one-layer single-head attention-only Transformer and a synthetic data model with orthonormal patterns, the authors quantify the required training samples, context examples, and iterations for developing CoT ability. They prove that the model can generalize to unseen tasks with distribution-shifted test data, even when reasoning examples contain noise and inaccuracies. The paper also theoretically compares CoT with in-context learning (ICL), showing CoT can succeed where ICL fails. Experiments on synthetic data support the theoretical results. One reviewer was notably more positive (score 8 vs. 6), highlighting the originality and generality of the setting, while the other three reviewers (each score 6) raised concerns about model simplicity, restrictive assumptions, formula errors, and originality relative to prior work.

### Strengths
1. **Novel theoretical framework**: First analysis of training dynamics for Transformers (nonlinear attention) specifically toward CoT generalization, filling a critical gap in the literature (R1, R2, R3, R4).
2. **Detailed quantitative analysis**: Characterizes sample complexity (number of context examples, training samples) and iterations required for successful CoT training, providing actionable insights (R1, R3).
3. **Generalization to distribution-shifted tasks**: Addresses a practical concern by proving CoT generalization on unseen tasks where test data differs from training data (R1, R2).
4. **Theoretically grounded CoT vs. ICL comparison**: Shows CoT can produce accurate outputs even when reasoning examples are noisy, while ICL requires examples to be dominantly correct—both theoretically and empirically supported (R1, R2, R4).
5. **Considers nonlinear attention and distribution shift**: More general setting than prior ICL analyses that assume linear attention or in-distribution test data (R2).
6. **Clear presentation with experimental support**: Theoretical results are clearly presented and verified on synthetic data, with helpful discussion and remarks (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Overly simplified architecture**: The analysis uses a one-layer single-head attention-only Transformer and binary classification tasks. This does not capture interactions in multi-layer/multi-head models or more complex multi-step reasoning, limiting generalizability to practical LLMs (R1, R3, R4). A reviewer also asked how results extend to varying reasoning step lengths (e.g., multi-token steps) (R2).
2. **Restrictive data assumptions**: Assumes orthonormality of training-relevant (TRR) and testing-relevant (TSR) patterns and a linear span between them. Real-world patterns are often correlated and complex; the paper lacks discussion of when these assumptions are violated or how conclusions might change (R1, R4).
3. **Formula errors**: Example 1 contains typos (affinity function notation and matrix indices mislabeled), undermining the credibility of theoretical derivations. All mathematical expressions need careful review (R1, R3).
4. **Theorem clarity and accessibility**: Conditions for theorems are not clearly defined in the main text, forcing reliance on the appendix. Ambiguity in reasoning steps and attention mechanism properties could lead to misconceptions (R3).
5. **Originality concerns**: The analytical techniques and writing logic bear strong similarity to prior work on how nonlinear Transformers learn in-context learning (Zhang et al.) and to induction head analysis (Olsson et al., 2022). The paper does not sufficiently differentiate its novel contributions or discuss how it extends beyond these works (R3, R4).
6. **Missing real-world validation**: No experiments on real datasets (e.g., natural language reasoning tasks). Reviewers note that such validation would significantly increase impact (R1, R2, R4).
7. **Labeling strategy and noise analysis**: A reviewer questioned why only the K-th step is used as the label instead of more flexible label strategies; another asked for more detailed analysis of how different noise levels affect performance—these points overlap with missing ablations or deeper investigation (R1).
