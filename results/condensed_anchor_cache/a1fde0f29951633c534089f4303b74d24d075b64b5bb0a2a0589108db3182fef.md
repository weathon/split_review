## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a unified framework for disentangled representation learning that targets both attribute (e.g., color, texture) and object disentanglement by maximizing compositionality of latent representations. Two images are encoded via a VAE encoder into multiple latent vectors; a mixing strategy combines these vectors (e.g., per-object or per-attribute mixing) to form a composite latent. A diffusion decoder reconstructs the composite image. The objective includes a latent denoising loss (as in Jung et al., 2024), a composite image likelihood term using a pretrained frozen diffusion model, and a consistency loss (NCE-like) that enforces the re-encoded latent of the generated image to be close to the original composite latent. Evaluations on synthetic datasets (e.g., Shapes3D, MPI3D, Cars3D for attributes; CLEVR6, LDS for objects) show the method surpasses or matches baselines like DisDiff, LSD, L2C. Most reviewers agree the paper is well-written and the idea is neat, but concerns about limited evaluation scope, missing real-world data, lack of segmentation tasks, and reliance on fixed mixing strategies dominate the criticism. One reviewer (score 8) is very positive; another (score 3) is highly critical.

### Strengths

1. **Unified treatment of attribute and object disentanglement** – The paper addresses both types within a single framework by developing appropriate mixing strategies for latents, which is helpful for steering the field toward disentangling different factors of variation. [R1, R2, R3]
2. **In‑depth analysis of compositionality** – The paper gives a detailed analysis of the intricacies involved in optimizing for compositionality. [R2]
3. **Clear and well‑written presentation** – The paper is polished, easy to read, and includes appropriate visualizations that complement the text. [R1, R2, R4, R5]
4. **Neat idea of enforcing compositional consistency** – The general idea of mixing latent representations and using a consistency loss is elegant and could possibly be extended to more challenging scenarios. [R4]
5. **Effective combination of techniques** – The framework leverages diffusion models, self‑supervised learning, and optimal transport in an interesting and simple way, with reported results surpassing or matching existing baselines on synthetic benchmarks. [R3, R5]

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited evaluation scope – only synthetic datasets** – All experiments are conducted on simple toy datasets (Shapes3D, MPI3D, Cars3D, CLEVR6, LDS) that do not represent the complexity of real-world data with non-linear relationships and complex distributions. Recent state‑of‑the‑art works also use realistic datasets (e.g., CelebA for attributes; Super‑CLEVR, MultiShapeNet, MOVi‑C, MOVi‑E, FFHQ for objects). The lack of evaluation on these datasets raises concerns about practical applicability and scalability. [R2, R3, R4, R5] **Reviewer 4 (score 3) and Reviewer 5 (score 6) both emphasize this as a major weakness.** [Minority contrast: Reviewer 2 (score 8) also notes “The impact of paper can be more by showing results on real world data.”]
2. **Missing object‑disentanglement benchmark: unsupervised segmentation** – Standard evaluation for object‑centric methods includes unsupervised segmentation with metrics like FG‑ARI, mIoU, mBO. These tests are absent, making it difficult to compare with recent works (e.g., Jung et al., 2024) and gauge practical utility for downstream tasks. [R5]
3. **Mis‑match with previously reported baseline results** – For the LDS dataset, property prediction numbers for baselines (e.g., LSD) differ significantly from their original papers (e.g., Shape accuracy 68.25% vs. 80.23% originally). This discrepancy needs thorough explanation and raises concerns about the validity of comparisons. [R5]
4. **Requires a priori knowledge of factor structure** – The method demands specification of which latent dimensions correspond to which factors (e.g., object identity, attribute type). The mixing strategies are tailored to the known ground‑truth factors of synthetic datasets; this limits applicability when factors are unknown or hard to define, and does not constitute a fully unsupervised approach. [R1, R3, R4]. **Reviewer 5 further questions whether the choice of mixing strategy is itself an inductive bias specific to objects or attributes, analogous to the biases the paper criticizes in prior work.** [R5]
5. **No joint attribute‑and‑object disentanglement experiments** – The evaluations are separate for each type; no experiment tests the ability to simultaneously disentangle attributes and objects in the same scene, leaving open the question of handling more complex scenarios with both types of factors. [R3, R4]
6. **Reliance on a pretrained diffusion model compromises interpretability and control** – The frozen diffusion model ensures composite image realism but does not guarantee alignment with the intended attribute/object combinations, especially for subtle or hierarchical relationships. The compositional consistency loss may be insufficient to enforce faithful latent‑to‑image correspondence. [R1, R4] *Reviewer 1 also asks: “why the maximum likelihood term is needed despite already enforcing low reconstruction error?”* This question is included here as it overlaps with a concern about necessity/justification of a model component.
7. **No identifiability guarantees** – The paper does not provide theoretical guarantees that the latent representations are identifiable, meaning the learned representations may not correspond to meaningful disentangled factors but could be arbitrary encodings that happen to yield realistic images. This limits interpretability and control. [R4] *Reviewer 4 also asks: “Under which conditions could your method provide identifiability guarantees?”* This is included as a missing analysis.
8. **Fixed mixing strategies are rigid** – The mixing strategies are hand‑designed for the simple cases studied; they do not adapt to more complex compositional structures (e.g., hierarchical relationships) and would likely need to be learned from data for real‑world scenes. [R4, R5] *Reviewer 5 further asks for qualitative examples when images contain two identical objects, to illustrate potential confusion.* [R5]
9. **Incomplete reporting of experimental setup** – Attribute disentanglement results include standard deviations but the number of runs is not stated; object disentanglement results lack standard deviations entirely. [R5]
10. **Minor writing issues and inconsistencies** – Numerous typos, grammatical problems, and inconsistencies (e.g., $L_{con}$ vs. $L_{cycle}$) are noted; specific lines include: line 34, 46, 70, 77, 97, 107, 122, 147, 163, 165, 167, 177, 188, 190, 197, 206, 216, 259, 295, 307, 310, and others. [R1, R4, R5]
11. **Additional questions requesting clarifications or comparisons** – (a) How would the method scale to dozens or hundreds of disentangled factors? [R3] (b) Could the mixing strategy be learned dynamically instead of fixed? [R4] (c) Provide details on the weighting function $w(t)$ and why it was omitted from the appendix. [R5] (d) Compare quantitatively with the alternative prior term from Jung et al. (2024) that avoids training an extra diffusion model. [R5] (e) Clarify the fairness of computing DCI after PCA – does this not rotate the dimensions and change the interpretation of disentanglement? [R5]
