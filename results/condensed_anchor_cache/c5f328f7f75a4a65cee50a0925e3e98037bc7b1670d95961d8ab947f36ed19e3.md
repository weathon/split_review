## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LoRA Dropout, a structured dropout applied to the rows and columns of LoRA’s low-rank matrices, intended as a sparsity regularizer to reduce overfitting. It includes a test-time ensemble strategy (MC-dropout style) to further improve generalization. Empirical evaluations are conducted on GLUE, SQuAD, MMLU, and Vicuna-Eval benchmarks using DeBERTaV3 and LLaMA2-7B.  
*Reviewer sentiment is mixed:* Reviewers 1 (score 6) and 4 (score 6) find the work well‑executed and empirically convincing, while Reviewers 2 (score 3) and 5 (score 3) consider the theory flawed and not novel; Reviewer 3 (score 5) praises the empirical side but calls the theoretical part weak and distracting.

### Strengths
- The idea is simple, practical, and intuitively sensible – applying dropout to the rows/columns of the low‑rank matrices avoids memory limitations of prior dropout methods. (R2, R3)
- Endorsement of the design choice to **not** apply dropout along the rank dimension. (R5)
- The empirical evaluation is comprehensive and convincing; in particular, Figure 5 is praised for directly addressing a key question. (R3)
- Improvements on DeBERTaV3 and LLaMA2‑7B over GLUE, SQuAD, MMLU, and Vicuna‑Eval are statistically significant. (R4)
- The paper is generally well‑structured, clear, and easy to follow. (R1, R2, R4)
- The method shows practical gains: LoRA Dropout helps existing methods (e.g., LoRA) achieve better performance. (R5)
- The theoretical analysis is **seen as thorough** by R1 (but strongly criticized by others – see Weaknesses). (R1)
- The test‑time ensemble strategy is a novel combination that can boost performance. (R2)

### Weaknesses
**Theoretical flaws and lack of novelty**

- **Assumptions too strong**: The analysis relies on η‑Lipschitz continuity and positive semi‑definiteness of the Hessian, which do not hold for typical non‑convex loss landscapes of deep networks. The authors do not discuss the magnitude of η; for large models (e.g., Llama 2) η could be very large, making the bounds vacuous. (R1, R2)
- **Proposition 2.2 is unsound**: “Closeness” between $\theta_L(S^i)$ and $\theta_L(S)$ is not clearly defined, and the proof uses a Taylor expansion that ignores the $O(\|\cdot\|^2)$ error term – a critical flaw. (R2)
- **Proposition 3.1 requires convexity**: The proof of the ensemble benefit assumes the loss is convex in the parameters (Jensen’s inequality), which is unrealistic for deep neural networks. (R2)
- **Eq. (1) is nonsensical / trivial**: The constraint is an equality that forces $\delta\theta=0$ (or equivalently reduces to a scaled L2 penalty), so the dropout rate merely rescales the regularization strength and offers no insight into dropout’s mechanism. (R3) – Reviewer 3 notes this can be fixed by an inequality constraint but the core issue remains.
- **Theorem 2.4 does not involve dropout**: The bound does not explicitly depend on the presence of dropout; the same argument could be made without it. (R3) – R5 adds that the theory in Section 2 is essentially a re‑derivation of results from [1] (Srivastava et al. 2014) with only a change of variables, presenting no new contribution.
- **Mismatch between theory and practice**: Section 2 discusses dropout on the full parameter vector (Bernoulli noise), while the actual algorithm (Section 3.2) uses structured dropout on the rows/columns of low‑rank matrices. This discrepancy undermines the theoretical analysis. (R3)
- **Missing discussion of other dropout methods**: No comparison or differentiation from existing approaches like DropKey, DropAttention, HiddenCut. (R1)

**Experimental limitations**

- **Limited model and task scope**: The method is evaluated only on DeBERTaV3 and LLaMA2‑7B (and only one task for the latter). More recent models are absent. (R4)
- **Narrow coverage of LoRA variants**: Experiments are restricted to LoRA and AdaLoRA; no results for LoRA+, VeRA, LoRA‑fa, etc. (R1)
- **Ablation studies lack statistical rigor**: The ablation (e.g., Figure 4) is performed only on MRPC and CoLA without standard deviations or test‑statistics. (R4)
- **Computational cost is high and under‑reported**: Training time increases drastically (e.g., CoLa: 18 min → 60 min; Table C.2 shows almost double). Inference with the ensemble strategy also adds overhead, but no quantitative data (per‑epoch overhead, latency) are provided. (R1, R4, R5) – R3 asks for the practical cost of test‑time ensembling.
- **Scalability not addressed**: It is unclear how the runtime and efficiency scale with larger model sizes; no recommendations are given. (R4)

**Writing and clarity issues**

- Several notations are undefined (e.g., $\mathbf{\theta}_L$), and important concepts (“closeness”, η‑Lipschitzness) are not explicitly defined. (R2)
- Equations contain mistakes: Eq. (1) and (3) misuse function/problem definitions; Lemma 2.3 and Theorem 2.4 may incorrectly represent the empirical/expected loss. (R2)
- Multiple typos (e.g., “faces” → “faces”, “introduce” → “introduce”, duplicate “on”, inconsistent use of “ΔW”, etc.) give an impression of rushed preparation. (R5)
- The introduction lacks logical coherence; the motivation (relationship between high‑rank overfitting and LoRA Dropout) is unclear, and the claim that AdaLoRA addresses high‑rank overfitting is disputed (R5).

**Remaining questions from reviewers**

- How should the dropout rate and rank be configured? (R5) – Also, the rank used for LoRA Dropout in Figure 4 is not stated (R3).
- Why does masking rows (vs. columns) improve performance? (R5)
- Why is increasing sparsity beneficial beyond a certain point? (R5)
- Does the original LoRA dropout (already present) play a role during training with LoRA Dropout? (R5)
