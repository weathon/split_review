## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes EvA, a post-hoc method for mitigating spurious correlations by erasing specific dimensions in the penultimate embedding space. Two metrics are introduced: “consistency” (EvA-C) when an unbiased dataset is available, and “evidence energy” (EvA-E) when no unbiased data exists. Spurious dimensions are identified via thresholding these metrics, their corresponding weights in the last linear layer are zeroed, and the remaining weights are retrained (logistic regression). The method is computationally efficient and yields strong improvements on standard benchmarks, especially in the absence of unbiased training data.

### Strengths

- **Post-hoc efficiency**: The method involves no additional training or heavy computational load—it identifies spurious dimensions, erases them, and solves a logistic regression. This is a major advantage over methods that require multiple rounds of retraining.
- **Two practical spuriousness metrics**: Consistency (Eq. 4) and evidence energy (Eq. 7) allow detection of spurious feature channels, with and without an unbiased dataset. These measures are well-motivated and supported by theoretical analysis.
- **Strong experimental results**: EvA achieves substantial gains over prior bias mitigation methods. For example, 6.2% relative gain on BAR and 4.8% relative gain on Waterbirds when no unbiased data is available. Gains are especially significant in the absence of unbiased training data.
- **Data efficiency**: The unbiased variant (EvA-C) requires fewer unbiased samples than comparable methods (e.g., DFR) to reach the same accuracy.
- **Validating the approach**: Experiments on CMNIST show that erased features indeed correspond to spurious ones, and a correlation between evidence energy and consistency is demonstrated (though only for one class of one dataset).

### Weaknesses

- **Feature independence assumption is flawed**: Both consistency and evidence energy are computed independently per feature dimension. However, features in deep networks can be in superposition (references [a,b]). Consequently, turning off a full dimension may (i) partially erase core features that are entangled with a spurious feature, and (ii) fail to fully erase the spurious feature if it is spread across multiple dimensions.
- **Sample imbalance in consistency metric (Eq. 4)**: The unbiased dataset is typically much smaller than the biased dataset. This skews the distance measure d, potentially making the consistency metric unreliable. The paper does not address how this imbalance is handled.
- **Missing ablation for feature selection**: EvA uses thresholding on consistency/evidence energy to decide which dimensions to erase. A comparison with simple baselines (e.g., random dimension dropout) is needed to justify that the chosen metrics are optimal.
- **Discrepancy in DFR baseline**: The reported DFR worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds (Table 1) is significantly lower than the original DFR paper. This undermines the validity of comparisons.
- **SOTA comparison is not always favorable**: Despite the claim in Line 93, EvA does not beat all prior methods on ResNet‑50. For example, EvA‑E achieves 86.6% worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds (Table 7), whereas [2] reports 89.1%. The primary use of ResNet‑18 (rather than the more common ResNet‑50) makes many numbers in the main paper not directly comparable to the literature.
- **Missing baselines in main table**: Methods such as JTT, SSA, AFR, and [3] (which also reweights the last layer without group labels) are omitted from Table 5. Including them would allow a fairer assessment.
- **Unbiased dataset definition and group label requirement**: It is unclear what constitutes an “unbiased” set (first introduced Line 156). In CelebA, the validation set itself has gender bias. If D_unbiased is group-balanced (equal distribution across spurious features), then group annotations are needed—contradicting the claim that the method requires no group labels (Lines 50‑51). Similarly, the approximation φ_test ≈ φ_unbiased in Eq. 4 is questionable because some test sets (e.g., CelebA) are also biased.
- **Hyperparameter (ε) selection**: 
    - For EvA‑E without unbiased data, no clear procedure is given for choosing ε. 
    - With unbiased data, hyperparameter sweeps across ε can become time-consuming despite a single forward pass being cheap.
    - The paper tunes hyperparameters based on Mean Accuracy rather than Worst Accuracy (Appendix E.2). This is debatable for the setting where DFR is applicable (which assumes an unbiased validation set). It is also unclear how hyperparameters were tuned for ResNet‑18 (mean vs. worst accuracy).
- **Lack of explanation why EvA‑C outperforms DFR**: Both methods use an unbiased dataset and retrain the last layer, yet EvA‑C achieves better results. The paper does not analyze why erasing spurious channels before retraining is more effective.
- **Scalability and realism concerns**: 
    - Experiments are limited to small toy settings with strong spurious correlations. No evaluation is performed on ImageNet‑scale spurious correlation benchmarks (e.g., Salient ImageNet, Spurious Features Everywhere), where the distinction between core and spurious features is more complex.
    - The correlation between evidence energy and consistency is shown only for one class of one dataset, which is insufficient to generalize.
- **Minor issues**: Font sizes in Fig. 1, Tab. 3, Tab. 4 are too small. The term “energy” actually corresponds to “free energy,” which could confuse readers. The paper only briefly mentions the connection to OOD methods in the conclusion, missing a more thorough discussion in the main text.
- **Notable reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 1 (score 8, confidence 4) is significantly more positive about the experimental gains and contribution, while Reviewers 2‑4 (scores 6) raise concerns about baselines, hyperparameter choices, and comparability with prior work. This contrast should be noted.
