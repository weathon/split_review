## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a non-adaptive layer-wise learning rate scheme, where each layer’s learning rate is preset based on the inverse of its gradient magnitude measured at fan-in initialization. The method is tested on convolutional (ResNet-50) and transformer architectures for image classification and autoregressive language modeling, showing improved training stability and convergence over a single global learning rate. Reviewers find the idea simple and the empirical observations interesting, but raise major concerns about novelty, theoretical grounding, experimental thoroughness, and the practical significance of the improvements.

### Strengths

- The proposed layer-wise learning rate assignment is **straightforward to implement** and does not require per-step curvature or second-order moment storage like Adam, reducing computational and memory overhead.
- The method **only requires the gradient magnitude at initialization**, avoiding the cumulative overhead of adaptive optimizers.
- The paper **identifies key empirical observations** (differences in gradient magnitude across layers at initialization) and combines them with findings from prior work on layer heterogeneity.
- Experiments are conducted on **multiple architectures** (ResNet-50, ViT, transformer) across different tasks and datasets.
- Compared to standard SGD, the layer-wise scheme **improves training stability** and **eases convergence for complex networks** on large datasets.
- One reviewer (score 5) emphasizes that the approach is **conceptually distinct from per-parameter adaptive methods** like Adam, and offers a lighter alternative.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**: Very similar ideas appear in prior work, e.g., Zhang et al. (2024) “Why transformers need Adam” (block-wise Hessian spectra) and “Adam-mini” (fewer learning rates). The evidence provided here is **primarily intuitive** and lacks a direct comparison to these methods.
- **No theoretical justification**: The paper provides no convergence analysis, no generalization error bounds, and no explanation for why the gradient magnitude at initialization should be a good proxy for appropriate learning rates throughout training. Without theory, it is unclear why the method works or when it might fail.
- **Lack of fundamental motivation**: Adam computes a preconditioner based on a Gauss-Newton estimate of the Hessian at every step (albeit approximated). The proposed method **does not use any curvature information** beyond the initial gradient, which may not correlate with curvature later. The motivation to “mitigate Adam’s computation time” is insufficient without justifying why the initial gradient suffices.
- **Marginal empirical gains**: Improvements over SGD or AdamW are small (e.g., 0.50% or 0.96%). These are **not substantial** compared to established regularizers like SAM or noise injection, which often outperform base optimizers by larger margins. It is **uncertain whether the gains are solely from the layer-wise lr or from interactions with cosine scheduling, small batch sizes, or other tricks**.
- **Insufficient experimental comparisons**: The evaluation does not compare against modern optimizers such as **Adam-Mini, Sophia, LARS, or LAMB** that also address learning rate heterogeneity or large-batch training. Missing comparisons limit assessment of relative performance and overhead.
- **Sensitivity to weight initialization**: The empirical results may depend heavily on the choice of weight initialization scheme. No experiments with different initializations are provided, so the claimed gradient magnitude patterns may not be robust.
- **Overhead of prior training steps**: The method requires several steps to approximate the gradient trajectory before setting static rates, potentially increasing total training time. The paper does not theoretically justify the required number of prior steps.
- **Empirical analysis misaligned with justification**: For ResNet-50, the paper states that using T=1 instead of 5004 results in a 2.66% average deviation in assigned learning rates, but the main experiments (Figure 1) run for 90 epochs – far longer than T=1. This inconsistency is not addressed.
- **Unclear definitions and possible typos**:  
  - Algorithm 1, lines 10–11: the sum may cancel out (possible typo).  
  - The index *t* is ambiguous: if it denotes a minibatch, the learning rate *L* should be indexed by *t* as well.  
  - The phrase “low learning rates to low-level layers and high to high-level layers” is undefined for different architectures (e.g., does “low-level” mean initial convolutional layers? How is it applied in transformers?).
- **Need for better intuitive justification**: No analysis of why the method improves training (e.g., plotting sharpness, correlation of layer size with learning rate). An ablation testing whether scaling by layer dimension alone achieves similar results is missing.
- **Remaining questions that overlap with criticisms** (require experiments or explanation):  
  - Why does the method work with the Lion optimizer, which already normalizes gradients via the sign operation? Provide theoretical rationale.  
  - What is the fundamental novelty compared to Zhang et al. (2024) and Kunstner et al. (2023) (on sign descent)?  
  - Are there ablation tests using different weight initialization mechanisms?  
  - Why is layer-wise rescaling used without considering the weight initialization scheme already in place?  
  - Can the number of prior steps be determined theoretically?  
  - Why not test with large batch sizes and optimizers designed for that setting (LARS, LAMB)?  

**Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 5) acknowledges the strengths and finds the method promising, but still raises the most substantial criticisms above (weak motivation, marginal improvement, missing justification). The other two reviewers (score 3) are more skeptical about novelty and empirical conclusiveness. All three agree on the need for stronger theoretical backing and more rigorous experiments.
