## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a surgical, training-free method for mitigating false refusal in LLMs via single vector ablation. The method extracts a false refusal vector from model activations using pseudo-harmful and harmless queries, then orthogonalizes it with a true refusal vector (from harmful vs. harmless queries) to avoid harming true refusal ability. Ablating this orthogonalized vector during inference reduces false refusal rates while preserving safety and general capabilities. Partial orthogonalization enables fine-grained calibration. Experiments on GEMMA-7B-IT, Llama2-7B-Chat, Llama3-8B-Chat, and Llama-2-13B-Chat across ORB-H, XSTest-S(H), OKTest, and HH-RLHF show increased compliance on false refusal datasets without significant safety degradation. The method is model-agnostic and claims no additional inference cost. Reviewers note the approach is simple, justified, and the fine-grained control via λ is useful. However, concerns are raised about theoretical justification, inference cost claims, lack of comparison to training-based methods, clarity of definitions and equations, sensitivity to query construction and data sources, greedy decoding assumptions, and limited evaluation on larger models and architectures.

### Strengths

1. **Important Problem**: Mitigating false refusals directly impacts LLM usability and reliability in real-world applications.
2. **Novel Approach**: The idea of using single vector ablation and orthogonalization to disentangle false from true refusal behaviors is innovative and contributes to LLM safety work.
3. **Surgical & Simple**: The approach is simple, well-justified, training-free, and model-agnostic, requiring no extra computation resources or memory during inference.
4. **Fine-Grained Control**: Partial orthogonalization (λ coefficient) allows tuning sensitivity to ambiguous queries, balancing over-restrictiveness and permissiveness; demonstrated quantitatively and qualitatively.
5. **Comprehensive Experiments**: Broad evaluation across multiple models (GEMMA-7B-IT, various Llama variants) and datasets (ORB-H, XSTest-S(H), OKTest, HH-RLHF, MMLU, GSM8K) demonstrates effectiveness and generalizability. The method effectively reduces false refusal while maintaining performance on general tasks and proper true refusals.
6. **Ablation Understanding**: The paper includes experiments enhancing understanding of different factors (e.g., effect of partial orthogonalization, system prompts).
7. **Identification of Non-Independence**: The authors correctly identify that true and false refusal vectors are not independent and apply orthogonalization to address this.

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient Theoretical Justification**: The paper lacks a robust theoretical framework explaining why single vector ablation and orthogonalization selectively target false refusals while preserving true refusals and general capabilities. There is no clear explanation of why a vector from pseudo-harmful queries would capture false refusal, nor theoretical guarantees or bounds supporting the approach. The reliance on orthogonalization lacks a theoretical basis in linear algebra or representation theory.
2. **Inference Cost Claim Is Inaccurate**: The claim that inference cost does not change is questionable because the operation in Equation (4) is applied at all layers. The paper does not report inference time or memory consumption numbers in Table 3, and should provide them to support the claim.
3. **Missing Comparison to Training-Based Methods**: The paper argues against training-based methods but does not compare to them as baselines. Such comparison would help understand performance gaps.
4. **Only Partial Mitigation**: False refusals are significantly reduced but not eliminated. The paper does not explore limitations in detail (e.g., types of queries that still fail) or discuss potential adversarial attacks exploiting the method’s weaknesses.
5. **Clarity & Presentation Issues**:
   - The definitions in Section 2.1 (lines 60-62) are unclear: how do harmless and harmful queries look? Are they minimal pairs? What is the physical meaning of average output over queries at a token position when lengths and tokens vary? Is the [/INST] token the only token position chosen for refusal vector extraction?
   - Equation (3): definition of \( p_t \) (token t’s probability at first token position) is omitted; thresholding process and sensitivity are unclear.
   - Potential typos: line 116 wrong subscript in Equation (7); line 182 three repeated \( D^{train}_{harmful} \); line 469 "BLUE score" should be "BLEU score".
   - No workflow figure to illustrate the process of extracting and ablating false refusal vectors.
6. **Sensitivity to Query Construction**: The method’s success depends on the selection of pseudo-harmful queries. The paper does not study sensitivity to different pseudo-harmful datasets. Samples of harmful, pseudo-harmful, and harmless come from different sources; it is unclear if data content or domain differences affect the method.
7. **Greedy Decoding Limitation**: Section 4 uses greedy decoding for text generation, which is not common in practice. It would be interesting to see how sampling decoding affects effectiveness.
8. **System Prompt Issue**: Section 5.1 shows Llama2-7B-Chat and Llama3-8B-Chat perform worse when adding the Llama2 system prompt; the reason is not explained.
9. **Impact on General Capabilities**: Even after vector ablation, general capability metrics (acc. or ppl.) still drop slightly (Table 2), despite significant CR improvements. The paper does not fully explain this.
10. **Assumption About Pseudo-Harmful Queries**: The method assumes all pseudo-harmful queries are treated as harmful by the model. For pseudo-harmful queries that the model already treats as harmless, the difference from truly harmful queries may not create a meaningful false refusal direction.
11. **Limited Scaling Evidence**: Experiments are on models up to 13B parameters. It is unclear how the method scales to larger models (e.g., 175B) or different architectures.
12. **Missing Comparison to Other Interventions**: The paper does not compare or discuss similarities/differences with other intervention techniques like causal tracing (e.g., Meng et al. 2022). Extension to other intervention methods is not discussed.
13. **Equation (5) Relevance Unclear**: It is not directly obvious how Equation (5) relates to the proposed method for reducing false refusals.

*Note: The above includes reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms, missing experiments, or requests for ablation (e.g., sensitivity to λ, data sources, decoding strategy, system prompt, thresholding, scaling, causal interventions). The minority reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 5) is more critical on presentation and clarity, which is preserved in weaknesses 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, and 13.*
