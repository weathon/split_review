## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Phase Transition Model Zoos, a structured collection of trained neural networks (ResNet-18/50 and ViT on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, TinyImageNet) with multiple checkpoints, covering a grid of model widths (load-like) and batch sizes (temperature-like). The dataset includes loss landscape metrics (Hessian trace/eigenvalues, mode connectivity, CKA) alongside performance metrics. The authors show that phases emerge across the population and demonstrate phase-related patterns in fine-tuning, transfer learning, pruning, ensembling, and weight averaging.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and organized, providing a clear and accessible background for readers unfamiliar with phase transitions (R1, R2).
- The curated dataset is a large-scale, structured resource focused specifically on phase transitions, and includes not just performance metrics but also meaningful observables like Hessian statistics and loss-landscape metrics. This could accelerate research by allowing direct use without retraining a large number of models (R1, R2, R4).
- The work demonstrates phase transitions across a more diverse set of architectures/datasets (ResNet, ViT; CIFAR, SVHN, TinyImageNet) compared to prior work, and across multiple applications (fine-tuning, transfer learning, pruning, weight averaging, ensembling) (R3, R4).
- The work encourages evaluating methods across different model phases, promoting more robust evaluation than single-point comparisons. Some analysis provides insight into how components like learning rate decay and data augmentation impact phases (R3).
- The heavy empirical results (Section 3 showing phases across populations, Section 4 relating phases to diverse training methods) are a strength (R4).

*Note: Reviewers 1–2 are more positive about the dataset’s value; Reviewers 3–4 consider it primarily an extension of prior work lacking substantial novelty, with Reviewer 4 expecting more theoretical rigor.*

### Weaknesses
**Scope and Generality**
- The model zoos are restricted to computer vision classification models (ResNet, ViT) and relatively small-scale datasets (CIFAR, SVHN, TinyImageNet). Including other domains (text, audio) or larger models/datasets (e.g., ImageNet) would increase impact and applicability (R1, R2, R3).
- Only two hyperparameters are varied: batch size (single temperature-like parameter) and model width (single load-like parameter). This makes it impossible to disentangle whether conclusions are specific to batch size/width or generalize to other temperature/load parameters (e.g., learning rate schedule, optimizer; number of layers, hidden dimensionality). The lack of diversity in hyperparameter types limits the conclusions that can be drawn (R2).
- The grid resolution (7–8 values per parameter) is low; it may be insufficient to distinguish smooth changes from genuine phase transitions (sharp edges). Higher resolution, as in Yang et al. (2001), would improve detection reliability (R2).

**Robustness of Conclusions and Demonstrations**
- The number of random seeds (three) used for averaging may be insufficient to substantiate claims and could impact robustness (R1).
- Many conclusions are drawn from very few examples, often only 2–3, and are weakened by counter-examples:
  - “Fine-tuned models exhibit clear phase transitions and phases overlap substantially with pre-trained models” is based on Figures 4, 9, 11 (only 2–3 examples; Figure 11 caption actually refers to weight averaging) (R2).
  - “Choosing the best pre-trained model does not result in the best transfer-learned model” is taken from Figures 5, 15, 16. In Figure 16, the best test accuracy occurs at the same point as best fine-tuning accuracy (batch size 128 and 256 width), contradicting the claim. The distinction between fine-tuning and transfer learning is unclear: all subplots are labeled “Fine-tuning Test Acc” (R2).
  - On pruning, “Models pre-trained in higher-temperature phases tend to perform better post-pruning” is based on a single example (Figure 6), and batch size 256 appears as a counter-example (R2).
  - On weight averaging, “Averaging over epochs improves performance in early phases where Hessian trace is large” – but Figure 14 shows negative Δ Test Acc Wise-FT where Hessian trace is large (top right), a counter-example (R2).
- Ablation experiments are missing: for pruning, varying the pruning ratio would help support claims (R4); for fine-tuning/transfer learning, different numbers of frozen layers (e.g., last block only) would clarify the settings (R4).
- The work primarily extends earlier findings to a broader set of models/datasets, but lacks substantial novelty beyond that extension (R3).

**Theoretical and Conceptual Depth**
- The term “phase transitions” is used without sufficient rigor. The prior study providing the basic setting is nearly five years old; more theoretical discussion is expected (e.g., what is the order parameter? Is the transition continuous or discontinuous?). Adding such discussion would improve concreteness for the general audience (R4).
- Where is the phase “transition” itself? The study discriminates distinct phases (e.g., Phase IV-A vs. IV-B), but an investigation of the transition curve between them (e.g., using CKA as order parameter and temperature as control parameter) is not provided (R4).

**Practical Utility**
- No concrete application is demonstrated that shows how the dataset can be used to identify phase transitions in new methods. The description in Section 5 (“help researchers systematically identify phase transitions in their methods”) is vague, and the dataset feels more like a static collection than a tool readily applicable to new research problems without extensive retraining (R2, R3).
- For example, can phase information predict which model will achieve the best pruning/fine-tuning performance? The paper does not showcase such predictive power (R2).

**Dataset Size/Clarity**
- The large size may be a practical barrier. Can users access only specific parts of the zoo? (R1)
- The reported total of 1829 models does not match a calculation of 1830: (2 ResNets × 4 datasets × 8×8 + 1 ViT × 2 datasets × 7×7) × 3 seeds = 1830. Please clarify (R2).

**Errors, Inconsistencies, and Missing Details**
- Seed inconsistency: lines 242/665 state seeds {1,2,3}, but Table 1 uses {0,1,2} (R1, R3).
- Figure 5 caption says the bottom grid uses STL-10, but the plot says CIFAR-10 (R3).
- Figure 6 caption describes top subplot as “before pruning” and bottom as “after pruning”, but the top subplot shows mode connectivity (R4). Additionally, the top subplot may be mislabelled (R2).
- Line 286 missing space before citation (R2).
- Inconsistent capitalization (R2).
- Figure 1 lacks a color bar, making it hard to interpret abrupt vs. smooth changes given the low grid resolution (R2).
- Many plots are small and lack phase annotations on all subplots, reducing readability (R2).
- The Wise-FT method for weight averaging is not mentioned in the main text, only in supplements; it should be introduced in Section 4.5 (R4).
- Figure/table references appear mismatched: line 305 mentions Figures 9 and 11 as relating to fine-tuning, but their captions say weight averaging; similarly lines 332, 385 (R4). Please verify and correct.
- Line 256: does “Phase IV-B” mean “Phase IV-A”? (R2)
- Line 258–259 claim about learning rate decay reducing Phase II and increasing Phase IV: it is unclear how this conclusion is reached; please elaborate (R3).
