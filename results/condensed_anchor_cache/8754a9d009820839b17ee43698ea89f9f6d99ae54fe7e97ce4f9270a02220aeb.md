- Scores: 5, 5, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a hierarchical motion estimation scheme for video frame interpolation (VFI) that combines pixel-level and target-level motion by incorporating semantic priors from open-world models (RAM, Grounding DINO, HQ‑SAM). A hybrid contextual feature extraction module (HCE) and a hierarchical motion and feature interactive refinement module (HIR) are designed to integrate these priors. The approach is presented as a plug‑in for existing VFI methods (IFRNet, AMT, EMA, etc.) and is validated on VIMEO90K and SNU‑FILM, reporting PSNR/SSIM improvements. Two reviewers scored 5, two scored 3, indicating polarized judgements: the higher scores note plausibility but also marginal gains; the lower scores stress insufficient novelty, generalization concerns, and writing issues.

### Strengths
- Motivation is plausible and easy to grasp: marrying target‑level and pixel‑level motion to improve motion estimation consistency (R1, R2).
- Method explanation is clear, with thorough interpretation of formulas and modules (R1).
- Successful integration of multiple open‑world models (RAM, Grounding DINO, HQ‑SAM) into a coherent framework (R2).
- Modular plug‑in design demonstrated on four existing VFI architectures (R3).
- Validation on two well‑known VFI datasets with comparisons to recent SoTA methods (R2).
- Attempt to explicitly address motion estimation challenges in VFI is valuable (R4).
- Figures are clear (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty.** The core idea of leveraging existing semantic models (RAM, Grounding DINO, HQ‑SAM) to improve VFI is foreseeable and does not represent a significant conceptual advance (R1, R4). The theoretical justification in Sec. 3.1 merely recites standard VFI formulas without linking them to the introduced segmentation priors (R4).
- **Marginal performance gains relative to high computational overhead.** The reported PSNR/SSIM improvements are small, while the method adds substantial inference time and resource consumption due to three heavy models in series (RAM, DINO, HQ‑SAM). FLOPs are mentioned but not explained; no inference speed or per‑video runtime analysis (e.g., one object vs. many objects) is provided (R1, R2, R4). R1 and R4 explicitly ask for runtime experiments; R2 questions whether the gain justifies the cost.
- **Poor writing quality.** The manuscript contains many typos, grammatical errors, and unclear phrasing (abstract included). Specific errors: “Grounding DIDO” repeatedly used instead of “DINO” (R2, R4), and a missing value “xxxdB” on line 413 (R1). Core technical contributions (how aggregation works) are not illustrated clearly (R3).
- **Lack of necessary experiments and analyses.**
  - No upper‑bound study using ground‑truth semantic segmentation to show how much quality of segmentation matters (R3).
  - No quantitative evaluation of the detection and segmentation models (RAM, DINO, HQ‑SAM) on VFI datasets (e.g., mAP, mIoU) to confirm accurate object covering (R4).
  - No ablation isolating the effect of each open‑vocabulary model (R4).
  - No evaluation on diverse or challenging datasets (e.g., XVFI, GOPRO, ×4/×8 frame rates, non‑uniform motion, rotation) (R4).
  - Qualitative comparison (Fig. 6) lacks labels, zoom‑ins, and explanatory text, making it hard to discern intended differences (R1).
- **Generalization and robustness concerns.**
  - The input frames often contain fast‑moving objects with motion blur; segmentation models (HQ‑SAM, DINO, RAM) may fail on blurry frames, and the paper does not analyze this failure mode (R2).
  - Binary segmentation masks are used despite blurry object boundaries; the alternative of soft masks is not explored or justified (R2).
  - The method relies on pretrained open‑vocabulary models, limiting its capability to handle object categories not seen during training (R2). For non‑rigid objects, SAM cannot obtain masks for intermediate frame moments, yet this case is not addressed (R4).
  - The number of moving objects in a video is unknown; SAM’s per‑object segmentation imposes unbounded resource consumption and may limit generalization (R4).
- **Missing clarifications and comparisons.**
  - No comparison to other plug‑and‑play enhancement techniques to demonstrate relative superiority (R1).
  - When and why the method’s performance drops significantly (e.g., with fast motion, many objects) is not discussed (R3).
  - Effect of combining the proposed method with other types of models (beyond the four tested) is not shown (R4).

**Reviewer disagreement note:** Reviewers 1 and 2 (score 5) acknowledged the plausible motivation and modularity but still flagged marginal gains and missing experiments. Reviewers 3 and 4 (score 3) were more critical, emphasizing insufficient novelty, unclear technical contribution, generalization limits, and poor writing. The lower‑score reviewers also demanded stronger experimental evidence (upper‑bound, segmentation metrics, broader datasets, runtime analysis) and questioned the value of introducing SAM given resource cost and rigidity assumptions.
