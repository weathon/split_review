## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes ColorCubeNet, a deep learning framework for Presentation Attack Detection (PAD) on mobile devices that addresses skin tone disparities. It constructs a 9‑channel “ColorCube” representation by combining RGB, HSV, and YCbCr color spaces, uses three EfficientNet‑B0 models fine‑tuned on this cube, and integrates a channel‑attention mechanism. Evaluated on six face and fingerprint PAD datasets, the method reportedly reduces skin tone bias by 50% compared to traditional RGB‑based models.

### Strengths
- **Addresses an important fairness gap** – The work tackles the underexplored problem of skin tone bias in biometric PAD, a critical social and security issue. (R1, R3)
- **Original application of multi‑color‑space fusion** – Combining RGB, HSV, and YCbCr into a unified ColorCube to improve skin‑tone inclusivity is a novel adaptation of existing color space theories for PAD. (R1)
- **Effective on both face and fingerprint PAD** – The model is shown to work across different biometric modalities, even though trained separately on each. (R2)
- **Validated on multiple datasets** – Experimental evaluation covers six datasets, achieving a 50% reduction in skin tone disparity. (R1, R3)
- **Good readability** – Paper is well‑structured and easy to follow. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty** – The approach relies on standard deep‑learning components (EfficientNet‑B0, channel attention) without deep justification for their suitability to skin‑tone bias. (R1) The core idea of using multiple color channels to improve PAD performance has been explored in prior work (e.g., Boulkenafet et al. 2017, He & Luo 2019, residual color texture representations) and the present paper does not clearly differentiate its method from those or from the related approach by Marasco & Vurity (2022). (R2, R3)
- **No comparison with state‑of‑the‑art PAD methods** – Baseline comparisons are limited to old CNNs; results are not benchmarked against leading PAD models from top conferences (e.g., Fang et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2024, Fang & Damer 2024). A fair SOTA comparison is essential to demonstrate competitive advantage. (R1, R2)
- **Lack of cross‑dataset generalizability** – Experiments are performed only within individual datasets; no cross‑dataset evaluation is conducted to assess robustness and fairness across different acquisition conditions. (R2)
- **Insufficient dataset and fairness analysis** – The paper does not report dataset size, skin‑tone distribution (e.g., ITA categories), or class balance. Underrepresentation of certain skin tones could skew results, but this is not analyzed. (R3) The role of skin‑tone labeling in training is unclear, and experiments under unseen skin‑tone conditions are not described. (R2) The proposed fairness metric (ITA‑based skin tone disparity) would benefit from additional quantification methods. (R3)
- **Missing ablation and interpretability studies** – No ablation study isolating the contribution of each color space individually or comparing score‑level vs feature‑level fusion. (R2) The SNR analysis provided is not clearly linked to bias; the paper would be strengthened by saliency maps or Grad‑CAM. (R1) The input features used for PCA are not specified (raw 9‑channel image vs. intermediate representations). (R1)
- **No computational cost analysis** – Runtime on mobile hardware is not reported, despite being critical for real‑world deployment. (R2, R3) All reviewers request efficiency metrics such as latency or throughput.
- **Missing standard PAD metrics** – Performance is reported only with selected metrics; the paper should include widely used measures like EER and HTER. (R2)
- **Choice of color spaces not justified** – The selection of RGB, HSV, YCbCr lacks theoretical or empirical justification; the perceptual uniform LAB space is a plausible alternative that should be tested in an ablation. (R1)
- **Disagreement on contribution magnitude** – While all three reviewers note limited novelty, Reviewer 3 (score 5) sees the work as addressing a critical gap, whereas Reviewers 1 and 2 (score 3) view the contribution as incremental and the paper as not aligned with ICLR’s core focus on fundamental deep learning advances. This contrast is preserved but does not change the consensus that technical differentiation and broader evaluations are needed.
