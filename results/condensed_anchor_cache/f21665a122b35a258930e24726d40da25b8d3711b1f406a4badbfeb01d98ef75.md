## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses memorization in text-guided diffusion models, where generated images sometimes replicate training data (e.g., the “Afghan girl” incident). The key observation is that for memorized prompts the text-conditional noise prediction has a high magnitude; for non-memorized prompts the magnitude is low. Using this, the authors propose a detection method based on the L2 norm of the text-conditional noise term divided by the number of sampling steps. The method works without access to training data, can detect memorization from the very first generation step, and achieves an AUC of 0.999 with low latency. Building on this, they introduce a way to identify “trigger tokens” by analyzing the gradient of the text‑conditional magnitude with respect to each token embedding, enabling users to modify or remove those tokens. Two mitigation strategies are proposed: inference‑time minimization of the text‑conditional magnitude (producing a perturbed prompt embedding) and training‑time filtering of memorized image‑text pairs. Experiments use the 500 memorized prompts from Webster 2023 for Stable Diffusion v1 (SSCD similarity >0.7) and compare against Carlini 2023 (and a variant using SSCD distance). The detection method outperforms baselines on AUC and true positive rate at 1% false positive rate. The mitigation strategies are evaluated against random token addition and show improved generation quality while reducing memorization.

### Strengths

- The detection method is based on a simple and clever observation (the impact of the text prompt on generation is higher for memorized prompts) and is computationally efficient. It does not require access to training data, multiple generations, or many sampling steps – reliable detection is possible even from the first step.
- The method achieves very high detection performance: an AUC of 0.999 with small latency, and better true positive rate at 1% false positive rate compared to baselines (L2 and SSCD metrics).
- The work provides an automated approach to identify “trigger tokens” – the specific tokens in a prompt that drive memorization – by evaluating the change in gradient when minimizing the text‑conditional prediction magnitude. This gives model owners a practical tool to advise users on prompt modification.
- Two mitigation strategies are proposed: (1) inference‑time minimization of the text‑conditional magnitude to obtain a perturbed prompt embedding; (2) training‑time filtering of potentially memorized image‑text pairs. Both strategies significantly reduce memorization while maintaining generation quality, outperforming the baseline of random token addition.
- The paper is a strong, enjoyable read and makes a significant contribution to the field of memorization in diffusion models.

### Weaknesses

- **Writing clarity**: The introduction is not very well written; Section 3.2 (motivation) was particularly helpful for setting the pace. One reviewer (R3) asked for clarifications: how is the metric computed with multiple generations? What does the “First 10 steps” column in Table 1 indicate? What is meant by “a perturbed prompt embedding e* is obtained as t=0 by minimizing Eq (5)” – is this minimization done via gradient descent, and on what data (the 200 LAION points? what are Figures 4(a) and 4(b) plotted over?).

- **Definition of memorization**: The paper should provide a clear definition of what constitutes memorization. Does it require an exact match between generated and training images? If a generated image differs slightly (e.g., 10 pixels, a different color palette, or a small object repositioned), is it still considered memorized? A threshold or metric (e.g., SSCD similarity >0.7) is used, but the rationale and practical implications need explanation.

- **Impact of mitigation on overall generation quality**: The mitigation strategies lead to a notable drop in CLIP score. In inference‑time mitigation, the CLIP score falls significantly below the score of the un‑fine‑tuned model (e.g., points that are between 0.29–0.3 in the un‑fine‑tuned model drop to lower values). It is unclear whether the method remains useful given this trade‑off. A more detailed analysis of how mitigation affects metrics such as FID, Inception Score, and CLIP score on a broad set of prompts (not only memorization cases) is needed.

- **Lack of interpretability in detection**: The detection method requires model owners to select an empirical threshold based on a predetermined false positive rate. The outcomes lack clear interpretability. A method that produces interpretable p‑values or confidence scores (e.g., using hypothesis testing or confidence intervals) would significantly aid trust and practical deployment.

- **Practical user experience of trigger‑token modification**: Advising users to modify or omit trigger tokens may be cumbersome in practice. Users may need to understand what trigger tokens are and how they affect the output. The paper should discuss the need for user education and provide examples of how different trigger tokens affect generated images. Moreover, it assumes that all prompts can be modified; in real‑world scenarios some prompts may be non‑negotiable. Alternative strategies (e.g., modifying the latent space or the diffusion process itself) should be considered.

- **Computational overhead**: While the method is described as efficient, implementing these strategies during inference and training may still introduce computational or operational overheads. A detailed analysis of memory usage, runtime, and a cost breakdown for detection, mitigation, and training phases is needed.

- **Missing experiments / ablations**:
  - The paper would benefit from experiments showing how the memorization ratio changes with the number of repetitions in the dataset (following the setup of Somepalli et al.).
  - Only two images of memorized inputs are shown; more examples and discussion would help evaluate the method’s performance.
  - One reviewer (R4) asks whether adversarial tests were performed to see if an attacker could still exploit memorization after mitigation.
  - Another reviewer (R4) asks if certain tokens are more susceptible to triggering memorization and whether a taxonomy or classification of trigger tokens exists.

- **Generalizability**: The application scenario for detecting memorized images is not fully discussed. What are the key practical use cases where identifying such images is important? (R2’s question). One reviewer (R4) notes that a discussion on how future methods could quantify memorization for benchmarking would be useful.

- **Reviewer confidence**: Reviewer 4 had lower confidence (2/4) than the other reviewers, particularly around the soundness of the claims and the need for more rigorous validation of the detection and mitigation strategies. This contrast should be noted; while all ratings are 8, the lower confidence suggests that some points (especially the impact on model utility and the lack of interpretability) may require stronger evidence.
