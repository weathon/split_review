## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces CS-Bench, a multilingual (English, Chinese, French, German) benchmark of approximately 10,000 test samples covering 26 subfields across four key CS areas (Data Structures and Algorithms, Computer Organization, Computer Networks, Operating Systems). It includes multiple task formats: multiple-choice, assertion, fill-in-the-blank, and open-ended questions. Over 30 LLMs (both open- and closed-source) are evaluated. The benchmark sources about 70% of its content from private sources not previously on the internet (university exams, teaching materials, blog articles). The paper provides extensive analysis including scale-performance scaling laws, error analysis, and cross-capability correlations between CS, math, and coding.

### Strengths
- Comprehensive and deep focus on a single domain (CS) rather than shallow breadth, improving over existing benchmarks like MMLU. The breadth of subfields and task formats (four response types) allows nuanced probing of knowledge and reasoning.
- Multilingual design facilitates testing generalization of CS knowledge across languages and helps measure contamination (since most training data is English).
- Extensive evaluation across model scales and over 30 LLMs, including both open- and closed-source. The logarithmic scaling law analysis is interesting.
- Commendable error analysis that identifies knowledge gaps and reasoning challenges, providing richer insights than simple aggregate scores.
- Sourcing from university exams and blogs, with ~70% from private sources not on the internet, adds novelty and reduces contamination risk from publicly available data.
- Focus on reasoning vs. knowledge questions is a good step toward comprehensive CS evaluation.

### Weaknesses
- **Purpose and novelty unclear to some reviewers**: The benchmark’s aim and added value over existing MCQA benchmarks is not sufficiently justified. LLMs are already strong on multiple-choice, fill-in-the-blank, true/false questions. The paper does not clearly articulate what pivotal improvement CS-Bench brings (Reviewer 2). Some reviewers, however, found the benchmark highly valuable and comprehensive (Reviewers 1,4). This disagreement should be explicitly addressed.
- **Contamination risk**: The benchmark is static and openly available online, allowing LLM developers to train directly on it. This is a growing problem. Suggested mitigations include: dynamic benchmarking (e.g., DynaBench), answer ordering permutation, number switching, or question negations. The paper lacks a fingerprinting mechanism to protect integrity.
- **Scoring methodology concerns**: 
  - The use of regular expression matching for scoring is ad-hoc; some reviewers find it less convincing than existing automated metrics (Reviewer 2). Reliance on GPT-4 for scoring generative tasks raises validity and reproducibility concerns. Alternative metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR) with domain-specific constraints or a smaller fine-tuned evaluator should be explored. Human evaluation details are missing (number of annotators, expertise, guidelines, inter-annotator agreement). (Reviewer 3)
  - The generative questions scored via autorater (GPT-4) are less convincing; reviewers suggest downgrading their weight (Reviewer 1) or providing more validation.
- **Lack of coding/terminal-based evaluation**: True CS skills, especially for software engineering, require access to a coding environment with a terminal. The benchmark currently only evaluates declarative knowledge, not procedural skills. Comparison to embodied evaluation approaches is needed.
- **Translation quality concerns**: Using GPT-4 for translating benchmarks from Chinese to other languages may yield poor translations for complex questions. The authors note manual checking, but a more robust validation (e.g., independent native speakers checking a random sample, full analysis of question-answer soundness) is needed. (Reviewer 4)
- **Benchmark level and biases**: The paper does not specify the difficulty level (high school, undergrad, grad, etc.) or the breakdown of questions by expertise level. The reliance on university-level content may not represent real-world CS tasks; biases from source selection should be discussed. Including tasks from introductory CS to advanced topics and industry applications would improve generalizability. (Reviewers 1,3)
- **Limited actionable insights from error analysis**: The analysis identifies knowledge gaps and reasoning challenges but only offers generic recommendations (knowledge supplementation, CS-specific reasoning). Concrete techniques (curriculum learning, external knowledge bases, fine-tuning on CS datasets) are not explored. (Reviewer 3)
- **Cross-capability analysis lacks depth**: Correlations between CS, math, and coding are shown but not mechanistically explained. No probing of internal representations, no analysis of architectural choices or training data characteristics. The analysis of expert LLMs is shallow. (Reviewer 3)
- **Presentation issues**: Writing is dense and occasionally over-philosophical (e.g., first sentence "Computer Science stands as a testament..."). Some figures (radar charts) are hard to interpret; bar charts or scatter plots would communicate key findings more clearly. (Reviewers 1,3)
- **Limited evaluation of benchmark value**: The paper does not demonstrate the benchmark’s impact via data valuation approaches such as perplexity after training on the benchmark, fine-tuning on the benchmark, or other value-of-data analyses. (Reviewer 2)
