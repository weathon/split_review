## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a data selection method for instruction tuning that uses noise injection to measure output distribution consistency (KL divergence) as a proxy for instruction quality, and combines it with inter-class and intra-class diversity strategies (clustering and cosine similarity). Experiments show that training on 12% of the dataset selected by this method outperforms training on the full dataset.

### Strengths
- **Innovative quality assessment**: Using noise injection to assess data quality is a novel perspective that avoids reliance on external scoring models. The intuition that low KL divergence after perturbation indicates alignment with pre-trained knowledge is reasonable and is supported by ablation studies showing the roles of consistency and diversity.
- **Clear and well-motivated presentation**: The method is presented in a detailed, easy-to-follow manner, making it accessible and reproducible. The research direction (selecting high-quality instruction data for LLM fine-tuning) is practically important.
- **Promising experimental results**: The method achieves strong performance on standard benchmarks using only 12% of the full dataset, demonstrating significant training cost reduction while improving performance. The ablation experiments effectively validate the contributions of consistency and diversity.
- **Consideration of both quality and diversity**: The framework explicitly addresses the balance between data quality and diversity, a gap in many existing methods that prioritize only one aspect.
- **Publication of curated dataset**: The authors release their selected high-quality instruction data as a resource for further research.
- **Contrast in reviewer opinion**: One reviewer (score 3, confidence 4) is considerably more critical than the others (scores 5,5,6, confidence 3), particularly questioning the paper’s motivation and underlying assumptions.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: The paper compares only against full-data training. No systematic comparison is provided with recent quality- and diversity-oriented data selection methods (e.g., those from 2024 such as [2405.00705](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00705), [2], [3] not specified), or with other existing selection techniques. This makes it difficult to assess the novelty and relative advantages of the proposed approach in performance, computational efficiency, and resource requirements.
- **Lack of theoretical justification for the quality metric**: The assumption that low KL divergence after noise perturbation indicates high-quality instruction data is presented as a heuristic without fundamental explanation or theoretical analysis. No controlled experiments are shown to establish the relationship between the magnitude of KL divergence and actual instruction quality. The reliance on the model’s behavior and assumptions (e.g., smoothness, clustering) is not validated; if these assumptions fail for certain datasets or models, the method’s effectiveness may be compromised.
- **One reviewer (score 3) specifically states the paper is not well-motivated**: The hypothesis that “instructions aligning with pre-training knowledge are more easily learned” is not supported by prior publications or experiments in the paper, and the method is built on this unverified idea.
- **Insufficient computational cost analysis**: The method involves passing every perturbed instruction through the LLM to compute KL divergence, which is computationally intensive. The paper does not provide time complexity or required hardware resources, nor does it discuss potential acceleration strategies. This limits assessment of practicality for large datasets and models.
- **Limited exploration of noise parameters**: The choice of noise intensity (e.g., scaling factor β) is not systematically explored via gradient experiments. The effects of different noise levels on data quality identification and downstream performance are not analyzed.
- **Generalizability across models not demonstrated**: It is unclear whether the dataset selected using one LLM (e.g., at 7B scale) can be effectively applied to fine‑tune different LLMs (e.g., Llama3 405B). The paper does not provide cross‑model transfer experiments, so the selected data may be optimal only for the specific model used during selection.
- **Narrow evaluation on a single dataset**: Experiments are conducted on only one instruction dataset (the Alpaca-derived dataset used in the paper). Using multiple or larger mixture datasets (e.g., Mods) would better illustrate the method’s generality.
- **Limited diversity strategy**: The diversity selection relies solely on k‑means clustering and cosine similarity, which may not capture complex or hierarchical differences within data types. More advanced coverage methods are not investigated.
- **Interpretability concerns**: The connection between noise injection and data quality is not intuitively explained, making it difficult for practitioners to fully trust the method.
- **Outdated related work comparisons**: The cited and compared methods are only up to 2023; newer data selection approaches are omitted both in related work and experiments.
