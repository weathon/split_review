## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Transformer compression method that merges feed-forward (FF) sublayers by aligning their weights via learned permutations and then averaging them. Consecutive layers are selected for merging using a sliding window, the permutations maximize the correlation of layer inputs, and the merged weights are tied during fine-tuning. Experiments span language modeling (GPT-2 perplexity), image classification (ViT on ImageNet), and machine translation. The method reduces up to 21% of total ViT parameters while maintaining 99% of original accuracy (80.3 → 79.2). The authors also analyze activation similarity across FF sublayers.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and clarity (minority positive view, Reviewer 1, score 5, confidence 4)** – The paper is well‑written with a clear logical structure. The idea of merging rather than pruning parameters is novel, and the permutation‑alignment step is a straightforward addition to weight averaging.
- **Neuron alignment context (Reviewer 2, confidence 5)** – The paper does a good job delineating the relevant context for neuron alignment and summarizing where the method falls among compression techniques. Ablation studies and visualizations are thorough.
- **Interesting idea with detailed exploration (Reviewer 3, confidence 4)** – Layer merging is an interesting direction; the permutation technique provides extra capacity. Experiments cover both vision transformers and language models, with detailed results for varying numbers of merged layers and layer positions.
- **Simplicity and memory reduction (Reviewer 4, confidence 5)** – The idea is easy to understand; the main novelty is averaging FFN weights after permutation alignment. The method can reduce stored parameters, which is useful for hardware with storage constraints.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of comparisons to pruning and other compression methods** – All reviewers identify this as a major gap. Reviewer 1 notes the method is not compared to parameter‑pruning techniques such as [1]: that method obtains nearly unchanged ViT accuracy (−0.07, 83.36 → 83.29) while reducing parameters by over 40% and achieving a 1.9× speedup, whereas the proposed method reduces ViT parameters by ~21% with a −1.1 accuracy drop (80.3 → 79.2) and no speed improvement. Reviewer 2 asks for a comparison with Wanda (prunes 50% one‑shot without fine‑tuning, no major accuracy loss). Reviewer 3 points out that structural pruning can achieve 2.57× lossless parameter reduction on ViT, while this method only removes 21%. Reviewer 4 requests magnitude pruning or zero‑activation pruning baselines, as well as methods specifically targeting FFN optimization (e.g., MoEfication, Go Wider Instead of Deeper). Also missing: head‑to‑head comparison with quantization (Table 4 only shows combining merging with quantization, not direct competition) and with structured pruning that removes entire blocks or layers (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). The absence of systematic baselines makes it hard to judge whether the method offers any advantage over existing techniques.
- **No computational speedup / limited practical use** – The method reduces stored parameters but does not change the inference computation graph (no FLOP reduction, no speedup), and requires fine‑tuning after merging (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). Reviewer 4 emphasizes that many real‑world deployments also care about latency and energy consumption; reducing only storage has narrow applicability. Reviewer 1 acknowledges that “parameter merging maintains inference speed but still requires fine‑tuning” as a drawback.
- **Novelty concerns and effectiveness of the permutation step** – Reviewer 3 notes that weight sharing across transformer blocks is well‑known (early efficient LMs, later ViTs and LLMs), and that the permute‑merge often does not significantly outperform naive (unpermuted) merging in Figures 2 and 3, and even behaves worse on GPT‑2. This raises doubts about the necessity and correctness of the permutation technique. Reviewer 4 asks whether a naive baseline (reuse FFN layers and fine‑tune from random initialization) would achieve similar results, and explicitly questions whether the permutation alignment is beneficial. (This contrasts with the positive novelty assessment of Reviewer 1, but the majority of reviewers find the novelty limited and the permute step’s effectiveness unclear.)
- **Limited scope to MLP layers** – The method is only applied to FF (MLP) sublayers, capping the achievable compression ratio (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 asks whether it can be extended to all layers. Reviewer 1 mentions the claim that patterns do not appear in attention sublayers (line 89), but Reviewer 4 counters with existing work showing attention weight reuse ([3], [4]).
- **Insufficient language modeling evaluation** – Reviewer 2 recommends adding MMLU accuracy beyond simple perplexity, and testing on LLaMA models (which are more widely used than GPT‑2) to increase the contribution.
- **Sliding window selection lacks motivation** – Both Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 4 question why a sliding window of consecutive layers is used instead of a similarity‑based selection across non‑consecutive layers. Reviewer 1 finds the approach straightforward but not well‑motivated.
- **Writing quality issues** – Reviewer 4 points out confusing wording at line 173 (“from the output of the parameter two parameter sets”) and misuse of “elude” (line 156). Reviewer 2 notes a typo at line 89 (“These same patterns do not in counterpart attention sublayers.”). (Reviewer 1 found the paper well‑written, so there is disagreement on presentation quality.)
- **Missing ablation on fine‑tuning necessity** – Reviewer 4 asks how much performance degrades if fine‑tuning is not applied after merging; Reviewer 1 implies fine‑tuning is required. This ablation is not provided.
- **Equation issue and conflicting claim** – Reviewer 4 notes Equation (4): the bias term \(b_i^{out}\) is written without the permutation matrix \(P_i\). Also, the statement at line 89 (patterns not in attention sublayers) contradicts works on attention weight reuse ([3], [4]).

**Reviewer confidence and disagreement note:** Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 4) viewed the paper as well‑written, novel, and effective. The other three reviewers (scores 3, confidence 4‑5) raised the substantial criticisms listed above regarding comparisons, effectiveness, novelty, and scope. This merged review preserves both the positive minority view and the majority concerns.
