## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies robustness of OLS regression to sample removal. It presents two algorithms—ACRE (for continuous features) and OHARE (for categorical/one-hot encoded features)—that provide upper and lower bounds \(U_k\) and \(L_k\) on \(\Delta_k(e)\), the maximum possible change in the projection of the OLS coefficient vector onto a direction \(e\) when \(k\) samples are removed. The algorithms reduce bounding \(\beta - \beta_S\) to solving Maximum Subset Norm (MSN) problems and use a Refined Triangle Inequality (RTI) greedy approach and knapsack-style dynamic programming. Theoretical results show that, under mild distributional assumptions (e.g., exponentially tailed distributions, well-behaved definitions), the ratio \(U_k/L_k\) is at most \(1 + O((d + k\sqrt{d})/n)\) for ACRE and \(1 + O(1/\sqrt{\log n})\) for OHARE. Experiments on the Nightlights study and 13 other landmark econometrics datasets (with \(n\) up to \(10^4\) and \(d\) up to hundreds) provide the first non-trivial robustness certificates for dimensions \(d \ge 4\). The implementation is available and runs on consumer-grade machines. One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, praising the clarity and thorough empirical evaluation, while the other two (scores 5) raised concerns about clarity, assumptions, and experimental methodology.

### Strengths
- Addresses the interesting and fundamental problem of auditing robustness of linear regressions to sample removal.
- Well-written main text with a clear explanation of ACRE and its connection to the Maximal Subset-sum Norm (MSN) problem.
- Algorithms improve over brute-force subset search and provide meaningful, non-trivial certificates on real datasets of hundreds of dimensions and tens of thousands of samples.
- Implementation is provided in an anonymous repository and runs on consumer-grade hardware for datasets with \(n \sim 10^4\).
- Rigorous theoretical analysis employing interesting techniques (MSN bounds, knapsack DP, RTI).
- Experiments are conducted on real-world regressions, including the Nightlights study.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: The paper is tough to follow in parts. Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 are presented before the methods are explained, and the role of concepts like bucket size is not intuitively described (R2). Section 3’s intuition is not precise enough, and the connection between theoretical guarantees and practical performance needs clearer explanation (R3). The narrative flow is confusing: ACRE is introduced and then immediately criticized to motivate OHARE (R2). One reviewer (score 8) found the paper well-written, but the other two (scores 5) noted these clarity problems.
- **Assumption handling and scope**: The paper claims “no assumptions whatsoever on X and Y” (line 135) but then uses “well-behaved” (Definition 1) and distributional assumptions for tightness. The distinction between unconditional truth and tightness under assumptions is not clearly articulated for each theorem; explicit assumptions should be stated per result (R2). Assumptions on the direction \(e\) should be specified in the main theorems (R2 line 441). The assumption of knowledge of the population covariance matrix (line 385) is unconventional, and it is unclear what to do when it is unknown (R2). The paper needs more concrete examples of which heavy-tailed distributions besides sub-exponential can be well-behaved (R2 line 253). The claim that the error term \(1/\sqrt{\log n}\) is “nearly tight” (line 295) lacks a formal proof or even a proof sketch (R2). The phrase “loose bounds suffice” (line 322) appears contradictory to “nearly tight”; the relationship between the looseness of the MSN bounds and the tightness of the final robustness bounds is not explained (R2).
- **Computational complexity and feasibility**: The greedy algorithm used to bound the first term (line 374) and for the MSN step (line 464) has computational cost that is not explicitly stated; it is unclear if it is practical for large datasets (R2). The overall complexity of the algorithm is \(O(n^2 d)\) (line 399); potential improvements should be discussed (R2). The paper criticizes existing works for relying on SDP, but does not provide a clear computational complexity comparison with those methods to justify the improvement (R2 line 497). One reviewer (score 8) acknowledged the algorithm may not be feasible for very large \(k\), but felt this was addressed by the empirical evaluation; the other two reviewers found this point insufficiently addressed.
- **Experimental methodology and validation**: The process that yields the values in Table 1 is unclear—how do the algorithm’s outputs \((U_k, L_k)\) translate to the final reported numbers? The comparison methodology with baselines is missing, and no cross-validation is performed (R3). Additional experiments would strengthen the results: testing on synthetic data with known ground truth, varying \(n\) and \(d\) for Theorems 1.2 and 1.3, and comparing with simple baselines like random sampling (R3). The paper claims “hundreds of dimensions” (line 22) but should specify exact \(d\) and \(n\); later the authors assume \(n \gg d\), making the phrase misleading (R2).
- **Missing details and questions that are effectively criticisms**:
  - The norm in the equation at line 352 is not specified (R2).
  - Typo: “direct” should be “direction” (line 440, R2).
  - The term “decision boundary” (line 79) is more appropriate for classification than regression (R2).
  - When do ACRE and OHARE produce the same bounds versus differ? (R2).
  - What happens when \(n\) is comparable to \(d\) or in high-dimensional settings? (R2).
  - How sensitive are the bound values \(U_k\) and \(L_k\) to the choice of MSN-bounding algorithm? (R3).
  - How do the algorithms perform when distributional assumptions are violated, e.g., on PowerLaw synthetic data? (R3).
