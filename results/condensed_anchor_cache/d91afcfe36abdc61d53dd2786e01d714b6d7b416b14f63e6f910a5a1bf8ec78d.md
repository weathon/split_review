## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an in-context fine-tuning methodology for time-series foundation models, specifically implemented on a pretrained TimesFM. The model undergoes a second-stage training where multiple related time-series examples are appended to the input, separated by a learned separator token, and trained with autoregressive MSE loss. Two context generation strategies are introduced: time-series-level (splitting a longer history) and dataset-level (sampling other series). Evaluated on Monash and long-sequence forecasting benchmarks, the method shows improved zero-shot performance over base TimesFM and even rivals target-domain fine-tuned versions. The work is timely and relevant, but several reviewers raised concerns about experimental rigor, baseline comparisons, and conceptual clarity.

### Strengths
- **Novel and timely adaptation** of in-context learning from NLP to time‑series forecasting, addressing the problem of efficiently adapting foundation models to new domains without parameter updates at inference.
- **Learned separator token** is a creative mechanism to mark boundaries between in-context examples, potentially serving as a building block for other multi-context models.
- **Decoder‑only architecture** choice enables scalability to long horizons and multiple in-context examples, which is beneficial for complex temporal dependencies.
- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation** on popular benchmarks (Monash, ETT, etc.) with detailed ablation studies (e.g., Figure 6) that validate the benefit of related examples and show performance improvement with more in-context examples. The finding that many in-context examples can outperform a long history is highlighted as surprising and meaningful.
- **Clear, well‑structured writing** that makes the methodology accessible, with illustrative figures and examples.
- **Significant practical implications** – the approach can improve forecasting accuracy without extensive task‑specific training data, and the concept extends beyond time series.
- One minority reviewer (very positive) particularly emphasized the potential to be a game‑changer for practitioners.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing training hyperparameters** (batch size, learning rate, optimizer, scheduler, etc.) for the second-stage training. It is unclear whether optimizer parameters were loaded from the base TimesFM checkpoint.
- **Lack of details for per-dataset fine-tuning** – was a best-effort hyperparameter search conducted for the fine-tuned baseline?
- **Missing baseline TimesFM-ICF (n=1)** for all datasets. This is needed to isolate the benefit of true in-context learning from the effect of the second-stage training alone, and to check whether specialization hurts vanilla zero-shot performance.
- **Insufficient comparison with existing foundation models** – the paper only compares with TimesFM. Stronger evidence would require comparisons on Monash with Chronos, on ETT with Moirai (Unified Training of Universal Time Series Transformers), and with LLM‑based solutions. One reviewer (score 3) considered this a major flaw, noting that Moirai is the most relevant prior work because it assigns variate IDs and handles related series similarly. A detailed comparison with Moirai is missing.
- **Lack of clarity in defining example “relevance”** – the criteria for selecting in-context examples are not well specified. An ablation comparing different selection strategies (e.g., similarity metrics, domain knowledge) is needed. Practical guidelines for practitioners are missing.
- **Separator token mechanism is underspecified** – there is no empirical evidence (attention maps, ablation) showing that the separator prevents blending of patterns across examples. The model may still mix representations, especially for series with similar trends.
- **Positional encoding concerns** – the paper relies on causal attention without explicit positional encodings (NoPE) for length generalization. This may lose precise temporal order when multiple examples with different time ranges are concatenated. Testing explicit or relative positional encodings is suggested to clarify this.
- **Handling of non‑homogeneous in-context examples** – different scales, lengths, and temporal patterns are not explicitly addressed. No normalization or padding strategy is described, which could introduce biases.
- **Contradictory wording in Section 6.4.1** – the claim of monotonic decrease in MSE is not fully supported by Table 5 in the appendix, which shows a general trend but non‑monotonic behaviour. The wording should be revised.
- **Potential data leakage** – it should be verified that context generation for both training and evaluation avoids leakage between series and time steps.
- **Limited dataset diversity** – benchmarks lack high seasonality, irregular sampling, and multivariate setups (e.g., LOTSA from Woo et al.). More diverse conditions would test generalization better.
- **Computational efficiency trade‑off** – as in-context examples increase, the computational cost grows substantially. A comparison of inference cost between TimesFM Base and TimesFM‑ICF, along with an analysis of diminishing returns, is needed.
- **Discussion of limitations is shallow** – failure cases when the target domain contains novel patterns not represented in in-context examples are not examined. No strategies for detecting out‑of‑distribution inputs are offered.
- **Trade‑offs between in-context fine-tuning and conventional fine-tuning** are not explored (e.g., which scenarios favour one over the other).
- Several review questions overlap with these weaknesses (e.g., “How does the model distinguish different examples?”, “What are the criteria for selecting examples?”, “How does it handle variable lengths?”, “Can performance across different forecast horizons be shown?”). These are implicitly covered above, but reinforce the need for additional experimentation and clarity.
