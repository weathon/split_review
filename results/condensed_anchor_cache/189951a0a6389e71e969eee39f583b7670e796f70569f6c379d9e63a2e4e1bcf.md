## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GBIR, a Gaussian-based iterative reconstruction framework using learnable 3D Gaussians for sparse-view CT and compressed-sensing MRI, and introduces the MORE dataset (over 70,000 CT and MRI slices). Reviews are mixed: three reviewers (scores 3) raise major concerns about novelty, methodology, comparisons, and dataset suitability; one reviewer (score 5) finds the method and dataset valuable but notes missing analyses. Overall, the work is seen as insufficient in literature coverage, experimental rigor, and justification of key design choices.

### Strengths
- **Efficient design strategies**: The framework incorporates 3-sigma truncation, decomposition and parallelization to reduce spatial and temporal complexity, plus a neighborhood processing approach to lower computational cost (R1, R3).  
- **Novel concept**: Learning Gaussian representations for personalized medical image reconstruction is a novel idea (R2, R4).  
- **Comprehensive dataset**: The MORE dataset, comprising over 70,000 CT and MRI slices across multiple body parts, would provide substantial value if open-sourced (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- **Performance claims**: GBIR is reported to outperform state-of-the-art methods in accuracy and speed under sparse measurements (R4).  
- **Results on multiple datasets**: The method is evaluated on different CT and MRI datasets (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty**: Several prior works already incorporate 3D Gaussian representations into medical image reconstruction [1–4], with some also avoiding the splatting process [1,3]. The paper’s novelty is thus overstated; a more comprehensive literature review and comparisons with these existing 3D Gaussian-based methods are needed (R1).  
- **Inaccurate claim about DIF‑Gaussian**: The paper states that DIF‑Gaussian uses “splatting”, but DIF‑Gaussian does not employ a splatting process (per its MICCAI 2024 publication). This claim is incorrect (R1).  
- **Incomplete literature on MRI reconstruction**: The related work on compressed-sensing MRI is very limited, omitting well-known physics-driven methods (ISTA‑Net, MoDL, Variational Network) and generative approaches (Robust Compressed‑Sensing MRI with Deep Generative Priors). The comparisons use outdated baselines (e.g., AUTOMAP) and do not include state-of-the-art unrolled networks or fastMRI challenge methods (R2, R3).  
- **Missing discussion of related Gaussian‑process methods**: The paper does not compare with reconstruction schemes based on Gaussian processes (e.g., Xu et al. 2023, Ye et al. 2024) or processing approaches (Andersson et al. 2015) (R3).  
- **Diffusion models barely addressed**: A deeper comparison with diffusion-based reconstruction methods is missing—both in reconstruction quality (fine details, noise handling) and computational cost (R4).  

- **Fundamental MRI modeling issues**:
  - The target volume in MRI is complex-valued, not real-valued; modeling it as \(\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}\) is incorrect. Complex-valued Gaussian random vectors are required (R2).  
  - Eq. (11) for MRI assumes single-coil, but virtually all clinical MRI raw data is multi‑coil (R3).  
  - The method treats CT and MRI as equivalent output (real‑valued vs. complex‑valued), which is not valid for general DL techniques (R3).  

- **SSIM used in measurement domain**: Eq. (12) applies SSIM between complex-valued k‑space measurements. SSIM cannot handle complex-valued inputs and is designed for images, not frequency-domain data. Definitions of \(\lambda_i\) in Eq. (12) are not explained (R2, R3).  

- **MRI dataset and inverse crimes**: The CS‑MRI experiments use the BRATS dataset, which contains only preprocessed magnitude images, no raw k‑space data. Transforming DICOM to k‑space and under‑sampling leads to inverse crimes (R2, R3). Public raw‑k‑space datasets (fastMRI, SKM‑TEA, M4Raw) should be used instead (R2, R3).  

- **Missing experimental details on MRI**: No undersampling parameters (acceleration factor, sampling pattern) are provided for the CS‑MRI task (R1, R2). Tables 4 and 5 report only average values without standard deviation or standard error (R2).  

- **Weak baselines**: The chosen baselines do not represent the state of the art (e.g., AUTOMAP is outdated). Recent unrolled networks (ISTA‑Net, MoDL, Variational Network) and fastMRI methods would be more appropriate (R2, R3).  

- **No qualitative comparisons**: The paper provides only quantitative tables; visual results are essential for medical image reconstruction (R1). “Visual results don’t lie.”  

- **Unclear modeling of covariance**: It is not discussed whether \(\Sigma_k\) in Eq. (4) is learned. If not learned, the “non-isotropic” formulation is moot; if learned, the paper omits how the full-rank Hermitian matrices are parameterized, initialized, and updated (R3).  
- **Inconsistency between isotropic and non-isotropic assumptions**: The neighborhood processing uses a \(k \times k \times k\) cuboid (implying isotropic Gaussians), yet the formulation specifies non-isotropic covariance \(\Sigma_k\). The truncation \(\delta\) should depend on the covariance (R3).  

- **Prohibitive reconstruction time**: Training per subject takes ~8 hours per dataset (Table 3), making the method clinically unusable. Scan-specific training for physics-driven methods (e.g., Yaman et al., ICLR 2022) exists but is not compared (R3).  

- **Lack of computational complexity analysis**: The paper does not provide a breakdown of the iterative optimization cost (forward/backward passes per Gaussian parameter update), making practical scalability assessment impossible (R4).  

- **Dataset uncertainties**:  
  - Demographics of MORE are missing (R3).  
  - It is unclear whether the MRI data in MORE contain raw k‑space or only DICOMs; the former is unlikely given the methodology (R3).  
  - Whether the CT and MRI data are matched per subject or simply concatenated is not stated (R3).  

- **Limited to volumetric reconstruction**: The method only handles volumetric data; many MRI scans are 2D with slice gaps—how this extends is unclear (R3).  

- **No framework figure**: The paper does not provide a figure illustrating the overall framework (R4).  

- **DuDoRNet unnamed in text**: The method “DuDoRNet” appears in a table but is not mentioned in the text (R2).
