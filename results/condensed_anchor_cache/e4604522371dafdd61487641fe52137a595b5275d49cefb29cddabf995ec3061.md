- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 6, 1, 8, 3, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a hierarchy of invariants for Euclidean graphs (vertices in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), straight-line edges, unordered vertices) under the special Euclidean group \(\text{SE}(n)\). The invariants range from simple, efficient ones to a complete invariant called the nested centred distribution (NCD), which is Lipschitz-continuous with respect to vertex perturbations and computable in polynomial time for fixed \(n\) (complexity \(O(m^{5.5}\log^3 m)\) for \(m\) vertices). The authors demonstrate that the hierarchy can distinguish all chemically different molecules in the QM9 database (130,808 molecules) by discarding atom types, within a few hours on a modest desktop. They claim this is the first practical complete classification of molecular graphs with edges.

Reviewers have mixed opinions: two (scores 6, 8) are generally positive about the theoretical contribution, while two (scores 1, 3) are very critical of the writing, motivation, and evaluation, and one (score 5) finds the paper technically sound but lacking clarity and intuition.

### Strengths
- **Provably complete and Lipschitz-continuous invariant**: The NCD is proven complete for geometric graphs under \(\text{SE}(n)\) and Lipschitz-continuous w.r.t. vertex perturbations (multiple reviewers). Reviewer 5 notes that Lipschitz continuity “basically falls from the well-chosen definition.” Reviewer 3 (score 8) calls the paper “technically solid” and “mathematically interesting.”
- **Polynomial-time computability for fixed dimension**: The complete invariant runs in \(O(m^{5.5}\log^3 m)\) for \(m\) vertices when ambient dimension \(n\) is fixed, covering many practical cases [Reviewers 1, 5].
- **Practical demonstration on a large dataset**: The invariants successfully distinguished all chemically different molecular graphs in the QM9 database (130K+ molecules) in a few hours on a modest desktop. Reviewer 3 finds the experiment “good” and highlights scalability. Reviewer 1 acknowledges the theoretical contribution despite limited evaluation.
- **Clear problem statement and motivation**: The problem of geometric graph isomorphism under rigid motion is well-defined and motivated (Reviewer 4: “stated clearly and motivated decently well”).
- **Good overview of limitations and future directions**: Reviewer 3 appreciates the discussion of limitations and potential extensions.
- **Many illustrative examples**: The paper includes examples to show the strengths of different invariants (Reviewer 5).
- **Technically sound**: Reviewer 2 (score 1) acknowledges the paper is “technically sound” despite strong criticisms elsewhere; Reviewer 4 agrees the invariants “seem to be technically valid.”

### Weaknesses
- **Poor writing clarity and organization**: Multiple reviewers (2, 3, 4, 5) criticize the exposition. Reviewer 2 (score 1) calls it “very poorly written, bombastic and lacks clarity in various places.” Reviewer 3 notes the writing is “colloquial” and not typical of CS papers (e.g., lacks bullet-pointed contributions). Reviewer 5 states the roadmap is unclear: “I had to read the paper twice to understand it.” Definitions are introduced without enough intuition (e.g., the role of NCD is only revealed on page 7). Reviewer 4 finds the construction of CR, CD, NCD unmotivated and unclear (why specific components? why nesting? why base sequence limited to dimension \(n\)?). There are over 8 acronyms, and important terms like \(R(G;A)\) and \(D(A)\) are missing from the lookup table (Reviewer 5).
- **Limited and incomplete evaluation**: Several reviewers find the experiments insufficient. Reviewer 1 notes the paper shows inter-cluster distances but not intra-cluster distances, making it hard to judge clustering difficulty. Reviewer 2 states the evaluation is limited: “no comparison to any other method,” and the QM9 database is not explained in context. Reviewer 4 argues the practical value is unclear: the claim that the invariant can serve as an informative embedding for property prediction is not justified; only the number of atoms is shown (Fig. 6); more interesting chemical properties should be tested. Reviewer 4 questions whether a simpler point-cloud plus connectivity check would suffice. Reviewer 3 asks for a concrete experimental comparison to existing work to show what breaks without Lipschitz continuity.
- **Exaggerated claims about novelty**: Reviewer 1 states that the claim “previously impossible” is “a bit exaggerated” because many methods have solved classification on QM9 even with incomplete invariants.
- **Missing comparisons to existing methods**: Reviewer 2 criticizes the comparison to methods like Gromov-Hausdorff or Gromov-Wasserstein distances as “remarkably ungenerous” and lacking serious engagement with the literature. Reviewer 4 asks why a simpler distance-based invariant (with negative distances for non-adjacent nodes) does not suffice. Reviewer 4 also asks about relation to Nigam et al. (2024) on completeness of atomic structure representations. No comparative experiments are provided.
- **Lack of motivation for the complete invariant and metric construction**: Reviewer 2 questions why a complete invariant is needed instead of just a metric. Reviewer 4 finds no motivation for the construction of the metric (strength, max metric over three distances) and wonders why not a sum. Reviewer 5 asks for intuition on why \(h=n\) is needed for completeness. The definition of the nested bottleneck metric (Def. 4.3) is unclear whether it is well-defined, as it uses knowledge of base sequences \(A, B\) rather than purely the invariants (Reviewer 5).
- **Formal problems and unclear definitions**: Multiple instances:
  - NCD is defined for \(h \ge 1\) but Example 3.6 uses \(h=0\) (Reviewer 4).
  - Definition 4.1 (strength) requires \(n\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) but is later used for \(h < n\) (Reviewer 4).
  - Definition 3.4 of the metric is not symmetric in \((G,A)\) and \((F,B)\) (Reviewer 4).
  - Notation for centred distributions does not indicate the final order \(h\), causing confusion (Reviewer 5).
  - Definition of Euclidean graphs is imprecise: what is the data (vertices as subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\)? edges as subsets of \(V^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^n\)? overlapping edges allowed? vertices on edges?) (Reviewer 5).
  - Problem 1 has notational inconsistencies (e.g., \(I\) denotes both the mapping and a value; \(G \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is confusing) (Reviewer 5).
  - Many minor issues listed by Reviewer 5 (e.g., line 59 “up to 6 permutations” should be “up to all permutations”; line 60 “triangular cone” phrasing; Figure 2 wording; missing spaces; definition of strength indexing; Lemma 4.2 epsilon not defined; Definition 4.5 accesses graph points instead of invariants).
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms** (included here as weaknesses):
  - Why is a complete invariant needed vs. just a metric? [Reviewer 2]
  - How does the method compare to approximating Gromov-Wasserstein distance? [Reviewers 2, 4]
  - Can the invariant distinguish mirror images? (Reviewer 4 suspects not, as it is distance-based; if so, the problem should refer to \(E(n)\) rather than \(\text{SE}(n)\).)
  - Can atom-type node features be incorporated? [Reviewer 4]
  - Is the vectorisation of NCD unique/canonical? [Reviewer 5]
  - Are there proofs of minimal distance between different graphs? [Reviewer 5]
  - Space requirements are not specified (Reviewer 1).
- **Minority extreme views**:
  - Reviewer 2 (score 1) is strongly negative: finds the paper poorly written, the comparison to existing work ungenerous, the need for a complete invariant unmotivated, and the evaluation insufficient with no real application.
  - Reviewer 3 (score 8) is strongly positive: considers the paper a “strong contribution” and “mathematically interesting,” though acknowledges writing problems and questions the relevance of Lipschitz continuity for molecular data. Reviewer 3 asks for intuition on other application areas and extension to graphical models or causal inference.
- **Complexity concerns**: Reviewer 1 notes that \(O(m^{5.5}\log^3 m)\) time complexity can be daunting for larger problems.
- **Relevance of Lipschitz continuity to molecule classification**: Reviewer 3 questions whether Lipschitz continuity is practically important for molecules, as molecular structures are inherently stable and not subject to random noise.
