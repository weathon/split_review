## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DynCL, a contrastive learning framework for system identification under non-linear observations, with theoretical identifiability guarantees. It connects self-supervised learning (SSL) to identifiable representation learning for linear, switching linear, and non-linear dynamics. The proofs show that under assumptions (e.g., invertible mixing, linear dynamics), the recovered latent dynamics are identifiable up to affine transformation. Synthetic experiments validate the approach, including extensive ablations and a delta-SLDS variant for switching dynamics.

### Strengths
- Novel connection between contrastive learning and dynamic system identification, potentially enabling simple encoder-only implementations in practice (R2).
- Well-organized paper; proofs use standard Jacobian analysis and are easy to follow (R1).
- Theorem is interesting and novel: identifiability without independent noise assumption, providing valuable insights into contrastive learning mechanisms (R3, R4).
- Synthetic experiments and ablation studies are extensive, investigating many aspects of the theoretical results (R2, R4, R5).
- The method is more suitable for non-linear time-series than time-contrastive learning (Hyvärinen & Morioka, 2016) because it fits a latent time-series rather than predicting categorical labels (R3).
- Iterative Jacobian linearizations for switching dynamics is a sensible adaptation that benefits performance (R3).
- The introduced CL variant is well presented and theoretically grounded, could inspire further research (R5).
- DynCL effectively identifies latent states and system dynamics on simulated data; good selection of ablations demonstrating impact of modeling choices (R5).
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (rating 8) was notably more positive, emphasizing the theoretical grounding and effectiveness on simulated data, though still raising concerns about realistic experiments and baselines.

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumptions limit applicability**: The assumption that the mixing function from latent states to observations is invertible/injective is unrealistic in many scenarios (e.g., LQG where \(d_y < d_x\) makes it non-invertible). Partial observability is a key challenge in non-linear system identification and reinforcement learning, and this assumption ignores it. The paper lacks thorough discussion of how assumptions affect practical applicability (R1, R2, R3). Time-Contrastive Learning is more amenable because it predicts categorical labels from chunks of data (R2).
- **Discrepancy between theorem and model equations**: Theorem 1 assumes noise for identifiability, but Equations (1-2) do not include noise. This discrepancy needs clarification (R3, R4).
- **Proof relies on unguaranteed optimization behavior**: The proof depends on Jacobian properties (full rank, non-singularity) under contrastive loss minimization; there is no guarantee that optimization achieves these (R1).
- **Control input role unclear**: It is presented as entering latent dynamics, but not reflected in the method; is it available to the learner? Equation (9) suggests stochastic noise vs. Equation (1). Experiments use a low-noise, nearly deterministic Lorenz system, ruling out persistency of excitation effects. The actuation matrix is not learned (R2). One reviewer asks: what is the effective difference from an autonomous latent system \(x_{t+1} = f(x_t) + \epsilon_t\)? (R3). Also, the assumption \(p(u_t)\) is normal seems strict for many applications (R5).
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: TCL (Time-Contrastive Learning) should be compared; the current baseline (no dynamics model) is weak. Other relevant methods include next-token prediction models (R5) and “Embed to Control” (Watter et al., 2015) (R3). Reviewers ask about marginal utility of DynCL vs. these methods (R3).
- **Missing evaluation metrics**: Metrics like Mean Correlation Coefficient (MCC) should be reported even if component-wise identifiability is not achieved (R3, R4).
- **Only simulated data**: Lack of experiments on real-world data limits impact. Suggested dataset: “Discovering State Variables Hidden in Experimental Data” (B. Chen et al.). If too challenging, authors should discuss what each theoretical assumption means in practice and what happens when they are violated (R5).
