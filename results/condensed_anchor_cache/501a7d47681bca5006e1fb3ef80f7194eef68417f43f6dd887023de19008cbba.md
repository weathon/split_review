## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Elementary, a heuristic search framework for evidence discovery that uses LLMs with multi-step prompts and “according to” prompts to assess evidence sufficiency via log-probability rewards. It proposes independent and pattern-aware reward mechanisms without domain-specific fine-tuning. Experiments on HoVer, PubMedQA, and CovidET datasets with Gemma-2b-it and Gemma-7b-it models show improvements over baselines in capturing complex evidence patterns. Reviewer 1 (score 6) found the approach innovative and the unsupervised value function interesting; Reviewer 2 (score 3) found the paper difficult to follow and the baselines unmotivated; Reviewer 3 (score 5) found the heuristic reward interesting but the contribution limited due to missing comparisons and weak pattern awareness.

### Strengths
- The framework formulates evidence discovery as a multi-step prompt construction with heuristic search and LLM-guided pattern exploration, a departure from single-step or retrieval-based techniques (Reviewer 1).
- The use of an “according to” prompt to force the LLM to decode the claim and compute log probability as a reward for evidence sufficiency is interesting (Reviewer 1).
- The unsupervised value function incorporating both sentence- and paragraph-level evidence dependence rewards is a contribution (Reviewer 1).
- The heuristic reward function acts as a look-ahead strategy to prune irrelevant evidence pathways, which is interesting (Reviewer 3).
- Section 5 and its analysis posed as questions is good and informative (Reviewer 2).
- Investing in better mechanisms to rank evidence/passages/context is a relevant area of research (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Paper clarity and framing**: The paper is difficult to follow; e.g., the meaning of “according to” is not set in context for the reader (Reviewer 2). The task and formulation are not connected to the recent and active area of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which would help readers; the paper cites only 2014 papers for task description (Reviewer 2). The task of evidence discovery is similar to the retrieval step in RAG using embedding models that rank passages by similarity, and quantifying support is similar to citations/attribution in RAG – these connections are not made (Reviewer 2).
- **Baseline motivation**: It is unclear why the baselines in Table 1 are the right ones; they should be motivated (Reviewer 2). The settings top-3 and top-5 are not justified (Reviewer 2). Metric calculation is never explained (Reviewer 2).
- **Statistical significance and impact**: The claim that Elementary consistently outperforms baselines is not supported by statistical significance tests; the improvement in reranking/context ranking is unclear (Reviewer 2). The impact of using or not using “according to” is unclear from Table 2, which reports log probabilities (Reviewer 2). One reviewer noted that the “according to” prompt does not eliminate hallucinations, only mitigates them, as shown in Section 5.5 (Reviewer 2).
- **Model choice and generalizability**: Experiments are limited to two Gemma models (2b-it and 7b-it), which may share similar training lexical properties; the generalizability to other base models of similar size is not shown (Reviewer 1). The choice of Gemma-2b-it is not well motivated; the paper discusses it later in Section 5.4 but the writing does not prepare the reader (Reviewer 2).
- **Computational cost**: The framework requires multiple computations between evidence and claim; the authors did not compare computational cost with baselines (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparison to Chain-of-Thought**: It is unclear how Elementary compares to chain-of-thought or chain-of-evidence; including CoT for comparison would clarify advantages. Without it, contribution is limited (Reviewer 3).
- **Pattern-awareness weakness**: In Table 3 and Figure 3, the EM score drops significantly from 2-hop to 3-hop evidence, indicating that pattern recognition is not powerful when >2 supporting evidences are needed; claiming “pattern-aware” is weak (Reviewer 3). The same EM score for FEVER-1 in Table 3 appears duplicated as F1 score, and the same duplication occurs in Table 4 (Reviewer 3). Best scores should be highlighted in tables for clarity (Reviewer 3).
- **Section 5.4 contribution**: This section only shows that instruction-tuned models are better at following instructions, which is obvious and not a significant contribution (Reviewer 3).
- **Writing and detail issues**: The phrase “force the LLM to decode” (line 160) is too strong; “guiding” is more appropriate (Reviewer 2). It is unclear if variations of the “according to” wording were tried, as wording changes may affect outcomes (Reviewer 2). When the paper says “typically, we employ LLMs…” a citation is needed to show it is typical (Reviewer 2). Notation for **a** and **s** is confusing; does **s** represent potentially relevant context and **a** a single sentence from it? (Reviewer 1). Are there variances in predicted results across runs given default decoding/sampling settings? (Reviewer 1). Figure and table captions need more detail; Figure 3’s caption is clear but others are not (Reviewer 1). TYPO: line 117 missing space after period (Reviewer 3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**:  
Reviewer 1 (score 6) found the approach innovative and the reward design interesting, while Reviewer 2 (score 3) found the paper poorly written and the experimental setup insufficient, and Reviewer 3 (score 5) considered the contribution limited due to missing comparisons and weak pattern awareness. The strengths from Reviewer 1 are not shared by the others; the weaknesses from Reviewer 2 and 3 are more critical.
