## Merged Review

### Summary
READ-SQL is a text-to-SQL framework that uses AST-based SQL decomposition (READER) into clauses, sub-SQLs, and reasoning paths. This decomposition supports data augmentation (question/SQL pairs and question/reason pairs) and a self-correction mechanism based on common/differing constraints. The framework consists of a Generator and a Corrector, both fine-tuned via LoRA. Experiments on BIRD, Spider, and Spider variants show that READ-SQL-3B achieves 57.37% execution accuracy on BIRD’s dev set, outperforming several 7B models and setting a new state-of-the-art for sub-3B-parameter models. The framework is also evaluated for broad applicability.

### Strengths
1. The READER SQL decomposer (using AST parsing) is novel, efficient, and scalable, effectively supporting multi-step reasoning by decomposing SQL into sub-queries and reasoning paths.
2. The self-correction method based on common and differing clauses (constraints) is innovative.
3. The framework achieves promising results using small LLMs (1B and 3B models), which is advantageous for local deployment, on-device AI, and reduced computational resources.
4. READ-SQL achieves state-of-the-art performance compared to all models under 3B parameters on both BIRD and Spider benchmarks, and surpasses several larger models on BIRD.
5. The idea of aligning natural language and structured syntax to correctly interpret constraints is interesting, and generating augmented training data via clause decomposition is novel.
6. The paper provides a detailed error analysis of common text-to-SQL errors and validates the framework on five well-recognized datasets (BIRD, Spider, and Spider variants), with extensive ablation studies and experimental setups demonstrating consistency and robustness.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient baselines:** The paper does not compare with more advanced recent methods (e.g., Distillery, CHASE-SQL, CHESS) or up-to-date fine-tuning approaches like zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting for reasoning step generation. While the main baseline is SFT CodeS, comparisons with methods using LLMs for few-shot/zero-shot reasoning are missing. This applies to both generation and correction (e.g., basic self-correction from DIN-SQL is not compared, making it hard to attribute gains to the proposed mechanism vs. an extra LLM call).
2. **Lack of runtime analysis:** Training and inference times are not reported, which would strengthen the case for the lightweight design and real-world efficiency.
3. **Modest and inconsistent improvements:** On the simpler Spider-based datasets, performance gains are less pronounced and sometimes negative (e.g., READ-SQL-3B shows –0.2 on Spider-DK, +1.3 on Spider-Syn, +0.4 on Spider-Realistic). This raises concerns about generalization and potential overfitting, especially given augmented data primarily from BIRD.
4. **Unfair comparison:** READ-SQL-3B uses two 3B models (generator + corrector), so direct comparison with single CodeS-3B may not be appropriate.
5. **Unclear pipeline and motivation:** The inference pipeline from text input to SQL output is not clearly described. The motivation linking constraint identification/decomposition to solving the stated problems (misinterpreted/omitted/unwanted constraints) is weak; the paper claims existing methods lack end-to-end structure and question-to-SQL-clause relationships, yet T5- and GPT-4-based approaches have similar end-to-end structure, and schema linking already establishes such relationships. The three error types highlighted in the introduction are not addressed or measured in the experimental section.
6. **Technical unclear and missing details:**
   - What constitutes a “constraint” or “sub-SQL” is not precisely defined.
   - The goals and usage of Generator vs. Corrector in SQL generation, and how the Corrector is trained and realized, are not fully clear.
   - The relationship between generated questions (from sub-SQL) and original questions and their consistency is not addressed.
   - Section 3.4 is incomplete: it describes extraction of common/different constraints but not the subsequent self-correction process; Figure 4 partially explains it, but the text hinders comprehension.
7. **Performance on robustness datasets is limited:** Improvement on challenging BIRD is strong (+2.35) but on Spider-Syn and Spider-Realistic it is small (+1.3 and +0.4), and on Spider-DK it is negative (–0.2). This suggests the framework does not markedly improve robustness to ambiguous or paraphrased questions.
8. **Complex processing:** Generating question/reason pairs involves complex steps, and the benefit over simpler alternatives (e.g., zero-shot CoT) is not isolated. Scalability to larger models (e.g., 13B, 34B, GPT-4-level) is unexplored.
9. **Presentation issues:** Line 301 lists only two data types instead of three; Figure 4 caption mentions yellow color not present; Table 4 duplicates “1.3B” in the first two rows. Writing could be more direct and accessible.
10. (Minority reviewer contrast: while some reviewers find the technical contributions novel and the framework sound, Reviewer 5 considers the improvements incremental and the technical contributions limited, and Reviewer 4 and 5 question the clarity of motivation and overall contribution.)
