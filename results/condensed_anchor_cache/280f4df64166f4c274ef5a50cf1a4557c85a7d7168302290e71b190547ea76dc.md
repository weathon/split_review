## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes doubly-efficient debate protocols for scalable AI safety. Two adversarial polynomial-time provers compete to convince a verifier (with access to a human-judgment oracle) of the correctness of a computation. This improves over the original debate framework (Irving et al., 2018) by reducing the honest prover’s simulation from exponential to polynomial steps, handling stochastic AI systems, and requiring only a constant number of human queries. The formalism covers deterministic, stochastic, and witness-assisted settings, and protocols are proven complete and sound.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and contribution**: The doubly-efficient debate model is an innovative approach to verification in AI safety, providing a new perspective on the debate framework. The protocols are simple yet yield guarantees that improve over prior work (e.g., Irving et al., 2018).
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is well written and enjoyable to read. It introduces relevant background, uses practical examples (e.g., legal/contract drafting) to motivate the protocols, and explains technical details clearly. One reviewer rated presentation as “excellent.”
- **Rigorous analysis**: The protocols are accompanied by formal proofs of soundness and completeness, which appear correct. The analysis covers multiple scenarios (deterministic human judgment, stochastic human judgment, settings with witnesses), enhancing reliability.
- **Significance and practical grounding**: The work addresses a critical challenge in aligning increasingly capable AI systems. The adversarial-prover mechanism is promising for real-world oversight, especially for high-stakes applications like law or contracts. The results will interest researchers in alignment and scalable oversight.
- **Minority reviewer’s more positive view**: One reviewer (score 8) highlighted the work’s originality, good quality, and significance for AI safety, noting that the approach “makes improvements to an existing literature” and that the adversarial provers/human oversight is promising.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of experimental evaluation**: The paper is purely theoretical. It would benefit from a concrete empirical demonstration akin to Irving et al. (2018), but focused on LLMs. Experiments comparing this work to prior debate-based approaches would be useful. One reviewer explicitly asks for a discussion on whether an LLM-based experiment could be set up.
- **Practical implementation unclear**: Two reviewers note that the real-world viability is not deeply explored. Questions remain about how the protocols would be implemented to train an LLM, their scalability, robustness, and efficiency in deployment. The authors are asked to clarify how practitioners could use the method.
- **Unaddressed oracle error handling**: The framework assumes the human oracle is correct and unbiased (or at most stochastic with known parameters). This is a strong assumption. Reviewers raise concerns about: (i) how the protocol would survive false proofs when the oracle makes errors (e.g., minor buried assumptions) or gives inconsistent/incoherent judgments; (ii) whether there are robust strategies to mitigate oracle errors; (iii) what happens if the oracle is not K-Lipschitz (Def. 6.1). These issues are mentioned in the conclusion but not fully resolved.
- **Missing comparison to sound-reasoning approaches**: One reviewer points out that the approach relies on unsound reasoners (the humans and AI systems) and argues that the autoformalization project (e.g., Jiang et al.’s Draft, Sketch, Prove), which relies more on sound reasoners, should be compared. The paper does not discuss such connections.
- **Need for a simple running example**: Especially for readers outside the field, the paper lacks a straightforward end-to-end example that illustrates the protocol and why it improves the bound over Irving et al. (2018) intuitively. A concrete demonstration of a false proof that survives due to oracular error is also requested.
- **Clarification on theoretical choices**: Two questions are raised: (i) Are the constants in Definition 3.2 merely illustrative, or would other fractions qualitatively change results? (ii) The connection between the proposed protocols and the multiagent debate framework (Du et al., 2023) should be explained.
- **Minor typos** (line-specific): p.2 “makes progress both” → “makes progress on both”; p.2 “currently know for delegating” → “currently known for delegating”; p.3 final sentence is a fragment; “for it's correctness” → “for its correctness”.
