## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Chemma-RC, a multimodal large language model for chemical reaction condition recommendation (RCR). It integrates SMILES, reaction graphs, and textual corpora via a Perceiver module for modality alignment, fine-tuned from Llama 2 on a 1.2 million pair Q&A instruction dataset. Experiments on USPTO benchmarks show strong in-domain and out-of-domain (OOD) performance and generalization to high-throughput experimentation (HTE) datasets. The work is presented as a new case demonstrating multimodal LLMs for drug discovery and reaction planning.

### Strengths
+ **Multimodal integration**: Chemma-RC captures chemical reaction complexity by combining SMILES, reaction graphs, and textual descriptions, potentially improving predictive capability and understanding (R1, R3, R4).
+ **Large-scale training data**: The 1.2 million Q&A instruction dataset enhances learning efficacy and accuracy, especially for high-throughput experiments (R1, R2).
+ **Generalization and robustness**: The model performs well on OOD and HTE datasets, demonstrating adaptability across different data distributions (R2, R3).
+ **Text-augmented instruction prompts**: These guide the model effectively in learning and generating reaction conditions (R1).
+ **Comprehensive experimental evaluation**: Ablation studies and experimental results are provided (R3).
+ **Well-illustrated method**: Figures aid readability, and experimental setups and baselines are properly introduced (R4).
+ **Significance for RCR**: The paper makes promising contributions to the reaction condition recommendation problem (R4).

### Weaknesses
+ **Insufficient evidence of advantage over simpler models**: The core techniques (modality alignment, instruction tuning) are not convincingly shown to outperform simpler baselines like T-Rex and TextReact. Broader baselines and specific comparisons are needed (R2, R3). For example, comparisons with current state-of-the-art chemical language models that address reaction planning (e.g., BioT5+, nach0) are recommended (R3).
+ **Reaction condition recommendation novelty not fully justified in the LLM era**: The paper lacks a thorough discussion of why research should shift to LLM-based RCR—whether LLMs provide higher accuracy, more interpretability, or if performance of LLMs in this task has been benchmarked (R4).
+ **Limited task versatility and evaluation**: The model is evaluated only on condition prediction; additional tasks (e.g., reaction condition recommendation with USPTO dataset used by TextReact, retrosynthesis) and dataset splits (random split, time split) are needed to demonstrate broader capability (R2).
+ **Purpose and contribution of the new dataset unclear**: The dataset is generated by integrating existing data via GPT-4 templates, but the specific novelty beyond templating is not clear, and the correctness of generated templates is not verified (R3).
+ **Marginal benefit of graph embeddings**: Ablation (Table 4) shows only slight improvement from adding reaction graphs, suggesting that SMILES + textual corpus may suffice. An additional ablation study is needed to demonstrate cases where graph embeddings significantly enhance performance (R3). Consider also including a study that enables SMILES and graphs but disables the corpus (i.e., disabling the LLM part) (R4).
+ **Two condition prediction modules without motivation** (line 76): The paper introduces two types of prediction modules but does not provide a rationale. LLMs are more naturally suited for generative tasks rather than prediction, so the need for a separate prediction module is unclear (R4).
+ **Missing details on Q&A dataset construction**: There is insufficient description of data collection, deduplication, and measures to prevent data leakage (R4).
+ **High complexity and resource demands**: The multimodal integration increases training time, debugging challenges, and computational costs (GPU time, hardware requirements); such details are not provided (R1, R2). Suggestions include reporting computational requirements and discussing strategies to reduce demands (R2).
+ **Interpretability concerns**: The model’s complex architecture may hinder tracing the rationale behind specific predictions, which is critical in chemistry (R1).
+ **Risk of overfitting and data dependency**: The model depends on large, high-quality multimodal data; in data-scarce environments, it may overfit or perform poorly. Details on regularization techniques and validation strategies are lacking (R1, R2).
+ **Limited transferability**: Performance may vary across different chemical conditions or experimental contexts (R1).
+ **Clarity issues in the paper**: Several terms and figures need clarification: “unified reaction representation” (R2), “low sparsity of chemical data” (R2), “strict matching policy” for top-k accuracy (R2), “top-1 partial match accuracy” (R1, R2), “non-empty count” and “density” in Appendix B (R2). Figure 1 should be improved to distinguish data and model components (R4); a step-by-step walkthrough of the architecture is needed (R2). Prompt construction examples should be expanded (R2). The Perceiver module is introduced without citation or context (R4). The distinction between USPTO 500MT Condition and USPTO-Condition datasets is unclear (R4). The reason for freezing all model parameters and only fine-tuning projectors (lines 300–303) is not explained (R4).
