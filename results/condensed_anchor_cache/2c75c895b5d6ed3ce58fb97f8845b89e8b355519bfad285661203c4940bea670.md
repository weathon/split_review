## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a method to quantify the amount of inductive bias required for generalization on a given task, defined as the negative log probability that a random hypothesis drawn from a hypothesis space achieves a test loss below a threshold ε. The method involves sampling hypotheses (via gradient-based training for neural networks or closed-form solutions for kernel methods), modeling the test error distribution (e.g., as a chi-squared), and estimating the probability. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, Omniglot, and inverted pendulum control are performed using Gaussian RBF kernels and MLPs, and results are compared to the bound-based approach of Boopathy et al. (2023). Two reviewers scored the paper 5 and two scored 3, reflecting a split: the higher-scoring reviewers found the method novel and well-motivated, while lower-scoring reviewers questioned the theoretical novelty, experimental depth, and presentation maturity.

### Strengths
- The problem of quantifying inductive bias is recognized as important and relevant (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The paper introduces a direct estimation method that avoids upper bounds, contrasting with prior work (R1, R4).
- The definition (Def. 1) and Fig. 1 are clear and illustrative (R2, R4).
- Several reviewers found the paper well-written and easy to follow (R1, R3), and noted the clear introduction of background concepts (R4).
- The approach is considered novel by some (R1, R4), with the potential to be applicable to diverse hypothesis spaces.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical novelty is overstated** (R3). The test error distribution result for linear models (chi-squared) and the finite-sample approximation are standard; the paper presents them as new contributions without proper acknowledgment.
- **Experimental support is limited and the key claims are not convincingly justified** (R1, R2, R3, R4). The claims that “higher dimensional tasks require more inductive bias” and “neural networks encode massive inductive bias” are based on sparse experiments without theoretical backing (R1). Experiments only compare to Boopathy (R2); no other generalization measures or baselines are included (R2). Within each function class, different kernels, architectures, or hyperparameters are not systematically compared (R3, R4). Only simple tasks and MLPs are tested; it is unclear if the method scales to CNNs, Transformers, or more complex settings (R4). Code is not provided, making results non-reproducible (R3).
- **Definition and conceptual grounding are questioned** (R1, R2, R3). The scalar measure depends critically on the arbitrary threshold ε and the hypothesis distribution p_h, and may produce essentially arbitrary numbers (R1). The distinction between hypothesis space and model class appears artificial; the experimental model class is not a subset of the hypothesis space as stated (R3). For kernel methods, the kernel and bandwidth already define the inductive bias; the role of the proposed probability is unclear (R3). R2 questions whether the method merely measures generalization by counting how many randomly initialized networks generalize, i.e., “Is inductive bias simply generalization?”
- **Methodological assumptions and clarity are insufficient** (R1, R4). The kernel-based sampling assumptions (e.g., h(x) = φ(x)θ) are unrealistic (R1). The derivation in Sec. 3.3 is kernel-specific and its transferability to other spaces is unclear (R4). Section 4.2 (neural network experiments) is hard to follow, and Fig. 4 is not explained (R4). The paper reads like a draft: introduction is long and imprecise, contributions are presented in absolute terms (R2). Minor issues: why p_θ is a Gaussian process rather than a Gaussian (R1); K is undefined (R1); randomness of training set vs. fixed is unclear (R1).
- **Missing analyses and requested clarifications** (R1, R2, R3, R4). The definition does not capture the interplay between inductive bias, sample complexity, and learning algorithm (R1). How does the method relate to other works beyond Boopathy (R2)? What are the main take-aways from the experiments? (R2). Insights from Fig. 3 and Table 1 are unclear; training losses should be shown to check for underfitting (R3). Task dimensionality should be formally defined (R1). A comparison of optimization-based vs. kernel-based sampling is needed (R1). Justification for the choice of ε is missing (R4).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (scoring 5) found the idea sound and well-motivated but noted many of the above concerns. Two reviewers (scoring 3) were notably more critical, questioning the soundness (scores 1–2), presentation (scores 1–2), and whether the method makes a significant contribution (scores 2). The more critical reviewers felt the method only partially developed and the experimental evaluation insufficient to support the paper’s claims.
