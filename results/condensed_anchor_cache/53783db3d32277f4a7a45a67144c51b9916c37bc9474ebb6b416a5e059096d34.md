## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Partitioned Learned Count-Min Sketch (PL-CMS), extending the Learned CMS of Hsu et al. (ICLR 2019) by partitioning items into multiple frequency ranges based on predicted scores, with a separate Count-Min Sketch per range. The authors provide theoretical analysis (convex program for threshold optimization, an upper bound on false positive rate) and experimental results on four real-world datasets showing lower false positive rates compared to LCMS and standard CMS. Reviewers find the idea sound and intuitive, and the theoretical contribution solid, with promising empirical gains even under weak prediction. However, concerns center on limited novelty relative to prior learned Bloom filter partitioning (Vaidya et al., ICLR 2021), lack of robustness analysis under noisy or adversarial prediction, missing comparisons with non-learned sketches (SpaceSaving, Count Sketch), circularity in parameter estimation, insufficient justification of model space overhead, and several presentation/technical issues.

### Strengths

- PL-CMS achieves lower false positive rates compared to LCMS and standard CMS on multiple benchmarks (Reviewers 1, 4). Improvements are significant even with relatively simple/weak prediction (Reviewer 4).
- The idea of partitioning items into multiple frequency ranges is nice and intuitive (Reviewers 3, 4). Similar approaches exist in non-learned sketches (e.g., AugmentedSketch, ElasticSketch, Panakos) (Reviewer 3).
- Fills a gap in the literature of learned Count-Min Sketch structures (Reviewer 1).
- Theoretical contribution is solid: the analysis of how to set thresholds for each range is clear (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The theoretical upper bound on the false positive rate is provided (Reviewer 1).
- The parameter optimization method (convex program) is interesting and may have broader impact (Reviewer 3).
- Presentation is clear and easy to follow (Reviewer 2; also noted as good/excellent by Reviewers 1, 3, 4 despite some issues).

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty and relation to prior work** – The solution is a simple extension of the partitioning idea from Dai and Shrivastava (2020) and Vaidya et al. (ICLR 2021) (originally for learned Bloom filters) to LCMS. This discounts novelty, though not a strong criticism (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 4 also notes similar ideas were considered in Vaidya et al. (2021).

2. **Robustness and prediction error analysis** – The paper does not study the case when the ML prediction is noisy or completely wrong, which is a central part of the algorithms-with-predictions paradigm. Partition into multiple ranges requires even higher prediction precision, making such analysis important (Reviewer 2). No formal analysis relates prediction error magnitude to the resulting false positive rate or estimation error (e.g., what if the model consistently overestimates a subset of items?) (Reviewer 2). There is no measure of prediction error and no tradeoff discussion between robustness and consistency (Reviewer 4).

3. **Missing comparisons** – The stream model assumption is not clarified. For insertion-only or bounded-deletion streams, the paper should compare with SpaceSaving (see arXiv:2309.12623 and arXiv:1803.08777). For turnstile model, include Count Sketch (Charikar, Chen, Farach-Colton) (Reviewer 3). Comparison with the two methods of Zhang et al. (2020) is also requested (Reviewer 1).

4. **Parameter choice and optimization issues** – The choice of thresholds and number of partitions is not well explained (page 7 para 4, Section 4.5). How does this relate to Fig 2? (Reviewer 1). The claim that using a larger number of score thresholds typically does not improve performance (page 6) seems to contradict Section 5’s statement that “determining the optimal number of partitions is a crucial next step that can significantly enhance its performance” (Reviewer 4). The introduction of a parameter \(p\) in the exponent (page 6, third paragraph) appears arbitrary; more intuition is needed on why the theoretical upper bound does not work well (Reviewer 4). The parameters \(E_i\) and \(F_i\) depend on the thresholds \(t_i\), but the method appears to estimate \(E_i\) and \(F_i\) first and then use them to set the thresholds – a circular dependency that needs clarification (Reviewer 4). How is the score \(l_i\) decided and learned? Example: Macbeth uses thresholds 200|100, 300|200|100 (Reviewer 3).

5. **Model space justification** – The paper states “We ignore the space of the learned model itself, which is lower order in our settings.” Reviewers request the actual space taken by the model and CMS tables together for each dataset to justify this claim (Reviewers 1, 4).

6. **AOL dataset controversy** – The AOL search log release is controversial; it is recommended to remove those experimental results (Reviewer 3).

7. **Presentation and citation issues** – Fig 2 needs legends (Reviewer 1). Typos: “which region is falls in” (should be “which region it falls in”); unpaired parenthesis in Sec 2.2 item 1 (Reviewer 4). Only the arXiv version of Vaidya et al. (ICLR 2021) is cited – the conference version should be used. Several references do not have their venues listed (e.g., Chabchoub et al., 2009; Dolera et al., 2022) (Reviewer 4). Code availability for replication is not provided (Reviewer 1).

8. **Definition of heavy items** – Can the analysis be extended to the definition \(f_i \ge \sum_j f_j / k\) (common in heavy-hitter literature) rather than \(f_i \ge n/k\)? (Reviewer 2 question, missing extension).

9. **Additional requested analysis** – In experiments, there is a gap between ideal and noisy prediction; a brief analysis of the precision of the current prediction model would be valuable (Reviewer 2). The algorithm requires additional information (\(E_i\), \(F_i\)) that are artifacts of the algorithm, not natural to the heavy-hitter problem (Reviewer 4).
