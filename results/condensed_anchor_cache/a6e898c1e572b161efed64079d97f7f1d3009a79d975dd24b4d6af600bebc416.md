## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a generative approach to behavioral stylometry—modeling individual player behavior in games. It casts stylometry as a multi-task learning problem, using parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with multi-head routing (MHR) to learn shared skill parameters and per‑player style vectors. These vectors enable both generating actions in a player’s style and steering behavior (e.g., interpolating between players). The method is tested on two games: chess (47,864 players) and Rocket League (2,000 players). The authors claim comparable performance to behavioral cloning methods that do full fine‑tuning, while being more parameter‑efficient and supporting few‑shot adaptation to new players.

### Strengths
- The method is novel in applying PEFT (specifically LoRA) and style vectors to individual behavior modeling, combining existing components (Polytropon, MHR) in a new context (behavioral stylometry for style‑customized agents).
- Style vectors are generative (can produce actions in a player’s style) and induce a latent space that is interpretable and manipulable (e.g., interpolation, steering toward desired attributes like strength).
- The approach is scalable to tens of thousands of players and parameter‑efficient: only a small number of additional parameters per player are needed.
- Experiments are carefully designed and confirm the representativeness of learned style vectors.
- The methodology is tested on two distinct gaming environments (chess and Rocket League) with large datasets.
- The paper provides sufficient detail for reproducibility; one reviewer felt confident in reproducing the method.
- The analysis of style vectors reveals they are consistent within a player and across players, and supports basic arithmetic operations (e.g., interpolating between weak and strong players).
- **Minority positive view (Reviewer 3, score 10):** Found the paper a “fascinating read” with compelling methodology and experiments; was left interested in applying the approach to other problem domains.

### Weaknesses
- The abstract is confusing about the purpose of modeling human behavior with AI and the broader applicability beyond games.
- The method’s domain specificity is a concern: contributions are restricted to stylistic adaptation in gaming, without convincing demonstration of generalization to other forms of human behavior modeling. No experiments on non‑game tasks are provided.
- Performance often lags behind state‑of‑the‑art behavioral cloning methods (Figure 2), and the performance of MHR‑MAIA decreases as the number of games increases; this is not adequately explained (analysis in Section 5.2 is late and insufficient).
- It is unclear if results in Table 1 are statistically significant; the comparisons may not be fair because results for McIlroy‑Young et al. are borrowed from previous papers using potentially different test sets. The authors should apply their model to the same test set as prior work.
- The base model used in experiments is not specified; missing citation for the original Maia model (lines 230‑231).
- The metric “move‑matching accuracy” is introduced without explanation of its computation (line 262).
- The claim that the paper “introduces the notion of style vectors” (line 89) is inaccurate; style vectors are already established in NLP literature.
- More baselines are needed: the Transformer‑based embedding method (even if not generative) should be compared; for extensions to image generation, comparisons to CLIP‑guided editing, DreamBooth, or StarGAN are missing.
- Experiments on Rocket League are limited; more replication of the chess analyses on the second environment is desired.
- No user study was conducted to assess whether generated actions are perceived as authentic by human observers.
- The analysis of few‑shot performance for unseen players as a function of the amount of tuning data (number of games) is missing; a figure showing move prediction accuracy vs. number of tuning games would be valuable.
- The handling of player behavior that changes significantly over time is not addressed.
- Figure 1 is unclear: it is difficult to distinguish MHR adapters from the routing matrix.
- Table 1: the meaning of bold numbers is not defined.
- Figure 7: the y‑axis label is missing or ambiguous.
- The “Random (%)” column in Table 1 shows 0.25 for all entries; this is not explained.
- For the few‑shot unseen players, only a comparison to McIlroy‑Young et al. (2021) is provided; the reason for not comparing to McIlroy‑Young et al. (2022b) is not given.
- The number of reference games available for each player during few‑shot learning is not reported.
- Additional clarifications are needed on context, figure interpretation, and metric definitions (see specific comments from reviewers).
