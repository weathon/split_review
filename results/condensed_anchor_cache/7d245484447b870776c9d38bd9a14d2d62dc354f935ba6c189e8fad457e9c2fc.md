## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies how decoder transformers learn nested structures (boolean logic simplification, Dyck language completion) and whether they extrapolate to deeper depths. The authors present a theoretical construction of a 1‑layer, 1‑head transformer with learned position encodings that can memorize a single training sequence and generalize to any depth for the Dyck language. Through experiments, they show that gradient‑trained transformers fail to extrapolate when the depth gap widens, even with large samples and embedding dimensions. Reviewer opinions are split: two reviewers (scores 6, confidence 3) find the theoretical construction interesting and the empirical studies valuable, while two reviewers (scores 3, confidence 3) criticize poor writing, limited experimental rigor, and a simplistic theoretical setup (e.g., assuming Q=0). There is consensus that the presentation needs improvement and more thorough analysis is required.

### Strengths
- The paper presents an interesting theoretical construction of a 1‑layer, 1‑head Transformer with learned position encodings that can generate valid Dyck language sequences for any prefix, which is different from previous works (R1, R3, R4).
- The analytical solution demonstrates that the problem is solvable with the given architecture, and the stability analysis with λ (Figure 1) is insightful (R3).
- Theorem 3.1 is notable: for appropriately scaled values v and γ, the model can either correctly predict the sequence or perform worse (when γ and v have opposing signs and are small) (R2).
- The observation that training on more difficult depths enables better performance on simpler depths is interesting (R2).
- Experiments with Dyck languages show that learned position encodings can cause the model to memorize how to finish a clause, offering insight into memorization (R2).
- Experiments on deeper layers and larger embedding dimensions show that when trained on sufficiently large samples, models can do well on the validation set (R2).
- The observation that the value matrix approximates −vI is interesting given the theory (R2).
- The paper provides a vast range of empirical studies to justify theoretical assumptions and demonstrate that the learned architecture differs from the generalizable construction (R1).
- The framing of memorization into a toy closed‑form model is original (R4).
- The usage of a specific problem with varying difficulty (boolean expression simplification with varying nesting depths) to test large models for generalization is a neat idea (R4).
- The consistent formatting (Training setup, Figure, Observations) is appreciated (R3).

### Weaknesses
- The writing is confusing and difficult to follow. The abstract is unclear and contains disconnected sentences. Key notations (e.g., γ, V) are introduced without definition. The paper contains irrelevant references (e.g., “See Appendix” with no valid reference) (R3, R4).
- The theoretical construction assumes Q=0, which eliminates the model’s ability to attend based on the query. This drastically simplifies the attention mechanism and may not reflect the behavior of standard transformers. It is unclear how results would change if Q were learnable or non‑zero (R2, R3).
- The theoretical solution is only demonstrated for a single training sequence. For multiple sequences with shared prefixes but different completions, the construction would not minimize training loss, explaining the discrepancy between theory and experiments. The results on multiple sequences also assume tokens are independent across sequences, which may not hold in practice (R1, R2).
- Missing ablation studies: it is unclear which of the hard‑coded inductive biases (embeddings set to −1/+1, Q=0, V=vI) are critical for generalization. Relaxing each constraint individually would help isolate the necessary conditions (R3).
- Experimental evaluation lacks rigor. For the boolean logic task, only 10 samples at depth 1 and 5 at depth 3 were tested, which is insufficient for meaningful conclusions. Error bars and statistical significance are absent across experiments (R4).
- Figure 3 evidence is unconvincing: the second largest singular value is positive, only one seed is shown, and the meaning of the “value matrix (times projection transpose)” is unclear. The connection between low rank and V=−vI is insufficiently explained (R3).
- The evaluation protocols are unclear. In Section 4.2, it is not specified how training and test sets are constructed with respect to depth parameter q. Figure 2 left: it is unclear if it is on a held‑out sample and what the training set size is. Figure 2 right: the testing depth range is ambiguous. Table 1: it is unclear whether results are from training on q≤N or only q=N, and the observation that train q=2/test q=3 in‑sample is worse than train q=5/test q=6 out‑of‑sample deserves explanation (possibly due to unequal token counts) (R3).
- The paper does not discuss how its results relate to prior work on depth generalization, and the high‑level conclusion (“models fail to extrapolate”) is not novel (R1, R3).
- The boolean simplification task may be too difficult to expect LLMs to solve, and the paper does not convincingly justify why this task is a good testbed for extrapolation (R2; contrast with R4 who found the idea neat).
- It is not verified whether boolean expression simplification accounts for permutation of variables (e.g., p3 & p2 vs p2 & p3), which could affect accuracy (R4).
- The rationale for specific model architecture choices (e.g., 8 layers) in the boolean logic experiments is not explained, and better settings might exist (R4).
- The paper does not demonstrate that the findings transfer to real-world models beyond the toy setup (1‑layer, 1‑head with Q=0) (R4).
