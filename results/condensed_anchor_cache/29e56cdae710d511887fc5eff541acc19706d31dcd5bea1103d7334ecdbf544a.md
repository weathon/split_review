## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a scale-time equivalence hypothesis: training a smaller model longer is functionally equivalent to training a larger model shorter. It analyzes a random subspace/feature model with gradient flow to derive theoretical bounds, and conducts experiments on small-scale vision benchmarks (MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10) with small MLPs and CNNs. The idea is applied to reinterpret double descent and other phenomena. One reviewer (R2) was more positive (score 6, later raised conditioned on promised changes) and found the theoretical take on scaling and double descent interesting and the paper mostly well-written with clear figures. The other three reviewers (R1, R3, R4) rated 3 and expressed significant concerns about overclaimed novelty, weak theoretical connection to neural networks, insufficient justification of key assumptions, and lack of empirical rigor. All reviewers fundamentally question whether the analysis extends beyond the specific proxy models to realistic neural network scaling.

### Strengths

- The idea of functional equivalence between model scale and training time is potentially attractive for practical neural network training, where compute-optimal trade-offs and inference-time benefits of smaller models are relevant. (R1, R3)
- The formalization of the relationship between training time and model scale is interesting from a theoretical perspective, especially in the context of neural scaling laws and its connection to double descent. (R1, R2, R4)
- The paper tests its claims empirically on multiple datasets (MNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10) and architectures (MLPs, CNNs). (R3)
- The analysis offers novel explanations for double descent, overparameterization sensitivity to label noise, and the effect of data volume. (R4)
- Figures are mostly clear. (R2)

### Weaknesses

1. **Overclaimed novelty and scope.** The abstract and introduction claim a theoretical characterization of “deep neural networks” and “large-scale networks”, but the theory only analyzes a simple random subspace/feature model with gradient flow, and experiments are limited to very small MNIST/SVHN/CIFAR-10 networks. This mismatch between claims and actual contribution is a major flaw. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Poor theoretical connection to neural networks.** The random subspace model (Eq. 2) is unusual and not convincingly motivated as an approximation for neural network training. The choice of parameters (r, p, P) is not explained; a constant low-rank subspace might suffice. Standard theoretical settings where scaling laws and double descent have been tractably studied (e.g., teacher-student, random feature models, Advani & Saxe 2017) are not used. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
   - One reviewer (R1) notes that the paper neither justifies why this model is a good approximation nor provides citations for prior use of similar models.
   - Another (R2) points out that the claim that a small NN is a subnetwork of a larger one would not produce a random matrix like R.
3. **Heuristic and unjustified definition of model scale.** The core definition “model scale = N^{1/3}” (cube root of parameter count) is arbitrary and ignores data complexity. The paper relies heavily on this heuristic to establish scale-time equivalence but provides only a weak argument in the main body. (R1, R2)
4. **Vacuous theoretical bound.** The bound in Eq. (4) appears to be dominated by the exponential term e^{pt}, so the distance between α_t and A_{pt} is upper bounded by a quantity that grows with p and t – rendering the bound trivial or meaningless. The appearance of A_{pt} and its derivation from Eq. (36) are not clearly explained. (R3)
5. **Missing empirical details and incomplete figures.** Several plots use vague legends (“darker lines indicate a smaller error threshold” / “more data” without numerical values). In Figure 3, Column 2, the predicted (dashed) lines are incomplete. (R3) The paper is over-condensed, with Section 4 particularly light on experimental details. (R2)
6. **Inadequate evaluation of learning rates and optimization.** Only two learning rates are tested; the optimal LR typically shifts with model scale (e.g., µP literature), yet this is not addressed. The theory assumes η independent of p (Eq. L152) and uses gradient flow (full-batch), while experiments use mini-batch SGD. The effect of batch size is not examined. (R1, R2, R4)
7. **Unconvincing double descent analysis.** Figure 5 shows no U-shaped curves (the hallmark of double descent), contradicting the claim that larger models have a smaller time to interpolation threshold. The paper does not reproduce double descent in model size and training time to validate scale-time equivalence predictions. (R1) The analysis in Section 5 is very similar to Advani & Saxe 2017 and does not clearly demonstrate novel contributions. (R3)
8. **Conceptual issues with capacity and underparameterization.** The claim that model scale and training time can be freely traded off (line 505) implies that a model with fewer parameters than the intrinsic data dimension (p < D) could still fit the data by training longer – a statement that contradicts standard capacity arguments. The crucial concept of “minimal capacity to fit data to a certain loss level” is never properly defined or addressed. (R1, R3)
9. **Scaling laws not rigorously derived for neural networks.** Equations (9,10) are derived for linear models, but the subsequent discussion (Sec. 5.2–5.5) treats them as if they apply directly to nonlinear NNs, relying on heuristic assumptions about signal/noise components without rigorous justification. (R4)
10. **Lack of related work and limitations discussion.** The paper fails to discuss important related lines of work: the correspondence between training time and regularization strength (early stopping), and more recent neural scaling law theories (e.g., linear models with one-pass SGD). (R1, R4) The paper also does not include an explicit limitations section. (R2, R4)
11. **Clarity issues and questions.** Several passages are unclear: (a) Section 3.1 and the applicability of gradient flow to SGD flat minima (L130), (b) the meaning of “minimum training time to achieve non-zero generalization” (L209), (c) why the cube root argument is not justified in the main body (L244), (d) the derivation of Eq. (10) from Eq. (9) and the role of parameter size p. (R2, R4) There are also textual typos (e.g., missing “the” in lines 367-369). (R1)
