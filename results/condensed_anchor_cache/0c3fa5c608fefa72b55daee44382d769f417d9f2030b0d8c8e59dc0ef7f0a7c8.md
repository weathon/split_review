## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper applies GFlowNets to automate the design of parameterized quantum circuit ansätze for Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs). The GFlowNet-based quantum architecture search (QAS) is evaluated on molecular ground state energy estimation (LiH, H₂) and the unweighted Max-Cut problem (up to 10 nodes). The method claims to achieve chemical accuracy while reducing parameter counts, gate counts, and circuit depth compared to baselines such as QuantumDARTS and UCCSD. Transferability across different bond lengths for LiH is also demonstrated.

### Strengths
1. **Novelty of applying GFlowNets to quantum circuit design**: To the reviewers’ knowledge, the application of GFlowNets to variational quantum circuits is novel. The GFlowNet framework fits the nature of quantum architecture search due to its close relationship with DAGs and state transitions. This can be explored further as an AI-for-quantum algorithm direction. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
2. **Use of forward mask to filter redundant gates**: The paper employs a forward mask to eliminate unnecessary gates, a practical design choice. (Reviewer 3)
3. **Well-structured and clearly presented**: The article is well-organized and selects appropriate experimental tasks to demonstrate the method’s efficiency. (Reviewer 1)
4. **Substantial reduction in circuit resources for ground state energy**: The method achieves chemical precision with an order-of-magnitude reduction in parameters, gate counts, and depth compared to baselines (e.g., QuantumDARTS, UCCSD). For unweighted Max-Cut, similar reductions are observed in circuit depth and gate counts. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
5. **Transferability**: The generated circuits for LiH transfer effectively across different bond lengths (Figure 2). (Reviewer 3)
6. **Interesting direction**: Leveraging generative models for quantum algorithms is considered an interesting topic. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
1. **Scalability and computational cost not adequately addressed**:  
   - The paper lacks a rigorous analysis of how the GFlowNet’s computational cost scales with increasing qubit number and circuit depth. Training time and resource growth with problem complexity are not reported. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)  
   - The state space design and how it handles exponential complexity are not discussed. (Reviewer 2)  
   - It is unclear how ansatz parameters and ansatz architecture are optimized simultaneously from a computational cost perspective. (Reviewer 2)  
   - The experiments are limited to very small problem sizes: LiH with 4 and 6 qubits for ground state energy and 10-node Max-Cut. One reviewer notes that related works have demonstrated QAS on circuits with over 100 qubits (e.g., QuantumNAS). The current scale does not sufficiently demonstrate scalability, and the reduction in parameters is not shown to scale favorably with problem size. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)

2. **Insufficient and weak baselines**:  
   - QuantumDARTS is not state-of-the-art; more recent methods exist, such as curriculum reinforcement learning for ground state energy (e.g., [2]) and other QAS works. A more thorough comparison with current SOTA methods is needed. (Reviewers 1, 2)  
   - Only one architecture search baseline (QDARTS) is compared in Figure 3 and Tables 1/3. One reviewer notes that QDARTS may be more efficient, making the comparison incomplete. (Reviewer 2)  
   - In Table 1, all methods except QCAS achieve chemical accuracy, and “Ours” does not produce the smallest error, raising questions about absolute performance. (Reviewer 3)

3. **Lack of theoretical guarantees and analysis**:  
   - The paper provides only Theorem 4.1; a deeper theoretical analysis of convergence properties, conditions for optimality, and robustness to hyperparameters/initial conditions is missing. (Reviewer 1)  
   - While trajectory balance (TB) is used for faster credit assignment and robustness to long trajectories, the paper does not explain how these features translate to effective circuit generation. “Long trajectories” in quantum circuit construction is not well-defined. (Reviewer 3)

4. **Missing comparisons and ablations for GFlowNet design choices**:  
   - The paper should compare other GFlowNet objectives (e.g., flow matching, subtrajectory balance, local credit). (Reviewer 3)  
   - When discussing transferability, Figure 2 only compares VQE-GFNs to HF; how does transferability compare for other generated circuits like UCCSD, QuantumDARTS, QCAS, DQAS? (Reviewer 3)  
   - The use of conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) for Max-Cut is unusual; more common metrics like average or maximum cut size should also be reported, and the operational meaning of CVaR needs explanation. (Reviewer 4)

5. **Missing consideration of hardware constraints**:  
   - The framework does not account for hardware topology or noise, which are crucial for NISQ devices. Such constraints could increase circuit depth and gate counts, making practical applicability questionable. (Reviewer 3)

6. **Experimental reporting issues**:  
   - In Figure 2, the energy values for both bond lengths are very close to the ground state, making it hard to distinguish. Energy errors (as in [1]) should be provided to assess precision. (Reviewer 1)  
   - Standard deviations are missing from Figure 3 and Tables 1 & 3. (Reviewer 2)  
   - One reviewer questions why circuits consistently have fewer parameters than the parameter limit (Figures 3, 7, 8, 9). Could the training stop too early? Are masks too strict? Will the model always return a circuit below the limit for more complex problems? (Reviewer 3)

7. **Incremental contribution and lack of problem structure**:  
   - The paper could be considered incremental, as it simply applies a different ML model (GFlowNets) to VQAs. There is little that particularly stands out compared to other QAS papers. (Reviewer 4; note: Reviewer 1 considered novelty higher)  
   - The GFlowNet search does not leverage problem-specific structure (e.g., permutation symmetry) to reduce the search space, which many VQAs exploit. (Reviewer 4)  
   - Minor grammatical issues and typos exist throughout the paper. (Reviewer 4)

**Note on Reviewer Disagreement**: One reviewer (score 6) found the paper novel and well-structured, while two reviewers (scores 3, 3) considered the novelty weak and the experiments insufficient. The fourth reviewer (score 5) noted novelty and strong results but raised concerns about incrementality and lack of problem structure.
