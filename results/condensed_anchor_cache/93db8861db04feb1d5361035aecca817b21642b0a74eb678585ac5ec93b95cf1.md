## Merged Review

### Summary
This work applies mechanistic interpretability techniques to understand how visual information is localized and processed in the language model component of LLaVA VLMs. Three main methods are used: (1) ablating object-region tokens (replaced with a global average token) versus register tokens, random tokens, and high-gradient tokens (via Integrated Gradients); (2) applying a Logit Lens (Nostalgebreist 2020) to decode intermediate-layer visual token representations into the vocabulary space; (3) attention knockout to block attention between specific token subsets. Findings: object identification accuracy drops by over 70% when object-specific tokens are removed; visual token representations become increasingly interpretable in vocabulary space across layers; the model extracts object information from refined visual token representations at the last token position for prediction, mirroring factual association processes in text-only LMs. Experiments are performed on two LLaVA-family models (LLaVA-1.5-7B and LLaVA-Phi-3-mini).

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: Addresses the crucial question of how visual knowledge is localized and processed in transformer-based VLMs (R1, R2, R4).
- **Well-written and well-organized**: Paper is clearly written with effective diagrams that make complex concepts accessible (R1, R2, R3).
- **Well-motivated, thorough analysis**: Each sub-question is well-motivated with well-designed methods and metrics; analyses are thorough and findings well-summarized (R1, R2).
- **Token ablation experiments are soundly constructed**: The filtration strategy and choice of baselines provide good coverage of alternative explanations, clearly showing object information is localized in object-region tokens (R3). Table 1 demonstrates significantly higher degradation when ablating object-level tokens compared to comparable numbers of random or high-gradient tokens (R3).
- **Innovative approach**: Insights on visual token alignment could inform techniques to improve VLM robustness and reduce hallucinations (R2). Quantitative analysis of object visual tokens' effect is novel (R4).
- **High confidence from two reviewers**: R1 and R3 gave scores of 8 (R1 raised from 6 after author response) and 8, respectively, indicating strong support (minority higher scores).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient model diversity**: Analysis is limited to two models from the LLaVA family, which share a common architecture and training methodology. It is unclear whether findings generalize to other transformer-based VLMs (e.g., those with different visual encoders, cross-attention mechanisms, or pre-training strategies). This limits the impact and representativeness of the conclusions (R1, R2). R4 additionally notes that the layer splitting used in Table 2 experiments may behave differently for models with fewer layers (e.g., Phi-3 vs. Vicuna-7B), and this difference is not discussed.
- **Logit Lens analysis lacks quantitative validation**: Only a handful of patch-token correspondences are shown, apparently from a single (unspecified) layer, with no systematic selection or evaluation methodology. The absence of quantitative metrics and analysis across multiple layers makes it difficult to assess whether the observed mappings are meaningful or cherry-picked; readers may assume confirmation bias (R1). R3 similarly asks for statistics on how reliably such correspondences are observed and whether the presented examples are representative (R3 questions L350-L352).
- **Attention flow experiments are inconclusive**: The knockout experiments in Table 2 show a performance degradation when blocking attention from object tokens to the last visual token, but the setup does not isolate visual token contributions from text token influences. An additional condition knocking out some variant of text token attention is needed to sufficiently test the claim about where information is aggregated (R3, per L219-L228). R4 also finds the definition of "last row of visual tokens" (e.g., CLIP-L 16×16 → last 16 tokens) confusing and requests clarification on why such experiments are required.
- **Missing experimental details affecting reproducibility**: Several implementation details are omitted: (a) How are object tokens identified and correspondences assigned—manual or automated? (R3 questions L242-L246, L262-L266, L350-L352). (b) What is the precise definition of "comparable number of tokens" for ablation? (R3 question L329-L330). (c) Was ablation also attempted by zeroing out embeddings instead of using the global average token, and why was that design choice made? (R3 question L257-L259). (d) For the Logit Lens analysis (Figure 3), at which layer are the unembeddings taken? If selected from different layers, how was that decided? (R1 question, R3 question L350-L352). These gaps make it difficult to assess robustness and generalizability.
- **Lack of demonstrated downstream application**: While the paper offers interesting insights, it does not show how they translate into practical improvements (e.g., reducing hallucinations, improving robustness). Concrete experiments manipulating object-specific tokens or modifying attention mechanisms based on findings are missing (R2, R4). R2 specifically asks whether the methods can be easily applied to downstream tasks; R4 suggests exploring whether simply repeating or augmenting object visual tokens, or using text/soft prompts, could improve performance.
- **Narrow scope on object tokens only**: The study focuses solely on object visual tokens. Other important token types (e.g., OCR tokens, text rendering tokens, knowledge-related tokens) are not investigated. Blocking these tokens could affect tasks like TextVQA, OCRVQA, MME, POPE, leaving unanswered questions about broader applicability (R4).
- **Minor reviewer disagreements**: Two reviewers (R2, R4) gave scores of 6 with moderate confidence (4 and 3, respectively) and raise concerns about generalizability, practical application, and missing details, contrasting with the two 8-score reviewers. R4 also rated presentation at 2, lower than others.
