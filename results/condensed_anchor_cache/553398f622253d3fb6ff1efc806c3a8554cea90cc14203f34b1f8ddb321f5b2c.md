## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates using sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to remove knowledge from language models, focusing on the biology subset of WMDP (Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy dataset, ~300 questions) and two models: Gemma-2b-it and Gemma-2-2b-it. It finds that negative scaling of biology-related SAE feature activations can unlearn a subset of WMDP-Bio questions with minimal side effects on other domains, while zero ablating features is ineffective. Intervening with multiple SAE features simultaneously can unlearn multiple topics but produces side effects similar to or larger than the fine-tuning-based RMU method. Overall, SAE-based unlearning currently underperforms RMU and requires further improvement.

### Strengths
- Applying SAEs to unlearning is an interesting and practical approach to controlled, interpretable knowledge removal (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The methodology is clearly defined, with good ablations (e.g., Section 4.2) and detailed analysis of individual SAE features (Reviewer 1).
- SAE interventions provide precise, transparent control over targeted knowledge by manipulating activations rather than modifying weights (Reviewer 4). This activation-based approach offers novelty over existing fine-tuning methods (Reviewer 1 notes interpretability advantage over RMU).
- Section 3 and Figure 2 are particularly strong (Reviewer 1).
- The work addresses an important AI safety issue (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only a small subset of WMDP is used (~300 questions, <8% of full dataset). Results on other subsets (Chemistry, Cybersecurity) are missing, which would better assess generalizability (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). One reviewer suggests testing on disjoint-task combinations (e.g., biology+chemistry) (Reviewer 1).
- **Performance deficit**: The proposed method underperforms the existing RMU baseline on WMDP and MMLU. Exact MMLU accuracy for Gemma models is unclear but appears close to random (~25%); authors should clarify the MMLU subset size, selection criteria, and whether overall performance improves or worsens (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of clarity on methodology and novelty**:
  * The novelty over simple negative activation in previous SAE work ([1], [2]) is not clearly differentiated (Reviewer 3).
  * The paper lacks a mathematical formulation; no equations explain the SAE framework. It reads like a conversation between engineers rather than a technical report (Reviewer 2).
  * Terminology is introduced late: by Section 2.4 (“How to Select Relevant SAE Features”), readers still have no formal definition of an SAE or what “feature” means. Section 3 is verbose and relies on “math-intuition” that is unintuitive (Reviewer 2).
- **Side effects poorly characterized**: While side effects on OpenWebText are reported, the nature of the performance drop is unexplained—what datapoints are misclassified and how many? The loss on unrelated domains is a significant weakness (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Interpretability of negative scaling**: According to monosemanticity, zeroing a feature should suppress that knowledge; it is unclear why negative values (e.g., -1×, -10×, -50×, -300×) work better. The meaning of negative scaling and whether feature activation is one-dimensional requires deeper explanation (Reviewer 3).
- **Evaluation limited to multiple-choice QA**: It is unclear how the method would perform on open-ended generation (Reviewer 4).
- **Practical concerns**: The method may require retraining SAEs for each new domain and needs access to domain-specific data. Training and inference costs of SAEs are not discussed, nor is transferability to other LLMs (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation issues**:
  * Figure placement is poor (Figure 10 cited before Figure 2; Figure 2 appears far from its text reference) (Reviewer 2).
  * Figure 1 is bare; could include results or WMDP examples to tailor it to the paper’s goal (Reviewer 1).
  * Figure 4 is hard to interpret; Figure 5 should use a distinct color for random decoder vectors (Reviewer 1).
  * Minor: inconsistent use of # symbol for both feature numbers and question numbers; typo “probably” for “probability”; L0 not defined for readers unfamiliar with SAEs; conclusion says “10-20 features” but only end values used; list in conclusion has three items not four (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing related work section**: The paper lacks a dedicated related work section to contextualize SAE usage for feature adjustment and unlearning (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete reporting**: Experiments in Section 3 use Gemma-2b-it, but main results in Section 4 only show Gemma-2-2b-it (Reviewer 1). A random feature ablation experiment is mentioned but results are not shown (Reviewer 1). Hyperparameter choices (e.g., scaled activations values: 1×, 10×, 50×, 300×; why 300× in Figure 5 but not Figure 4) are not justified (Reviewer 1).
- **Disagreement on presentation**: One reviewer finds methodology clearly defined (Reviewer 1), while another finds the paper not concise and poorly presented (Reviewer 2; Presentation: 1 vs 3). One reviewer gives a higher score (6) and is more positive about the approach overall, but still notes side effects and limited evaluation (Reviewer 4).

**Reviewer questions folded into weaknesses**:
- Why were specific clamped activation values (1×, 10×, 50×, 300×) and layers chosen? (Reviewer 1)
- Why the discrepancy in using 300× in Figure 5 but not Figure 4? (Reviewer 1)
- Which intermediate layers were SAEs trained on? (Reviewer 1 reports layers 3 and 9 are used at different points.)
- Why no results for Gemma-2b-it in the main evaluation body? (Reviewer 1)
- What is the rationale for a “perfectly unlearned model” target score below 6? (Reviewer 3)
- Is the negative scaling limited to one-dimensional activation explanation? (Reviewer 3)
- How does the method handle open-ended questions? (Reviewer 4)
- What are the additional training and inference costs of SAE? (Reviewer 4)
- Can a trained SAE be transferred to other LLMs? (Reviewer 4)
