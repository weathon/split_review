## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses few-shot sim-to-real RL by proposing a trust-region penalty that constrains the state-action visitation distribution between the simulator and the real environment, creating a method that resembles a combination of GAIL and TRPO. In the experimental setup, a source simulator and a target simulator (acting as the “real” environment) are used. The policy maximizes simulation reward while minimizing a divergence between state(-action) marginals in the source and target. The method outperforms DARC, ANE, and CQL baselines on locomotion tasks (OpenAI Gym, Minitaur). Reviewers are divided: two rate the paper 5 (positive), two rate it 3 (negative/neutral), with overall concerns about novelty, theoretical justification, experimental detail, and lack of real-world evaluation.

### Strengths
- The problem is well-motivated and of great interest to the community (R1, R4).
- The approach is simple and intuitive, and appears novel to at least one reviewer (R1); it makes fewer assumptions than related works (e.g., DualDICE) and can be applied in more settings (R1).
- The evaluation suite is extensive, covering high-dimensional systems and classical RL benchmarks (R1, R3).
- The literature review is clear and complete, distinguishing classes of sim-to-real approaches (R4).
- Experimental construction is clear and well-justified, with a sensitivity analysis on the key hyperparameter α (R4).
- The method is supported by theoretical justification connecting it to trust-region and imitation learning concepts (R3).
- Across most tested environments, the proposed method shows performance improvements over DARC, ANE, and CQL baselines (R1, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and theoretical contribution**  
  Multiple reviewers find the method a straightforward combination of RL (TRPO) and imitation learning (GAIL), with insufficient novelty (R2, R3). One reviewer (R1) disagrees and considers the approach novel.  
  - The theoretical part mainly follows derivations from TRPO/GAIL; no novel theoretical contribution is made (R3).  
  - The definition of \(V_{imit}^\pi\) in Eq. 5 is not provided, and the equation is not justified with proof (R2).  
  - Proposition 4.1 is not clearly related to the practical algorithm: it bounds state-action-state visitation while FOOD minimizes state-action (or state) visitation; moreover, \(J\) is never defined and \(\epsilon\) (the trust-region size) does not appear in the result (R2, R4). It is unclear whether the proposition applies per iteration or to the final outcome (R4).  
  - The sentences above Section 4.2 are difficult to follow (R4).

- **Algorithm description and clarity**  
  - Algorithm 1 is confusing: “select best trajectories” is not explained, nor is the criterion for “best” (R2).  
  - The objectives for updating the value function and the imitation value function are not specified (R2).  
  - The notation for the expectation in Section 3.1 is ambiguous (e.g., in Eq. 1 the dependence of \(s\) and \(a\) is unclear); the role of \(b_P^\pi\) is not clarified when introduced (R4).  
  - It is unclear where \(\mathcal{D}_t\) and \(\mathcal{M}_s\) appear in Eq. 4 (R2).

- **Limited data and practical estimation**  
  - The method relies on estimating \(d_{P_t}^{\pi_\theta}\) from a few target domain trajectories; kernel density estimation is known to be data-inefficient, and a fixed bandwidth in high-dimensional state spaces may affect accuracy (R4).  
  - Having only a handful of real transitions limits the ability of a GAIL-style discriminator to learn well (R1).  
  - Using the state-action marginal of the current policy in the target environment as regularization can harm learning if the policy is suboptimal (R1).  
  - The paper does not study performance as a function of the amount of target data (R4).

- **Baseline selection and comparison**  
  - The CQL baseline is not clearly described: the text says it does not leverage the simulator, but it is unclear whether CQL is trained on simulated data at all or what regularization is used (R1, R2).  
  - Simple, natural baselines such as TD3+BC (training TD3 in simulation with BC regularization from real data) are missing (R1).  
  - Domain randomization is an important sim-to-real approach and is not included; the paper’s assumption that the simulator is a black box does not fully justify its omission (R3).  
  - DARC is re-implemented using the same RL algorithm as the proposed method, but results from the original DARC implementation would be informative (R3).

- **Experimental rigor and presentation**  
  - Only 4 random seeds are used, and dividing the standard deviation by two in plots is statistically meaningless and misleading; showing individual runs or min/max would be more informative; the limitations of such small-sample statistics should be acknowledged (R4).  
  - The discussion below Fig. 1 is confusing: it is unclear how a high α (inducing replication of real trajectories) could lead to behavior close to RL_sim; no clear pattern emerges and the hyperparameter’s effect appears highly environment-dependent (R4).  
  - The experimental protocol removes trajectories that perform poorly in the real environment, which seems contradictory because those transitions are essential to the problem the method aims to fix; the trust-region constraint effectively forces the simulation to visit the same states/actions as the real environment regardless of reward, which is counter-intuitive (R4).  
  - The claim about minimizing a “state(-action) visitation distribution” (Table 1 caption) is imprecise – one minimizes a metric of that distribution, not the distribution itself (R4).  
  - A chronological error: the Nachum 2019 paper is mentioned as coming after Touati 2020 (R4).  
  - No evaluation is performed on a real robot or physical setup, despite the paper’s “sim-to-real” title; such a test would greatly strengthen the claim of practicality, and there is little discussion of real-world data complexity (R3, R4).
