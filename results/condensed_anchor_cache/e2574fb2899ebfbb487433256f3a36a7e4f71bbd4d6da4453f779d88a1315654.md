## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper demonstrates that automatic LLM benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard-Auto, MT-Bench) are vulnerable to a “null model” that always outputs a constant, irrelevant response. By combining a structured template (exploiting formatting biases) with an adversarial prefix optimized via random search (universal adversarial attack style), the null model achieves SOTA win rates (86.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto, 9.55 on MT-Bench) without accessing benchmark instructions. The attack transfers across benchmarks and is partly robust to trivial defenses (perplexity filters, paraphrasing). The paper calls for anti-cheating mechanisms. Most reviewers (R1,R2,R3,R4,R8) find the work important and well-executed, while three reviewers (R5,R6,R7) rate it lower (6) citing practicality and novelty concerns.

### Strengths
1. **Clear demonstration of a critical vulnerability.** All reviewers agree the paper convincingly shows that even a null model with a constant, non‑sensical output can cheat LLM‑as‑a‑judge benchmarks, achieving top scores (86.5% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto, 9.55 on MT-Bench). R1, R2, R4, R8 highlight the surprising effectiveness and importance of this finding for the community.
2. **Strong threat model.** The attack succeeds under a realistic scenario: the null model outputs the same string for every input, without access to the (supposedly private) benchmark instructions. R2, R5 emphasize this makes the vulnerability stark.
3. **Transferability.** The crafted constant response works across three popular benchmarks (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard-Auto, MT-Bench) and different instruction samples. R1, R2, R3 note this generalization is well evaluated (validation/test split).
4. **Extensive ablation and anti-cheating analysis.** The paper dissects the contribution of the structured template vs. the optimized prefix, and tests defenses (perplexity filters, template paraphrasing), showing both are insufficient. R2, R5, R8 appreciate this completeness.
5. **Gap between LLM and human perception.** R1 notes that humans immediately recognize the null model responses as wrong, highlighting the brittleness of LLM judges.
6. **Clear writing and strong presentation.** R1, R2, R4, R7, R8 find the paper easy to follow, with good figures (e.g., Figure 2 showing the need for careful optimization). R8 additionally praises the paper for describing the threat model of cheating on LLM‑graded evaluations (potentially for the first time).
7. **Method non‑trivial.** R1 points out that a simple adversarial suffix (e.g., naive suffix) is not effective; the structured response plus random search is required, making the attack more than an obvious exploit.

### Weaknesses
1. **Practicality and real‑world relevance.** Three reviewers (R5, R6, R7) question whether this attack represents a genuine current threat. R5 argues any human inspecting outputs would immediately see they are wrong, and the fear of being called out likely deters cheating; the results are more academic proof‑of‑concept. R7 notes that the notion that LLM evaluation is imperfect is already widely known, making the paper’s message incremental despite the vulnerability. R6 and R7 also stress that the constant responses are obviously random (e.g., “too random” per R4) and lack naturalness; a more imperceptible example (even with lower win rate) would raise greater awareness. R4 similarly asks for an example with higher naturality.
2. **Insufficient comparison to prior attacks.** R2 and R6 criticize the lack of empirical comparison with existing attacks on LLM‑as‑a‑judge (e.g., Raina et al. 2024, Shi et al. 2024, Chen et al. 2024c) adapted to the null model setting. R6 also requests comparison with gradient‑based or reinforcement‑learning optimization methods instead of random search.
3. **Missing details on method and optimization.**
   - **Manual crafting of structured responses.** R6 and R7 note the paper does not describe the process used to develop the structured template (e.g., what rules, how many iterations). R3 also asks how much manual iteration was performed.
   - **Optimization objective.** R6 and R7 point out that the loss function / objective for the random search (y‑axis of Figure 2; Algorithm 1) is not clearly defined. R6 asks for the specific loss used and why gradient‑based methods were not considered.
   - **Ineffective adversarial suffix.** R1 mentions an “optimized adversarial suffix” in Section 3 but does not describe what suffixes were tested; R6 and R7 also request clarification on this baseline (e.g., L152‑153 “what was the ineffective suffix?”).
4. **Incomplete defense evaluation.**
   - **Stronger defenses untested.** R2 notes that baseline perplexity filters and paraphrasing are weak; many stronger prompt‑injection defenses exist (e.g., trained classifiers or LLM‑based filters from https://github.com/tldrsec/prompt-injection-defenses). The paper should test out‑of‑the‑box LLM filters.
   - **Paraphrasing claim needs qualification.** R2 argues the paper should state that only trivial paraphrasing was tested; more targeted template changes (different format, explicit “ignore injection” instructions) could thwart the attack.
   - **PPL filter effectiveness for open‑source annotators.** R6 asks whether perplexity filters would work better against Llama‑3‑8B/70B auto‑annotators (which may have different PPL thresholds).
5. **Limited scope to LLM‑graded evaluations.** R8 emphasizes the analysis only covers benchmarks that use an LLM auto‑annotator (e.g., AlpacaEval, MT‑Bench). Many modern evaluations (LiveBench, MATH, WikiQA) rely on ground‑truth scoring and are not covered; this limitation should be stated prominently in abstract/intro/conclusion.
6. **Overly broad language.** R8 notes the paper uses terms like “open‑source auto‑annotators” but only tests two Llama‑3 variants; the claim should be qualified. Similarly, R7 notes the paper lacks discussion on how to design structured cheating for new benchmarks.
7. **Connections to positional bias.** R2 and R8 question how the attack interacts with the known positional bias of GPT‑4 (preferring first response). The paper does not disentangle whether the structured response exploits this bias. R8 specifically asks whether the Index‑0 method is fixed across experiments (Figures 3, 4, Table 6) and speculates why GPT‑4 is susceptible while Llama‑3‑70B is not (R2 also asks for instruction‑following scores to back the claim).
8. **Missing experiments / ablated studies.**
   - **Only adversarial prefix without structured response.** R6 asks whether the prefix alone achieves competitive win rates, and whether template rephrasing could then defend.
   - **Combining with normal responses.** R6 requests more detail on how the cheating response was appended to real model outputs (Section 6.2).
   - **Transferability across judges.** R5 asks to what degree the optimized tokens transfer between different auto‑annotator models (e.g., different GPT‑4 versions, other LLMs).
   - **Random selection among paraphrases.** R7 asks whether using a random sample from multiple paraphrased versions during evaluation would still be susceptible.
   - **Informing the judge about cheating.** R7 asks whether instructing the LLM annotator to prioritize the first instruction or to be aware of possible cheating could mitigate the attack.
9. **Clarity issues.**
   - **Figure 1.** R7 notes the difference between blue and black text in Figure 1 is unclear; it should specify that blue is the optimized prefix.
   - **Section 3 introduction.** R8 observes that “optimized adversarial suffix” is introduced without prior explanation, leaving readers confused.
   - **Reference error.** R6 spots a likely incorrect reference to Table 5 (L312‑313) that should be Table 4.
10. **Novelty concerns.** R4 notes the random search method is not new and suggests the paper may be more suitable as a position paper. R5 and R7 echo this, stating the technical contribution (template + random search) is modest relative to the impact of the findings. (Minority: R1,R2,R3,R8 view the demonstration as sufficiently original and valuable despite the known techniques.)
11. **Missing comparator baselines.** R3 suggests additional baselines with varying degrees of structured response complexity. R2 requests evidence for whether benchmark gaming is already occurring in practice.

*Note: The above weaknesses preserve the contrast between the majority (who view the paper as strong) and the minority reviewers (R5, R6, R7) who rated it lower. The minority’s concerns about practicality, insufficient method detail, and overclaiming are explicitly included, while the majority’s contrary views are captured in the strengths (e.g., that the attack is non‑trivial and the threat model is strong).*
