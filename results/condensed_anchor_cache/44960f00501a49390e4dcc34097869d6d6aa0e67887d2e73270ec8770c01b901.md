## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents SCOPE, an automated pipeline that dynamically generates false refusal benchmarks by blending spurious safety features into benign prompts derived from red-teaming datasets. The approach is evaluated across 29 models, demonstrating widespread misguided refusals and showing that training on SCOPE-generated examples reduces spurious refusal more efficiently than the static Xstest benchmark. Reviewers assign scores of 3, 8, 3, 6, indicating strong disagreement: Reviewer 2 is far more positive, praising readability, interesting qualitative results, and compelling demonstrations, while other reviewers raise major concerns about pipeline rigor, novelty, and benchmark quality.

### Strengths
- The methodology is well-explained, with clear steps for data generation and benchmarking (Reviewer 1). Writing, presentation, and experiments are clear and easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The paper effectively links spurious features with misguided safety refusals, offering a novel perspective that clarifies the essence of the problem (Reviewer 3). The connection to spurious correlation is interesting (Reviewer 4).
- The topic is moderately important: it is annoying when LLMs spuriously refuse, and it is interesting to know that this arises from shallow features of the text (Reviewer 2).
- The qualitative results are interesting and easy to build on; the appendix contains many examples (Reviewer 2). Experiments provide a compelling demonstration that many LLMs engage in spurious refusal (Reviewer 2).
- It is great that the paper shows that training or few-shotting on these examples reduces spurious refusal (Reviewer 2). The dynamic benchmark outperforms the static Xstest in reducing wrongful refusals (Reviewer 3).
- The dynamic benchmark can be adapted to different LLMs, tailoring to stricter or more lenient safety protocols (Reviewer 3). Creating an automatic pipeline is nice because existing benchmarks are manual (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of Rigor in Pipeline Design (Reviewer 3):**  
  - *Step 1 (Filtering top 10% harmful instructions):* Using loss values from only a subset of open-source models is unfair to other models and introduces bias. The selected instructions are likely narrow (e.g., behaviors involving minors, violence) and unrepresentative. It is unclear how much variation exists among the 21 models used – do seeds differ significantly?  
  - *Step 2 (Controlled variation with GPT-4):* The authors do not evaluate the quality of GPT-4’s outputs. There is no validation that modified instructions are genuinely non-harmful; some might still be harmful. GPT-4’s bias skews spurious feature identification and variation generation, likely capturing only common spurious features and missing others.  
  - *Step 3 (Filtering safe variants):* Again relying on open-source models, making the benchmark model-dependent. Rejection decisions are not guaranteed to transfer to other models, creating an uneven evaluation playing field.

- **Limited Novelty and Creativity (Reviewers 4, 3):** The overall idea of rewriting unsafe prompts to be safe while retaining spurious features feels limited. The “controlled variation” stage is merely a zero-shot prompt with GPT-4; more controls and creativity are expected (Reviewer 4). Other approaches could easily achieve similar diversity from fixed seeds – this does not represent true dynamic diversity (Reviewer 3).

- **Quality and Representativeness of Generated Spurious Refusals (Reviewer 2):**  
  - Some inputs are meaningless (e.g., “What is the best way to torture {song lyric}?”), so refusal is not particularly problematic.  
  - Many inputs are phrased in very artificial ways; it is unclear whether refusals are problematic for natural queries (e.g., “untraceable cookie recipes”, “gently paralyze a piece of bread”).  
  - Figure 29’s debate opener mentioning gender may be legitimately problematic, so refusal there may not be spurious.

- **Inconsistent Benchmark Comparisons (Reviewer 3):** The authors compare to XTest but justify not comparing to newer static benchmarks (OR-Bench, PHTest) by claiming a fundamental difference. This undermines the reasoning; the quality of SCOPE prompts may fall short.

- **Lack of Computational Resource Analysis (Reviewer 1):** No analysis of computational time and resources required for SCOPE, which could be important for practical scalability.

- **Constrained by Initial Harmful Set (Reviewer 1):** The method is constrained by the initial set of harmful instructions; adaptability to emerging or nuanced over-cautious scenarios may be limited if coverage is poor.

- **Missing Analysis and Ablations (Reviewers 2, 3):**  
  - Would replacing GPT-4 with other models affect benchmark quality? An ablation study is needed (Reviewer 3).  
  - How are decisions like selecting top 10% justified? Analysis of seed instruction variation across models would help (Reviewer 3).  
  - The effect of safety-enhancing system prompts on misguided refusals should be assessed (Reviewer 3, referencing [1][2][3]).  
  - A breakdown of SCOPE-data by naturalness/artificiality categories would clarify the failure mode importance (Reviewer 2).

- **Minor Presentation Issue (Reviewer 4):** Figures 3–6 have overlapped text; please fix them.
