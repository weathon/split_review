## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper trains a family of AlexNet models with parametrically varied dropout proportion $p$ (applied only to fc6 and fc7) to study the trade-off between high-dimensional efficient codes and robustness. Increasing dropout yields a smoother, lower-dimensional representational space in fc6. Optimal robustness to simulated neuron lesioning (at inference) is observed at $p=0.7$, after which both accuracy and robustness decline. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) using 7T fMRI data from occipitotemporal cortex (OTC) in the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) shows that this same dropout level yields the highest neural predictivity. Denoised eigenspectrum estimates from human fMRI also match the eigenspectrum decay rate of the $p=0.7$ model. The authors interpret this as suggesting that the brain balances the efficiency of high-dimensional codes and the robustness of low-dimensional codes. All four reviewers assign a score of 6 (positive but with limitations). The work is considered an interesting empirical extension of Stringer et al. 2019 to human high-level visual cortex, using a novel noise‑covariance separation method. However, reviewers note the approach is simple and intuitive, empirical rather than theoretical, and has several open questions regarding layer selection, dataset mismatch, single architecture, and the need for additional analyses.

### Strengths

- High consistency among three key results: robustness to lesioning, neural predictivity (RSA), and eigenspectrum decay rate all peak at the same dropout level ($p=0.7$).
- Novel approach to separate signal and noise covariance in fMRI population responses to obtain denoised eigenspectrum estimates.
- Systematic analysis of dropout and neuron lesioning from a neurobiological perspective, illuminating the relationship between sparse (efficient) and distributed (robust) codes.
- Use of simulated lesions to evaluate robustness is an interesting innovation.
- Well-written and clearly organized presentation; the best alignment between human fMRI and the most robust model convincingly suggests robustness is a key criterion for brain representations.
- Utilizes a large-scale 7T fMRI dataset (NSD) to extend the analysis of coding principles from mouse V1 to human high-level visual cortex.
- Authors acknowledge the limitation of only considering one form of regularization (dropout).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of direct sparsity measure**: The paper does not directly demonstrate how dropout affects the sparseness of unit responses (e.g., number of positive responses or responses above threshold in relu6) even though this is central to the claimed link between dropout and coding sparsity.
- **Lesion level interpretation**: Robustness improvements from dropout are only apparent at very high levels of lesioning. The ecological relevance for the brain is unclear – do we expect such high levels of unexpected neuron inactivation in object recognition regions?
- **Dropout placement rationale**: Dropout was only applied to fc6 and fc7, whereas typical training applies dropout to all layers. The rationale for this restriction is not explained, and it is unknown how dropout at earlier layers would influence the coding scheme at the layer of investigation (fc6/relu6).
- **Layer‑specificity concerns**:  
  - The selection of relu6 for lesioning (and fc6 for analysis) is not justified; results may not hold if a different layer were chosen.  
  - No results are reported for fc7 (also fully connected and directly connected to fc6) in the analyses of Figs. 2 and 3, leaving the layer‑specific nature of the findings unclear.
- **Discrepancy in optimal dropout criteria**: Dropout $p=0.5$ gives the best Top‑5 ImageNet accuracy (Fig. 2A), while $p=0.7$ gives the best accuracy under unit lesioning (Fig. 2D). The paper does not discuss this trade‑off or clarify which metric is more relevant for “coding efficiency.”
- **Dataset mismatch**: Human fMRI responses were collected using the Natural Scenes Dataset, but network responses were evaluated on ImageNet. The motivation for not running networks on the NSD images (or using a single dataset) is not explained.
- **Dimensionality mismatch between brain and model**: The number of voxels used in fMRI and the number of fc6 units are likely very different. This large difference could influence the RSA results; the authors should quantify and discuss its potential impact.
- **Absence of cross‑area eigenspectrum analysis**: The ROI in NSD includes multiple low- and high-level visual areas. Stringer et al. 2019 only examined mouse V1. The paper does not analyze whether the eigenspectrum differs across human visual areas, which would be a novel contribution beyond replication.
- **Single architecture**: Results are reported only for AlexNet and may not generalize to other neural network architectures. The phrase “schemes within a family of deep neural network” should be revised to reflect that the family is defined by varying dropout within a fixed architecture, not by architectural variations.
- **Empirical, not theoretical**: The work is entirely empirical and lacks theoretical analysis (e.g., analytical derivation of the optimal dropout level or the trade‑off). The idea is intuitive and the approach simple – a reviewer notes this could be seen as a weakness despite the empirical value.
- **Potential numerical instability of GSM method**: The Gaussian‑scale mixture (GSM) subtraction of the empirical noise covariance matrix does not guarantee a positive‑definite signal covariance matrix (eigenvalues could become negative), which may limit generalizability of the method.
- **Clarity and presentation issues**: The central definition of “lesioning” (pruning neurons in a layer) appears only in a figure caption (Figure 2) instead of the main text. The main message of the paper is somewhat difficult to extract, and significance for the machine‑learning community is not discussed. The effect of dropout versus the underlying architecture on the representational trajectories (Fig. 1B) is not disentangled.
