## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies whether LLMs can adapt to corrected misinformation within a conversation, formalizing this as Knowledge Editing in Conversation (KEIC) and Memory Re-Encoding (MRE). It builds a dataset from CoQA by inserting conflicting statements and corrections, and proposes four prompting methods (One-Turn Correction, Verification, Recall, Deletion) under Correction After Mistake (CAM) and Correction Before Asking (CBA) settings. Experiments on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o, Gemma, LLaMA, and Vicuna show deletion is most effective, recall offers the best trade-off between effectiveness and token efficiency, and GPT-4o is surprisingly less reactive than GPT-3.5. Reviewers agree the topic is important, but raise major concerns about dataset scope, evaluation depth, clarity, and the conceptual framing of MRE. Reviewer 2 (score 6) is notably more positive, praising the formalization and dataset; the other four reviewers scored 3 or 5 and highlight significant weaknesses.

### Strengths

- Relevant and important topic: addressing LLM faithfulness over growing contexts and the challenge of integrating corrections in conversation (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- Interesting and unexpected findings, e.g., GPT-4o being "stubborn" and less reactive to updates than GPT-3.5 (R1, R2).
- Thorough cost evaluation and token-efficiency analysis (R1).
- Valuable dataset contribution: sizable, built on CoQA, can be extended beyond Yes/No to other question formats (R2, R3, R5). R2 calls it an important dataset for in-context knowledge editing.
- Extensive experiments with multiple plausible settings (CAM/CBA), four model-agnostic methods, and varied models (GPT, LLaMA, Vicuna, Gemma); results reveal crucial gaps in current LLMs (R2, R5).
- Structured approach to formalizing the KEIC process (R2, R4).
- Comprehensive problem analysis and high-quality dataset (R5).
- Methods require no parameter tuning (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **MRE framing and conceptualisation:** The connection to memory re-encoding (MRE) is tenuous; MRE in psychology involves updating memory, which is more analogous to parametric knowledge editing. The paper does not provide a comprehensive review or concrete connections between the task and MRE (R1). R3 argues the task is more like adversarial QA in a long-term context than true memory editing—corrections do not lead to a persistent update of the model’s knowledge base.
- **Benchmark limitations (multiple reviewers):**
  - Only Yes/No questions from CoQA, and conversations are brief; no consideration of deductive reasoning or inferences (e.g., if “Mary had blue and yellow paint” is corrected to “Mary had blue and purple paint,” the answer to “Can Mary make green paint?” changes) (R1). R4 adds that only yes/no questions narrow the evaluation; expanding question types would offer richer insights.
  - Stories are always positioned at the start of the dialogue, and corrections appear only adjacent to the story or right before the question, limiting conversational realism (R4).
  - The dataset only covers one kind of correction: fixing errors in previously reported events. It does not include temporal knowledge changes (e.g., change in Presidents), evolving user preferences/opinions, or other plausible scenarios (R2).
  - No distinction between real-world facts and narrative fiction; it is unclear if parametric memory (models possibly trained on CoQA) affects results (R2).
  - R1 recommends using datasets with longer conversations and topic switches, such as TopiOCQA.
- **Missing analyses and ablations:**
  - No analysis of how performance varies with distance between the update turn (T_u) and the test turn (T_i), or between the error turn (T_e) and T_u (R2, R4).
  - No per-template results showing which of the 15 correction templates consistently perform best (R2).
  - No evaluation of how models adapt when multiple facts are corrected in a single conversation (R1).
  - No experiments with latest open-source models like LLaMA 3.1 or OlMo, or attention weight analysis (R2).
  - Missing dialogue quality metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) beyond exact match for yes/no answers; the effect of the proposed methods on fluency and coherence is not assessed (R5). R1 also notes that evaluations do not test performance on questions unrelated to the modified information after correction.
  - The probing method uses only a single prompt; exploring prompts that explicitly ask the model to answer based on the most recent correction would better test the memory update process (R3).
  - R1’s question: For the deletion method, did the authors try rewriting inconsistent turns instead of deleting them, since a turn may contain relevant information despite some incorrect facts? Also, how was inconsistency determined by the LLM?
- **Clarity, notation, and presentation issues:**
  - Excessive and confusing notation, e.g., two notations for the same object (T_i vs. (u_i, b_i)), overbearing subscripts (T_f, T_u, T_i, T_p, T_c, T_v, T_r, T_d), and mixing of italic and normal text for relations (r vs. $r$) (R1, R5).
  - Writing in the introduction is confusing (e.g., “Recall” used before definition, possibly confused with the metric) and section headings are uninformative (e.g., Section 2.2 titled “Fact”) (R1). Sections 3–5 are also unclear (R4).
  - Figures are placed far from their first mention (R5).
  - Benchmark details are mostly in the appendix (R1).
  - Discrepancy between the motivating figures (human-human conversation) and the actual dataset (human-AI QA-style); using real data examples would align visuals with the task (R3).
  - Confusing jargon: “Update %” is unnecessary (should be “Accuracy”), using “Recall” as a method name conflicts with the evaluation metric, and “Previous Phase” refers to “Irrelevant turns” (R2).
  - Terms in Table 2 (“Update/No Update/Upper Bound”) and the concept of “top-K upper bound performance” are not clearly defined (R4). Figure 5’s baseline of 56.5% “update” is confusing (R4).
- **Practicality and efficiency concerns:**
  - Advanced methods (Verification, Recall, Deletion) cost substantially more tokens (Table 1), increasing latency and complexity, which may hinder real-time deployment (R5).
  - The deletion method also shows resource differences between CAM and CBA that are unexplained (R4).
- **Doubts about problem persistency:** R2 questions whether this is an independent problem or one that will be solved by better positional encodings, attention architectures, or RAG with external memory – suggesting that poor performance may simply reflect a general in-context attention issue.
- **Contrasting reviewer opinion:** One reviewer (R2, score 6) found the paper well-written and praised the formalization and dataset, while others (R1, R4, R5) criticize clarity, notation, and presentation. This minority positive view is noted but does not outweigh the majority concerns.
