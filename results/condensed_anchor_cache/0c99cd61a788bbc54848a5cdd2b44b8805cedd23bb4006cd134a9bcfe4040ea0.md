## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends TabPFN (an in-context learning transformer for tabular data) to a fine-tuning setting, and proposes generating more complex pre-training data via a forest-based generator (TabForest) to improve fine-Tuning performance. The authors show that fine-tuning yields better downstream performance and more complex decision boundaries. They propose TabForestPFN, combining original and forest-based generators, which achieves good fine-tuning results on some datasets (WhyTrees, TabZilla). However, reviewers question the novelty, the marginal gains over strong baselines, the clarity of the decision-boundary claims, and the sufficiency of baselines and analyses. One reviewer (score 3) is substantially more negative than the others (scores 5 and 5).

### Strengths
1. The paper identifies that fine-tuning is needed for best performance and proposes a simple but novel way to generate more complex pre-training data using decision trees, which leads to performance gains when fine-tuning.
2. The transformer-based model achieves state-of-the-art results on some datasets, and visualization of decision boundaries (Figure 1) helps explain the effectiveness of fine-tuning.
3. Extensive experiments are conducted across multiple benchmark suites (WhyTrees, TabZilla).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of novelty.** Extending TabPFN from ICL to fine-tuning is a straightforward extension since training (fine-tuning) a model typically outperforms zero-shot prompting. Similarly, obtaining more complex decision boundaries after fine-tuning is expected, not surprising. It is unclear whether the ICL-transformers themselves are essential for this effect, or if the same would hold for any model fine-tuned on downstream data. (One reviewer, score 3, is particularly critical of this point.)
2. **Marginal improvements and missing benefit.** In Table 3, TabPFN outperforms XGBoost by only about 0.03–0.04. Moreover, when both are fine-tuned, TabForestPFN shows no benefit over TabPFN on TabZilla, which requires explanation.
3. **Unconvincing decision-boundary complexity claim.** In Figure 1, the fine-tuned model’s decision boundary does not appear more complex than a random forest. The claim that fine-tuned models create “complex decision boundaries similar to tree-based methods” is not clearly supported.
4. **Insufficient explanation of performance improvement.** It is not convincing that the improvement comes solely from complicating the decision boundary. The paper should explain how one can be sure the performance gain stems from the decision boundary rather than other factors. Additionally, the argument that TabPFN achieves better zero-shot performance because its training data is more realistic is questionable; it might be possible to create realistic yet complex data.
5. **Limited comparisons and missing details.** Comparisons are limited to traditional models (SVM, MLP, Decision Tree, XGBoost) that are over a decade old. The paper should compare with more recent state-of-the-art methods and report model parameters and inference time. The claim of a 2× GPU memory reduction is not supported by any quantitative numbers.
6. **Lack of ablation of pre-training data usage.** A reviewer asks: Is there a result of training TabPFN including the data used when fine-tuning TabForestPFN? Could that data be used in pre-training to improve zero-shot performance? This missing ablation leaves the benefit of fine-tuning-only usage unclear.
7. **Superficial analysis and unclear presentation.** The analysis in Section 5.6 provides only intuitive observations (e.g., fine-tuning excels with more samples). Deeper insights are needed, particularly why larger support sizes (Figure 7b) show more benefit of fine-tuning over zero-shot. Algorithm 1 is hard to digest and would benefit from a step-by-step visualization. Figures and overall writing need improvement (one reviewer notes the paper’s figures are especially weak).
