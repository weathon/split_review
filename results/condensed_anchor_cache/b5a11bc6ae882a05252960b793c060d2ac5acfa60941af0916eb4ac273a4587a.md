## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends the recently proposed Q-Exponential Process (Q-EP) to a deep probabilistic model (deep Q-EP) by stacking multiple layers, analogous to deep Gaussian processes. A variational inference strategy with sparse inducing points is derived. Experiments on regression and classification benchmarks compare deep Q-EP against deep GP and other baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written, well-organized, and draws connections to the relevant literature (Li et al., 2023; Damianou & Lawrence, 2013) consistently.
- The idea of generalizing Gaussian processes to Q-exponential distributions is interesting and has the potential to be influential in machine learning.
- The variational inference method for Q-EP and the application of multi-layer deep GP to Q-EP appear to be novel contributions.
- The presented material is technically correct and understandable; the literature survey is comprehensive.
- The paper reports a comprehensive set of experiments.
- The model inherits flexible regularization through the controlled parameter q, which is advantageous for learning latent representations while modeling data inhomogeneity.
- The proposed inference uses a scalable variational approach based on sparse approximation via inducing points.

### Weaknesses
- The novelty is limited: stacking Q-EP into a deep model is a straightforward combination of deep GP and Q-EP; the calculations are incremental and no concrete scientific problem motivates the enterprise.
- Limited insight is given on when and why deep Q-EP outperforms deep GP. No guidance is provided for practitioners on selecting q; only q=1 is used in experiments without justification, and sensitivity to the choice of q is not explored.
- Experimental results are mixed and unconvincing. Performance improvements over deep GP are often small or ignorable (especially for classification). R2 scores are not reported for regression, making it hard to quantify absolute improvement. Standard deviations are missing for many results. For CIFAR-10, all methods perform around 70% accuracy, which is too low to be a practical use case.
- Figure 2 claims deep Q-EP gives the most accurate prediction, but the caption indicates deep GP performs marginally better; this contradiction is not explained. Figure 3’s take-home message is unclear: all models appear comparable at the center and fail at the corners, and no analysis explains why deep Q-EP’s decision boundary might be better.
- Section 2 presents heavy theory without sufficient explanations or intuitions.
- The claim in Line 214 that Bayesian training (Titsias, 2010) is robust to overfitting compared to optimization (Lawrance, 2003) is stated without justification.
- The inference method may involve O(N³) matrix inversion and scalability is not clearly demonstrated. Alternative approaches such as sequential Monte Carlo (Wang et al., 2016) are not considered.
- Experiments are mostly on toy data; there is no experiment on non-stationary noise (heteroscedasticity) where the method’s advantages could be demonstrated.
- The sparse approximation via inducing points is not clearly explained; the choice of inducing points is not elaborated.
- Mean-field variational inference may be suboptimal due to ignoring posterior correlations; a comparison with MCMC (as used in the original Q-EP paper) is not provided.
- In Figure 1, insight into why q-EP identifies different features than GP-LVM is missing.
- Equation (2) assumes noise has the same q as the prior; it is not discussed whether a different q for likelihood (e.g., Gaussian with q=2) would be more realistic or how it would change the posterior.
- It is unclear whether the approach is appropriate for realistic applications such as coupled tomography imaging (Li, 2003).
