## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether aligned LLMs can forget unsafe content (bias, toxicity, harmfulness) when first fine-tuned on a noisy dataset and then fine-tuned again on safe data (safety review). The authors find that unsafe examples are forgotten more than safe ones during this second stage, and that larger models exhibit stronger forgetting. Motivated by this, they propose ForgetFilter (FF), which filters out examples from the original fine-tuning set that are rapidly forgotten during safety review, under the assumption that those are the unsafe ones. FF is compared to safety replay and moral self-correction, and is reported to reduce toxicity scores by 75% relative to no safety measure and 62% relative to self-correction.

### Strengths
- The problem of ensuring safety in customized fine-tuning is important and timely (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The experimental setup is extensive: multiple model architectures (small to large), multiple types of unsafe content (bias, toxicity, harmfulness), and consistent findings across them (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The ForgetFilter algorithm is simple, well-motivated by the forgetting analysis, and shows strong empirical performance, especially in long-term safety evaluations (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The observation that larger models are better at forgetting unsafe content during safety review adds an interesting dimension (Reviewer 4).
- The collection of datasets (e.g., BBQ, toxicity, harmfulness) is well chosen and expressive (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Core claim of “unsafe content is more forgettable” is disputed.** Reviewer 3 argues that forgetting during safety review is simply domain shift: fine-tuning on safe examples from one domain reduces performance on any out-of-distribution point (safe or unsafe) from the original domain. They contend that if one did an “unsafe review” by fine-tuning on unsafe data, the opposite trend would hold, so the claim that unsafe points are fundamentally more forgettable is not corroborated. This directly challenges a main premise of the paper.
- **Limited and artificial setup.** Reviewers 1 and 4 note that the first fine-tuning stage with a small mixed dataset of safe and unsafe content is not realistic. In practice, unsafe content may exist in internet-scale pre-training data, and fine-tuning datasets are typically small and high-quality. Reviewer 1 also warns that the model may be forgetting out-of-distribution features rather than explicitly forgetting “unsafe” content. Reviewer 4 suggests testing whether FF can filter unsafe content from the pre-training dataset. Reviewer 2 questions the practicality when no safe dataset is available for the same task.
- **Missing explanation for the forgetting phenomenon.** Reviewer 4 asks for an empirical or theoretical explanation of *why* unsafe content is forgotten more, and under what conditions this holds. This is needed to understand when FF can be applied.
- **Inadequate baselines.** Reviewer 3 points out that the main competitors (Safety Replay and Moral Self-Correction) are not data filtering methods; the paper should compare FF against simple or existing safety-oriented data filtering techniques (e.g., dataset cartography, second-split forgetting, or other learning-dynamics-based filters). Without such comparisons, it is unclear whether FF is actually an improvement over prior filtering approaches.
- **Missing related work on learning dynamics and data selection.** Both Reviewers 1 and 2 note that the idea of using forgetting / learning dynamics to identify data points has been explored before. They specifically mention: Maini et al. (NeurIPS 2022) on “Characterizing Datapoints via Second-Split Forgetting” (also referenced by Reviewer 1), and Swayamdipta et al. (EMNLP 2020) on “Dataset Cartography”. Reviewer 3 also notes the connection to memorization literature (e.g., “Does Learning Require Memorization? A Short Tale about a Long Tail”) is missing.
- **No jailbreaking evaluation.** Reviewer 1 asks for tests of jailbreaking attacks on models fine-tuned with the filtered dataset; if unsafe content is removed, the model should be less vulnerable. The authors did not provide this evidence after rebuttal, and Reviewer 1 maintains their score.
- **Unexplained downstream performance improvement.** Reviewer 2 asks why FF improves downstream performance in Table 2, given that it may also falsely remove safe examples. They also ask what proportion of forgotten examples are actually harmful (Figure 2 shows up to 80% harmful forgotten, but not the reverse).
- **Writing and presentation issues.**
  - Reviewer 3: The list of contributions on page 2 is too sparse and lacks detail.
  - Section 3.1 is difficult to follow; chronological reordering is recommended.
  - Spelling and grammatical errors (e.g., “promot”, missing comma after “default”).
  - The reporting metric is the empirical average, not the “expected value”; terminology should be corrected.
- **Minor questions and suggestions.**
  - Reviewer 3: Why are ground truth responses in the BBQ dataset modified to a stereotypical choice?
  - Reviewer 3: Why were only learning rate and batch size changed from default hyperparameters? Does the phenomenon depend on hyperparameter selection?
  - Reviewer 4: A diagram illustrating the two-stage fine-tuning procedure would improve clarity.
