## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies value inflation in KNN-Shapley data valuation, where positive Shapley values can misidentify harmful samples as beneficial. It proposes Calibrated KNN-Shapley (CKNN-Shapley), which constrains training subsets to sizes above a threshold (T = N - 2K, effectively using only the 2K nearest neighbors) to recalibrate the zero threshold. Experiments across multiple datasets and settings (mislabeled data, online learning, active learning) show reduced inflation and improved accuracy. The solution is simple, heuristic, and computationally faster than the original KNN-Shapley.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, self-contained, and easy to follow; helpful plots and references enable quick understanding.
- The proposed CKNN-Shapley provides a simple, incremental, yet impactful solution to the inflation problem; it also reduces computational cost by directly assigning zero to certain subsets.
- Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks (image and text data) demonstrate broad applicability and improvements over KNN-Shapley and its variants.
- The approach is well-motivated by a practical problem (data valuation) and addresses a significant limitation of KNN-Shapley in real-world applications.
- The evidence for the inflation phenomenon is convincing and the experimental improvements are large enough to be likely valid despite methodological concerns (Reviewer 4).
- The paper explores applications in mislabeled data, online learning, and active learning, showing versatility.
- The calibration method and subset size constraints provide a theoretical foundation that enhances interpretability of data valuations (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivational exaggeration**: The abstract and introduction claim inflation exists for all Shapley value methods, but experiments only demonstrate it for KNN-Shapley. The sentence on line 012 is too strong (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Heuristic nature and lack of optimality**: The CKNN variation is heuristic with little evidence of optimality. The motivation for introducing hyperparameter T and the choice T = N - 2K should be explained more clearly and persuasively (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Vague link between motivation and method**: It is unclear whether using T completely solves misclassification; there may still be misidentified samples even with T. The paper lacks theoretical analysis of how to systematically choose T—it is only noted that “the choice of T is contingent upon the dataset characteristics” (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing definition of “semi-value”**: The term appears multiple times but is never defined (Reviewers 3, 4, line 197).
- **Statistical significance absent**: Many experiments lack p-values or statistical tests (e.g., McNemar). In Table 2, accuracies are multiples of 0.01 or 0.005, suggesting test sets of only 100–200 points—differences are likely not statistically significant (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Use of independent test sets unclear**: It is not specified which experiments used a separate i.i.d. test set. Measuring accuracy on the training set (after removing samples) is not meaningful; improvements on the training set are expected (Reviewer 4). For Figure 1 and Table 7, it should be clarified that a fixed independent test set is used.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Accuracy achieved with CKNN is highly sensitive to the choice of K and T (e.g., Figures 3B, 3C show narrow optimal ranges with small test sets). This reduces practical usefulness. Figure 3B should include results for K < 5, and the numerical values for Wind in Table 2 (0.8950, 0.9100) differ from Figure 3B—why? (Reviewer 4)
- **Small test sets and limited datasets**: Test sets are small, leading to high variance. The number and variety of datasets is limited; Table 7 shows no clearly better test accuracy across the board, indicating no free lunch (Reviewer 4).
- **Lack of comprehensive comparisons**: CKNN-Shapley is not benchmarked against other semi-value or game-theoretic methods (e.g., Banzhaf values) or non-Shapley data valuation methods. Such comparisons are needed to understand its relative effectiveness (Reviewer 2). Additionally, no comparison with existing KNN-Shapley variants like Threshold KNN-Shapley (Wang et al. 2023) is provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Scalability concerns**: While more efficient than the original KNN-Shapley, CKNN still incurs notable computational costs for large datasets or high-dimensional data. The paper lacks a detailed complexity analysis (scaling with n and k) and empirical scaling behavior. Feasibility for real-time data valuation in dynamic environments is not addressed (Reviewer 2).
- **Real-world deployment lacking**: Experiments are limited to simulated scenarios (mislabeled data, online learning, active learning). No real-world case study (e.g., medical, financial data) is provided to validate robustness. Sensitivity to hyperparameters in dynamic environments (batch size, noise level, data drift) is under-explored (Reviewer 2).
- **Reliance on KNN as surrogate**: CKNN-Shapley inherits the limitations of KNN, which may be ineffective for complex decision boundaries. No sensitivity analysis for the choice of k or distance metrics; no discussion on generalizing the calibrated approach to other surrogate models (Reviewer 2).
- **Alternative interpretation of inflation**: The green region in Figure 1 covers about half the dataset; it is surprising that so many points are harmful. Is this relative only to the training set? Many fewer points may be harmful on an independent test set (Reviewer 4).
- **First term in equation not impactful**: The first term in the KNN-Shapley formula has denominator N >> K, so it is small and can be ignored. The proposed remedy focuses on this term, but an alternative intuition is that the most distant neighbors are irrelevant; the 2K nearest points are more heavily weighted (Reviewer 4).
- **Why is empirically best threshold closer to zero important?**: Instead of calibrating the threshold, one could simply use a validation set to determine a non-zero threshold for standard KNN-Shapley (Reviewer 4). The paper does not argue why zero is the better anchor.
- **Missing guidelines for choosing T and hyperparameters**: Practitioners need heuristics or theoretical guidance for selecting T across different datasets. The paper does not provide clear recommendations for setting T, K, batch size in streaming data, or sample removal thresholds (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Figure 1 issues**: Should use the same binning for red and blue plots. The red line shows accuracy with a single bin removed; it would be more intuitive to remove that bin and all bins to its left. The line is measured on the training set, not on a separate test set—this is problematic (Reviewer 4).
- **Figure 3C**: T varies in the narrow range 1950–1990; this should be explained and reconciled with the recommendation T = N - 2K on line 377 (Reviewer 4).
- **Fixed % removal vs. zero threshold**: In experiments (Section 4.3), a fixed % of points is removed, but the advantage of CKNN is that the zero threshold tells approximately how many points to remove. This inconsistency needs clarification (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing experiments**: Since KNN is not state-of-the-art, the paper should include experiments where CKNN is used to clean data, then a high-performing algorithm (e.g., GBM, XGBoost, SVM) is trained. Would the improvement transfer? (Reviewer 1)
- **Citing errors**: The reference to (Jia et al., 2019b) is incorrect—KNN-Shapley does not appear there. Scalability vs. utility (CVPR, 2021) is also inappropriate; the method is due to VLDB, 2019. Bibliography formatting must be fixed so labels like “(Jia et al., 2019b)” are visible in the PDF (Reviewer 4).
- **Typographical errors**: Line 651 has a typo; line 728 has an error in table title. Table 7 is important and should be placed in the main paper (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor presentation issues**: Indentation is inconsistent (e.g., line 154), and the long caption of Figure 1 merges with the main text (Reviewer 1).
