## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Value Explicit Pretraining (VEP), a method for learning transferable representations for reinforcement learning. VEP uses a self-supervised contrastive loss based on Bellman return estimates to relate states across different tasks, enabling transfer to tasks sharing similar objectives despite changes in appearance and dynamics. The method is pretrained from offline play data with sparse rewards, no actions or dense rewards, using a suboptimal agent. Experiments on Atari games and egocentric visual navigation tasks claim up to 2× improvement in rewards and 3× improvement in sample efficiency. One reviewer (rating 5) found the work well-written and the value-based contrastive idea innovative, especially for navigation. The other three reviewers (ratings 3, 3, 3) acknowledged the challenging problem setup but raised concerns about novelty, significance of improvements, and experimental scope.

### Strengths
- The problem setup is interesting and challenging: learning generalizable representations from offline data with sparse rewards, no action labels/dense rewards, collected by a suboptimal agent.
- The core idea of using contrastive learning based on Bellman return estimates is novel and differs from prior methods relying on image reconstruction or temporal consistency.
- VEP introduces a practical annotation trick: labeling the final step of play data as reward 1 to denote progress, saving annotation cost and eliminating the need for actions/rewards in the offline dataset.
- The method learns representations from actions and sparse rewards without requiring additional fine-tuning for downstream tasks, which has promising potential for efficient representation learning.
- Experiments on visual navigation tasks are especially noteworthy, showing significant generalization to previously unseen tasks.
- The paper is well-structured and easy to follow, with clear explanation of methodology.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (multiple reviewers):** Pretraining encoders with self-supervised learning (SSL) for RL is now common. The paper does not sufficiently differentiate VEP from existing SSL pretraining methods (e.g., latent inverse dynamics [1], Genie [2], video prediction as rewards [3], State-to-Go Transformer [4]; also Zhang et al. ICLR 2021 [5] uses contrastive learning to bring behaviorally similar trajectories together). A thorough comparison and ablation are needed to substantiate the claim that state contrastive learning from returns across similar tasks is a key contribution.
- **Limited performance gains in practice:** In Atari games, improvements over baselines (TCN, VIP, etc.) are not large in most cases. In Demon Attack and Space Invaders, VEP performs comparably or falls short of TCN; in Air Raid, pretrained representations even degrade performance relative to random representations. The paper claims “up to 2×/3× improvement”, but this language overstates the actual gap seen in most experiments.
- **Task progress suitability for transfer (Reviewer 2):** Using Bellman return estimates (task progress) to define positive pairs may be problematic when tasks differ in sub-task ordering. For example, two tasks requiring the same four sub-tasks (S1–S4) but in different orders would force semantically distinct states (S1 and S2, S2 and S3, S3 and S1) to become closer, ignoring semantics. This suggests VEP might work for the same task with different appearances but not for truly out-of-distribution tasks with different structure.
- **Experiment scope limitations:**
  - Only short-horizon tasks with discrete action spaces (Atari, navigation) are tested. No evidence for long-horizon tasks or continuous action spaces (e.g., DeepMind Control, Meta-World, Minecraft).
  - The navigation tasks only involve simple behaviors (“Fire” in Atari, “Move & Turn”); more complex behaviors like obstacle-avoidance or path-finding are not addressed.
  - Experiments appear to focus more on in-distribution tasks than truly out-of-distribution ones.
- **Data and pretraining details:**
  - The method heavily relies on high-quality expert data closely related to the target tasks; applicability when source and target share only visual similarities but have significantly different reward definitions is unclear.
  - It is unclear how offline data is used: are failure trajectories (collected by the suboptimal agent) excluded from pretraining or incorporated? Computing Bellman returns seems to require successful episodes.
  - The role of odometry information in navigation is ambiguous; in real-world scenarios, odometry can be noisy. Ablations analysis on whether VEP works purely from egocentric RGB would strengthen the paper.
  - Fixed training batch size \(b_G \times b_T\) details are not justified; fair comparison across baselines requires clarification.
- **Writing and presentation issues:**
  - Figures 3–5 are poorly positioned; some figures (e.g., Fig. 1) are not referenced in text; Fig. 6 missing the described brown line.
  - Citation style should use \citep for parenthetical citations.
  - The paragraph titled 'Baselines for VEP' in Related Work appears incomplete.
  - A discussion of failure cases (e.g., underperformance in Air Raid) is needed for better insight.
- **Missing comparisons:**
  - Comparisons with large vision-language models pretrained on semantic tasks (e.g., finetuned on limited play data) are absent; these models could provide strong visual representations.
  - Wall-time comparison in Fig. 5 is misleading: total pretraining wall time is not counted, and end-to-end learning may catch up if training is extended.
- **One reviewer (rating 5) was more positive** about the value of the navigation experiments and the innovative contrastive objective, but the majority (rating 3) found the contributions limited and the evidence for strong transfer insufficient.
