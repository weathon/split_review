## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a steepest-descent algorithm on the duality gap for computing approximate Nash equilibria in bilinear zero-sum games. The algorithm solves a smaller LP per iteration to find a descent direction and achieves linear convergence with iteration complexity \(O(\frac{1}{\rho}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\). Experiments on random matrices and block games (up to 1000 strategies) show running time comparable to standard LP solvers in some classes. While some reviewers found the algorithm interesting and the linear convergence notable, most reviewers agreed that the contributions, analysis, and experimental evaluation are insufficient for the venue.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and clearly organized, with clear mathematical exposition.
- The idea of performing gradient descent directly on the duality gap is interesting.
- The algorithm achieves linear convergence in the exploitability/duality gap.
- The method appears to generate sparse support in practice.
- Experiments demonstrate the efficacy of the theoretical results and show running time comparable to LP solvers on some game classes.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty and unclear contribution**: Two-player zero-sum games can be solved efficiently via a single LP; the paper does not convincingly justify why an iterative method solving a series of smaller LPs is beneficial. The motivation is weak, especially given that existing no-regret algorithms have broader applicability (e.g., convergence to CE/CCE in multiplayer games) and are the standard challenge for last-iterate convergence.
- **Lack of precise computational complexity analysis**: The iteration complexity \(O(\frac{1}{\rho}\log\frac{1}{\delta})\) is not informative because the per-iteration cost depends on \(\rho\) (which controls LP size). A time-complexity analysis that accounts for the size of the LP as a function of \(\rho\) is missing, making it impossible to assess whether the approach can outperform solving the full LP once. How to choose \(\rho\) in practice remains unclear.
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation and missing comparisons**:
  - No comparison to gradient-based first-order methods that are state-of-the-art for large-scale zero-sum games, such as Extragradient, Optimistic Gradient Descent/Ascent (OGDA, OMWU), Regret Matching+, or Primal-Dual Hybrid Gradient methods. These methods achieve instance-dependent linear convergence with per-iteration cost of a gradient step.
  - No systematic plots showing convergence vs. the approximation parameter \(\delta\) for a fixed \(\rho\), or vice versa. The x-axis in Figures 2 and 3 is not clearly defined.
  - The game classes used in Table 1 (e.g., block games) are not described in sufficient detail, and only positive results are shown; classes where the method performs poorly are omitted.
  - The experiments are limited to strategies up to 1000; the claim of scaling to “thousands” is misleading without larger-scale tests and comparisons.
- **Known results presented as theorems**: Theorems 1–4 are standard, well-known results presented without proper attribution, detracting from the perceived novelty of the work.
- **Incomplete literature review and missing connections**:
  - The linear convergence of Extragradient/Optimistic Gradient for bilinear saddle-point problems over polyhedral domains based on error bounds (Tseng 1995, Wei et al.) is not discussed.
  - Related work on descent methods using the Nikaido-Isoda function is not mentioned.
  - The paper states that the best rate for the duality gap is \(O(\sqrt{1/T})\) but does not cite accelerated methods achieving \(O(1/T)\).
- **Theoretical analysis lacks depth**: The algorithm re-solves an LP at every step, and the analysis does not address why this is necessary or how it compares to simpler gradient-based updates. The paper does not engage with the challenge of obtaining last-iterate convergence for no-regret algorithms, which is the focus of recent literature.

*Note: While Reviewer 3 found the algorithm interesting and the linear convergence notable, the majority of reviewers judged the contributions, analysis, and experimental evaluation insufficient for the venue.*
