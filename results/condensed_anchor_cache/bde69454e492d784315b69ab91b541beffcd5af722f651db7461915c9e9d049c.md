## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents the Graph-based Supply Prediction (GSP) model, an attention-based GNN for predicting supplies, inventory, and imbalances in supply chain networks. Inputs include graph-structured historical data, demand forecasts, and original supply plans. The model uses a probabilistic formulation and iterative inference to satisfy constraints. It is evaluated on a real-world dataset from a global consumer goods company, comparing against Croston’s method and planned shipments.

### Strengths
- Good detailing of the problem statement and a literature review that clearly distinguishes the contribution from traditional prediction scenarios (Reviewer 1).
- The thought process behind the GNN architecture, encoding domain knowledge for performance and reliability, including constraint satisfaction via iterative inference (Reviewer 2).
- Originality in applying AI to an uncommon domain, design of a new loss function and iterative procedure for ensuring feasibility (Reviewer 3).
- Clear motivation and effective use of figures and tables that help understand the background and methodology (Reviewer 3). (Note: This clarity is contested by other reviewers who find the presentation lacking.)
- The method demonstrates efficacy on a real historical dataset from a global consumer goods company (Reviewer 3) and appears to solve the problem for the industry sponsor (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
**Problem formulation and clarity:**
- The paper lacks a clear definition of key concepts: what constitutes a “planned shipment” vs actualized shipments, what are the inputs to the prediction model (historical data, planned schedules, or combination), and which parameters are known a priori vs decision variables (Reviewer 3). Without this, the feasibility and practicality are hard to assess.
- The notation is overwhelming and unreadable (e.g., \(\hat{q}^{day, t, Iter=n}_{u, v, est at w, recv}\) with seven sub/superscripts), and too many parameters are introduced without sufficient explanation (Reviewer 3).
- Terminology: “lane” is used instead of the standard graph term “edge/arc” in both the supply chain and graph learning literature, causing confusion (Reviewers 3 and 4).
- Overuse of footnotes; important material should be integrated into the main text or supplemental (Reviewer 3).
- Minor typo: page 4 footnote 3 “dailiy” should be “daily” (Reviewer 1).
- Section 3.1 on GAT needs more detail to improve readability (Reviewer 1).
- No information is provided on whether stabilization (e.g., multi-head attention) or transformation/concatenation is applied to output features (Reviewer 1).

**Ablations and baselines:**
- The set of baselines is extremely limited—only Croston’s method and planned shipments are compared. Additional learning-based prediction models from the literature or sensible alternatives are needed (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- No ablations are provided to validate specific design choices, such as discrete time-deltas vs continuous representation, delta vs non-delta encoding, and quantity multiplier vs absolute number (Reviewer 2).
- Numerical results (Tables 1 and 2) only evaluate the loss weight hyperparameter \(\lambda\) from {0, 0.5, 1}; a more comprehensive hyperparameter search and comparison with other GNN architectures and traditional time-series models are required (Reviewer 4).
- The rationale for incorporating demand prediction inputs into inventory prediction is not clearly stated, and its impact is not analyzed (Reviewer 4).

**Novelty and contributions:**
- The paper does not clearly state its contributions relative to existing Graph Attention Networks; the method appears to apply a standard GAT without significant modifications (Reviewer 4).
- There are no innovations in the neural network architecture beyond the specific application; the design feels ad-hoc and lacks theoretical justification for its soundness (Reviewer 4).
- The methodology is extremely tailored to this specific supply chain problem; its generalizability to other applications is unclear (Reviewers 2 and 4).

**Practical relevance and robustness:**
- Real-world supply chains are largely decentralized, making centralized data collection and prediction potentially infeasible and unnecessary. Local decision makers often have more controlled relationships and can predict locally more accurately (Reviewer 3).
- The method’s robustness to disruptions (e.g., SKU shutdown, sudden labor shortage like during a pandemic) is not addressed; it is unclear whether the method can quickly capture such dynamics (Reviewer 3).
- The loss function is a bi-objective weighted combination of daily and weekly cumulative errors, but these objectives are on different scales, making the trade-off hard to interpret. A sensitivity analysis and justification of the weight hyperparameter are needed (Reviewer 3).

**Contribution to the ICLR community:**
- The paper appears to be a highly specific industry application with limited generic insights, more suitable for a domain-specific venue than ICLR (Reviewers 2 and 4).
- Sections 3.2 and 3.3 read like a project report to the industry partner, focusing on data-processing steps rather than novel machine learning contributions (Reviewer 4).
- Lead time predictions, a key problem in supply chains, are not addressed (Reviewer 1).
