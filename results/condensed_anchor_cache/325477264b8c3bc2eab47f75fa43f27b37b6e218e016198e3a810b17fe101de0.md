## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Mixture-of-Masks (MoM), a method that converts a pre-trained dense language model into a sparsely-activated model by learning binary masks for each expert using L0 regularization. Starting from all weights active, the model is progressively sparsified to minimize activated parameters while retaining performance. Experiments on 300M and 8B models show up to 97% performance retention with 50% of feed-forward parameters activated. The reviews are mixed: two reviewers (scores 5) praise the novelty and results, two (scores 3) find the method interesting but have significant concerns, and one (score 1) strongly criticizes the evaluation, missing baselines, and practical relevance.

### Strengths
- The topic of efficient training and inference for LLMs is impactful.
- The idea of using learned, overlapping masks as a flexible MoE alternative (Mixture-of-Masks) is novel and interesting, especially in the context of MoEfication.
- The learning-based approach with L0 regularization does not depend on prior knowledge of masks, potentially generalizing better than fixed-splitting methods.
- The analysis of “shared,” “independent,” and “redundant” experts is insightful and provides a valuable framework for understanding expert roles.
- The method description is clear and straightforward, with thorough detail in the analysis (Sections 2.4 and 3.4).
- Experiments are executed at respectable model sizes (300M and 8B) and training durations.
- Results on the 300M scale beat the MoEfication baseline across five commonsense and reading comprehension benchmarks.
- The effort to evaluate on larger models is commendable (even though limited to loss).

### Weaknesses
**Evaluation and Baselines**
- The only baseline compared is MoEfication. Multiple recent baselines (e.g., Sparse Upcycling, MoEBert, Deja Vu, Llama-MoE, ReLU Strikes Back for activation sparsity) should be included. One reviewer (R4) strongly argues the evaluation is extremely weak and misleading.
- The use of “activation ratio” (or activated parameters) as the primary metric is inappropriate because it ignores costs like routing, attention, and non-uniform expert sizes. FLOPs of the full model and wall-clock time are more meaningful. R4 considers the comparison to pruning unfair and misleading.
- No wall-clock time measurements are provided for training or inference (batched or unbatched). This is critical for a method claiming faster inference; R3 and R4 note that non-homogeneous experts could make the method slower than the dense baseline.
- Downstream evaluations are only shown for the 300M model; for the 8B model, only training loss/perplexity is reported. Whether performance on downstream tasks collapses for larger models remains unknown (R1, R2, R4).
- The model used in Table 2 is not specified (R2). Also, the evaluation metric for Table 2 needs clarification (R5).
- Comparisons to pruning baselines (Figure 4) show pruning achieving better performance, though with a different activated-parameter ratio. R3 suggests a Pareto-frontier analysis (varying L0/L1 weight) to properly compare.
- The authors claim “activation pruning” is a new concept, but similar terms (activation sparsity, dynamic pruning) have existed since at least 2019; related work on activation sparsity is missing (R4).
- No code is provided, limiting reproducibility (R4).

**Methodological Details and Clarity**
- The formulation of the FFN (Equations 2–3) deviates from standard Transformer FFN (e.g. Llama’s SwiGLU). It omits the down-projection (w3) and does not make clear whether W^g and W^u correspond to gate and up projections. R1 and R4 ask: why is the down-projection not sparsified? Is it part of the MoM layer?
- The router mechanism is not described: architecture (depth, width), whether Top-k is used and how k is set, how experts’ outputs are combined, and training details (end-to-end or separate). R4 also asks: is each MoM layer considered an FFN replacement?
- The training scheme for learnable masks is unclear. Reviewer 2 asks: does the method require training a mask for each token in the vocabulary? If not, how are masks determined for different tokens? Since token embeddings change after aggregation in middle layers, how does a token-wise mask adapt? Does training need to be performed separately for each sparsity ratio?
- The total loss is a direct sum of language model loss and mask loss; R5 asks whether weighted combinations ($L_{lm} + \lambda L_{mask}$) were tried.
- In Figures 2–4, it is unclear which loss (LM, mask, or combined) is plotted. R5 also asks for explanation of loss trends (initial spikes, plateaus).
- Several figures lack clarity: y-axis label in Figure 2c appears incorrect; no reference to Figure 1 in text; “Mem” column in Table 1 is vague (R4). R5 requests a diagram to illustrate implementation variants.
- Reviewers ask how experts with the same index across different layers are related (R3, R4). Are they randomly initialized independently?
- Mask values are in [0,1] during training; are they binarized at inference? (R4)

**Practical Applicability and Hardware Effectiveness**
- The method only considers activated parameters in the Feed-Forward layer, not the whole model (attention remains at full cost). This limits potential speedup to at most 3×, and likely much less (R3).
- Because experts have non-uniform sizes (e.g., FH, FW, FHW variants resemble unstructured pruning), parallel execution on GPUs is difficult. Without an efficient GPU implementation for batched inputs, the method may have little practical impact (R3, R4). Inference cost comparisons are missing (R1).
- MoE typically enables balanced router load for expert parallelism; it is unclear whether MoM supports such parallelism (R1).

**Novelty**
- The L0 regularization approach is directly adopted from Louizos et al. (2017), which limits novelty. R4 views the contribution as limited, though other reviewers note the method is novel within the MoEfication context.

**Writing and Presentation**
- The paper contains many grammar and spelling errors (e.g., “seperatedly”, “scaline law”, “which dimensions to token-specific”, confusing phrasing). These issues make the text difficult to read (R2, R4).
- Several expressions are unclear or incomplete, particularly in the methodology and experimental sections (R2, R4).
