## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a denoising affine transform that stabilizes neural network training under quantization and sparsification by modeling these operations as perturbations and applying ridge regression. The method is compatible with standard architectures and achieves competitive results at ultra-low precision (e.g., A4W4, A1W1) on ResNet-50 and Transformer models. Reviewers diverge on the novelty and clarity: R1 and R3 consider the approach novel and theoretically grounded, while R2 and R4 find the contributions overstated, the math ambiguous, and the experimental validation incomplete.

### Strengths
- Unified framework that simultaneously handles quantization and sparsification (R1, R2).
- Novel denoising affine transform for training stability, with theoretical grounding via ridge regression, parameter sensitivity analysis, and a shortcut for quantized matrix multiplication and sub-channel quantization (R1, R2). R4 notes simplicity and implementation-friendliness.
- Compatibility with standard architectures and off-the-shelf training recipes, making the approach practical (R1).
- Competitive performance on ResNet-50 and Transformer at low precision; results sometimes exceed full-precision baselines (e.g., A4W4 vs. A32W32 in Tables 1/2/3) (R1, R3). R3 highlights state-of-the-art results at very low precisions.
- Empirically works at extreme quantization levels such as A1W1 (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient validation of sparsification:** Only BLEU scores in Table 6 are provided; unique sparsity challenges (e.g., dead weights, gradient instability) are not addressed; extreme sparsity (>90%) is not tested (R1). The method’s adaptation to sparsification lacks technical detail, especially for structured sparsity (R2).
- **Limited architecture and quantization diversity:** Experiments only on ResNet-50 and Transformer; missing tests on MobileNet, EfficientNet, RNNs, and vision transformers (R1, R2, R4). Only group-wise quantization is used; no channel-wise results (R2, R4). It is unclear whether the experiments use quantization-aware training (QAT) or post-training quantization (PTQ) (R2).
- **Missing runtime and memory analysis:** Actual running time and memory consumption are not reported, despite their importance for low-precision networks (R3). The added denoising operations and ridge regression may introduce computational overhead (R3). Continuous variables *a* and *b* appear to prevent training on low-precision hardware alone (R4).
- **Overstated contributions:** The fundamental challenge is the zero-gradient issue from discrete operations, but the proposed affine transform does not directly address this (it is a dequantization scheme orthogonal to gradient approximation like STE) (R2). The “piecewise constant backbone model” and “performance lower bound” are not mathematically defined or explained in the main text (R2).
- **Ambiguous or inaccurate mathematical expressions:** ε in Eq. (1) is undefined (R3). Line 162: δ_i instead of δ may be a typo (R3). Eqs. (3–8): dimensionality of variables is not clarified (e.g., **x** bold vs. *b* regular, **x** – *b* subtracting scalar from vector, Cov_{xq} undefined) (R4). Line 161: δ = round(f(x)) – f(x) makes the addition of δ in Eq. (2) mathematically meaningless (R4).
- **Lack of comparative experimental validation:** Comparisons for A4W4 (Tables 2, 4) show negligible accuracy differences without standard deviations; ultra-low precision (A1W1) comparison is limited to a single cell in Table 5 (R4). No analysis of stability against hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate) or training dynamics (R4). Table 8 shows NaN when λ=0 with no explanation (R3). The result that A4W4 outperforms A32W32 is surprising and requires explanation (R3).
- **Missing fine-tuning experiments:** It is unclear how the method handles small weight shifts in fine-tuning scenarios, where errors may accumulate (R1). Layer-specific sparsity/quantization requirements are not discussed (R1).
