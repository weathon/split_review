## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes invariant spatiotemporal representation learning for cross-patient seizure classification using heterogeneous risk minimization to partition EEG data into environments. A masked Diffusion Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (DCRNN) backbone with self-supervised, supervised, and invariant risk minimization (IRM) losses is evaluated on the Temple University Hospital Seizure Corpus (TUSZ). Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 4) is positive and finds the method effective, while Reviewer 2 (score 1, confidence 5) considers it lacking novelty and mathematical rigor. Reviewers 3 and 4 (both score 3, confidence 4 and 5) acknowledge the good motivation but raise concerns about limited technical contribution, insufficient baselines, questionable IRM effectiveness, and lack of ablation studies.

### Strengths

- Tackling distribution shift and improving model generalization is important for clinical settings (R3, R4).
- The masking function for capturing invariant spatiotemporal information and using it for self-supervised learning is a positive design choice (R1).
- Using the variance of gradients as a penalty term for invariant learning is noted (R1).
- Learning cross-domain invariant feature representations is reasonable, and comparison results against Corr-DCRNN show effectiveness (R3).
- Pictorial representation and coordination of sections are well done (R2). Results showcasing is adequate (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited technical novelty:** The method is very similar to Corr-DCRNN (Tang et al., ICLR 2022) and applying a masking strategy to DCRNN is an established approach. Overall technical contribution is limited (R2, R3). The method lacks a specialized design for seizure detection vs. other EEG-based disease tasks, making it appear generic (R4).
- **Insufficient ablation and validation:** No ablation study is provided for the three loss components (self-supervised, supervised, IRM) or for alternative domain generalization methods (R3, R4). Clustering subjects with K-means to define environments for IRM is not justified; each subject should be treated as a distinct domain (R4). A subject ID discrimination task is suggested to validate this (R4). The model’s performance on other large epilepsy datasets (TUEP, TUSL) or other EEG databases is not shown (R1, R3). Training on TUSZ and testing on another database would strengthen generalizability claims (R3, R4). The paper lacks in-depth analysis of per-patient group differences and reflection on limitations (R1, R4).
- **IRM effectiveness is questionable:** Personal experience with IRM on subject-independent EEG tasks shows little to no improvement (R4). The authors do not demonstrate that IRM loss actually removes spurious correlations or why cross-patient variability remains a significant problem (R1, R4).
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons:** The baseline set is incomplete. Comparisons with TCN (at least 6 layers for 1-second 200 Hz samples) and vanilla Transformer are missing (R4). More domain generalization methods should also be compared (R3).
- **Missing mathematical details and unclear operations:** The mathematical derivation of the spatiotemporal IRM (ST-IRM) loss is missing, and the masking function is not explained with intuitive or formal detail (R1, R2). The roles of the ‘k’ operation and ‘m’ operation are unclear (R2). It is not explained how spatiotemporal features are utilized given that EEG contains frequency-domain information (R2).
- **Notation and figure clarity issues:** Figure 2 is not informative; it should show how the three loss modules are obtained and combined rather than depicting the entire DCRNN pipeline (R4). There is a typo: DCRNN is incorrectly denoted as DRCNN in Figure 2 and the methodology section (R2). Notations are confusing and overcomplicate simple concepts (R4).
- **Unexpected performance pattern:** On TUSZ, 60-second trials perform worse than 12-second trials, which is counterintuitive given more behavioral/spatial data in longer windows. This requires explanation (R2).
