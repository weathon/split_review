## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper extends the Lewis signaling game with a feedback channel (from receiver back to sender) and adds token-level noise to simulate communication failure. In both symbolic and image-based referential games, models with feedback achieve higher generalization under noise, yet produce less compositional languages. The results are robust across hyperparameters and paradigms. The paper’s scope is well-defined, the methods are reasonable, and the empirical support is adequate, though the contributions are considered modest and incremental by all reviewers.

### Strengths

- The setup introduces multi-step bidirectional communication between sender and receiver, moving beyond the standard unidirectional signaling game and better resembling realistic human communication with repair mechanisms.
- Experiments are well-designed with multiple evaluation groups (varying noise levels, game configurations) and replicated in two distinct paradigms (symbolic attributes and naturalistic images). Results are robust across settings.
- The contrast between improved generalization and reduced compositionality is interesting and worth further investigation.
- Code is open-sourced; the paper is clearly written and coherent, with appropriate ablations and controls.
- Allowing conversational repair is an important step toward more realistic models of language evolution. The paper serves as a good building block in the field’s literature.

### Weaknesses

- **Noise injection method (most significant concern – reviewer 1):** Noise is implemented as random i.i.d. replacement of tokens with a special noise symbol. This models uncertainty over message *form*, not uncertainty over *meaning* or interpretation. Real misunderstandings often involve semantically confusable substitutions (e.g., via a confusion matrix or semantic similarity), and the current choice may bias the results. It is unclear how many findings are general consequences of repair versus artifacts of this specific noise setup.
- **Missing discussion of prior work on repair in signaling games (reviewer 1):** The paper does not cite several relevant works that introduced interactive repair mechanisms, e.g., van Arkel et al. (CoNLL 2022), Tria et al. (PLOS ONE 2012), de Ruiter & Cummins (SemDial 2012), Silva & Roberts (EVOLANG 2016), and White et al. (EMNLP 2022). A comparison to at least van Arkel et al. is needed to situate the advances.
- **Binary feedback channel is restrictive (reviewers 1 and 2):** The feedback signal is a single binary token. A richer feedback space (e.g., indicating *which* attribute or token was unclear) could lead to different emergent languages and repair strategies. The impact of this restriction is unexplored.
- **Noise is only applied to the sender's messages, not the receiver's feedback (reviewer 1):** In realistic communication, the feedback channel is equally susceptible to noise. This asymmetry may overestimate the benefit of the feedback mechanism; including noise on feedback could shrink the performance gap.
- **Lack of analysis of the feedback token’s semantics (reviewer 2):** The paper does not examine what the feedback token encodes—e.g., whether it signals interaction regulation (“continue,” “clarify”) or provides attribute-level information. A correlation analysis between the presence of noise tokens and feedback activation is needed (also noted by reviewer 3 as a missing experiment).
- **No analysis of how the sender’s messages change across iterations in response to feedback (reviewer 2):** It is unclear whether the sender refines the original message or switches to a completely different strategy. Visualization of message embeddings for different objects (with and without feedback) is requested to see if feedback makes messages more separable.
- **No analysis of whether feedback becomes less informative as agents build common ground (reviewer 2):** The paper does not investigate whether the receiver’s feedback converges to a stable state or becomes more concise over time. Measuring feedback entropy or information content across iterations would be valuable.
- **No robust explanation for the drop in topographical similarity (compositionality) when feedback is enabled (reviewers 1, 2, 3):** While the drop is reported, the paper does not provide an empirical or mechanistic account of why repair discourages compositionality. This is a central finding and warrants deeper analysis.
- **Missing methodological details and ablation (reviewer 2):** The paper does not specify how the game ends (fixed number of steps?), nor how the receiver generates the feedback token (via a separate MLP layer?). Additionally, it asks whether sharing the same vocabulary between sender and receiver would affect compositionality.
- **Presentation issues (reviewers 1 and 3):** Minor typos: “instable” should be “unstable” (p.5); notation inconsistency: “defined by the number of symbols in the vocabulary V” but V was earlier defined as the set of possible attribute values. Reviewer 3 suggests using 95% confidence intervals instead of raw standard error.
- **Missing connections to related work (reviewer 3):** The phrase “jointly co-constructing” is related to Kottur et al. (2017); a sentence connecting the two works would be appropriate. Also, the paper by LaCroix on reflexivity and compositionality is relevant and could be cited.
- **Modest significance of contributions (reviewers 1 and 3):** The contributions are incremental; the core idea (bi-directional communication for repair) has been explored in prior work, and the specific findings may be partly artifacts of the noise model. Reviewer 1 rates the contribution as “fair” (2/4), while reviewer 3 rates it “good” (3/4) but notes the paper’s overall significance is modest. Reviewer 2 gives “good” (3/4) but requests substantial additional analysis, indicating the work is not yet complete.
