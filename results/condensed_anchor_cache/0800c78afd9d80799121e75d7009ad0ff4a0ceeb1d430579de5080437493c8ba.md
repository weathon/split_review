## Merged Review

### Summary

The reviewers agree on the paper’s clarity and interesting theoretical foundation (TV lower bound via witness functions), but there is a significant split: one reviewer (score 3) is critical of gaps in finite-sample analysis and bound looseness, while two reviewers (scores 6, 6) see good originality and empirical promise. The positive reviewers note clarity issues and limited architecture variety, but overall find the theoretical contributions and the WitnessPrune algorithm valuable. The negative reviewer emphasizes that the bound is only stated in population terms and can be extremely loose, and that no finite-sample or high-dimensional analysis is provided, which is a major theoretical weakness for the pruning application.

### Strengths

- Well-written and clearly presented; the simplicity of the TV lower bound and its application to pruning is appreciated.
- Originality and significance: deviates from the standard Gaussian assumption for class-conditional features and introduces a witness‑function framework that establishes new connections between TV distance and discriminant‑based classifiers (Fisher discriminants, Minimax Probability machines).
- The theoretical derivations are interesting and insightful; proofs in Appendix A.1 and A.2 are simple, clean, and to the point.
- The WitnessPrune algorithm is derived from the theoretical bound and shows up to 7% greater accuracy for similar sparsity in hard‑to‑prune layers compared to the state‑of‑the‑art, demonstrating empirical promise.
- The paper bridges theory and practice by linking the lower bound to well‑known classifiers, potentially enhancing model robustness.
- The topic is relevant to ICLR.

### Weaknesses

- **Finite‑sample and high‑dimensional analysis is missing.** The TV lower bound is stated in terms of population moments, but pruning relies on estimates from finite samples. In typical neural‑network pruning settings, the dimension grows with the number of samples, yet no finite‑sample analysis of the moment estimators or the resulting saliency scores is provided. This is a major theoretical gap. *(Reviewer 1 – score 3, high severity)*
- **Lower bound can be extremely loose.** For any positive integer \(k\), there exists a discrete distribution that matches the first \(k\) moments of a standard Gaussian; if \(P\) is such a distribution and \(Q\) is the standard Gaussian, \(\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)=1\) but the lower bound is 0. The paper does not discuss the practical impact of such looseness or provide guidance on when the bound can be trusted. *(Reviewer 1 – score 3, high severity)*
- **Assumptions about witness functions and moments may be unrealistic.** The paper assumes the existence of certain moments and boundedness of witness functions; it does not explore sensitivity to violations of these assumptions (e.g., heavy‑tailed distributions). The strictness of the assumption that functions \(g\) and \(G\) exist (Section 4.1) is not justified. *(Reviewers 1 and 3 – moderate severity)*
- **Limited architectural and dataset diversity.** Experiments are conducted only on VGG‑16 and VGG‑19, both of which are older architectures. No tests on modern architectures (e.g., ResNets, Transformers) or on a wider range of data modalities and distributions are provided. This raises concerns about generalizability. *(Reviewer 3 – high severity; also noted by Reviewer 2 regarding VGG‑19 missing CHIP results)*
- **Missing experiments and ablation studies.** 
  - For VGG‑19, the CHIP baseline is not reported; the authors should explain why and ideally provide the numbers (Reviewer 2).
  - The sparsity levels in Table 1 are not explained; how were they selected? (Reviewer 2)
  - Variants of WitnessPrune are mentioned but not thoroughly discussed or experimentally evaluated (Reviewer 2).
  - No theoretical justification is given for why WitnessPrune should be generally preferable to prior methods like TVSPrune (Reviewer 2).
- **Scalability and computational overhead are not analyzed.** There is no discussion of how the method scales with deeper networks or of the computational cost of computing moment estimates compared to simpler pruning methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity and presentation issues.** 
  - Potential breach of anonymity: Table 1 lists “TVSPrune (Murti et al., 2022) (our work)”. (Reviewer 2)
  - Formatting of references often lacks separation from text, obstructing readability (Reviewer 2).
  - Unclear definition: Section 2.2 first sentence ends abruptly with “..., and let.” (Reviewer 2)
  - On page 6, the saliency score \(r_j^\ell\) has reversed subscript and superscript before Eq. (7) (Reviewer 1).
  - The term “robust” in “robust lower bound” is misleading as no estimation analysis is performed (Reviewer 1).
- The practical implications of the bound looseness and the finite‑sample regime are not addressed, leaving the overall contribution of the lower bound unclear for real‑world pruning. *(Reviewer 1 – score 3, contrast: the negative reviewer is substantially more critical on theoretical grounds, whereas the positive reviewers deem the contributions sufficient.)*
