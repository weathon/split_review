## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes graphical criteria that yield a sufficient condition for a predictor to be counterfactually invariant (CI) in terms of a conditional independence in the observational distribution. To learn such predictors, it introduces Counterfactually Invariant Prediction (CIP), a model-agnostic framework using the Hilbert-Schmidt Conditional Independence Criterion (HSCIC). Experiments on simulated and real-world datasets, including scalar and multivariate settings, demonstrate the method's effectiveness.

### Strengths
1. The problem of learning counterfactually invariant predictors solely from observational data is relevant and important (all reviewers).
2. The theoretical analysis provides sufficient graphical criteria and is sound (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3).
3. The method is validated extensively on both synthetic and real datasets, showing effectiveness across various settings (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Counterfactual vs. observational independence**: Counterfactual invariance is an individual-level property, whereas the proposed regularization only enforces population-level independence; it remains questionable whether CI can be achieved from observational data without strong bounds (Reviewer 1; soundness confidence 5).
2. **Novelty concerns**: Such invariance learning is not new; similar approaches exist in the literature (Reviewer 1).
3. **Restrictive causal graph assumption**: The method requires a known prior causal graph, which is limiting in realistic applications (Reviewer 1).
4. **Notation ambiguity**: In Figure 1(a), it is unclear what distinguishes S from X and how one decides which variable belongs to which set. Additionally, the phrasing “counterfactually invariant in A with respect to W” vs. “with respect to X” seems inconsistent—do they have the same meaning? (Reviewer 2)
5. **Lack of finite-sample guarantees**: Corollary 3.6 gives only a population result; no theoretical results on the finite-sample estimator are provided (Reviewer 2).
6. **Other applications missing**: Besides fairness, the paper does not discuss other potential applications of learning counterfactually invariant predictors (Reviewer 2).
7. **Strong assumption in Theorem 3.2**: The assumption that \(X = g(X,\ldots)\) (or the injectivity condition) is restrictive. It implies \(\mathbf{A} \cup \mathbf{X}\) is a maximal connected graph and \(Y\) is a root node (no parent in \(\mathbf{X} \cup \mathbf{A}\)). Consequently, in the fairness example (Fig. 1e), the adjustment set \(\mathbf{S}\) for \((\mathbf{A} \cup \mathbf{W}, Y)\) should be empty, yet a subset of \(\mathbf{A} \cup \mathbf{W}\) is used as the adjustment set (Appendix F.5, Fig. 8). A similar issue arises in the synthetic and image experiments, where \(\mathbf{W}\) is taken as all observed variables except \(Y\) and \(\mathbf{A}\) (Reviewer 3; soundness 2).
8. **Injectivity assumption not justified**: The term “injectivity” is mentioned in Theorem 3.2 but not found in the proof; how it is used is unclear. The paper claims CIP works even when assumptions are violated, but tests only one violation scenario (no unobserved confounders). More experiments under general violations are needed (Reviewer 3).
9. **Scalability and computational cost**: Experiments are limited to simple causal graphs with few nodes. Scalability to larger graphs (e.g., 20–30 nodes) is not tested, and no analysis of computational complexity for finding the adjustment set or estimating HSCIC is provided (Reviewer 3).
10. **Organizational issues**: Section 2.2 (Related Work) is poorly structured; it discusses almost sure conditional independence, distributional conditional independence, and F-CI without clear connection to the paper’s contributions, while missing a focused overview of prior work on counterfactually invariant predictors (Reviewer 3).

**Contrast of opinions**: One reviewer (Reviewer 2) was more positive about the empirical results and overall contribution (score 5), while the other two reviewers (scores 3, 3) raised substantial concerns about the theoretical assumptions, novelty, and experimental validation.
