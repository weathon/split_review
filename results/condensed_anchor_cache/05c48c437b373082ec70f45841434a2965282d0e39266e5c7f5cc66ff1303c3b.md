## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers generally found the paper clearly written and well-organized, with a sound motivation for mixing local and global updates in personalized federated learning via an NTK-based convergence rate. The method (LG-Mix) uses the trace ratio of feature matrices to set the mixing coefficient and is compared against many relevant baselines. Scores and depth of assessment differ: one reviewer (score 8, confidence 2) was highly positive about the originality and clarity, while another (score 5, confidence 2) raised several concerns about experimental fairness, missing derivations, and applicability assumptions. The third reviewer (score 6, confidence 3) appreciated the ablation studies and convergence analysis but noted missing efficiency metrics and convergence verification.

### Strengths

- Original and innovative approach that leverages Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) convergence to quantify importance of local vs. global updates, providing a new perspective on feature heterogeneity in PFL.
- Writing is clear, easy to follow, with well-organized sections and informative figures and tables.
- Comprehensive comparison with many state-of-the-art methods on five datasets (natural and medical images), including ablation studies that verify details of the algorithm.
- The idea of mixing local and global updates via the Gram trace is simple and intuitive.
- A brief convergence analysis is provided.

### Weaknesses

- Derivation of eq. 4 is unclear and lacks explanation (what is the meaning of `y - w(t)`?); no details or reference are given. [R1, R3]
- Computational cost (GPU memory, training wall‑clock time, storage) is not reported or compared against baselines; the algorithm may require storing a global model locally, which should be assessed via storage comparison. [R1, R2]
- The fairness of the experimental comparisons is questionable: it is unclear whether each baseline was independently optimized with sufficient hyperparameter tuning (e.g., a fixed learning rate is used across all methods). [R3]
- The method’s reliance on the NTK approximation may not hold for very deep or complex network architectures; this limitation is not discussed. [R1]
- Scalability to larger numbers of clients, highly imbalanced data distributions, and more complex datasets (beyond the five tested) is not addressed. [R1]
- No empirical demonstration of Proposition 1 (the optimality of the mixing ratio) or of the method’s advantage over a fixed (optimized) mixing ratio. [R3]
- Loss curves or gradient norms are not provided to verify that the method actually achieves faster convergence or lower training loss as claimed. [R2]
- The problem setup is not clearly defined from the beginning: the type of heterogeneity (covariate shift vs. concept drift) and the specific personalized settings to which the method applies should be explicitly stated. [R3]
- The convergence analysis section feels disintegrated from the rest of the paper; its practical implications and how it differs from existing results are not explained. [R3]
- Simple experiments that highlight the method’s applicability (e.g., comparison with fixed local‑global ratios) are missing. [R3]
- Several minor writing issues: typos (“paramters” → “parameters”), confusion between subscript/footnote, “tr” should be roman in eq. 7. [R3]

*Contrast in reviewer opinion:* One reviewer (score 8) strongly praised the originality and clarity, noting only the need for scalability discussion and hyperparameter sensitivity. Another reviewer (score 5) was more critical, questioning the fairness of baselines, clarity of the setup, missing experiments (fixed ratio comparison, Proposition 1 demonstration), and the disconnection of the convergence analysis. The third reviewer (score 6) was positive about the motivation and ablation studies but requested convergence verification and efficiency metrics.
