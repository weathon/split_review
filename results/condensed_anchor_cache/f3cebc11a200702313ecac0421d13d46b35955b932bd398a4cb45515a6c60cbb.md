## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Inner Product (IP), a simplified influence metric that replaces the inverse Hessian with an identity matrix, making it essentially the gradient dot product (Grad-Dot) of TracIn restricted to the final model. IP is extended to fairness (by substituting fairness measures for the gradient sum) and robustness (using adversarial gradients), and is enhanced via an ensemble of dropout-perturbed models. Experiments cover noisy label detection, sample selection for LLM fine-tuning, and defense against adversarial attacks. Reviewers note the method is simple and efficient, and the synthetic experiments are interesting, but they raise major concerns about novelty (the core idea already exists), theoretical justification (order consistency argument is flawed, confusion between influence function and TracIn), experimental fairness (missing baselines, cherry-picked results, limited improvements), and lack of clarity in the ensemble procedure.

### Strengths
- The paper is well written and easy to understand.
- Efficiency is an important topic for training data attribution.
- Extensive experiments with statistical ranges and confidence intervals.
- Sanity checks on synthetic data yield interesting findings: for non-linear models, IP can separate corrupted samples where the Hessian-based influence function cannot.
- The method is simple, easy to implement, and computationally efficient.
- The extension of influence estimation to algorithmic fairness metrics is interesting.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and positioning**: The IP method (gradient dot product) has already been proposed as Grad-Dot in earlier work (Chhabra et al. 2024) and used as a baseline in many papers. The claim of defining IP is inaccurate; the paper should frame it as “revisited”. Similarly, replacing the loss gradient with metrics for fairness/robustness has been tried before. The relationship between influence function (leave-one-out approximation) and TracIn (parameter-space trajectory) is confused; IP takes elements from both, making it unclear what notion of influence it adopts.
- **Theoretical justification is weak**: The order consistency argument (Figure 1) is flawed. For extreme ends (regions I and III) ordering matters most in applications like removing the most detrimental examples, but the analysis only claims sign preservation, not order. Moreover, one can easily construct counterexamples where order is reversed within region II by adjusting gradient norms. The conditions under which the angle α between the gradient and the direction is small are not discussed; there is no rigorous analysis relating cosine similarity to true influence. The paper does not explain why magnitude of IP is a reliable proxy for inverse-Hessian influence magnitude.
- **Missing comparisons and unfair experimental setup**: Several SOTA gradient-based methods (e.g., TRAK) and efficiency tools like random projection are omitted. The dropout trick could also benefit other methods, but this is not tested. The manually added noisy data in Figure 2(D) appears cherry-picked; the negative performance of the full Hessian method seems very unlikely. The improvement over existing approaches is very limited—Table 1 shows all methods performing similarly when variance is considered.
- **Lack of rigorous validation**: The paper does not compare IP scores directly with actual influence (inverse Hessian) or with leave-one-out retraining, even on a small subset. Metrics such as correlation to influence function, precision-recall for label perturbation detection, or similarity of predictions are missing. This makes it hard to assess the quality of influence estimates.
- **Ensemble procedure is unclear and not justified**: The process for generating ensembles is not formally defined. The paper states that diverse models can be generated by dropout on the final checkpoint without retraining, but it is unclear whether this enjoys the same benefits as ensembles of independently retrained models. When ensemble size increases, the method closely approximates full TracIn, diminishing the claimed contribution. The meaning of U(0, 0.01) in the dropout description is not explained.
- **Fairness evaluation is limited**: Section 6 benchmarks primarily focus on accuracy, which does not adequately validate the fairness claims.
- **Adversarial defense results show limited benefit**: The improvement from IP in adversarial robustness appears marginal.
- **Insufficient explanation of Section 4 results**: The failure of the Hessian-based method on synthetic data is attributed to approximation errors, but these errors are not discussed or demonstrated; it is unclear why a setup without approximation errors cannot be tested on more complex, non-linearly separable data. Figure 3 lacks sufficient explanation.
- **Minor issues**: Some claims lack evidence (e.g., the statement that “sample influence should be assessed using a fixed model” contradicts the dynamic nature of TracIn). The related work section does not effectively contextualize the paper’s contributions. The paper would benefit from a more precise understanding of the influence function literature (specifically the role of the inverse Hessian).
