## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes StructuralGLIP for zero-shot medical object detection. It introduces an auxiliary branch that encodes prompts into a latent knowledge bank, then uses a mutual selection mechanism across encoder layers to select highly similar features from image representations and the knowledge bank, forming structural representations for fine-grained alignment. Evaluated on seven zero-shot medical detection benchmarks across four modalities (endoscopy, microscopy, radiology), it achieves +4.1% AP over prior state-of-the-art zero-shot methods and +3.2% AP on fine-tuned endoscopic models.

### Strengths
- The motivation is strong and clearly articulated: improving generalization of foundation models for medical image understanding with limited data access, addressing the lack of object-level prompts and failure to capture diverse image features in existing methods (R2).
- The dual-branch architecture with a knowledge bank and mutual selection is a novel and efficient approach for context-aware, adaptive vision-language alignment, enabling fine-grained matching between image patches and target descriptions (R1, R2, R3).
- StructuralGLIP achieves significant and consistent performance improvements over prior zero-shot models across diverse medical imaging benchmarks, with +4.1% AP over state-of-the-art zero-shot methods and +3.2% AP on fine-tuned endoscopic datasets (R1, R2, R3).
- The method performs well in both zero-shot and few-shot settings, maintaining high detection accuracy with limited labeled data (R3).
- The prompt generation module extracts information from prior knowledge of unseen classes, enhancing adaptability across domains (R3).
- The evaluation is comprehensive, covering four different medical image modalities (R4).
- The paper's representation and structure are mostly clear and easy to follow (R4; but note also language and formatting issues, see weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and conceptual concerns**: One reviewer (R4) strongly criticized the novelty as incremental compared to standard contextual prompt methods. The auxiliary branch's components (MHA, f, RPN in Eq. 8-10) appear to require training or fine-tuning, yet the zero-shot setting is claimed to be training-free; this contradiction is not explained. The paper does not specify what data is used for such training, nor whether the GLIP baseline received the same training, making the noise prompt argument questionable. Another reviewer (R2) identified the Top-K selection operation as non-differentiable, whereas previous methods are differentiable; they suggest incorporating a Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization to enable fine-tuning for potentially better performance.
- **Training and zero-shot setting ambiguity**: The term "zero-shot enhancement" is misleading. When the target dataset's labeled data is used for fine-tuning the GLIP base model, the setting becomes weakly supervised rather than zero-shot. The paper does not clearly describe what components of StructuralGLIP are trained and what data is used in the zero-shot detection setting (R4). The performance gain from the zero-shot enhancement setting (fine-tuned GLIP) appears larger than that from StructuralGLIP's design itself, and the choice of VQA model can sometimes outperform StructuralGLIP (e.g., AutoPrompter + Qwen2-VL-7B achieving 75.0 AP on CVC), raising questions about the viability of zero-shot medical detection (R4).
- **Lack of empirical evidence for claimed mechanisms**: The authors argue that StructuralGLIP prevents domain shift by fusing prompts in early layers so the RPN only sees the target name CLS token, but this claim lacks direct empirical evidence (e.g., feature distribution comparison). Final performance is a surrogate measure that does not directly support the domain shift argument (R4). The term "fine-grained" (L201, L256) is not clearly defined (R4).
- **Dependence on prompt quality and VQA/LLM choice**: The model heavily relies on prompt quality; low-quality prompts negatively impact performance. The paper does not analyze how the choice of LLM (e.g., LLaMA, Gemini) affects results, nor does it evaluate the quality of prompts generated by VQA methods, which themselves may perform poorly on medical datasets (R3, R4). Performance variation across different VQA models (Table S8-1/2) is significant, suggesting instability (R4).
- **Missing analyses and ablations**:
  - Missing robustness experiment: adding noisy knowledge (e.g., knowledge for another target) would test the mutual selection process (R2).
  - No analysis of performance across different classes, especially those with limited representation, to address potential data imbalance (R1).
  - Limited evaluation metrics: only AP is reported; AP50 is not reported in Table S9 without justification (R4). Other metrics and clinical outcome feedback are not considered (R1).
  - No analysis of how the prompt generation module ensures valuable prompts for unseen classes without introducing distracting information (R3).
  - Generalization to other modalities, less common diseases, and natural image datasets is not tested (R1, R3).
- **Computational and practical limitations**: The dual-branch structure with knowledge bank increases computational cost and hardware demands, potentially limiting deployment in resource-poor environments (R1, R3). The complex prompt selection and knowledge bank structure reduce transparency, making it difficult for practitioners to interpret decisions, which is critical in clinical applications (R1, R3).
- **Misalignment and reliability concerns**: Despite improvements, there may still be misalignment between visual features and prompts, especially for atypical presentations, leading to missed detections. The reliance on textual prompts may not fully capture visual anomalies (R1). The clinical need for zero-shot detection is questioned, as clinicians would not trust zero-shot settings in practice (R4).
- **Technical and presentation issues**:
  - Incomplete sentence at Line 234 (R2, R4).
  - Word choice: "medical detection" is misleading for object detection in medical images (R4).
  - Equations 6&7: Top-P/Q^{max} function could be simplified using argmax/argsort (R4).
  - Formatting error: unexpected horizontal line in Table 5 (R3).
