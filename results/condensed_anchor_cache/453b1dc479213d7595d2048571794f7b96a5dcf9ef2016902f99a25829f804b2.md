## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the ability of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to count substructures (cycles, cliques, quasi-cliques, connected components) using message-passing with random node features. By taking statistical moments of the output, the authors obtain deterministic equivariant representations. They prove that with increasing moment order, GNNs can count up to 7-cycles and other substructures. They also establish an equivalence to a deterministic graph filtering model using Hadamard products of polynomial filters. Experiments on cycle counting, substructure detection, graph classification, and molecular property prediction (ZINC) validate the approach.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and well-motivated problem: substructure counting is critical for graph representation learning (R2, R4).
- The proposed method is novel and interesting: it uses random node initialization but achieves equivariance via expectation in the final layer, enabling linear complexity compared to higher-order GNNs (R1, R3). The connection to a deterministic graph filtering with Hadamard product of polynomial filters is insightful (R3).
- The theoretical analysis is extensive, rigorous, and provides detailed constructions for counting specific substructures (R1, R2, R4). In particular, the authors prove that moment-based GNNs can count up to 7-cycles, improving over standard MPNNs (R3).
- Empirical experiments align with theoretical findings and demonstrate the method’s effectiveness on cycle counting, substructure detection, graph classification, and ZINC regression (R2, R3, R4).
- The method has linear complexity in graph size, a practical advantage over higher-order GNNs (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Writing quality**: The paper is poorly written. It contains editing errors (exposed author name “Charilaos” on page 6, redundant use of “equation”, incorrect equation spacing and referencing), unclear definitions (e.g., “x is anonymous” on page 4, “stationary random vector”), and lacks clear section navigation (R1, R4). The role of the characteristic function on page 3 is unclear (R1). The abstract may mislead readers into thinking the counting ability of regular MPNNs is unknown, whereas prior work (Chen et al., 2020) already established clear limitations linked to the 1-FWL test (R3).
- **Theoretical completeness and tightness**: Only positive results are provided; it is unclear whether the GNN fails without high-order moments. For instance, can a 6-cycle be counted using only a 2-order moment? Can a 7-cycle be counted using a 3-order moment? (R1). Theorem 4.1 on counting connected components of a single graph is trivial—it reduces to fitting a constant (R1). There is no upper bound on the expressive power of Moment‑GNN; is it bounded by 3‑WL like subgraph GNNs? (R2). The model’s ability to count larger cycles (e.g., 8‑cycles) is unaddressed theoretically, even though experiments show perfect counting; the required moment order is not specified (R1, R3).
- **Experimental comparisons and baselines**: Many powerful and well-adopted GNN baselines are missing: e.g., GNN‑AK, GraphGPS, CIN, NGNN, and subgraph-based methods like EGO (R2). On the ZINC regression task, the method “Graph Neural Networks with Local Graph Parameters” (NeurIPS 2021) outperforms the current method and should be included (R4). Comparisons on cycle counting and ZINC are insufficient overall (R3). The paper does not adequately differentiate itself from existing substructure-encoding GNNs, making it difficult to assess novelty and practical advantages (R2).
- **Implementation and dataset details**: The method relies on taking an expectation in the final layer; the implementation (e.g., number of random samples) is not clarified (R1). For the ZINC graph regression task, it is unclear whether the 10,000/1,000/1,000 split is random or from ZINC‑100K; a full-dataset evaluation would be more fair (R2).
- **Generalizability to downstream tasks**: While substructure counting ability is demonstrated, it may not directly translate to better performance on other tasks. Testing on more node-level and graph-level benchmark datasets is needed to show that the method improves downstream task performance (R4).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer rated the presentation and contribution more positively (Presentation 3, Contribution 3), while others rated presentation as poor and contribution as fair (Presentation 1, Contribution 2) or fair (Presentation 3, Contribution 2) (R1, R3, R4 vs R2).
