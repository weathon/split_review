## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper targets KV cache compression for LLM inference by identifying critical KV entries. Unlike prior methods that rely solely on attention weights, it formulates a perturbation-constrained selection algorithm based on an L1 output perturbation bound that incorporates both attention scores and value vector norms. The approach is integrated into existing eviction strategies (SnapKV, Pyramid, AdaKV) and evaluated on LongBench tasks with LLaMA-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B, showing consistent improvements in generation quality at small cache budgets.

### Strengths
- Well-motivated: identifies that current KV eviction methods relying solely on attention weights are not a proper metric; the problem of KV cache compression in long-context scenarios is critical and timely. (R1, R2, R4)
- Provides a theoretical framework: the perturbation-constrained selection algorithm is derived from first principles with formal analysis, proofs, and a clear worst-case output perturbation bound. (R1, R3, R4)
- Algorithm is intuitive and straightforward to integrate into existing KV eviction frameworks (SnapKV, Pyramid, AdaKV) and sparse-attention serving systems. (R2, R3, R4)
- Evaluates three distinct eviction strategies with comparable improvements, and includes ablation studies that demonstrate reduced attention output perturbations compared to heuristics-based approaches. (R2, R4)
- Writing is well-structured and easy to follow (R1), though other reviewers note areas needing improvement (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental and marginal gains:** Contributions are considered incremental; the average improvement is often marginal, and performance degradation at the tested budget \(b\) appears too severe for practical deployment. (R1, R3)
- **Unsupported claims and missing theoretical justification:** Several key claims lack explanation or theoretical backup, including:
  - Why a subset of KV is sufficient for LLM inference. (R1)
  - Why L1 distance is an appropriate metric for output perturbation, given its known sparsity-sensitivity while attention outputs are dense. (R1)
  - The relationship between output perturbation and actual generation quality (i.e., when does a deviation change the output token?). (R1)
- **Questionable choice of hyperparameter \(\alpha\):** The fixed \(\alpha = 0.25\) is not justified; deriving it from the attention weight distribution would be more principled. Appendix A does not sufficiently address whether model performance changes with varying \(\alpha\) or whether conclusions remain robust. (R1, R3)
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons:** Missing comparisons with StreamingLLM (important empirical work) and with the full attention baseline (no KV dropping) to show the generation quality gap. (R1) The connection and differences with FastGen and MInference, which yield similar algorithms, should be discussed. (R2) No discussion of the output perturbation concept’s adaptation in other deep learning works (e.g., [3], [4] cited in R2).
- **Limited experimental scope:**
  - Evaluated only on long-context tasks; the method applies to all generation tasks, so experiments on other popular benchmarks (e.g., AlpacaEval) are necessary. (R2)
  - Experiments are only on smaller models (~7B); results on larger models (e.g., 70B) and needle-in-the-haystack tasks would strengthen the paper and better assess critical KV identification, especially for complex multi-turn reasoning where evicted KVs may become crucial later. (R4)
- **Writing and notation clarity issues:**
  - Section 3.2 needs reformatting and rewriting for conciseness; many notations are introduced ambiguously and references placed haphazardly. (R3)
  - An alias for \(\frac{N \cdot A}{\sum_n N_i A_i}\) should be created to simplify equations. (R3)
  - Assumption 1 and Theorem 3 should be in their own subsection as the core approach. (R3)
  - The transition from defining \(L\) as the L1 norm of attention output deviation (Eq. 6) to directly setting \(L\) equal to the derived upper bound (Eqs. 12–15) is confusing; it should use \(L \le\) or a separate variable. This undermines the clarity of the theoretical foundation. (R4)
- **Assumption 1 inconsistency:** The paper criticizes reliance on attention weights in prior work (Lines 17–19, 60–63, 128–129), yet Assumption 1 uses attention scores to represent KV entry importance. This contradiction needs reconciliation. (R3)
- **Theorem 3 and its proof:**
  - The claim \(\arg\max_N \gamma = \arg\min_N \theta\) may be incorrect; a counter-example occurs when \(\sum_i N_i A_i = 1\) (selected tokens capture all mass) yielding \(\min\theta=0\) while \(\max\gamma>0\). (R4)
  - The proof is not straightforward: the inequality \(\sum_i^n N_i A_i > \sum \text{Top}_k (A, b') > 0.5\) is not guaranteed for arbitrary \(N_i\) (the selection may avoid top-\(k\) tokens), and even when it holds, the derivation that \(\arg\max_N\gamma = \arg\min_N\theta\) does not follow from the stated assumption. (R4) Suggested fix: move algorithm description before the theorem or modify Assumption 1 to \(>0.51\) to properly bound the term. (R4)
- **Missing correlation analysis:** There is no theoretical or empirical analysis correlating worst-case output perturbation predictions with actual perturbation measurements in real-world applications. (R2)
- **Performance at intermediate budgets:** Need insight on budget values between 1024 and 6711 (e.g., 2048, 4096) where both the proposed method and other eviction strategies achieve performance comparable to the full baseline, and whether the proposed method offers any advantage in that regime. (R3)
