## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes EDBA, a min-max backdoor attack framework for federated learning that decouples the backdoor task from the main task to improve effectiveness and durability. The maximization phase generates triggers using universal adversarial perturbation to widen the performance gap between poisoned and benign samples; the minimization phase trains the backdoor model. Experiments cover image classification (including Tiny-ImageNet) and NLP (emotion analysis), comparing three attack methods under six defenses. Scores: 3, 5, 3, 3. One reviewer (score 5) praised the idea’s novelty and organization, while the other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) raised major concerns about methodology, related work coverage, evaluation completeness, and practical relevance.

### Strengths
- Backdoor attacks in federated learning remain a relevant and important problem (R1).
- The idea of decoupling the backdoor task from the main task to enhance effectiveness and durability is novel and less studied in prior work; EDBA appears competitive with related methods (R2, R3).
- The paper is well-organized, well-written, and mostly easy to follow (R2, R3; R1 and R4 disagreed on clarity and completeness).
- Extensive experiments demonstrate effectiveness even with a single attacker among 200 clients (R2).
- Evaluation includes both computer vision and natural language processing tasks, and the Tiny-ImageNet dataset (R4).
- Detailed configuration of training parameters is provided (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Outdated and incomplete related work.** The comparison attacks are dated (e.g., all cited defenses published before 2018; R1). The survey misses several key durable backdoor attacks (A3FL NeurIPS 2023, Chameleon ICML 2023, CerP AAAI 2023) and state-of-the-art defenses (FLAME, BayBFed, DeepSight, FreqFed) (R1, R4). The paper’s claim that existing methods rely on unrealistic assumptions (large attacker count, scaled contributions) is inaccurate: recent works [1,2] require only a single client per round and no scaled contributions (R3).
- **Unclear methodology and missing justification.** The paper does not clearly explain why the min-max framework works or what specific advantage it brings to backdoor attacks (R1). The argument that “invisibility of triggers in the local model is not a crucial metric” (Section 3.2) contradicts existing works [1,2,3] that emphasize trigger invisibility via norm constraints, fewer perturbed pixels, or semantic features; if triggers are visually apparent, benign clients could detect them through simple inspection (R2). The claimed technical innovation is weak—using UAP as a trigger is a similar idea to IBA (R4). No specific evasion modules for defenses are designed, yet the paper claims EDBA bypasses defenses; this phenomenon requires explanation (R4). The relationship between generated triggers and out-of-distribution (OOD) features is not clarified; a comparison with the edge-case method (Wang et al. 2020) is strongly recommended (R2).
- **Insufficient and outdated evaluation.** Defenses considered are all from before 2022 (R4) and only four outdated defenses used (NDC 2019, Krum/Multi-Krum 2017, Median 2018, RLR 2021); missing more recent methods (FLAME, BayBFed, DeepSight, FreqFed) (R1, R4). Attacks and defenses are only evaluated on one dataset per task—exhaustive evaluation across all datasets is expected (R1). Important ablation studies are missing: impact of attacker proportion, degree of Non-IID data, and adaptive defenses (R4).
- **Durability evaluation is not convincing.** Durability is tested without deploying any defenses, and no comparison is made with existing durable backdoor approaches (IBA, A3FL, Chameleon) (R4). The paper should also evaluate against BackdoorIndicator, as the decoupling process may make poisoned updates OOD, aligning with that defense’s rationale (R3).
- **Trigger generation and practicality concerns.** The adversary would need to attach fine-grained triggers with unlimited range (same size as the whole image) and detailed pixel values; a realistic scenario where this is feasible is not specified (R3). It is unclear what the *final* trigger is for evaluation: is it generated from the last global round the adversary participates in? Do earlier rounds’ triggers contribute to later rounds? The evolution of triggers across rounds needs clarification (R3). For NLP tasks, Eq. 5 calculates an importance score for each word position, raising significant efficiency concerns; complexity analysis is needed (R2).
- **Norm constraint and fairness of comparison.** A critical question: does EDBA apply a norm constraint on trigger size? If not, could a clean sample from the target class be directly used as the trigger? If so, the Max phase is unnecessary. A fair comparison with IBA, which includes a norm constraint, should be reported (R2). Regarding Eq. 5 for NLP: if the classification of a sentence remains unchanged after adding the trigger at a specific position, does that imply the backdoor task and the main task are still coupled? (R2).
- **Presentation issues.** The paper contains multiple typos and incomplete arguments (R1). For example, “benigh” in Fig. 6(a) and (c) should be “benign” (R2). The description of Fig. 6 is unclear—especially Fig. 6(b) and (d)—and needs more detail to explain whether poisoned samples are clustered by original categories (R2). Line 231 has a typo “participantsaAz” (R3).

*Note: One reviewer (score 5) found the idea novel, the paper well-organized, and the experiments extensive, but this view was not shared by the other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3), who raised the above concerns.*
