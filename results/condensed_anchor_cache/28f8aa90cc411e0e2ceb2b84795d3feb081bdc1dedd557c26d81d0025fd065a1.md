## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces AIR-BENCH 2024, a safety benchmark for LLMs grounded in risk categories from 8 government regulations and 16 corporate policies. The authors construct a four-tier taxonomy with 314 granular risk categories and generate 5,694 manually curated prompts. They evaluate 22 LLMs using automated refusal-based evaluation, with human validation. The benchmark aims to bridge the gap between existing safety benchmarks and the specific risk categories outlined in legal and policy frameworks.

### Strengths
- **Regulatory and policy grounding**: The benchmark is uniquely aligned with real-world regulations from the EU, US, and China, as well as corporate acceptable-use policies. Multiple reviewers highlighted this as a novel feature that addresses a gap in existing benchmarks and increases practical relevance.
- **Granular risk taxonomy**: The four-level structure with 314 specific risk categories enables detailed identification of model deficiencies across many risk types. This granularity was specifically appreciated by one reviewer.
- **Comprehensive model evaluation**: The study compares 22 prominent LLMs and identifies key trends and safety gaps. One reviewer praised the detailed comparison and clear trends.
- **Automated evaluation with human validation**: An automated system for evaluating refusals was created and validated against human raters. One reviewer noted this as a concrete contribution, especially given the diversity and breadth of categories covered compared to prior benchmarks.
- **Well-structured and readable**: Multiple reviewers found the paper easy to read, well-organized, and supported by helpful color-coding. The related work coverage was also praised as thorough.
- **Relevance and timeliness**: One reviewer rated the paper very highly (10/10) and called it a “big step” toward automated safety assessment, underscoring the topic’s importance.

### Weaknesses
- **Oversimplification of “safety”**: One reviewer (score 6) strongly criticized the paper for conflating refusal rate with model safety, arguing that information itself is not unsafe—only actions based on it may be. They recommended repositioning the work as measuring refusal of undesirable content rather than inherent “safety.” The paper uses “refusal” only in Section 4 but “safety” elsewhere, which this reviewer found misleading.
- **Undefined handling of regulatory conflicts**: Two reviewers raised concerns about cross-jurisdictional conflicts (e.g., differing privacy laws, varying definitions of “appropriate” outputs). The paper does not explain how it balances or trades off contradictory regulations from different countries/regions. The question of practical value for cross-border AI services remains unanswered.
- **Unclear treatment of role-specific restrictions**: A reviewer noted that regulations impose different obligations on providers, distributors, and deployers (e.g., API refusal of protected decision-making unless oversight is guaranteed). The benchmark’s handling of these role-specific restrictions is not obvious, and deeper discussion is needed beyond a brief mention in Section 4.4.
- **Bias from GPT-4 reliance**: Multiple reviewers flagged the use of GPT-4 for both prompt generation and evaluation. One reviewer noted that scaling from simple, handwritten templates with a single model introduces artificiality and potential bias. Another added that about 70% of judge prompts remain model-generated, which may cause inconsistencies in judgment fidelity. The paper acknowledges this, but it remains a significant limitation.
- **Assumption of standalone model**: One reviewer pointed out that many real-world applications involve human oversight, which could mitigate risks differently than the benchmark assumes.
- **Lack of non-English evaluation**: One reviewer identified that the benchmark only covers English. Harmful outputs in other languages are therefore not assessed, creating a blind spot.
- **Limited actionable insights from results**: A reviewer found the extensive result tables overwhelming and lacking deep takeaways, calling for more in-depth discussion to help readers identify critical findings.
- **Outdated quickly; no future-proofing plan**: One reviewer noted the benchmark may become outdated as regulations evolve; the authors only mention this briefly without offering a concrete path for updates.
- **Missing classification for ambiguous responses**: A reviewer asked how responses that are neither explicitly refused nor overtly harmful are classified within the framework—an important detail left unclear.
- **Insufficient detail on prompt curation**: A reviewer requested more specifics on the methods, criteria, and protocols used in the manual review of expanded prompts.
- **Reproducibility concern (IP region)**: A reviewer pointed out that model providers may apply different guardrails based on user location; the paper does not report the IP region from which experiments were run, undermining reproducibility.
- **Potential societal harm**: One reviewer raised the concern that malicious actors might use the benchmark to identify weakly guarded models and accelerate harmful activities—a side effect the paper does not discuss.
