## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes NeuralMD, a machine learning surrogate for protein-ligand binding dynamics simulation. The method combines (1) BindingNet, a physics-informed multi-grained group symmetric network using vector frames to capture multi-level protein-ligand interactions, and (2) an augmented neural ordinary differential equation solver that learns trajectories under Newtonian mechanics. Evaluated on the MISATO dataset (first large-scale binding dynamics dataset, released May 2023), the model is tested on ten single-trajectory and three multi-trajectory tasks, achieving a 2000× speedup over standard numerical MD and outperforming ML baselines by up to ~80% under the stability metric. Reviewers noted that learning long-timescale dynamics directly with a neural ODE is an interesting idea, and the use of the MISATO dataset is encouraging. However, multiple weaknesses were raised, including fundamental concerns about the problem formulation and mathematical correctness, missing baselines and ablation studies, insufficient experimental details, and limited discussion of limitations and practical implications.

### Strengths
- **Performance and speedup**: All reviewers acknowledge the 2000× speedup over standard numerical MD and up to ~80% improvement in stability over other ML approaches. The method is evaluated on 13 distinct binding simulation tasks (single and multi-trajectory).
- **First to leverage MISATO dataset**: One reviewer (R3) highlights that this is the first work to explore the large-scale binding dynamics dataset released in May 2023.
- **Physics-informed design**: The architecture incorporates Newtonian mechanics via a second-order neural ODE, and the multi-grained group symmetric framework is novel.
- **Multi-trajectory generalization**: The model shows good generalization across multiple trajectories, not only single-trajectory predictions (R3).
- **Novel problem formulation**: One reviewer (R4) considers directly learning long-timescale dynamics with a neural ODE (rather than fixed-timestep next-step prediction) an interesting idea that merits broader exploration, despite concerns with the deterministic approach.
- **Encouraging use of MISATO**: R4 appreciates the work for making use of this rich dataset for the difficult problem of protein-ligand binding.

### Weaknesses
- **Problem formulation concerns**: One reviewer (R4) strongly questions the deterministic neural ODE formulation, arguing that MD simulation is fundamentally stochastic due to thermostats, barostats, and the removal of explicit water molecules. This reviewer is skeptical that any neural ODE can faithfully capture long-timescale dynamics under these conditions. (Contrast with other reviewers who did not raise this concern.)
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**:
  - RMSF (Root Mean Square Fluctuation) baseline is missing; this is the best achievable result from a single static structure and is essential for interpreting MAE values (R4). The reported MAE for ligand coordinates (2–6 Å in single-trajectory, ~7 Å in multi-trajectory) is large, but without RMSF it is not meaningful.
  - No direct comparison with other ML methods in terms of computational efficiency (FLOPS, memory usage, wall-clock time) (R3). The speed claim is only benchmarked against one numerical MD method, not against other ML-based MD approaches (R3).
  - Ablation studies are absent: replacing BindingNet with EquiFormer or EGNN, and isolating the benefit of the multi-level representation (R2). The effectiveness of the “multi-grained group symmetric framework” is not experimentally proven (R3).
  - Evaluation is limited to a single dataset (MISATO); other datasets with different time scales or simulation conditions are not considered (R3, R1). For instance, validation on femtosecond-scale data is missing (R1).
- **Metric and experimental detail issues**:
  - It is unclear whether MAE/MSE is calculated over the entire trajectory (every time step) or only the final step (R2).
  - Stability metrics are only marginally better than DenoisingMD and do not represent a qualitative resolution of the problem (R4).
  - The integration timestep (and whether adaptive) and the number of steps required for an 8 ns simulation are not reported (R4).
  - The handling of protein movement under the “semi-flexible” assumption is not explained, even though MISATO trajectories include protein motion (R4).
  - The temporal division in the single-trajectory setting is not described (R4).
  - Training computational cost is not discussed, which is crucial for evaluating overall efficiency (R1). Scalability with system size and complexity is also not addressed (R1).
  - Experimental details are missing: simulation software, conditions (temperature, pressure, force fields), and model parameters (R3).
- **Mathematical and architectural flaws**:
  - The construction of atom-level and residue-level frames appears non-equivariant: the cross product is not translation equivariant (only rotation equivariance is proven), and the main text uses absolute positions $x_i, x_j$ while the appendix uses a different construction with a nearest-neighbor $x_k$ (R4).
  - The title’s claim of “group symmetric” is misleading, as the model only achieves SE(3)-equivariance—not a more general symmetry (R4).
  - The architecture is not a significant technical advancement over existing frame-based architectures (R4).
  - Architectural details (Eq. 5, Fig. 4) are unclear; embedding dimensionalities and symbols are not properly defined (R4). The type of message-passing and number of layers are ambiguous.
  - How BindingNet captures multi-level protein-ligand interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces) is not explained in sufficient detail (R3).
- **Lack of discussion of limitations**:
  - Potential numerical instability for long simulation times, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and ability to handle large conformational changes are not discussed (R3).
  - Real-world applications (drug discovery, protein engineering, binding affinity prediction) are not explored (R3).
- **Language quality**: The paper contains grammatical errors and spelling mistakes (R3).
- **Missing quantitative stability evidence**: One reviewer (R1) asks whether the qualitative claim of stable binding predictions can be quantitatively demonstrated—this overlaps with the weak stability gains noted by R4.
