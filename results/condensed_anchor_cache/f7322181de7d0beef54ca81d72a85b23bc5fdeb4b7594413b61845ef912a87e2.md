## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper provides a detailed developmental account of masked language models’ (MLMs) acquisition of grammatical abilities. It studies Syntactic Attention Structure (SAS)—attention heads that focus on syntactic dependencies—and measures when SAS and grammatical performance (BLiMP, GLUE) emerge during training. The authors identify a brief window in pretraining when models abruptly acquire SAS, concurrent with a steep loss drop, followed immediately by a spike in grammatical capabilities. Using a regularization loss to manipulate SAS, they find that SAS is necessary for grammatical capabilities, but that briefly suppressing SAS then lifting the suppression before the “phase transition” can improve final model quality. The paper interprets these dynamics as a real-world example of simplicity bias and breakthrough training behavior.

### Strengths

1. **Analysis across training time.** The paper provides a very detailed analysis of the emergence of specific knowledge (SAS) and skills (grammaticality judgments) over the course of pretraining, rather than only studying the final model. (R1, R2, R3)
2. **Connection between loss drops and skill acquisition.** It demonstrates that sudden drops in the MLM loss correspond to acquisition of syntactic knowledge, which then translates into grammatical performance. (R1, R2)
3. **Methodological innovation.** The use of regularization as a causal intervention on attention patterns moves the probing field forward and allows stronger claims about the role of SAS. While most reviewers view this as a strength (R1), one reviewer (R4) questions whether the intervention can support causal claims.
4. **Wide range of experiments.** The extensive experiments help isolate which features of training and measurement matter, and show that the emergence phenomena are not measurement artifacts, since they persist when training time is replaced with continuous measures. (R1, R4)
5. **Intriguing and well‑presented findings.** The research idea and results (including appendices) are intriguing, the paper is well‑written and easy to follow, and the findings have potential to stimulate further work on learning dynamics in transformers. (R2, R3)
6. **Empirical demonstration of relationship between syntax and downstream tasks.** The close relationship between the spike in SAS, grammar capability, and GLUE scores is interesting and may validate the role of syntactic structure for downstream tasks. (R4)

### Weaknesses

1. **Causal relationship not well substantiated (R4, high severity).** The intervention experiments in Sections 4.2 and 4.3 cannot rule out that a latent factor X causes both SAS and BLiMP/GLUE scores. The regularization term, while targeting SAS, could inadvertently affect other latent factors that contribute to both. Correlation alone does not imply causation, and the experimental design does not fully address confounding. This is a fundamental concern; one reviewer (R4) rates soundness as “fair” while others rate it “excellent” or “good” (R1, R3).
2. **Unclear and counterintuitive framing of simplicity bias (R2, R4, moderate severity).** The paper equates syntax‑like attention patterns with simplicity bias and calls them “simple heuristics” (Section 5.1). In the NLP literature, “simple heuristics” typically refers to undesirable reliance on surface lexical patterns (e.g., bigrams), whereas reliance on syntax is considered the opposite. The paper needs to clarify whether it means a preference for spurious features or a less complex but still valid solution, and provide evidence that the model is actually “biased” rather than simply learning SAS. (R2, R4)
3. **Single model architecture (R1, R3, moderate severity).** All experiments use only BERT‑base. It is unknown whether the observed connection between sudden loss drops and syntactic knowledge would hold at larger scales, with different random seeds, or in causally‑masked (decoder‑only) models. Reviewers suggest adding RoBERTa or evaluating on newer benchmarks. (R1, R3)
4. **Lack of clear motivation and broader significance (R4, moderate severity).** The paper does not clearly articulate why studying the development of these capabilities is important or how the findings can translate into practical improvements in model training or architecture. The study appears exploratory without a strong sense of purpose. (R4)
5. **Terminology and clarity issues (R1, R2, R4).** Several terms are imprecisely defined or used inconsistently:
   - “Capabilities” should be defined more specifically (R4).
   - “Phase transition” in Section 4.2 is used without explicit definition; it likely refers to the period between structure onset and capabilities onset (R4).
   - The discussion of the Information Bottleneck theory (Section 4.1.1) is hard to follow and its connection to the findings is unclear (R1, R4).
   - The relationship between the study’s findings and “phase transition” as a general phenomenon is not motivated (R4).
6. **Missing experiments and details (R1, R2, R3).**
   - The paper uses silver Stanford parser dependencies for WSJ data; reviewers ask why gold dependencies (from manually created trees) were not used, and whether other “data‑free” regularizers (e.g., attention sparsity) would impact SAS similarly. (R1, R2)
   - Positional encoding method is not specified; since syntactic dependencies relate to positional information, the choice of original sinusoidal vs. Rotary (RoPE) could affect results (R3).
   - The paper does not report whether code and data will be made publicly available (R1).
   - Some relevant prior work is missing: Liu et al. 2021 “Probing Across Time” and explicit citations for causal methods (R1).
7. **Minor presentation concern.** The paper makes very frequent reference to appendices, making it sometimes hard to figure out exactly what is being reported (e.g., the Information Bottleneck discussion). (R1)

### Reviewer Disagreement

There is notable disagreement across reviewers. Three reviewers rated the paper positively (8, 8, 5) and consider the findings intriguing, the methods innovative, and the contribution solid. One reviewer (R1), however, gave a score of 1 and raised concerns about causality, clarity, and missing experiments, though also acknowledged many strengths. The contrast is most stark regarding the causal interpretation of the intervention experiments: most reviewers accept the causal claims or do not raise strong objections, while R4 argues that the design does not rule out confounding. Similarly, the simplicity bias framing is seen as confusing by some but not others.
