## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PACE, an equivariant network that enhances the Atomic Cluster Expansion (ACE) framework to approximate high-degree equivariant polynomial functions. The model introduces a polynomial many-body interaction layer with theoretical guarantees (Theorem 2) and is evaluated on rMD17 and 3BPA datasets. Reviews are split: one reviewer (rating 3) finds the empirical improvement marginal and the theoretical contribution limited; two reviewers (ratings 6, 6) and one reviewer (rating 8) judge the method well-motivated and achieving strong benchmark performance.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The theoretical analysis in Section 3.3 showing the proposed message-passing scheme can approximate any equivariant polynomial function with a fixed degree is solid and well-grounded (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The model is well-motivated: it expands the ACE framework and provably surpasses previous models in the ability to represent high-degree equivariant polynomials (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Benchmark results on rMD17 and 3BPA are strong, with state-of-the-art or competitive performance (Reviewers 2, 3, 4; but see weakness from Reviewer 1 on marginal improvement).

### Weaknesses
- **Marginal empirical improvement and limited application validation**: The improvements over baselines (Tables 2, 3) are marginal, and the advantage of a NN force field should be judged on macroscopic properties or long-time large-scale MD sampling, not just energy/force errors (Reviewer 1). One reviewer notes the model is slower than MACE, reducing practical utility (Reviewer 1). In contrast, Reviewer 3 rates the benchmark results as strong (disagreement).
- **Theoretical contribution debated**: Theorem 2 shows the polynomial many-body interaction module can approximate any polynomial equivariant function, but the entire neural network is already a universal approximator for any equivariant function (e.g., via MLPs), limiting the value of the theorem (Reviewer 1). Other reviewers appreciate the theory (Reviewers 2, 4); this disagreement should be noted.
- **Missing ablation study**: The key difference from MACE is the self-interaction layers that map atomic basis \(A_i\) to different subspaces for different body orders. The paper lacks a direct ablation study analyzing this operation; it is unclear whether PACE and MACE share the same hyperparameters (e.g., those in Table 5) (Reviewer 2). A direct ablation experiment is needed.
- **Unclear logic in Section 3.1**: The sentence “While TFN has the ability to approximate all equivariant polynomial functions with an infinite number of layers, the approximation capacity of the equivariant message passing scheme is inherently limited” is not easy to follow; the logic between the two clauses should be clarified (Reviewer 2).
- **Notation and exposition clarity**: The paper should better define the equivariant nature of the \(c\) and \(A\) coefficients in Section 2.3 and provide more foreshadowing about what elements are similar/different versus ACE (Reviewer 3). The \(v\) indices are unclear: it is ambiguous whether the \(v\) in Equation 5 is the same as in Equation 7, and how \(v\) relates to atomic index \(i\) (Reviewer 3). Figure 3 is extremely helpful for parsing Equation 10; adding a second-order version of the diagram would aid understanding, and Figure 3 should be prioritized over Figure 2 (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing empirical verification of polynomial approximation**: The paper should include experiments that empirically show PACE can approximate equivariant high-degree polynomials and surpass existing works on that specific task (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing benchmark**: Other popular benchmarks like AcAc (used in Batatia et al. 2022a,b) should be included (Reviewer 4).
- **Section mislabeling**: Section 4.3 should be titled “Algorithm efficiency” rather than “Ablation study” (Reviewer 4).
- **Unanswered questions**: How does the degree \(v\) affect the error and runtime/memory cost of the model? (Reviewer 1). Given that all baselines except MACE have universal approximation ability for equivariant polynomials, why do they not achieve performance comparable to PACE? (Reviewer 2). (These are classified as weaknesses because they overlap with missing experiments or missing explanation.)
