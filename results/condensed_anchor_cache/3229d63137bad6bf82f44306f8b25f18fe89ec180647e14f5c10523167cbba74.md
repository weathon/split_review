## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents HierT2S, a framework that improves part-level text-driven 3D shape generation by embedding a hierarchical tree representation into a conditional diffusion model. The method segments text into clusters to build a hierarchical tree, processes clusters with a relation graph module using self-attention, and uses the resulting features to condition a diffusion model for shape generation. The approach is designed to better align long, structured text descriptions with part-level shape details and also supports progressive part-level shape manipulation via modified text prompts. Experiments are conducted only on the Text2Shape dataset (chairs and tables) and compare against AutoSDF, Shape-IMLE, SDFusion, and 3DQD, reporting quantitative improvements in IoU, CLIP-S, TMD, and EMD. The reviewers acknowledged the value of the problem and the reasonableness of the framework, but raised significant concerns about novelty, evaluation breadth, result quality, and insufficient analysis.

### Strengths
1. **Well-motivated problem and idea.** Multiple reviewers (R1, R2, R3, R5, R6) agreed that modeling hierarchical structure for part-level text-to-shape generation is an important direction and the idea of decomposing text into a tree structure to align with shape parts is sensible. R1 specifically liked the concept of using course-to-fine conditions for the diffusion model.
2. **Part-level shape manipulation.** The model supports localized shape edits via updated text prompts, which R1 and R2 see as a practical contribution, though R2 noted that edits are not fully part-disentangled. R6 also highlighted this as a strong point for interactive applications.
3. **Strong quantitative results on Text2Shape.** The method outperforms baselines (AutoSDF, Shape-IMLE, SDFusion) on IoU, CLIP-S, TMD, and EMD, as noted by R1, R2, R5, and R6. Ablation studies demonstrate the contribution of each module (hierarchical structure, segmentation cluster matrix, cross-attention) – a point appreciated by R3, R5, and R6.
4. **Clear writing and structure.** R4, R5, and R6 found the paper well-written and easy to follow, with logical step-by-step methodology and helpful figures (especially Figures 1 and 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty; combination of existing methods.** Several reviewers (R1, R4, R5) argued that the methodological novelty is low. R5 noted that the approach is similar to SDFusion except for text feature learning, and that the segmentation cluster matrix does not substantially improve over Tree-transformer, while the relation graph module is just attention. R1 pointed out that hierarchical modeling of parts is conceptually similar to StructureNet, PartNet, and Hierarchical Surface Prediction, making the contribution incremental. R4 and R5 stated that the paper lacks a clear rationale for specific design choices (e.g., why BERT over other PLMs, why the text parsing method is preferred).
2. **Insufficient comparisons and narrow evaluation.**
    - The method is evaluated only on the Text2Shape dataset (chairs and tables), which R3, R4, and R5 consider old and simple. R3 and R4 suggest testing on newer datasets like ABO, Objaverse, or ShapeTalk (the latter also noted by R2).
    - Missing comparison with existing hierarchical text-to-shape methods: R5 explicitly lists ShapeScaffolder, ShapeCrafter, and HyperSDFusion. R6 also notes a lack of comparison with recent part-level or modular methods.
    - No comparison with methods that produce textured 3D objects (e.g., TAPS3D, Wonder3D, Unique3D), as raised by R4.
    - Missing experiments on long text descriptions: R5 points out that the abstract claims the method addresses long text, but no such experiments are shown.
    - No training time or computational efficiency comparison, as requested by R4 and R6.
3. **Low-quality generation results.** R3 and R6 observe that generated shapes have fuzzy geometry details and minor artifacts, especially at part transitions. R3 notes that sampling only 2,048 points may explain the loss of fine details (e.g., back strip of a chair) and suggests using 8,192 points. R6 also mentions that visual coherence is sometimes degraded by artifacts.
4. **Part-disentanglement not achieved in edits.** R2 shows a clear example: adding armrests to a chair also changes leg thickness, back width, and shape. R2 references two prior works (arXiv:2112.06390, arXiv:2212.05011) that achieve natural part-disentanglement and asks whether their regularizations apply here.
5. **Lack of analysis and visualization of hierarchical structure.** R3, R5, and R4 call for visual examples of the learned hierarchical trees to understand how text clusters map to shape parts. R4 questions whether the extracted trees have meaningful variance given the simple “{Adj.} {Noun.}” format of ShapeNet captions. R3 also asks for in-depth analysis of how part-level node connections affect final generation.
6. **Robustness and handling of complex prompts.** R1 questions the method’s performance with noisy, ambiguous, or very complex text descriptions (e.g., nested descriptors). R6 similarly asks for examples and techniques to handle such cases, suggesting dynamic clustering as an alternative. R1 further asks whether using synthetic captions from 2D captioning models (e.g., TAPS3D, TPA3D) would break the text parsing network.
7. **Missing metric definitions.** R5 notes that TMD and EMD are used on page 8 without definitions or references.
8. **Disagreement among reviewers:** While R2 and R6 rated the paper higher (score 6) and highlighted strong empirical results and clear writing, they also raised specific concerns (part-disentanglement, artifacts). R1 (score 5) appreciated the idea but found the novelty lacking and the experiments too narrow. R3, R4, and R5 (score 3) were more critical, citing limited novelty, insufficient evaluation, low-quality results, and missing comparisons as major weaknesses.
