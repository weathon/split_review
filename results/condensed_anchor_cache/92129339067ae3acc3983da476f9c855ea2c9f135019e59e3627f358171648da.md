## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviews collectively recognize that the paper proposes a new stochastic matching bandit framework that incorporates Multinomial Logit (MNL) choice behavior on the arm side, including an outside option, and uses feature information. The main algorithm, SMB (elimination-based with three phases), is analyzed for regret, and an approximate version addresses computational hardness. Reviewers found the model setting well-motivated and relevant for real-world applications like ride-hailing and online labor markets. However, there is strong disagreement on novelty and significance: one reviewer (R4) viewed the extensions as trivial and the algorithm as a minor modification of prior work, while others (R1, R3, R5) considered the stochastic arm behavior a genuine advance. Other common criticisms include the disconnect between the MNL preference learning and the objective, the unusual \(K\sqrt{K}\) regret dependency, lack of lower bounds, unsuitable baselines, insufficient experimental demonstration, and missing analysis of the approximation oracle. Most reviewers rated the paper as marginal (scores 5, 5, 6, 3, 5; decision Reject).

### Strengths
- The paper introduces a novel matching bandit framework where arms stochastically choose among assigned agents (including an outside option) according to an MNL model with features. This extends prior deterministic-matching works and is well-motivated for applications such as ride-hailing and online job markets.
- An elimination-based algorithm (SMB) is developed with three phases (estimation, elimination, exploration). The regret bound is proved as \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{dK^3 T}/\kappa)\), with a detailed theoretical analysis that appears correct.
- The paper addresses computational issues by proposing an \(\alpha\)-approximation oracle version (Algorithm 3) and discusses its regret implications.
- The writing is clear, with a good presentation of related work and the mathematical model.
- The model allows for probabilistic rejection and preference learning, which is a meaningful departure from deterministic arm behavior in earlier matching bandits.

### Weaknesses
1. **Disconnect between MNL model and objective** (R2, supported by R4): The objective only cares about the expected number of successful matches (any match), not which specific agents are matched. Since the MNL model learns preferences between agents and arms, this objective does not use the learned preferences, undermining the motivation for the model. The role of the MNL is unclear if the goal is simply to fill slots.
2. **Questionable regret bound dependency** (R2, R3, R5): The regret bound includes a factor of \(K\sqrt{K}\) (i.e., \(\sqrt{K^3}\)), which is unusual in bandit literature; typical bandit papers have \(\sqrt{K}\) scaling. Reviewers questioned whether this is unavoidable or an artifact of the proof, and a tightness (lower bound) analysis is missing. R3 also asked about the \(\kappa\) term and whether it can be removed using recent GLM confidence sequences (Lee et al., 2024).
3. **Unsuitable baselines** (R2, R3, R4): The experiments only compare against ETC-GS and UCB-GS, which are designed for a deterministic matching objective. Since the target dynamics are fundamentally different, the comparison is not fair. R5 additionally suggested that the CTS algorithm (Wang & Chen, ICML 2018) should be compared. No comparison with deterministic-preference baselines is provided.
4. **Limited novelty and technical contributions** (R3, R4, somewhat R2, R5): 
   * The extension to a null-agent/outside option is considered trivial by R4, as many previous models could incorporate it. 
   * The elimination-based algorithm closely follows prior work (Lattimore & Szepesvári), with minor modifications; the theoretical analysis also follows existing frameworks. 
   * The \(\alpha\)-approximation oracle is a common technique (Kakade et al., 2007), and the approximate version only achieves \(\alpha^2\)-approximate regret.
   * R3 noted that the SMB algorithm itself is not efficient, and the approximate version's exact \(\alpha\) value is not given.
5. **Weak experimental demonstration** (R1, R4, R5): 
   * Experiments use only uniformly distributed features and do not demonstrate learning of arm performances. 
   * The approximate oracle is not tested or shown to improve computation time. 
   * There is no experiment with a real-world dataset, which would better support the claim of practical relevance.
   * R4 asked for a comparison with deterministic-preference settings to show the impact of stochastic choice.
6. **Missing lower bound** (R2, R5): The paper claims a tight bound but provides no lower bound to assess the upper bound quantitatively. R5 suggests that Merlis et al. (2020) could help analyze the lower bound for MNL.
7. **Unclear/insufficient handling of model extensions** (R2, R4, R5):
   * Collision (two arms choosing the same agent) is not considered; R1 raised this as a potential issue.
   * The parameter \(\kappa\) is global and simplistic; a more granular \(\kappa_{ij}\) per agent-arm pair would be more realistic (R5).
   * R4 questioned the use of SVD (decomposition \(X = U\Sigma V^T\)) without clear definition from the inputs.
   * R4 asked whether the algorithm can handle time-varying preferences.
8. **Monotonicity assumption and generality** (R3, R4): The algorithm relies on a monotone property of the reward function (increasing an arm's \(x_n^T\theta_k\) increases reward). If this is absent (e.g., maximizing average score), the method may not apply; R3 asked what approximate oracles would be possible then. R4 noted that MNL is a restriction, and real preference probabilities may be more general.
9. **Presentation and proof clarity** (R1, R3): The proof sketch for Theorem 1 is long and could be revised to highlight novelty and how the three main-stage steps contribute to the regret. R3 pointed out minor typos: in Assumption 2, should be \(\inf_{\|\theta\|_2 \le 1}\); in Eq. (4), it should be \(R^{LCB}\) not \(R^{LBC}\).
10. **Computational complexity not fully addressed** (R5, R1): The paper does not adequately discuss how the algorithm scales with \(N\) and \(K\), and the approximation oracle's trade-off in solution quality is not explored.

**Reviewer disagreement**: R4 was notably more negative, considering the model extensions trivial and the algorithm a simple adaptation. R1 and R3 were more positive, endorsing the new setting and proof. R2 and R5 were critical of specific technical choices and experiment design.
