## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Direct Ranking Preference Optimization (DRPO), which reframes LLM preference alignment as a Learning-to-Rank task by directly optimizing a differentiable approximation of NDCG (diffNDCG) using sorting networks (Petersen et al. 2021). DRPO replaces the pairwise preference objective in DPO with a listwise ranking loss, incorporating an Adaptive Rank Policy Score (ARPS) that adjusts margins based on response positions. Experiments on HH-RLHF, UltraFeedback, and VLFeedback show improvements over DPO and other baselines on reward model win rates and GPT-4 evaluations. Code and models are provided.

### Strengths

- **Novel framing and motivation.** Several reviewers note the contribution of treating preference alignment as a listwise ranking problem and using NDCG to capture the importance of top-ranked responses, aligning better with human preference structure than pairwise methods. The use of log-likelihoods as gains in NDCG is seen as an interesting idea.
- **Technically sound approach.** The use of differentiable sorting networks to approximate NDCG is technically correct, and the combination with the Adaptive Rank Policy Score enables end-to-end training.
- **Strong empirical results.** The method shows significant performance gains over existing baselines (DPO, iterative DPO, KTO, SimPO) across multiple model sizes (up to Mistral-7B) and datasets, as measured by RM win rates and GPT-4 win rates. The code and models are publicly available.
- **Comprehensive analysis.** An ablation study examines the impact of the Adaptive Rank Policy Score and different discount factors; the authors also validate applying the ARP score to other methods. Previous reviewer concerns (choice of discount factors, comparison with other differentiable approximations) have been addressed.

**Minority contrast:** One reviewer (score 3, confidence 4) finds the claimed improvements extraordinary and questions whether they are meaningful, noting that the gains on Mistral-7B are less significant and that the evaluation methodology (ground truth from reward models, not humans) may capture biases. The same reviewer views the differentiable NDCG contribution as incremental (a straightforward application of sorting networks from Petersen et al. 2021).

### Weaknesses

1. **Evaluation methodology and evidence gaps**
   - The paper argues that “win rate” is more correlated with NDCG, but the supporting evidence (Figure 4, Table 11) is limited: Figure 4 is conditioned on DRPO itself, so it does not validate correlation across methods. Table 11 lacks correlation with the pairwise approach used in DPO, and Kendall Tau would be more appropriate for comparing ranked lists. The discussion of LambdaRank (a well-established LTR loss based on pairwise comparisons) is missing, and the connection to DPO plus lambda weights is not addressed.
   - Ground truth rankings are derived from other models (DeBERTa, GPT-4), not human evaluators. Biases in the evaluation model may propagate to DRPO; if the evaluation model is changed, numbers could change unpredictably. This limitation appears only in Appendix B and should be stated upfront.
   - The paper lacks real-world human evaluation of generated responses to supplement reward model proxies.
   - No statistical significance tests or confidence intervals are reported for key results (Table 2, Table 3), making it difficult to assess whether improvements are meaningful beyond random variation.

2. **Model misspecification and bias concerns**
   - The transformation from scores to probabilities (via the scoring function) is not explicitly defined or justified. Different choices could lead to significantly different ranking outcomes, and sensitivity to this transformation is not discussed.
   - diffNDCG could amplify position bias or selection bias if the scoring function is miscalibrated or weights higher positions based on position rather than relevance. The paper does not discuss mitigation strategies for such biases, despite a large literature on ranking bias.
   - The Adaptive Rank Policy Score (Equation 5) uses moving averages of scores for each position, which may fail under distribution shift (e.g., aligning over different types of tasks or questions). This assumption is not analyzed.

3. **Clarity, writing, and presentation issues**
   - The description and motivation for the Adaptive Rank Policy Score (Equations 4–5) could be clearer; the paper goes back and forth between Section 3 and results, making sections not self-contained.
   - Tables (especially Table 2 and Table 3) do not stand alone: acronyms (DRPO, ARP, etc.) are not defined in notes, and it is not immediately clear whether higher values indicate better performance. Figures are dense with very small fonts (e.g., Figure 3).
   - Excessive passive voice (e.g., “have demonstrated,” “have been proposed”) reduces readability.
   - Notation imprecision: in Equation (5), \(V_{q(y)}\) appears on both sides, reducing the quantity to a linear combination of \(\log\pi\); iteration indexing is needed.

4. **Novelty and contribution concerns (minority view)**
   - One reviewer (R3) questions the novelty of the diffNDCG loss, calling it a straightforward application of sorting networks (Petersen et al. 2021) to NDCG optimization. The main contribution is the scoring approach (ARPS), but its benefits over simpler alternatives are not rigorously justified.
   - A previously requested discussion of novelty against LiPO (listwise preference optimization) is missing. The authors only mention that “diffNDCG can utilize list data more effectively than existing methods” but do not address the “reward hacking challenge” or why diffNDCG is a proper solution.
   - The reason DRPO outperforms other differentiable ranking approaches (e.g., PiRank) so significantly is unclear—whether due to better differentiable quality, specific experimental settings, or small model sizes (Qwen 0.5B, 1.8B, with less significant gains on Mistral-7B).

5. **Experimental details and reproducibility**
   - The experimental setup is not fully transparent: it is unclear whether clicks/data are simulated, what the validation data is (size, source, characteristics), and which specific parameters are used for training (e.g., alpha in diffNDCG is critical as it controls the temperature trade-off; comparisons with PiRank require specifying their temperature).
   - Table 1 contains imprecision: entries labeled “ListNet,” “PiRank,” “Neural Sort” are actually PPR and ARP methods using those subroutines as subroutines (line 406 states both “PPR and ARP”). This needs explicit distinction.
   - The choice of the odd-even sorting network is justified only by simplicity; other sorting networks with better time complexity are mentioned but not compared.
   - Reliance on scalar scores (e.g., using DeBERTa for HH-RLHF) raises the question of whether using GPT-4 or a larger model would change learning behavior.

6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and generalizability**
   - The paper provides no guide for setting hyperparameters (especially those for ARPS and the sorting network), even though policy optimization can be unstable.
   - The experiments focus on three specific datasets; generalizability to other preference datasets (e.g., more diverse tasks, dynamic preferences) is not discussed.
   - It appears that LiPO’s RM win rate grows faster with list size than DRPO (Figure 3); the gap may close or reverse at larger list sizes, but this is not examined.
