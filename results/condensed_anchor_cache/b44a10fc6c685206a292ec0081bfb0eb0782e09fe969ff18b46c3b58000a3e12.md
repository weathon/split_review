## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the adequacy of multiple-choice questions (MCQs) for evaluating LLMs in medical contexts by constructing a fictional medical benchmark (the non‑existent Glianorex gland). GPT‑4 generated a textbook and corresponding MCQs in English and French; human experts verified the content and provided a performance baseline. Several LLMs (proprietary, open‑source, domain‑specific; 7B–110B parameters) were tested zero‑shot. Models achieved ~67% accuracy (above chance), with small differences between model sizes and languages, and fine‑tuned medical models improved in English but not French. The authors interpret the results as evidence that traditional MCQ benchmarks may measure pattern‑recognition / test‑taking ability rather than genuine clinical reasoning. The study is novel in its approach and has potentially important implications for AI evaluation methodology, but significant methodological concerns limit its contribution: the LLM‑generated pipeline creates a closed loop, the claim of isolating LLMs from pre‑existing knowledge is overstated, and the work largely revalidates already known limitations of LLM reasoning.

### Strengths
- **Novel and creative experimental design**: The fictional gland (Glianorex) is an innovative way to control for memorized real‑world knowledge, and the textbook is generated in a comprehensive, detailed manner (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Thorough model coverage**: Evaluation includes a wide range of models – proprietary (e.g., GPT‑4), open‑source (Mistral, Llama), and domain‑specific medical models – across scales (7B–110B) and in both English and French, offering cross‑linguistic insight (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Human expert involvement**: Experts validated the generated medical content and questions and provided a human baseline, making the experiments accountable and trustworthy (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Doctors found no major flaws in the generated QA (Reviewer 1).
- **Solid statistical analysis**: The use of Cohen’s d effect sizes and significance testing provides quantitative backing; code is openly available (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Clear motivation and well‑framed implications**: The inadequacy of MCQ‑based evaluation in high‑stakes clinical settings is a significant and timely issue. The study’s core concept – testing reasoning gaps with fictional content – is important for patient safety (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Transparency about limitations**: Authors acknowledge limitations of their approach (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Overstatement that fictional content removes pre‑existing data influence**: Several reviewers note this claim is central yet problematic.
    - The LLM’s prior knowledge about *other* organs/glands and general anatomy can still inform answers (Reviewer 1).
    - The LLM‑generated pipeline (textbook → questions → answers) is a closed loop that primarily tests LLM‑to‑LLM pattern recognition (Reviewer 2).
    - The fictional content still adheres to real medical frameworks (e.g., how a gland functions), so “entirely fictional medical knowledge” is an overstatement – prior foundational knowledge remains embedded (Reviewer 3).
    - The LLMs used to generate the synthetic data may have imputed real knowledge into the fictional domain (Reviewer 4).
    (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Limited novelty beyond well‑established findings**: Numerous studies already demonstrate that LLMs lack true reasoning (GSM8K, Physics of Language Models, lexical substitution benchmarking). This study revalidates that observation without advancing understanding (Reviewer 2) and makes a limited case for contribution (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparisons and missed analytical opportunities**:
    - No comparison between performance on fictional MCQs vs. real medical benchmarks like USMLE (Reviewer 2).
    - No comparison to human‑generated alternative versions (e.g., with drug‑name substitutions) (Reviewer 2).
    - No analysis of which questions models got right/wrong (e.g., by subtopic, difficulty, or similarity to errors in prior work) (Reviewers 1, 4).
    - No direct comparison of human answers with model answers, despite using only 100 questions – the human‑tested subset may be harder (Reviewer 4).
    - No use of answer alignment metrics such as Cohen’s kappa; only performance differences and a hard‑to‑interpret figure on answer distribution (Reviewer 4).
    - No investigation of what specific patterns in medical MCQs make them vulnerable to exploitation by LLMs, which would be more useful than merely confirming pattern matching (Reviewer 2).
- **Methodological gaps in experimental design**:
    - Positional/selection bias for option order (e.g., favoring option A) is not addressed; it could significantly affect performance (Reviewer 3).
    - Unclear whether the textbook was available during testing or only used for QA generation, and whether questions provided context (Reviewer 4).
    - Limited information on how the textbook content was steered (prompts used), input uniformity across models given different context windows, and paragraph lengths used for question generation (Reviewer 1).
    - Only a single fictional topic (Glianorex) is tested; it is unclear if results generalise to other domains (Reviewer 4).
- **Statistical analysis concerns**:
    - Discrepancy between near‑negligible effect sizes and visible performance differences in Figure 1 is not explained (Reviewer 3).
    - Details of SEM computation (mean over what?), human error bar calculation, and two‑way ANOVA factors are missing (Reviewer 4).
- **Discussion quality and clarity issues**:
    - Terms like “inferential strategies” are uncommon and need elaboration (Reviewer 3).
    - The paragraph on language in the Discussion is unclear (Reviewer 3).
    - Figure 2 (answer distribution) is not informative; x‑axes should have same scale (Reviewer 4).
- **No proposed solution**: The paper highlights problems with MCQ evaluation but does not offer or develop a method to mitigate the issue, reducing impact (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation flaws and incomplete sentences**:
    - Line 457: missing text. Line 485: incomplete sentence (Reviewer 1).
    - “Knowledge coherence” and “Model selection” paragraphs in Limitations are unfinished (e.g., ends with “This selection”) (Reviewer 3).
