## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Self-Evolution Continual Reinforcement Learning (SE-CRL), a setting where the agent's action space continually changes. To address this, the authors introduce ARC-RL, which learns an action representation space via self-supervised learning (reconstruction loss) on transitions, then decodes to the current action set. The decoder is expanded or masked upon action space changes and fine-tuned with an EWC-style regularization. Experiments are conducted on MiniGrid (Empty grid) and Procgen (Bigfish) environments.

### Strengths

- The problem of continual learning with varying action spaces is interesting and valuable for research. (R1, R2, R4)
- The idea of learning action representation space via self-supervised learning is simple and yields useful benefits for training deep neural networks. (R1)
- The regularized fine-tuning approach combining self-supervised loss and elastic weight consolidation (EWC) is new and useful in continual RL to retain past learnings. (R1)
- The writing is very clear, with clear schematic diagrams, experimental presentation, and comprehensible formulas. (R2, R3, R4)
- The SE-CRL problem is formalized with a precise and standardized problem statement. (R3)
- Source code is included. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and related work deficiencies** (R1: high severity, high confidence; R4: high severity, high confidence; R3: moderate severity): The self-supervised action representation learning is not new; it was introduced in Chandak et al. (ICML 2019) [1]. Applying it to changing action spaces in continual RL is also not novel, as the same authors extended it in Chandak et al. (AAAI 2020) [2], which is very close to ARC-RL and theoretically grounded. The paper does not adequately distinguish its approach from these works, nor use [2] as a baseline. The claim of being the first to address changing action spaces is inaccurate; [2] and [3] (Khetarpal et al. JAIR 2022) have discussed this subproblem. Additionally, Jain et al. (ICML 2020, ICLR 2021), Tennenholtz & Mannor (ICML 2019), and Farquhar et al. (ICML 2020) are missing from related work. (R1, R4, R3). The problem with changes only in action space is a subset of problems also extending state space (e.g., Wei Ding's paper) (R3). The novelty of Eq.6 (self-supervised loss + EWC) is insufficiently highlighted, appearing incremental (R1, R4). The regularized fine-tuning shows minimal impact: Table 1 indicates the regularization term has little effect on forgetting and forward transfer metrics (R2, moderate confidence). Therefore, the method does not demonstrate significant performance improvements over baselines; reported improvements are marginal, especially in simpler environments (R2, moderate confidence).

- **Insufficient and problematic experimental validation** (R1: high severity, high confidence; R2: high severity, moderate confidence; R4: high severity, high confidence; R3: moderate severity): Experiments are only on MiniGrid (Empty) and Procgen (Bigfish), both very simple. Only action expansion and contraction are tested, not all four categories from Sec 2.2. The authors should construct 4-5 discrete action space scenarios (including other MiniGrid and Procgen environments) and 2-3 continuous action space scenarios (e.g., Ant with varying leg counts 4/6/8). (R1, R2). The conclusions do not match the plots: In Fig 4 (expansion), ARC-RL already achieves optimal performance on tasks 2 and 3 when trained on task 1, with no further improvement from new actions, raising doubt about whether the action representation effectively leverages expanded spaces (R1, high confidence). In Fig 5 (contraction), performance on task 2 decreases when action space drops from 7 to 5, but stays unchanged when later dropping to 3 – this inconsistent behavior is not explained (R1, high confidence). The claim that an expanded action space can discover more optimal solutions is not supported by Fig 4 or other results; additional evidence or analysis is needed (R3, moderate confidence). There is no description of baselines or hyperparameter tuning; some baselines offer little value. The authors should include a baseline using all actions and the method from [2] (Chandak et al. AAAI 2020) (R1, high confidence). Figures 4 and 5 lack detailed experimental timing: Was 0-3M in Fig 4b trained? Was a checkpoint of task1 used for test? Why is the purple curve different? For Fig 4c, only 6-9M were trained; what happened during 0-6M (FT?) (R3, moderate confidence). The experimental setting needs more explanation, including exploration vs. learning stages and how the action set changes sequentially (R4, high confidence). (Note: R3's questions about training/test phases are included here as they point to missing details.)

- **Missing key details and questions that overlap with criticisms** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
  - The paper incorrectly states "assumes agent's capabilities remain static" in CRL; actually, continual adaptation is the desiderata (R1, cites [4],[5]).
  - No summary of task descriptions (how actions change from task to task) is provided in the main paper (R1).
  - In Sec 3.2 (just before Eq.4), the double union symbol is not explained (R1).
  - In Fig 2 caption, "the probability distribution is relatively stable" is unclear (R1).
  - The paper fails to discuss that when the action space changes, the transition function also changes (since transition is S×A → Δ(S)), which is an oversight limiting improvements (R2, moderate confidence).
  - The proposed approach (learning action representation with decoder mapping to action probabilities) is not compared to an alternative: mapping action representation to the action directly, as in Chandak et al. (ICML 2019) (R4).
  - Minor: "new neurons are initialized randomly" should be "parameters" or "weights" (R3).
  - Simpler approaches exist (e.g., directly adding/masking output layer neurons and training only the last layer), but methods like FT (Fine-Tuning) and Mask are not formally defined in the text (R3).
