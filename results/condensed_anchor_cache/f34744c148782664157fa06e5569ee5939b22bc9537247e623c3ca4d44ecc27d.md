## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a conditionally activated anomaly detection mechanism for federated learning that uses cross-round and cross-client detection (based on cosine similarity and deviation from reference models) to identify and remove malicious client models. It incorporates zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) for clients to verify the server’s honest execution of the removal process. Evaluated on benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR-10, MNIST) against label flipping, backdoor, and some Byzantine attacks, the method aims to be practical by activating defense only when attacks are detected and minimizing accuracy loss in benign settings.

### Strengths
- The paper is well written, well organized, and easy to follow (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The method efficiently removes malicious client models when an attack occurs, without negatively impacting the local models of benign clients (Reviewer 2). It also aims to minimize accuracy loss when no attackers are present (Reviewer 1).
- The idea of using zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) for verifying server integrity in FL defense is interesting (Reviewer 4). This enables clients to check the trustworthiness of the aggregator, which can be applicable in scenarios where the aggregator is not fully trusted (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Evaluations are conducted in a real-world setting (Reviewer 3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 (score 6) is substantially more positive than the other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3), who raise significant concerns about the method’s assumptions, evaluation scope, and missing comparisons.

### Weaknesses
- **Assumption that malicious updates are outliers is weak:** The method relies on the assumption that malicious local models deviate significantly from reference models (previous global model, client’s local model). This only holds against weak attacks. Advanced attacks (e.g., LIE, MinMax, MinSum, 3DFed, Bhagoji et al., Fung et al., Fang et al., distributed backdoor) can produce minimal differences between malicious and benign models, making detection extremely challenging. The authors neither discuss these attacks in the Related Work nor evaluate against them (Reviewers 1, 3). The cross-client detection based on cosine similarity is particularly limited against subtle poisoning strategies (Reviewer 1).
- **Outdated and insufficient attack/defense evaluation:** The paper evaluates only four weak Byzantine and backdoor attacks (all published before 2020), and label flipping is not a backdoor attack. On the defense side, only five weak and outdated baselines are considered. Advanced baselines such as FLTrust, FLDetector, and other state-of-the-art detection methods (e.g., Li et al., Muñoz-González et al., Zhang et al.) are not included (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). There is no table clearly comparing baselines under both IID and non-IID settings (Reviewer 4).
- **Unrealistic default scenario and scalability concerns:** Most experiments use only 10 clients, which is unrealistic for real-world FL (which often involves hundreds or thousands of participants). The default of 10 clients is claimed to correspond to real-world applications, but this is unsupported (e.g., Gboard uses much larger numbers). While Figure 4(d) explores different client counts, the overall confidence in the method’s effectiveness is limited (Reviewers 1, 3). Storing reference models from all participants in cross-device settings with hundreds of thousands of clients is computationally expensive and not addressed (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing critical evaluations:**  
  - No analysis of global model accuracy in non-adversarial settings (Reviewer 3).  
  - No evaluation of defense effectiveness under attacker-dominated conditions (Reviewer 3).  
  - No study of varying levels of data heterogeneity (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The assumption that local model and reference model similarity is high appears to hold only under IID; under non-IID, similarity can be low even for benign clients (Reviewer 4). The justification of the 3-sigma rule for anomaly detection under non-IID distributions is insufficient (Reviewer 1).  
  - No ablation study or experimental analysis of robustness against adaptive attacks (Reviewers 1, 3).  
  - Results in Figures 4(c,d) and Figure 5 do not show performance after convergence; more training rounds are needed (Reviewer 1).  
  - No analysis of how the threshold sensitivity affects performance (Reviewer 1 question).
- **No theoretical guarantees:** The paper provides no theorems or convergence analysis to guarantee defensive performance or explain why cross-round and cross-client detection are effective (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Vulnerability from computational complexity optimization:** Section 3.3 reduces complexity by selecting only one layer for reference models. Attackers can then freely modify parameters from other layers and bypass detection; how the defense handles this is unclear (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited scope to horizontal FL:** The study only addresses horizontal FL, with no mention of possible limitations or applicability to vertical FL (Reviewer 2).
- **Open questions on client selection and reference model validity:** When a client is not selected for several rounds, its model update may differ greatly from the stored reference, potentially triggering false anomalies. The paper does not define how long a reference model remains valid (Reviewer 1 question).
