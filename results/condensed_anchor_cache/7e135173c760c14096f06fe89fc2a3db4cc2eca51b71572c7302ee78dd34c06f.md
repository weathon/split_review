## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a counterfactual treatment optimization framework for irregularly-sampled, confounded observational data in continuous time. It models outcomes as a marked temporal point process (Hawkes process) with discrete latent states capturing hidden confounding. The pipeline comprises: (1) an EM algorithm to infer latent states and noise, (2) counterfactual sampling of outcome events under new decision rules, and (3) gradient-based policy learning. Experiments on one synthetic dataset and one real-world dataset (MIMIC-III) are reported.

### Strengths
- Tackles a challenging, under-addressed problem: time-varying off-policy learning with irregularly-sampled, confounded observational data (R1, R2, R4).
- Integrates temporal point processes (Hawkes process) to rigorously model event sequences (R2, R3).
- Provides theoretical identifiability proofs for model parameters in the presence of latent states (R2).  
- The probabilistic formulation is flexible and potentially allows for uncertainty quantification, supporting both counterfactual prediction and policy learning (R4).
- The Hawkes process + EM algorithm is intuitive and natural, and the paper contributes to a field with little prior work (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
**Missing causal assumptions and identification**
- The paper does not clearly state which causal framework it assumes (e.g., non-Markovian potential outcomes, MDP) or what assumptions (consistency, positivity, ignorability, no unobserved confounders) are required (R1, R4).
- No derivation is given for identification of the target causal quantity – the policy value under intervention (Theorem 1 identifies latent variables alone, not counterfactual outcomes) (R1, R4).  
- The do‑operator in Eq. (7) is used without defining an appropriate SCM or potential outcomes; conditioning is only on latent states \(z\), not on observed history, which contradicts the paper’s stated goal of modifying treatments given observed trajectories (R4).  
- The method does not account for observed time-varying confounders common in medicine, which would require specialized adjustment (R4). Reviewer 4 notes that ignoring these confounders severely limits practical applicability.  
- It remains unclear whether the paper targets interventional or counterfactual quantities; the latter (intervening on past treatments) requires stronger assumptions such as invertibility of latent noise (R1).

**Lack of baselines and comparisons**
- The synthetic benchmark includes no baselines despite many relevant methods being cited in the related work (R1).  
- No comparison with other off‑policy optimization methods (e.g., off‑policy RL or causal inference) is provided, making it difficult to assess relative performance (R2).

**Incomplete empirical evaluation**
- For the MIMIC‑III real‑world dataset, quantitative analyses like convergence plots and reward comparisons (analogous to Fig. 3) are missing (R2).  
- The number and timing of latent states is assumed known in simulation but not explained for the real data; no sensitivity analysis varying the number of latent states is conducted (R3).  
- Reviewer 3 suggests adding an additional simulation baseline: “pretending we observe latent states and then directly applying the decision rule optimization” (in addition to the “without latent states” baseline already included).

**Parametric modeling concerns**
- The parametric linear intensity model (Eq. 3) is highly restrictive; in data‑rich ICU settings with complex influences, such modeling is untrustworthy (R3). Need justification from literature or a simulation study investigating mild, moderate, and severe violations of the assumption (R3).  
- The model does not incorporate marks (e.g., dosage, disease progression) in the intensity or likelihood (Eq. 8–9); the likelihood of marks \(m_j\) disappears, yet marks should influence the intensity and contribute to the likelihood (R3). This raises questions about the gradient update computed without marks (R3).  
- The heavy reliance on parametric assumptions limits flexibility (R4). Reviewer 3 suggests relaxing the model, e.g., keeping only the latent‑state part linear and using non‑parametric history functions, citing Rytgaard et al. for fully non‑parametric continuous‑time identification.

**Identifiability and EM algorithm issues**
- Theorem 1 assumes the latent variables \(z\) are identified, but it is unclear how to guarantee this in practice (R4).  
- The EM algorithm routinely suffers from convergence and identification issues; the assumptions behind Theorem 1 (from Bonnet et al. 2023) are not clearly presented. They need discussion on when they hold or fail, how to interpret them (especially for the real‑data application), whether they can be verified in simulations, and whether sensitivity analysis is possible (R3).

**Notation, presentation, and clarity**
- Multiple notations are undefined or confusing: \(\phi(t-s)\) and triggering functions; \(x\) in line 208; Fig. 1 shows \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) as distinct nodes (should \(H_0\) be part of \(H_1\)?); “mixture distribution” in line 152 is ambiguous (a mixture of categorical variables is still categorical) (R1).  
- The do‑operator in line 212 lacks a mathematical illustration of how the intervention \(do(H_a(T)=H'_a(T')|...)\) modifies the temporal point process (R2).  
- Notation switches: dosage is first \(x\) (Sec. 3.2) then \(m_{d,k}\) (Sec. 6); it is unclear whether \(z(t) = [z_k]_{k \le t}\) (R3).  
- The term “baseline” in Fig. 3 is not defined (R3).  
- The method works with “meta‑rules” rather than learning a fully data‑driven policy; it is unclear how to handle scenarios where meta‑rules are unavailable (R2, R4).

**Other questions functioning as criticisms / missing ablations**
- What if the number of latent factors \(K\) is unknown or the latent states are not categorical – can identifiability still hold? (R2)  
- Why are only the timings of events modeled but not the marks? (R3)  
- How are the decision rule parameters initialized in Step 1 of Sec. 6? (R3)  
- Is the gradient update in Sec. 6 computed without marks? (R3)  
- What is the practical intuition for counterfactual interventions on past treatments (conditioning and intervening on the same treatments)? Should regular future interventions suffice? (R1)

**Reviewer disagreement**
One reviewer (R2, score 5) was notably more positive, emphasizing the strong theoretical guarantees and practical applicability. Other reviewers (R1, score 3; R3, score 3; R4, score 3) questioned the sufficiency of the identification proof for counterfactual quantities, the restrictive modeling assumptions, and the lack of baselines and clear assumptions. The paper was rejected overall.
