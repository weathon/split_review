## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DUCAT (Dummy Classes-based Adversarial Training), a new AT paradigm that introduces an additional dummy class per original class to relax the overstrict assumption that clean and adversarial samples must be classified into the same category. DUCAT uses soft labels and is designed as a plug-and-play module for existing AT methods. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet evaluate DUCAT combined with PGD-AT, TRADES, MART, and Consistency-AT. Reviewers agree the motivation (revealing that up to 40% of adversarial samples fail under current AT) is strong and the paper is well-written. However, they raise concerns about limited gains over TRADES, incremental novelty relative to prior dummy-class and label-smoothing works, misleading claims about “breaking the trade-off,” lack of transfer attack analysis, unclear notations, and insufficient justification for design choices. One reviewer (score 5) is more critical of the paper’s claims and novelty; the other three (score 6) acknowledge merits but cite substantive weaknesses.

### Strengths
- **Strong motivation and problem identification**: The paper reveals that up to 40% of CIFAR-10 adversarial samples are always misclassified across various AT methods and robust models, explicitly demonstrating the deficiency of the overstrict assumption that clean and adversarial samples must share the same label. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Well-written and easy to follow**: The paper is clearly organized, making the intuition behind dummy classes accessible. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Plug-and-play applicability**: DUCAT can be integrated into existing AT frameworks (PGD-AT, TRADES, MART, Consistency-AT) without major architectural changes. (Reviewer 2)
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Experiments span three datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet) and multiple baseline methods and architectures (e.g., ResNet-18). (Reviewer 3)
- **Intuitive concept**: The idea of dummy classes to relax the clean-robust label conflict is intuitive and sensible. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental improvement over TRADES and limited novelty**:  
  - On CIFAR-10, DUCAT sacrifices clean accuracy more than the robustness gains it provides compared to TRADES, which already addresses the trade-off. On CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet, improvements are marginal, while additional computational resources are required (Figure 6). (Reviewer 1)  
  - Multiple reviewers note that the use of dummy classes and label smoothing (soft labels) has been explored in prior work [1–4], including label smoothing for adversarial robustness, dummy risk minimization, and one-versus-the-rest losses. The paper does not adequately clarify its unique contribution relative to these works. (Reviewers 2, 4)  
  - The one-versus-the-rest loss in [4] shares conceptual similarity with DUCAT’s approach. (Reviewer 4)  
  - The reasons for specific design choices (e.g., number of dummy classes, soft label construction) are not justified. (Reviewer 2)

- **Claim of “breaking the inherent trade-off” is misleading**:  
  - Clean accuracy under DUCAT is still significantly lower than standard training (e.g., ResNet-18 standard training >93% on CIFAR-10). The paper should rephrase to describe reducing the loss of clean accuracy while improving robustness, and should include standard training accuracy as a reference. (Reviewer 4)  
  - This concern is rated as severe by Reviewer 4 (score 5), who is more negative than the other three reviewers.

- **Performance far from state-of-the-art AT methods**:  
  - The absolute clean and robust accuracies achieved by DUCAT-enhanced AT baselines remain far below advanced methods that use diffusion models or more sophisticated data augmentation. Even as a plug-and-play module, DUCAT does not close this gap, limiting its practical impact. (Reviewer 3)

- **Missing transfer attack analysis**:  
  - The paper does not evaluate adversarial robustness under transfer attacks (e.g., adversarial examples generated from a ResNet-18 trained with DUCAT transferred to baseline models, or vice versa). This leaves a gap in understanding the generalizability of the robustness. The authors should show such results for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 in a table format (similar to Figure 3). (Reviewer 1)

- **Unclear notations and missing details**:  
  - In Eq. 2, the distribution \(\mathcal{P}(x_i)\) is not defined. (Reviewer 3)  
  - In Eq. 4, \(\dot{\mathbf{y}}\) and \(\ddot{\mathbf{y}}\) are not explained; is \(\mathbf{y} = \dot{\mathbf{y}} + \ddot{\mathbf{y}}\)? (Reviewer 3)  
  - It is unclear whether \(h_\theta^{Dummy}(\cdot)\) is a separate neural network or simply an enlargement of the last layer (Figure 1 suggests the latter). (Reviewer 3)  
  - How are the dummy classes assigned per example? (Reviewer 3)  
  - The term “SOTAs” in Sec. 3.2 is ambiguous: does it refer to AT-based methods or more advanced robust models? (Reviewer 3)

- **Limited empirical evidence and statistical analysis**:  
  - The performance improvements over TRADES are marginal (Table I). A breakdown by dataset, attack type, and statistical significance tests is recommended. (Reviewer 2)  
  - Analysis of class-specific differences in “always-failed” samples (Figure 2) is lacking; factors such as visual complexity or PCA analysis could explain variations across classes. (Reviewer 1)
