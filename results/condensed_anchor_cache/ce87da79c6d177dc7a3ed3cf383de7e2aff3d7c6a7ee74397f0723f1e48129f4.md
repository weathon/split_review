## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Adam with Combinatorial Bandit Sampling (AdamCB), which integrates combinatorial bandit techniques into Adam to enable adaptive batch selection. It identifies and corrects theoretical flaws in the convergence guarantees of both Adam and the prior bandit-based variant AdamBS, providing new analysis under more general assumptions. AdamCB is shown to achieve faster convergence with increasing batch size. Experiments on small-scale MLP and CNN tasks demonstrate improvement over baselines. One reviewer rated the paper 8, highlighting strong theoretical contributions; two reviewers rated 6, raising concerns about practical scale and applicability.

### Strengths
- Writing is clear and easy to understand.
- Rigorously identifies and corrects theoretical flaws in existing convergence guarantees for Adam and AdamBS; the refined proofs are of independent value to the community.
- Assumptions required for convergence analysis are more general than in prior work, and the theory covers Adam, AdamBS, and AdamCB.
- The proposed AdamCB is shown to be both theoretically robust and empirically effective, with rigorous analysis and extensive experimental validation.
- Fair and thorough comparison with Adam and AdamBS, using corrected theoretical guarantees.
- Results on simple datasets (MLP, CNN tasks) are better than baselines.

### Weaknesses
- Motivation lacks clarity: the paper states uniform sampling leads to inefficiencies but does not specify the type (convergence rate, solution quality, memory, etc.). It does not provide direct evidence that alternative sampling improves Adam efficiency or argue why uniform sampling is suboptimal for Adam.
- Algorithm 3 requires prior knowledge of \(L\) for the weight update rule. Practical estimation of \(L\), sensitivity to its choice, and guidelines for selection are not discussed.
- Why the sum of sampling probabilities must equal \(K\) (batch size) rather than 1 is inadequately explained; the need for threshold \(\tau\) is unclear.
- The theoretical justification for faster convergence with increasing sample size \(n\) is missing; only a claim is given without intuitive or formal explanation.
- Experiments are limited to small-scale MLP and CNN tasks and are not reflective of practical settings with large models and datasets.
- Benefits of adaptive selection are likely marginal in large model dimensionality because the dominant term \(d\sqrt{T}\) in the convergence rate overshadows the adaptive term \(\sqrt{d}/n^{3/4}T^{3/4}\); this limitation is not discussed in the main paper (one reviewer questioned the expected speedup for models with >100 million parameters).
- It is unclear how useful bandit-based batch selection will be in settings where the dataset is only partially looped (e.g., LLM pretraining); one reviewer asked what benefits are expected for >100B parameter models with rare full data passes.
- The theoretical analysis has significant overlap with Tran et al. 2019, which is only acknowledged in the appendix; this detracts from the novelty of the theoretical contribution.
- Applicability in real-world scenarios is questionable: can the method be directly integrated into existing practices? Does performance remain robust given the extra effort of assigning sampling bias during batch construction?
- Compatibility with semi-supervised learning is not addressed (e.g., weighting unlabeled testing samples).
- Interaction with sample-agnostic regularizations (weight decay, dropout) and output-only regularizations (total variation loss) is not discussed.
- The method assigns high weight to high-gradient samples, but this may over-weight noisy or adversarial samples; it is unclear whether learning from such samples is beneficial.
- Compatibility with data augmentation is not addressed; augmented samples share strong similarity, possibly introducing unexpected bias.
- No comparison with other adaptive sampling methods for LLMs (e.g., DoReMi – NeurIPS 2023) and no demonstration in an LLM pretraining setting.
