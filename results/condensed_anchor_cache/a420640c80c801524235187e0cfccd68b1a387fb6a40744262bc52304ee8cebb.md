## Merged Review

### Summary

The proposed Audio-Visual Speech-Driven Synthesis (AV-SDS) method uses a two-stage framework (AV-S2UM and Unit2X modules) based on speech discrete units to achieve noise-robust body language synthesis from audio-visual speech. The approach maps audio-visual speech to discrete units (semantic Uˢ and acoustic Uᵃ) and then to multiple body language outputs (gestures, facial movements, posture), leveraging separate datasets for each output. The work is motivated by the lack of paired visual speech and body language data and aims to improve robustness in noisy environments (tested up to -15dB SNR). Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem and the effectiveness of the approach in noisy conditions, but raise major concerns about clarity, reproducibility, real-world applicability, and incremental novelty. One reviewer (R2, rating 6) praised the innovation and experimental rigor, while others (R3, R4, rating 3) cited significant weaknesses in presentation and justification.

### Strengths

- The two-stage framework using discrete speech units as an intermediate representation for mapping audio-visual speech to body language is innovative and versatile across different synthesis tasks (gestures, talking heads, facial expressions, posture). [R1, R2, R5]
- The method effectively leverages both semantic and acoustic discrete units, enabling high-quality synthesis even in noisy environments (tested up to -15dB SNR). [R1, R2, R4]
- Experimental evaluation is thorough, including both objective metrics (FGD, LMD, diversity) and subjective Mean Opinion Scores, on standard datasets (LSR3, etc.). [R2, R3, R5]
- The qualitative results shown on the demo page are compelling, demonstrating synchronized and natural-looking outputs. [R3, R5]
- The problem is well-motivated: adding visual speech (lip movements) improves noise robustness, as supported by prior work in the speech domain. [R4, R5]
- The work makes effective use of multiple open datasets for different aspects of generation (e.g., gesture, talking head, face mesh). [R3]
- One reviewer (R2) specifically commended the experimental rigor, controls for audio-only vs. audio-visual scenarios, and strong results under noisy conditions; another (R5) found the controls adequate and the discrete-unit substitution convincing. [R2, R5]

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and terminology**: The paper conflates data modality (video, mesh, landmarks) with communication modality (speech, gesture), causing confusion throughout (e.g., Figure 2 caption lists "modalities" as mesh and co-speech gesture). [R3, R4] Terms such as U_t, U^a, U^s, U^a_lip are not clearly defined; it is unclear whether U^a_lip differs from U^a and how they are obtained. [R3, R4] The number of discrete units in the vocabulary is never specified. [R4] The term "speech discrete units" would be more natural than "discrete speech units". [R3] FGD is not defined in the main paper. [R3] Loss terms L_lip, L_sync, and L_G in Equation (4) are not explained. [R3] The MOS setup and discussion are missing from the main text. [R3] It is not stated that the generator/discriminator form an adversarial model until late in the text. [R3]

- **Methodology details and reproducibility**: The paper lacks sufficient detail for reproducibility and does not state that code will be released. [R1, R3, R5] Specific missing details include: how the face decoder is constructed and how f_s (from reference/pose frames) is combined with f_a (added, concatenated?). [R1, R3] Architecture of the discriminator and generator. [R1] How acoustic and semantic discrete units are extracted—whether two separate AV-S2UM modules are used. [R4] Implementation of the lookup table in Section 3.3. [R4] The selection of "randomly selected speaker reference frames and pose prior frames" is unclear (individual frames or sequences? How does selection affect generation?). [R3] The use of 8 levels of RVQ is not justified; it is unclear if signal remains at that quantization level. [R3] The reader is assumed to be familiar with RVQ quantization. [R1] In Section 3.2, the use of AV-S2UM to predict both acoustic and semantic discrete units is unclear; Equation (1) may lack a time index for A and V. [R3] The cross-entropy loss in Equation (2) appears to be missing a term. [R3] An illustration in the appendix showing model design, inputs, outputs, and submodules would be helpful. [R4, R5]

- **Missing ablation and comparisons**: No ablation study shows how each component (U_s vs. U_a, AV-S2UM, etc.) contributes to performance. [R1] Comparisons with state-of-the-art methods like DiffGesture and Audio2Gestures for gesture evaluation are omitted. [R1] In Table 1, the baseline S2X outperforms the proposed methods (Unit2S+S2X and Unit2X) on some metrics (e.g., talking head LMD for both "Mesh" ? and "Landmarks") but these values are not bolded as best, undermining claims of superiority. [R3] The paper does not compare against direct audio-visual body language synthesis methods that could use video input directly. [R4]

- **Real-world applicability and motivation**: The usefulness of the system is questionable: if video speech (lip movements) is already available, one could directly synthesize the talking face or estimate landmarks/mesh from the video using pose/mesh estimation techniques, bypassing the pipeline. [R4] The motivating example (construction sites) is considered weak and niche. [R3] The paper does not adequately address why such a system is needed in realistic scenarios. [R4]

- **Training fairness and generalization**: AV-SDS is trained with additional audio-visual data (e.g., LSR3) that baselines may not have access to; this unfair comparison may inflate performance, especially in clean conditions. [R4] The method's reliance on paired data limits its applicability to domains with less paired training data. [R2] Generalization to other environmental conditions beyond the tested dataset is not shown; cross-dataset evaluation is needed. [R1]

- **Noise robustness justification**: The assumption that discrete units provide noise robustness is not clearly justified—noise could affect the mapping to discrete units as well. [R3] One reviewer was surprised that the system achieves good lip synchronization at -15dB babble noise, suggesting the method may rely on speech separation capabilities of the SSL model rather than the proposed discrete-unit framework. [R3]

- **Missing experiments**: The following experiments are missing or insufficient: robustness when one modality (audio or video) is partially missing. [R1] Handling variations in gesture expressiveness with different speech prosody. [R1] Sequence length limitations—can the model generate frames continuously without degradation? Examples for different durations (3, 6, 10 seconds) are needed. [R1] Inference speed comparison with number of parameters for a given input length (e.g., 10 seconds) on common hardware is absent. [R1] The noise type used in experiments (e.g., babble, white, etc.) is not specified; multiple noise types should be considered as in the speech domain. [R4] Error propagation between AV-S2UM and Unit2X under varying noise conditions is not investigated. [R2] Scalability to more complex body language modalities (e.g., emotion-based responses) is not discussed. [R2]

- **Novelty and incremental nature**: While the approach combines existing techniques (AV-HuBERT, discrete units, adversarial training) in a novel pipeline, several reviewers note it is largely an integration of prior work. [R2, R5] The paper does not clearly distinguish UNIT2X from Prajwal et al. 2020, nor AV-S2UM from AV-HuBERT (the difference appears to be additional convolutional layers). [R5] The first three contributions could be merged. [R5] Figure 1 is considered unnecessary and could be replaced with more methodological detail. [R5]

- **Domain shift**: The Unit2S+S2X pipeline (reconstructing speech from units then synthesizing body language) performs poorly compared to direct S2X, suggesting a domain shift between reconstructed and real speech. Using the output of Unit2S to train S2X could mitigate this. [R3]

- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (R2, rating 6) found the approach innovative, well-explained, and experimentally rigorous, while others (R3, R4, rating 3) rated it low, emphasizing severe clarity issues and weak motivation. This disagreement highlights the need for improved presentation and stronger justification.
