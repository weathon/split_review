## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a certified defense framework against poisoning attacks in offline reinforcement learning using differential privacy (DP). The method provides both action-level (per-state) and policy-level (expected cumulative return) guarantees, and extends to transition-level and trajectory-level poisoning, continuous action spaces, and stochastic environments. Empirical results show performance drops to no more than 50% with up to 7% of training data poisoned, a drastic improvement over prior work COPA (0.008%), with certified radii 5 times larger. The theoretical contributions are supported by proofs in the appendix.

### Strengths
- **Well motivated and presented.** The paper is clearly organized with logical flow, clear definitions, lemmas, and theorems (R1, R4). As a non-expert in RL poisoning or certified defenses, the exposition is easy to follow (R1).
- **Strong theoretical guarantees.** The authors provide theoretical lower bounds on performance and upper bounds on action changes, with convincing proofs in the appendix (R2, R4). The connection between DP and robustness in DRL is interesting (R2).
- **Significant empirical improvement.** The method dramatically outperforms the prior baseline COPA, achieving practical certified robustness where COPA’s guarantees were negligible (R2). The experiments are thorough with concrete evaluations (R1, R3).
- **Extends beyond prior work.** The framework handles continuous action spaces, stochastic transitions, and transition-level attacks, which were not covered in previous work (R3). It is a framework that can make a class of RL algorithms robust, not just a single algorithm (R2).
- **First practical certified defense.** Previous work (COPA) had unrealistic performance; this work develops the first practical and certifiably robust DRL framework (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clarity in algorithmic and experimental details.** 
  - The paper does not clarify how DP is used—it is assumed to be via DP-SGD per sub-policy, but this is omitted (R3). An outline of the training/aggregation/certification algorithm would significantly improve readability (R3). The meaning of “depending on whether the DP training algorithm provides transition- or trajectory-level guarantees” is ambiguous (R3).
  - In the experiments, the definition of \(\bar{r}\) is unclear and the evaluation process is not transparent. It is not specified whether datasets are actually poisoned or just evaluated at theoretical poisoning levels, and if poisoning is performed, the specific attack method is not described (R4). Figure legends are missing, with explanations only in captions (R2).
- **Insufficient comparison with prior work.** 
  - The paper highlights “multi-level” robustness, but action-level and policy-level guarantees were already proposed in Wu et al. (2022). The novelty of Section 3.2 is not clear (R3).
  - The comparison with prior work is limited to PARL; the stronger baselines TPARL and DPARL from the same prior work are omitted. It is unclear how the proposed method compares to those (R3).
  - Algorithmically, the differences enabling continuous and stochastic settings are not explained, making it hard to assess the contribution (R3).
- **Limitations and assumptions not fully discussed.** 
  - The theoretical analysis assumes the attacker can arbitrarily modify trajectories without constraints. In practice, attackers may be limited (e.g., only modify a few transitions, small perturbations). This makes the attack model unrealistic (R2).
  - The paper lacks a discussion of the practical threat model: how do poisoning attacks manifest in real offline RL systems, and what are the specific attack vectors? (R1, also echoed by R2’s concern about unrealistic attacks).
  - Backdoor attacks are not considered. Strong backdoor attacks may break action-level robustness without violating the certification assumptions; this should be discussed (R4).
  - The lower bound of the optimal robust guarantee (i.e., the gap between the proposed method and the theoretical optimum) is not investigated (R2).
- **Experimental gaps and questions.** 
  - Trajectory-level results (Table 1) do not always show improvement from the proposed method (e.g., Breakout) (R1).
  - It is unclear how the algorithm would behave under practical attacks that deviate from worst-case bounds—theoretical guarantees may not be tight (R2).
  - The need to train multiple policies may be cumbersome for practitioners (R1).
- **Missing related work and technical rigor.** 
  - The discussion of DP in RL in related work is missing (R2).
  - There are typos and a lack of mathematical rigor in places (R3). A value network is introduced without prior context (line 317), SimuEM is not described (line 373), and the binary search over \(\mathcal{K}\) is unclear (line 377) (R3).
  - The choice of ADP vs RDP is not motivated, and tighter DP accountants (e.g., Gopi et al., Doroshenko et al.) are not discussed. RDP dominates ADP in results, but the insight behind this is not explained (R3).

*Note: The minority reviewer (R3, score 3) was particularly critical of the lack of clarity and insufficient comparison with prior work, while other reviewers (R1, R2, R4, scores 8) found the paper well-presented, theoretically strong, and practically significant, though they also noted some of the same weaknesses (clarity, attack model realism, missing comparisons) albeit with less severity.*
