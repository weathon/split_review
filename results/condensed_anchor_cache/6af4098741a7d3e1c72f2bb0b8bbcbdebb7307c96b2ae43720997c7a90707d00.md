## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes *brain-tuning*: fine-tuning pretrained speech language models (Wav2vec2.0, HuBERT, Whisper) using fMRI recordings of people listening to natural stories. The goal is to improve semantic understanding by aligning models with brain responses in semantic language regions. The authors evaluate on three fronts: (1) alignment with new brain recordings, (2) reduction in the contribution of low-level speech features (e.g., Tri-Phones, Articulation) to that alignment, and (3) performance on downstream tasks requiring semantics. They compare against off-the-shelf models, a random-fMRI baseline, and BigSLM fine-tuned on non-speech brain data. Results are reported on the LeBel dataset (8 subjects). The paper claims brain-tuning yields consistent improvements across all three evaluation axes and is the first to show that incorporating brain signals into speech model training improves semantic understanding.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: First application of brain-tuning to speech models, closing the loop on prior work showing emergent brain–model alignment.  
- **Noteworthy downstream improvements**: Brain-tuning yields strong improvements on automatic speech recognition (ASR), which is impressive if confirmed (see Weaknesses). Prior work has not shown such consistent downstream gains from neural signals.  
- **Reduced low‑level feature impact**: The formal analysis shows low-level acoustic features (Tri-Phones, Articulation) become less predictive of brain alignment after brain-tuning, indicating models rely more on semantics.  
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Three model families tested on brain alignment, low-level feature impact, and multiple downstream tasks. Appendix C further quantifies the phonetic–semantic preference shift.  
- **Motivation and clarity**: Clear motivation, well‑written, with detailed experimental setting. The random‑fMRI and BigSLM baselines increase confidence that recorded brain activity is a useful target.  
- **Cross‑disciplinary impact**: Bridges neuroscience and AI, valuable for both communities.  

*Minority view* (Reviewer 3, score 8): Strongly positive about novelty and thoroughness, but notes that results are only convincing in the restricted comparison models (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient and weak baselines**  
   – The method is only compared against two baselines: BigSLM (fine-tuned on unrelated fMRI) and random‑fMRI. Random‑fMRI is near‑invalid (will definitely deteriorate performance), leaving only one valid comparison. BigSLM itself sometimes degrades performance (Fig. 4).  
   – Missing comparisons against other valid fine-tuning approaches, e.g., using pretrained language model embeddings (e.g., BERT) as a semantic target, or controlling for specific brain voxels/areas.  
   – The claimed ASR improvement over off‑the‑shelf wav2vec2/HuBERT is striking but needs clarification: Are there architectural enhancements (e.g., extra projection layers)? The reported off‑the‑shelf ASR scores are much lower than those in the original wav2vec2 paper (>0.8 on LibriSpeech). A distribution mismatch between pretraining data (LibriSpeech) and podcast audio may explain the gain, not brain‑specific semantics.  
   – Gold‑standard experiment: fine‑tune on one set of fMRI‑audio pairs and test on a completely new audio dataset; report performance on original evaluation tasks (e.g., LibriSpeech) to rule out simple domain adaptation.

2. **Catastrophic forgetting / performance degradation**  
   – It is surprising that fine‑tuning on a noisy proxy (fMRI) preserves the original speech representations with no explicit incentive. The authors should report performance on original evaluation datasets (e.g., LibriSpeech) to quantify any forgetting.  
   – A performance drop is observed on the emotion‑prediction task; benchmarking 1‑2 low‑level audio tasks (e.g., phonetic classification) is needed to assess whether semantics is gained at the cost of phonetic understanding.

3. **Unclear reasoning for reduced low‑level feature impact**  
   – The paper finds that brain‑tuning *decreases* the contribution of low‑level features to brain alignment. Yet lines 237–238 cite evidence that brain‑tuning *increases* model–brain alignment, so one might expect low‑level reliance to increase as well. The mechanism is not explained.

4. **Dataset and generalizability concerns**  
   – The LeBel dataset includes only 8 subjects, raising questions about robustness. The claim (line 54) that this is the largest fMRI dataset for naturalistic language comprehension is inaccurate; the Narratives dataset contains hundreds of subjects and is recommended as a stronger testbed.  
   – Inter‑subject variability and limited sample size limit the generalizability of conclusions.  
   – Brain‑tuning requires fMRI data, which is hard to acquire (only 8 participants in the largest public dataset), but this is noted as a practical limitation rather than a flaw of the idea.

5. **Missing analyses and experimental details**  
   – The influence of model parameters (size/architecture) on brain‑tuning is not investigated.  
   – Methods for obtaining the four low‑level speech features are only cited; a description should be included (at least in the Appendix).  
   – Details about the fMRI target: Which brain areas are covered by the voxels (semantic vs. other areas)? How is cortical parcellation performed across subjects (LeBel dataset may not provide standardized space)?  
   – Why is brain‑tuning not effective for Whisper? More analysis on architecture (encoder‑decoder vs. encoder‑only) is needed.

6. **Minor issues**  
   – Figure 1c: bar charts lack an x‑axis label.  
   – Line 257: incorrect open‑quote LaTeX syntax.  
   – (Nit) Consider citing [1] (another neural‑driven fine‑tuning paper) in related work.
