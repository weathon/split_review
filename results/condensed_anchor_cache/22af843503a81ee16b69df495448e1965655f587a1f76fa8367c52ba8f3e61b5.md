## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Advantage Alignment (AdAlign), a family of opponent-shaping algorithms that align the advantages of interacting agents. The method uses the product of advantages from the player's and opponent's value networks to update the policy, increasing the probability of mutually beneficial actions when advantages have the same sign. The paper proves that prior methods (LOLA, LOQA) implicitly perform advantage alignment, simplifies their formulation, reduces computational burden, and extends to continuous actions. Experiments are conducted on the Coin Game, Negotiation Game, and Commons Harvest Open, with baselines including LOLA, LOQA, PPO, and others. The paper is generally well-written and motivated, but several reviewers raised concerns about technical derivations, experimental clarity, and differentiation from LOQA.

### Strengths
- The derivation of advantage alignment is novel, well motivated, and provides new theoretical insight into opponent shaping (R1, R3, R4). Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 unify LOLA and LOQA under the advantage alignment paradigm (R4).
- The paper is theoretically grounded, with assumptions explained and most proofs correct to the best of reviewer knowledge (R1, R3).
- The method reduces computational complexity compared to prior opponent-shaping approaches and extends to continuous action domains (R3, R4).
- Extensive experiments on multiple social-dilemma benchmarks (Coin Game, Negotiation Game, Commons Harvest Open) with a broad suite of baselines (R1, R4). The results include both numeric results and test-case demonstrations (R3).
- The paper is well written, builds intuition (Section 4), and is accessible to readers new to opponent shaping (R2, R3).
- The research problem (social dilemma) is significant, and the opponent-shaping direction, while requiring opponent knowledge, is a necessary step toward weaker assumptions (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Differentiation from LOQA**: The core mechanism of modifying advantages based on past interactions is very similar to LOQA, and the paper does not clearly delineate the novel contributions (R2, R4). A controlled ablation study and direct comparison on more complex environments are needed to understand whether performance gains come from the advantage alignment formulation itself or from the use of PPO as the base optimizer (R2). In the Coin Game always-defect case, LOQA achieves slightly higher returns than AdAlign (R2), which raises further questions about practical advantages.
- **Efficiency claims not quantified**: The paper claims greater efficiency but does not quantify it (e.g., training time or computational resources) (R4).
- **Scaling to n agents**: The product of advantages over all agents (implicit in the multi-agent setting) means that a single non-cooperating agent causes the entire alignment product to fail, limiting scalability and robustness in larger multi-agent systems (R1).
- **Negotiation Game limitations**: Full knowledge of opponents’ value functions makes the problem a simple optimization task rather than a true test of emergent cooperation; with orthogonal utilities there is no real dilemma, so insights from this experiment are limited (R1).
- **Stability concerns**: Figure 6 shows that AdAlign is not stable in some settings (increasingly noisy rewards, partial collapse against AC and AD near the end of training), and it is unclear whether this is due to hyperparameter sensitivity (R1). Additionally, Figure 4 selects the best agent out of 10 seeds—given the variance seen in Figure 6 (even though it is a different game), the representativeness of these results is questionable (R1).
- **Evaluation protocol not provided**: It is unclear whether agents are trained prior to evaluation or adapt online during evaluation episodes (zero-shot vs. adapting)—this is critical for understanding robustness and generalization (R4).
- **Mathematical derivations unclear**: Several steps in the appendix are not sufficiently explained—e.g., the disappearance of the β term (line 720), derivation of Eq. (16) from (15), transformation from (19) to (8), derivation of Eq. (24) from (23), and the use of the orthonormal-gradients assumption to get Eq. (27) from (26) (R3). The limitations of the orthonormality assumption are not discussed (R3). There is a typo in line 1001 (“equation equation”) (R3). The advantage function is not defined mathematically in the main text (R4). Equation (2) appears incorrect, and the connection between Eq. (5) and Eq. (6) is not explained (R4).
- **Intuition for negative-negative advantages**: The design choice of increasing the log probability when both agents’ advantages are negative (Eq. 8, Fig. 1a) is not well justified, and the game-theoretic scenarios that benefit from this behavior are not clarified (R4).
- **Question about assumption**: Playing against fixed policies (AD/AC) may break Assumption 1 (which assumes opponent adapts to the agent’s policy), yet AdAlign is evaluated against such fixed opponents (R1). This point also relates to the evaluation-protocol weakness.
- **Asymmetry in Coin Game**: The asymmetry between CD and DC results in Figure 1b is not interpretively explained (R3).
- **Minor note**: In Figure 3, cooperation values are mentioned as green but are not visible in the figure (R2).
- **Hyperparameter tuning**: The sum of past advantages (Appendix 6, Eq. 59) uses the opponent’s advantage at the current time step plus the agent’s sum of past advantages; the sensitivity to the number of past/future terms included is not discussed (R2).

**Minority perspective**: One reviewer (R3, score 6) expressed significant concerns about the mathematical rigor of the derivations and considered the paper reject-worthy based on those unclarities, while the other three reviewers (scores 8, 8, 8) were substantially more positive and recommended acceptance. This contrast is preserved.
