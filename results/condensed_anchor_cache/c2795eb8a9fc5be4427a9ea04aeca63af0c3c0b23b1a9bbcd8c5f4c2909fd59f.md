## Merged Review

### Summary

Reviewers acknowledge the paper’s central aim: improving drug-likeness prediction by integrating biomedical knowledge (via a multi-modal alignment with a softened CLIP loss) and learning a decision boundary via an EM-like algorithm. The work is generally well-motivated and clearly written, with comprehensive experimental comparisons (scaffold- and time-based splits, zero-shot toxicity tests, case studies) and provided code. Opinions divided sharply on novelty, technical soundness, and experimental rigor. One reviewer (score 6) found the method well-designed, the theoretical proof convincing, and the results the strongest state-of-the-art across metrics. Two reviewers (scores 3) raised fundamental concerns about problem formalisation (PU learning ambiguity, lack of true negatives in test sets) and the practical relevance of a fully data-driven boundary. The other two reviewers (scores 5) highlighted interesting components but pointed to significant weaknesses in evaluation, comparisons, and theoretical gaps (local optima, sensitivity to initialization, oversimplified ATC hierarchy). The reported “five orders of magnitude” improvement was seen as suspicious by one reviewer.

### Strengths

*   The work tackles a recognised challenge in drug candidacy prediction (absence of a clear negative class, vast and dispersed drug space) with a clear, well-motivated research question.
*   The proposed multi-modal alignment (softened CLIP loss) to integrate biomedical knowledge graph information with molecular structural representations is a novel attempt, building on established components (DREAMwalk, Geodesic Mixup).
*   The EM-like boundary optimization is formally presented, with a mathematical proof for boundary convergence (Theorem 1) that was considered well-written and supportive by one reviewer.
*   Experiments are extensive: they include scaffold-based and time-based splits, zero-shot toxic compound filtering, comprehensive case studies, and standard deviations across cross-validation folds.
*   The method consistently outperforms several baselines on the main drug vs. compound discrimination task (DrugBank vs. ZINC) on multiple metrics; one reviewer noted “visible advantages in results.”
*   Code and benchmark data are provided, supporting reproducibility.
*   The paper is generally well-written, self-contained, and easy to follow, with detailed descriptions of each technical component.

### Weaknesses

*   **Novelty and prior work**: Several reviewers noted that multi-modal contrastive alignment (including softening the CLIP loss) has been done before in property prediction [1,2,3] and that the KS-mix component lacks novelty. The claim that no prior work aligned biomedical KG embeddings with structural embeddings for downstream tasks was disputed with explicit prior references.
*   **Problem formalisation and PU learning**: The paper does not clearly acknowledge that this is a standard PU learning setting. The statement (line 106) that existing PU methods rely on negative samples is incorrect (PU learning trains on positives and unlabeled only). This confusion undermines the claimed distinction from prior work and raises questions about the experimental setup’s necessity. If the test set lacks definite negatives, metrics like F1 may be inappropriate; recall-based measures would be more suitable.
*   **Dataset construction and distribution**: Only DrugBank (positive) and ZINC (unlabeled) are used. The paper does not analyse the initial overlap between drug and compound distributions, nor justify why these datasets are representative of real-world screening challenges. The scaffold distribution (1.97 molecules per scaffold) indicates extreme sparsity, but its impact on generalisation is not analysed.
*   **Evaluation metrics and conclusions**:  
    *   The “five orders of magnitude improvement” claim was flagged as suspicious (potential data leakage or flawed experimental design).  
    *   The ICR (in-boundary compound ratio) is lower than DeepDL and D-GCAN – suggesting the decision boundary may actually be _poorer_ at excluding compounds.  
    *   The IDR/ICR ratio cannot reflect a trade‑off (the two metrics are not correlated), a sum would be more appropriate.  
    *   In Figure 6, the method only beats SOTA when the drug/compound ratio ≥ 1:50; the training/test ratio used is not reported.  
    *   The caption of Figure 8 (“more distinct distribution”) is not supported by the visible plots.  
    *   No statistical significance tests are provided for any comparison.
*   **Baseline comparisons and fairness**: Baselines may have been run with sub‑optimal default parameters; it is unclear whether they were given access to the same enriched biomedical context (knowledge graph data). Performance gains could simply arise from having additional information rather than from the EM boundary method.
*   **Missing ablation and model analysis**:  
    *   No ablation study on the geodesic mixup component.  
    *   Missing baseline: why not train an MLP on the contrastive embeddings (or simply concatenate structural and KG embeddings and feed into an MLP)? This would directly test whether the EM boundary adds value beyond the learned representation.  
    *   The intuition for why an MLP cannot learn a proper boundary (beyond iterative encoder updating) is not explained.
*   **Theoretical concerns**: The proof of Theorem 1 relies on assumptions that are not fully justified (local optima, impact of initialization). The EM-like optimisation shows “concerning sensitivity to initialisation” (R1). No theoretical guarantees for avoiding local minima are provided.
*   **Implementation details**: The 2-layer MLP with 512‑d hidden layers lacks justification; hyperparameter selection appears arbitrary. The convergence criterion based on in‑boundary compound ratio is potentially unstable. Computational complexity is not analysed.
*   **Limited real‑world validation**:  
    *   Zero-shot toxicity predictions lack error analysis.  
    *   Time‑based split does not account for temporal evolution in drug discovery practices.  
    *   Only one specific molecule (Imatinib) is used to show pdf increase; a single example cannot demonstrate generalisability.  
    *   The case study (Table R4‑1) only reports number of remaining molecules – not whether those molecules have better drug‑like properties. No comparison to property‑driven screening (e.g., binding affinity, ADMET) is given.  
    *   The method lacks interpretability; no structural analysis (scaffold, functional motif, non‑covalent interactions) of filtered molecules is provided.  
    *   QED, Ro5, and toxicity alone are insufficient – binding affinity is a crucial missing metric.
*   **Overall appropriateness of a data‑driven boundary**: Several reviewers questioned whether a fully data‑driven boundary learned from known drugs can outperform property‑based filtering in practice. Distinguishing “drug‑like” from “non‑drug” is inherently ambiguous; a distance‑based measurement may be more rational. The paper does not demonstrate that BounDr.E enhances screening outcomes beyond reducing the number of candidates, nor does it provide evidence of selecting molecules with better downstream developability.
