- Scores: 5, 6, 6, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies continual linear classification where gradient descent (GD) is run for a fixed budget of iterations per task under cyclic or random task order. When tasks are jointly linearly separable, the full training loss converges to zero and the classifier direction converges to the joint offline max-margin solution – an implicit bias that holds for both orderings. Non-asymptotic bounds on cycle-averaged forgetting (\(O(\ln^4 J / J^2)\)) and joint loss (\(O(\ln^2 J / J)\)) are given for the separable cyclic case, and a faster rate \(\tilde{O}(J^{-2})\) is provided for the non-separable cyclic case. Reviewers found the paper clearly written and its theoretical analysis novel in extending prior work (Evron et al., ICML 2023) to an unregularized, fixed‑budget setting. However, they also raised substantial concerns: limited experiments (only one toy example with six points, no real‑world or deep‑learning baselines), questionable practical relevance of the theoretical framework (cyclic order, fixed per‑task iterations), insufficient proof sketches and intuition, and mixed opinions on the novelty/significance of the contribution.

### Strengths
- **Clarity and presentation:** The paper is well written and easy to follow; theorems are stated clearly and the main contributions are explicitly demonstrated. (All reviewers)
- **Soundness and originality:** The theoretical results are sound. The analysis of gradient descent with a *fixed budget of iterations per task* is an improvement over the Sequential Max‑Margin (SMM) framework of Evron et al. (2023), which considered a weakly‑regularized setup trained to convergence. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Novel implicit bias results:** Standard GD without regularization not only solves each task but also directionally converges to the joint max‑margin solution under both cyclic *and* random task ordering – this is new and extends our understanding of implicit bias in continual learning. (Reviewers 3, 4; Reviewer 2 called the analysis “highly novel and interesting”.)
- **Non-asymptotic convergence rates:** For the cyclic separable case, concrete rates on cycle‑averaged forgetting (\(O(\ln^4 J / J^2)\)) and on the joint loss (\(O(\ln^2 J / J)\)) are provided; for the non‑separable cyclic case, a faster rate \(\tilde{O}(J^{-2})\) toward the joint loss minimum is established. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Separation from prior work:** The results show a clear behavioral difference from the weakly‑regularized setting of Evron et al. (2023). Reviewer 4 highlighted this as a significant contribution to the theoretical continual‑learning field.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experiments:** All reviewers noted the lack of meaningful empirical validation.
    - Only a single toy experiment with six data points is presented (Figure 1). No real‑world dataset (e.g., standard continual‑learning benchmarks) or realistic synthetic data is used. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
    - No comparisons with any baseline methods are provided, including deep‑learning setups for continual learning. (Reviewer 2, 3)
    - Sensitivity to hyperparameters (learning rate, iterations per task) is not explored. (Reviewer 3)
- **Limited practical relevance and novelty concerns:**
    - Reviewer 1 argued that the setting may not be novel: if a single linear classifier exists for all tasks, one could simply combine all tasks into one dataset and run SGD with batches (cyclic or random) – a standard procedure. The paper may be “fancily calling batches as tasks”.
    - Reviewer 1 also stated that the paper provides no connection to specific standard datasets or realistic scenarios, and offers no recommendations for practice.
    - Reviewer 3 noted that the theoretical framework (cyclic order, fixed per‑task iterations) does not fully align with practical deep‑learning continual learning, where tasks vary in difficulty and training is monitored until convergence.
    - Reviewer 4 observed that the proved behavior (convergence to the joint max‑margin solution) is only informative when tasks recur; unlike Evron et al. (2023), it does not yield insight for arbitrary non‑repeating sequences. (While Reviewer 4 still found the results significant, this was noted as a limitation.)
    - Reviewer 1 questioned whether tasks could be replaced by any batch in SGD, undermining the novelty of the continual‑learning framing.
- **Missing proof sketches and technical intuition:**
    - The appendices are hard to follow; many steps are insufficiently explained (e.g., how Eq. (20) follows from Eq. (19); details in Eq. (26)). Reviewer 4 could not quickly validate a random subset of the proofs.
    - No high‑level proof sketches or technical overview are given for the main results. The paper lacks a clear narrative of how the analysis improves upon the techniques of Evron et al. (2023) and what new technical challenges were overcome. (Reviewers 3, 4)
    - Reviewer 2 asked for an intuitive explanation of Assumption 3.4 (Tight Exponential Tail).
    - Reviewer 3 requested geometric interpretations and proof sketches similar to those provided in Evron et al. (2022, 2023) and Goldfarb et al. (2024).
- **Unclear definitions and metrics:**
    - Reviewer 2 questioned the definition of *cycle‑averaged forgetting* \(\mathcal{CF}(J)\): it only considers the loss within the current cycle and does not guarantee that performance on task \(m\) in cycle \(J\) is better than in any earlier cycle. Reviewer 2 suggested using \(\min_{j}\mathcal{L}_m(w_K^{Mj+m})\) as baseline to align with conventional forgetting measures.
    - Reviewer 2 asked for a clear definition of *task similarity* and whether the quantities \(N_{p,q},\bar{N}_{p,q}\) in Theorem 3.4 capture it; also whether task similarity should affect the training loss (not just forgetting).
    - Reviewer 3 noted that Figure 1 uses all tasks with label 1, which seems unrealistic; asked how to make the setting more general.
- **Model limitations:** The analysis uses linear models and logistic loss. Reviewer 2 questioned whether the results can be generalized to large foundation models and cross‑entropy loss. Reviewer 3 echoed that the theoretical framework may not transfer to deep learning.
- **Presentation errors and minor issues:**
    - Several lines need rephrasing or correction: Line 62 (SMM “cannot be shown to converge” should be “does not always converge”); Line 105 (unclear difference between forgetting and loss); Line 112 (“symptotic”); Line 143 (grammar); Line 212 (word choice “generic”); Line 432 (spanning whole space); Line 434 (strictly vs strongly); Line 436 (“faster than what?”). (Reviewer 4)
    - Figure 2 is unclear; Reviewer 4 suggested adding a 2‑D parameter‑space panel.
    - Many formulas in the appendices exceed page margins (pages 19–20).
    - Some citations are incomplete or inaccurate (e.g., Shakibi et al. AISTATS 2019, Chaudhry et al. ICML 2019).
    - Reviewer 2 noted a typo on Line 412: \(\lim_{t\to\infty}x_i^\top w_k^{(t)}=\infty\) appears to be incorrectly stated.
    - Reviewer 4 pointed out that Theorem 5.2 does not explicitly explain how the iterate‑gap bound yields bounds on loss and forgetting, nor how the non‑separable case differs from the separable one.
- **Reviewer disagreement on overall significance:** Reviewers 1 and 4 held opposing views. Reviewer 1 rated the contribution 2 out of 5, questioning the novelty and practical value, while Reviewer 4 rated the contribution 3 out of 5 and emphasized the separation from prior work and the significance to the theoretical continual‑learning field.
