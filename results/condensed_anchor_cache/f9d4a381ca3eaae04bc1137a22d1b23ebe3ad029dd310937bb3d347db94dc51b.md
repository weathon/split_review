## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a gradient-based attribution method (CAD) to identify which components of a diffusion model contribute positively or negatively to a given concept (object, style, nudity, adversarial prompt). The identified components are then zeroed out for concept erasure or left unablated for concept amplification. Experiments compare against methods like ESD, CA, and others on object removal, style removal, nudity removal, and amplification, reporting quantitative metrics (e.g., FID, detection accuracy, CLIP scores) and qualitative examples.

### Strengths
- Concept attribution is an important problem with applications to safety, copyright, and privacy in image generation. *(R1, high confidence)*
- The idea of negative contributions (components that suppress a concept) is interesting, enabling concept amplification, which appears novel. *(R1)*
- Experiments show effectiveness on many concepts (objects, nudity, styles, adversarial prompts), with strong quantitative results that compete with state-of-the-art erasure methods. *(R1, R3)*
- The method is able to remove weights while maintaining performance for allowed outputs (e.g., nudity removal). *(R4)*
- The approach of comparing one-step diffusion outputs with and without a concept to compute attributions is elegant. *(R4)*
- Methodology and motivation are clearly explained. *(R2)*
- The introduction of positive vs. negative attributions is intriguing. *(R3)*

### Weaknesses
**Method novelty and similarity to existing work**  
- The core idea (first-order Taylor expansion of the loss to score weights) is essentially the same as gradient-based attribution / attribution patching used in language models (Self-Attention Attribution, Attribution Patching, Axiomatic Attribution) and in the Cones paper for diffusion models. The technical novelty is limited to applying the concept to diffusion models, not a new method. *(R1, R3, R4)*
- Some reviewers note the method closely resembles “almost the first part of fine-tuning” – taking the gradient, scaling by the weight, but not actually descending. *(R4)*

**Method quality and assumptions**  
- The derivative-based importance measure is too local: it assumes the model is linear. For non‑linear behaviors (e.g., sigmoid, step functions) the gradient at the current weight may be near zero while the weight is actually crucial, making the measure less accurate than brute-force zeroing (as in Shah et al.). *(R4)*  
- The paper builds on two strong hypotheses: (1) knowledge is localized in few components, (2) removing those components erases the concept without affecting others. These are not fully validated. For example, treating “style” and “object” the same may be incorrect. *(R2)*  
- With only 860M parameters and countless concepts, a single parameter may contribute to many concepts; zeroing one set may inadvertently damage unrelated knowledge. *(R2)*  
- The method does not include ablations (e.g., zeroing random weights, taking a few gradient steps instead of zeroing) to confirm the attribution measure is meaningful. *(R4)*  
- Missing explanation: what parameters (all layers? cross-attention only?) are scored, and what the distribution of scores looks like. *(R3)*  
- The objective \(J\) is defined only for concept removal; it should be defined earlier, and the paper should clarify whether the attribution is computed on the concept itself or on a different objective (e.g., “ ” prompt). *(R3, R4)*  

**Experimental validation**  
- **Pearson correlation**: Figure 1 reports a correlation of 0.35 with brute-force scoring, which is weak to moderate – not strong evidence for the method’s accuracy. *(R4)*  
- **Qualitative results**:  
  - Concept removal (Fig. 4) does not fully erase the style (e.g., Van Gogh still obvious). *(R2, R3)*  
  - Generated images after removal often lack content or appear degraded (e.g., “a few boats out on a river” fails; “a photo of a church” gives grayscale images). *(R2)*  
  - In nudity removal (Fig. 3 rightmost), a trace of inappropriate content remains, suggesting the concept is not truly deleted. *(R4)*  
- **Missing baselines**: No comparison of the ablated model’s output for allowed prompts against the original model; if the image changes drastically, clean concept removal is not achieved. *(R4)*  
- **Image quality**: FID scores of the ablated model are not reported, leaving the impact on general generation quality unquantified. *(R2)*  
- **Fine-tuning attribution neurons** (instead of zeroing) is not tried; it could validate whether those neurons are indeed responsible for the concept. *(R3)*  

**Presentation and claims**  
- Figures use inconsistent bolding (bold, bold+underscore, no highlight) for best scores. *(R2)*  
- Line 425 has an incomplete sentence. *(R2)*  
- Calling the experiments “editing” is an over-claim; they are better described as demonstrations of the interpretability method, not competitive editing. *(R4)*  
- The paper does not discuss how CAD complements attribution methods from language models (e.g., Syed et al.) or how its findings connect to Cones (Liu et al.). *(R3)*  

**Questions with overlapping criticisms**  
- How is the proposed method different from gradient-based attribution / attribution patching? *(R1)*  
- Are there experiments (e.g., FID) to confirm image quality does not drop significantly after modification? *(R2)*  
- What is the distribution of attribution scores for different concepts, and what neurons are scored? *(R3)*  
- When applying CAD to erasure with the objective from Kumari et al., is the attribution computed on the concept itself or on making the model output something else? *(R3)*  
- Instead of ablating, what happens if fine-tuning is restricted to only the attributed neurons? Would it perform well compared to full fine-tuning? *(R3)*  

**Overall reviewer disagreement**  
Two reviewers (R1, R3) see the method as promising, with strong quantitative results and a novel application; two reviewers (R2, R4) consider the method’s novelty limited, its assumptions insufficiently validated, and the experimental evidence (especially qualitative) weak. The split reflects differing opinions on the sufficiency of the technical contribution and the persuasiveness of the evaluation.
