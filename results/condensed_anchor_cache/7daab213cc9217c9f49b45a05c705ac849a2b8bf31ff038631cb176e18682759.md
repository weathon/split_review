## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper argues that LLMs possess intrinsic self-correction (SC) ability, identifying zero temperature and unbiased prompts as critical factors. It provides theoretical analysis (comparing SC to chain-of-thought and self-verification) and experiments on six benchmarks with four LLMs. Reviewers are sharply split: one finds the theory solid and contributions high (score 10), while the other three rate the paper low (3,5,3) citing flawed proofs, minimal empirical gains, limited model scope, and overclaimed conclusions. The positive reviewer defers to others on theoretical gripes. The negative reviewers stress that performance improvements are often below 2% (e.g., 75.92%→76.00%), many theoretical equations are incorrect or trivial, and the selection of models (two API-based, two open-source) is too narrow. Some acknowledge the topic’s importance and the value of analyzing temperature, but find the execution inadequate.

### Strengths
- **Theoretical framing and connection to CoT**: One reviewer found the theoretical argument solid and easy to follow, with experiments well connected to analysis – “neither part feels like an afterthought.” Another noted the insight of comparing intrinsic SC to chain-of-thought and self-verification as a “novel perspective.”
- **Practical recommendations**: Identifying unbiased prompts and zero temperature as key factors is directly applicable to real-world deployment. A reviewer highlighted these as “valuable insights.”
- **Temperature analysis**: The systematic study of temperature’s influence on SC is underexplored and appreciated by multiple reviewers.
- **Decomposition of SC mechanism**: Breaking self-improvement into stages (Section 2.2) is “interesting and potentially useful.”
- **Relevance of the problem**: The topic of intrinsic SC is of great interest given recent debates – several reviewers acknowledge its importance.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical flaws and lack of rigor** (multiple reviewers):
  - Equations 3 and 4 are mathematically incorrect (e.g., Eq3 should be \(p(A, R \mid \tau)\) instead of \(p(A \mid \tau)\); a typo: \(\tau\) should be \(\tau_1\)).
  - Proof 2.1 (line 869) is confusing: symbols like \(\lambda\) (portion of correct answers?), \(h\) (portion of hallucinated responses?) are left undefined; expressions like \(h \times (1-\lambda)/(k-1)\) and the inequality \(h \times \lambda > h \times (1-\lambda)/(k-1)\) are not justified. The assumption that hallucinated tokens randomly flip answers is dubious because tokens have unequal importance.
  - Section 5.1 makes an unreasonable assumption that biased prompts cause random answer flipping without justification.
  - The analysis in Section 4.1 states “the randomness in decision-making diminishes as the temperature decreases” – this is trivial. Equation 8 takes temperature to extremes to show answer flips, which is obvious and has no meaningful connection to self-improvement in practical settings.
  - The theory is more analogy than rigorous framework; it does not explore how internal representations change or why SC succeeds/fails at a deeper level.
- **Overstatement and limited practical gains**:
  - Performance improvements are marginal even under ideal conditions. In Table 1, the increase is 0.08% (75.92%→76.00%). Across Tables 1&2, gains are typically less than 2%. One reviewer questions the practical impact given these minimal gains and no comparison of computational cost.
  - Line 311 claims “GPT-3.5 follows Order 1 of Eq. (5), whereas GPT4 follows Order 2” without quantitative evidence.
  - The philosophical claim “LLMs are naturally underperforming their intrinsic ability” is underdetermined (positive reviewer’s own criticism).
- **Limited model scope and missing ablations**:
  - Only two API-based (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) and two open-source models. No ablation on model sizes within a series (e.g., 7B, 13B, 70B) or on other architectures.
  - This restricts generalizability – unclear if SC behavior holds across model capacities or other architectures (e.g., non-transformer).
- **Presentation and readability issues**:
  - Paper is “very difficult to read” (two reviewers). The equation-heavy sections lack explanations.
  - Figure 1 lacks key notation (\(\tau_1, R_1, D\)). Critical prompt examples are relegated to the appendix. The term “unbiased” in the caption is not clearly defined.
  - Minor typo in line 283: “(a1/T −(1−α)1/T)” should be “α” not “a”.
- **Missing related work and discussions**:
  - The paper rebuts prior works (e.g., Huang et al.) but omits several relevant studies that cast doubt on intrinsic SC: “Self-[In]Correct: LLMs Struggle with Discriminating Self-Generated Responses” (2024), “LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location” (2024, Google). It also misses works on introspection (Crystal, 2023), self-correction learning (“Generating sequences by learning to self-correct”, 2023), and Self-Instruct (2023). These should be discussed explicitly.
  - Misleading phrasing: “Feng et al. (2023) argues that CoT increases the effective depth of the circuit…” – Feng et al.’s argument applies to some choice of weights, not automatically to pre-trained LLMs.
- **Questions not adequately addressed**:
  - What are the novel contributions over prior work on intrinsic SC (e.g., Pan et al. survey 2023)?
  - Why do some experimental results fail to show a clear advantage of SC? Is it from the method itself or other failure modes?
  - The marginal gains are not analyzed for their causes (model biases, reasoning limits, etc.).
