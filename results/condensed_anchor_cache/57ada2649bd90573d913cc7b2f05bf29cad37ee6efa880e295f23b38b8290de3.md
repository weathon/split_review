## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper reviews and critically examines assumptions in prior work on simplicity bias (SB) and shortcut learning. It argues that (1) the notion of “spurious features” depends on knowledge of the test domain, (2) definitions of simplicity are inconsistent or hard to quantify, and (3) the common “two features” assumption (one core, one spurious) does not reflect real data. A toy experiment with many features is introduced to analyze SB in a controlled setting. The authors then survey existing mitigation methods and critique their assumptions.

### Strengths
- The paper surveys the simplicity bias field fairly comprehensively and is clearly written, with extensive citations from the literature.
- It correctly shows that input sensitivity alone is not always a good proxy for simplicity bias, via a constructed example where a model has low complexity but high input sensitivity (Section 2.3.1).
- A toy dataset comprising many features is introduced to study SB in a controlled setting.
- Experiments and arguments against the “two features” hypothesis are nice, suggesting that current SB mitigation methods may not scale to real-world data.
- The paper tests and compares various definitions of simplicity from prior work, raising useful conceptual questions.

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and contribution.**  
- The core argument that “spurious features” are only meaningful relative to an assumed or known test domain is not a new message to the community. Distribution shift between train and test is a necessary condition for spurious correlations to cause performance degradation – this is well-established (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
- Section 3.1’s observation that with sufficient training data and no distribution shift, models can learn any predictive feature is neither surprising nor a blind spot in earlier work (Reviewer 1).  
- Section 3.2’s finding that spurious features can lead to suboptimal in-distribution performance was previously demonstrated (e.g., Section 5 of arXiv:2006.07710) (Reviewer 1).  
- Similar points about learning under unknown distribution shifts have been made before (e.g., Adepalli et al. 2023 also considers unknown shifts and shows reducing SB improves generalization) (Reviewer 2).  
- The paper overstates the prevalence of the two-features assumption. Several prior works show SB with multiple features of varying predictivity/simplicity (e.g., arXiv:2006.12433, arXiv:2006.07710; even waterbirds exhibits fine-grained subpopulation biases per arXiv:2211.12491) (Reviewer 1). Works that detect multiple spurious correlations (Singla & Feizi 2021, Moayeri et al. 2023) go beyond the two-features model and are not cited (Reviewer 2).

**Definitions.**  
- The paper critiques others’ definitions of “simple features” as hard to quantify, but its own adopted definition of “features” from Ilyas et al. (any function mapping input to a scalar) is so general it provides limited practical insight (Reviewer 1).  
- The counter-example against input sensitivity as a definition of simplicity ignores other definitions of simplicity that also exist in the literature (Reviewer 2).

**Empirical evidence.**  
- The paper lacks experiments on real datasets and practical neural network architectures (e.g., CNNs, transformers); all experiments are on a toy MLP model (Reviewers 3, 4).  
- Counter-examples appear very contrived; while this is acknowledged, a discussion of practical relevance is missing (Reviewer 2).  
- The paper provides no empirical or theoretical support for its critiques of common assumptions, beyond listing papers (Reviewer 4).  
- For the toy counter-example, it would be important to test whether any existing SB mitigation method can learn the complex feature (e.g., \(f_5\)); if some can, the claims weaken (Reviewer 2).  
- The statement “not learning the simplest feature does not ensure the most complex is learnt” oversimplifies the relationship – whether models learn complex features or overfit depends critically on dataset size (Reviewer 1).

**Missing recommendations.**  
- The paper would benefit from concrete recommendations: a better definition of simple features, a dataset beyond the two-feature assumption, or guidance on which assumptions future SB work can relax (Reviewer 2).

**Scope and framing.**  
- The paper reads more like a survey or blog post than a research paper with a focused problem statement, technical contribution, or theoretical framework (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
- While a survey can be valuable, at a top venue like ICLR it requires original insights grounded in strong evidence, which this paper lacks (Reviewer 4).

**Minor.**  
- Line 66: “conversational” should be “conventional” (Reviewer 3).
