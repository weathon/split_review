## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates performance heterogeneity in graph-level tasks for message-passing and transformer-based GNNs. It introduces heterogeneity profiles, uses Tree Mover’s Distance (TMD) to show that both topology and features influence heterogeneity, and proposes a selective rewiring approach and a depth-selection heuristic based on spectral alignment. Experiments on several benchmarks are used to validate these techniques.

### Strengths
- **Important research problem.** Performance heterogeneity is a well‑recognized and valuable problem in graph learning (Reviewer 1; also noted as important by Reviewers 3 and 4 despite disagreement on novelty).
- **New analytical tool – heterogeneity profiles.** The introduction of heterogeneity profiles offers a new methodological avenue for analyzing performance across graphs in graph‑level learning (Reviewer 2). (Reviewer 3 questions the novelty of this technique – see Weaknesses.)
- **Promising selective rewiring approach.** The proposed selective rewiring is a pragmatic solution that may simplify model training by only targeting graphs whose performance benefits from rewiring (Reviewers 1 and 2).
- **Intriguing depth‑spectrum link.** The observation that optimal network depth depends on the graph’s spectrum is interesting, and the resulting heuristic for selecting GNN layers is validated by experimental results (Reviewer 1).
- **Well‑designed experiments.** The experiments cover multiple datasets and configurations (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Generalization and evaluation scale.** The study uses relatively small datasets (MUTAG, PROTEINS) that are known to be unstable and have high variance (Reviewer 4). It is unclear whether conclusions hold on challenging large‑scale benchmarks such as OGB (e.g., ogbg‑ppa, ogbg‑code2) (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Moreover, evaluation choices are inconsistent across sections: Section 3.2 uses three datasets with GCN and GraphGPS; Section 3.3 shifts to MUTAG and GIN; Section 4 tests GAT but not GraphGPS, even though all sections address the same heterogeneity claim (Reviewer 4). Only one graph transformer (GraphGPS) is tested, which is insufficient to support the claim that both “message‑passing and transformer‑based GNNs display performance heterogeneity” (line 102); a diverse range of graph transformers (e.g., from [3,4]) should be considered (Reviewer 4).

2. **Limited novelty and insight.** Multiple reviewers found the contributions insufficiently novel. It is already well‑known that GNN performance varies and that different models perform differently across datasets (Reviewer 4, citing [1,2]). The claim that topology alone cannot explain heterogeneity is not new; prior node‑level work [1] has considered this (Reviewer 3). The “heterogeneity profiles” appear to be a standard random experiment / k‑fold validation rather than a novel technique (Reviewer 3). The Tree Mover’s Distance is directly adopted from another paper, and it is unclear what new insight it provides over other metrics (e.g., FID) that also combine structure and features (Reviewer 3). While two reviewers (1,2) found the contributions promising, two reviewers (3,4) considered the novelty and insights insufficient.

3. **Lack of state‑of‑the‑art comparisons and concrete empirical evidence.** The paper does not compare its methods against existing state‑of‑the‑art approaches that address similar challenges in GNNs (e.g., attention mechanisms, advanced architectures). There is no demonstration that the proposed methods achieve state‑of‑the‑art results on even one or two benchmark datasets, undermining their perceived effectiveness and practical relevance (Reviewer 2). The paper fails to propose a concrete, well‑validated solution to the heterogeneity problem (Reviewer 4).

4. **Incomplete experimental and hyperparameter details.** The paper does not describe the hyperparameter tuning process (search space, optimization strategy such as random/grid/Bayesian) and lacks ablation studies on hyperparameters (hidden dimensions, dropout, activation, normalization). This makes it difficult to assess the sensitivity of results to these choices (Reviewer 2). Additionally, the determination of “difficult” graphs in line 298 is unclear: Figure 3 provides quantitative metrics but does not specify which exact graphs are considered difficult for both GIN and GraphGPS, despite the claim that they are nearly identical (Reviewer 2).

5. **Shallow theoretical depth and unclear connections.** The paper attempts to link heterogeneity with graph rewiring and over‑smoothing via the diffusion property (Fiedler eigenvalue), but the explanation is not strong. It is unclear how the diffusive property of graph structure affects training or generalization, and why this concept, which primarily affects node‑level tasks, is applicable to graph‑level tasks (Reviewer 3). The selective rewiring criteria are not derived from the empirical or theoretical results; more details are needed (Reviewer 3). The connections among performance heterogeneity, over‑smoothing, and rewiring are presented superficially, possibly due to page limits (Reviewer 3).

6. **Unclear applicability to new scenarios.** For new graph datasets, there is no confidence measure or guidance on how to apply the selective rewiring or the heuristic depth prediction. The proposed solutions lack a clear protocol for deployment on novel data (Reviewer 1).

7. **Poor writing and presentation quality.** The paper contains numerous grammatical errors and incomplete sentences (e.g., lines 250, 352, 168, 576, 187). Some sentences lack clear connections to surrounding text (e.g., “Size generalization in GNNs has been studied in…”). The overall English quality is inadequate and unprofessional, detracting from clarity and credibility (Reviewer 4). The presentation attempts to connect too many concepts without sufficient depth (Reviewer 3).
