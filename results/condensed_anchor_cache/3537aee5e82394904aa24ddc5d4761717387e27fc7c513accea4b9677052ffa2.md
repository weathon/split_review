## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a CLIP-based multimodal data selection framework that leverages both image and text information to improve robustness and generalizability in sample selection. The framework consists of three modules: dataset adaptation (using adapters to transfer pretrained knowledge to target data), sample scoring (semantic alignment and diversity scores based on multimodal features), and selection optimization (multi-objective SGD to select semantically representative and diverse samples). Extensive experiments on several benchmarks show state-of-the-art results across various datasets, settings, and architectures.

### Strengths
- The idea of exploiting multimodal features from a pretrained vision-language model (CLIP) for data selection is novel and distinguishes this work from prior unimodal approaches.
- The method is simple, easy to understand and follow; the algorithm and overall writing are clear (reviewers 1-4).
- The use of an SGD-based selection optimization with a multi-objective (alignment + diversity + selection ratio) is interesting and efficient, differing from existing combinatorial sampling strategies.
- The scoring system (SAS and SDS) is well-defined, and visualizations effectively demonstrate the advantages of multimodal selection.
- Experimental results are strong: the method consistently outperforms baselines on diverse datasets, under noisy/corrupted conditions, and across different model architectures.
- The framework has broad applications for improving dataset quality and model robustness, potentially benefiting many machine learning domains.

### Weaknesses
- **Bias from CLIP:** The method inherits biases (demographic, object representation, etc.) from the pretrained CLIP model, which may propagate into the selected dataset. No bias mitigation or analysis is provided, and it is unclear whether the optimization for alignment and diversity amplifies or reduces these biases. The paper does not discuss controlling bias in the selected dataset or downstream models.
- **Adapter training with noisy/corrupted data:** The adapter training assumes clean, perfect data. In realistic settings with high noise ratios (e.g., 50%), using the same noisy data to train the adapter may degrade performance—reviewers question whether it would be better to omit the adapter under such conditions. The authors should discuss and ideally evaluate the adapter’s effectiveness when trained on imperfect/noisy data.
- **Comparison to unimodal prototype baselines:** The class label is used as a text prototype. Prior work (e.g., using average image features as prototypes and Euclidean distance, similar to SAS) could achieve a similar effect without text. The paper lacks a comparison against such unimodal prototype-based methods to demonstrate that introducing text is essential.
- **Missing recent methods and low-ratio baselines:** MoSo is cited as NeurIPS’23 (not ’24). The evaluation omits methods from 2024 (e.g., [c]) and some typical approaches known to work well at low sampling ratios (e.g., [d]).
- **Selection optimization complexity:** The multi-objective optimization module may be computationally expensive for very large datasets; details on computational efficiency and scalability are needed.
- **Generality beyond vision:** The framework is only tested on vision-based datasets. Its applicability to text or mixed-modality datasets, or scenarios with high modality imbalance, remains unclear.
- **Convergence and stability of STE:** The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) used in the optimization might cause convergence issues given the non-differentiable selection process.
- **Parameter sensitivity (selection loss):** Ablation shows that selection loss has a significant impact, but the paper does not discuss sensitivity to this parameter.
- **Fairness of cost comparison:** The Actual Selection Costs reported in Appendix G should clarify whether they include adapter training time; otherwise the comparison may be unfair.
- **Notation inconsistency and typos:** The variable `d` (sample-wise parameter) can be confused with feature dimension `d`; `d` is missing from Figure 1, and in Figure 2 the learnable parameter should be `d` rather than `w`. Table 3 caption needs correction and could be merged with Table 2.
- **Definition of “noisy” in real-world datasets:** The paper does not clarify how noisy/damaged samples are defined and quantified for the real-world noisy data experiments.
