- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 8, 3, 8, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes RILe, a trainer-student framework for adversarial imitation learning. A trainer agent (trained via RL) generates a dynamic reward function for the student using discriminator feedback and its own action, aiming to provide graduated guidance beyond binary expert/non-expert classifications. Evaluated on LocoMujoco tasks, RILe outperforms GAIL and AIRL. Reviewers found the idea novel and the motivation compelling, but raised substantial concerns about insufficient support for key claims, limited experimental scope (only GAIL and AIRL on one benchmark, missing standard deviations, hyperparameters, and task details), unclear reward design (Eq. 8, role of \(a^T\)), theoretical issues (ill-defined MDP due to stacked RL agents, incomplete Lemma 1 proof), organizational inconsistencies, lack of ablation studies, and no quantitative analysis of computational efficiency. Scores: 8, 3, 8, 5 — indicating strong disagreement; one reviewer (score 3) had particularly critical concerns about the method’s theoretical soundness and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- **Novel decoupling**: Separating reward function learning from the student and discriminator is an innovative approach (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Compelling motivation**: Reducing reliance on static discriminators in complex tasks is a valuable problem and the intuition behind the trainer is clear (Reviewer 3).
- **Technical contribution**: The trainer’s dynamic reward enables progressive feedback, addressing insufficient guidance in early training (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Clear positioning**: The background and related work are well-organized, effectively situating RILe relative to existing methods (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **Effective visualizations**: Figure 3 clearly illustrates the evolution of reward functions compared to baselines (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Significant performance gains**: Table 1 shows 2x improvement over GAIL/AIRL on complex LocoMujoco tasks (Reviewers 1, 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient support for key claims**:  
   - *Adaptability claim*: Only supported by Figure 3, which uses a single simple setting; moreover, in the second and third columns of Fig. 3, GAIL appears to learn faster and its reward contours are closer to the ground truth (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
   - *Outperformance claim*: Lacks experimental details (task settings, hyperparameters of baselines, standard deviations or confidence intervals), and only GAIL and AIRL are compared. Missing more recent adversarial IL methods (e.g., DRAIL [1]) that also address learning efficiency (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
   - *Computational efficiency claim*: No quantitative analysis, even though RILe adds a trainer network compared to GAIL (Reviewers 3, 4).  
   - *Lemma 1*: The proof is incomplete; the connection between assumptions and the result is not clearly established, and several intermediate steps are missing (Reviewer 2).  

2. **Theoretical and framework concerns**:  
   - *Ill-defined MDP*: Stacking the trainer (RL agent) on the student (RL agent) creates a dependency where the trainer’s environment transition is not exogenous — it depends on the student’s policy, which in turn depends on the trainer’s policy. This makes the MDP ill-defined and introduces potential training instability (Reviewer 2).  
   - *Ad-hoc workaround*: The paper mentions freezing the trainer mid-training to address instability, but this is only discussed in the Discussion section and its implementation in experiments is not described (Reviewer 2).  
   - *Long-term performance claim*: Line 241 states the trainer optimizes for long-term performance, but all AIL methods use RL for policy learning. How does RILe specifically benefit from RL in a way that other methods do not? (Reviewers 3, 4).  

3. **Unclear trainer reward design**:  
   - Equation 8: The reward \(v(D_{\phi}(s,a))\,a^T\) is not well-motivated. It is unclear how this combination encourages long-term planning or better feedback. The term \(a^T\) (binary) seems to incentivize the trainer to always output \(a^T=1\) without further constraint, and the explanation that it “learns to adjust its policy based on the effectiveness of its previous actions” is insufficient (Reviewers 1, 2).  

4. **Missing experimental details**:  
   - Number of training environment steps per task not reported (Reviewer 1).  
   - No confidence intervals in Table 1 (Reviewer 1, also raised by Reviewer 2).  
   - Hyperparameter values for baseline methods (GAIL, AIRL) and for RILe not provided (Reviewer 2).  
   - Figure 4: definition of normalized scores and steps unclear; their purpose for RILe not explained (Reviewers 3, 4).  

5. **Choice of benchmark and scope**:  
   - LocoMujoco lacks action labels, making it a Learning-from-Observation (LfO) setting. The reviewer recommends justifying task selection or performing separate LfO experiments with appropriate baselines (e.g., BCO, GAIfO). Standard LfD tasks (e.g., MuJoCo) would allow action labels and comparisons with methods like IQLearn (Reviewers 3, 4).  

6. **Organizational errors and inconsistencies**:  
   - Entropy regularization is introduced in Section 3.2, but the RL formulation in Section 3.3 omits it before adding it again in Eq. 2 (Reviewer 2).  
   - Equation 14 has a typo: the first term should be \(v(D_{\phi}(s_t))a_t\) instead of \(D_{\phi}(s_t)\) to match Eq. 11 (Reviewer 2).  
   - Freezing the trainer is only mentioned in the Discussion, not in the experimental setting, leaving ambiguity about how it was applied (Reviewer 2).  

7. **Additional criticisms reflected in reviewer questions**:  
   - How does the dynamic reward handle rapidly changing or stochastic environments? (Reviewer 3).  
   - Sensitivity of RILe to hyperparameters across trainer, student, and discriminator; no tuning strategy recommended (Reviewer 3).  
   - Robustness to changing noise distribution or sparse expert data not analyzed (Reviewer 3).  
   - In Figure 4, with 100% expert data in the trainer’s replay buffer, performance remains good even though the trainer should not be trained on student trajectories — this is puzzling (Reviewer 2).  
   - The constant \(v(x)=2x-1\) is not explained (Reviewer 1).  

[1] Lai et al., “Diffusion-Reward Adversarial Imitation Learning,” arXiv:2405.16194, 2024.
