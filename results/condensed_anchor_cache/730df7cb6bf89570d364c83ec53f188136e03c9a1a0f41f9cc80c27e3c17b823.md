## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces layer-wise linear mode connectivity (LLMC), studying the loss barriers when averaging only certain layers of neural networks. Across architectures (CNNs, ViTs, LLMs) and datasets, the authors find that individual layers often have no barrier even when the full model has one. The paper also examines cumulative barriers from groups of layers, a toy deep linear network, the relationship to random perturbations, and implications for personalized federated learning.

### Strengths
- The motivation for studying LLMC is novel and interesting, contributing new ideas to the linear mode connectivity community (R2, R3).
- The empirical study is thorough across models/datasets and includes ViTs and LLMs, showing prevalence across architectures (R1, R3).
- Section 4 and 5.2 are strong, emphasizing that certain layers may provide better averaging directions in the loss surface (R1). For example, the most sensitive layers of ViTs are the early attention and fully-connected weights (R2).
- The finding that averaging directions have much higher curvature than random perturbations of the same norm is intriguing (R2).
- Narrative construction is good and the literature review is comprehensive (R2). The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R4).
- The paper provides specific practice guidance for federated learning (R4).
- Certain experimental outcomes are interesting: middle layers cause cumulative barriers, and averaging directions are special compared to random ones (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Need for baselines to show LLMC is not trivial:** The core observation may be a natural consequence of averaging fewer parameters. The paper does not compare the observed barrier from averaging a subset of layers to what would be expected from averaging the same proportion of parameters randomly (e.g., if averaging \(1/s\) layers leads to a loss increase significantly less than \(1/s\) of the full-model barrier, that would support LLMC). A baseline of random perturbations of the same norm as the interpolation is also missing (R1, R2). Reviewer 1 suggests: “what would the barrier be for a random perturbation of the same size as \(\alpha=0.5\)? If you get the same result, there seems minimal benefit to averaging.”
- **Theoretical analysis mismatch with experiments:** Theorem 4.1 handles single-layer interpolation, while experiments average groups of layers. Analysis should extend to multi-layer interpolation. Additionally, the theorem does not incorporate activation functions (ReLU, Sigmoid), so it does not reflect practical network complexities (R2, R3).
- **Robustness explanation not deeper than same-basin explanation:** Section 5.1 claims robustness to random perturbations explains LLMC, but it is not surprising that the interpolation direction (pointing toward the basin) has lower curvature than a random direction; this is already implied by the original LMC concept (R4). Reviewer 4 also asks how robustness to random perturbations is exactly calculated and which definition of LLMC (single layer vs cumulative) is used in Section 5.1.
- **Section 5.1 conclusions hard to parse:** It is unclear whether claims about higher curvature for averaging directions refer only to layerwise results (the full model shows the opposite trend). The random perturbation should be per-layer with the same norm as the layer-wise interpolation (R1).
- **Cumulative LLMC analysis insufficient:** The experiments with groups of layers do not control for the number of layers aggregated. A moving-window experiment with a fixed window size \(l\) (e.g., for a 20-layer network with \(l=5\), test LLMC of layers 1–5, 2–6, …, 16–20) is needed to support the conclusion that middle layers cause barriers, because the current setup confounds barrier magnitude with number of layers (R3).
- **Personalized federated learning discussion underdeveloped:** The paper should implement and compare methods for personalized layers or layer-wise aggregation, propose a new method based on the findings, and run experiments on feature-shift non-iid to verify that layer-wise connectivity can handle domain adaptation across clients (R3). Table 1 uses ‘non-iid’ and ‘path.’ (pathological non-iid); partial averaging performs better in the pathological setting than in less severe non-iid, which is counterintuitive and requires explanation (R2).
- **Definitional clarity needed:** The LLMC barrier is defined as the maximum error relative to the average of the two full models, not the average of one full model and that model with a specified layer swapped (R1, question). A figure similar to Figure 3 but showing barrier computation would help (R1). There are two competing definitions (single-layer vs cumulative); Section 5.1 uses which? (R4)
- **Figure interpretation unclear:** In Fig. 2(a), both middle and deep layer averaging result in high losses, which contradicts the text claim that middle layers cause barriers (R2).
- One reviewer (R2) rated soundness 2 (fair), noting the novelty may be incremental and the theoretical-experimental gap, while others rated soundness 3 (good). This presents a minority view that the paper is less rigorous than claimed.
