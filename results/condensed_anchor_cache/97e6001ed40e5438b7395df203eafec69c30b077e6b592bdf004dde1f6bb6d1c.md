## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces U-MATH, a benchmark of 1,125 unpublished, open-ended university-level problems sourced from real teaching materials, balanced across six core subjects (Pre-Calculus, Algebra, Differential Calculus, Integral Calculus, Multivariable Calculus, Sequences & Series), with approximately 20% of problems requiring image understanding. A complementary meta-evaluation dataset, µ-MATH (340 problems with LLM-generated solutions manually labeled correct/incorrect), is designed to assess LLMs’ ability to judge solution correctness. Evaluations of general-domain, math-specific, and multimodal LLMs show the highest text accuracy is 53% and visual accuracy 30% (while the abstract claims 63% and 45% respectively, a notable discrepancy). The best LLM judge achieves an F1-score of 76% on µ-MATH (abstract states 80%). The datasets and evaluation code are open-sourced.

### Strengths
1. **University-level focus**: The inclusion of college-level problems (covering Pre-Calculus, Algebra, Differential Calculus, Integral Calculus, Multivariable Calculus, Sequences & Series) is a significant advancement over existing benchmarks that mainly target elementary or high-school tasks.
2. **Multimodal inclusion**: 20% of problems involve visual components (images), challenging LLMs to reason across textual and visual formats.
3. **Meta-evaluation dataset (µ-MATH)**: A novel approach to evaluate LLMs’ ability to assess free-form mathematical solutions, addressing biases and limitations in current evaluation practices. It contains 340 questions with LLM-generated answers manually labeled as correct or incorrect.
4. **Authentic sourcing**: Problems are collected from actual university teaching materials, ensuring practical relevance and alignment with real academic standards.
5. **Subject balance**: The dataset is well-balanced across six core mathematical subjects, enabling comprehensive evaluation of LLMs across different areas.
6. **Model comparison**: The paper compares multiple LLMs (general, math-specific, multimodal) and reports clear results (text accuracy up to 53%, visual accuracy 30%), demonstrating the persistent challenges in advanced mathematical reasoning and visual problem-solving.
7. **Open-source release**: Datasets and evaluation code are publicly available to facilitate further research.

### Weaknesses
1. **Small dataset size**: At 1,125 samples, the U-MATH dataset is considered too small to convincingly evaluate LLMs’ mathematical abilities at the university level. Coverage of advanced topics within each subject appears limited (e.g., calculus may lack multivariable calculus or differential equations; linear algebra may not extend to vector spaces or linear transformations). The dataset may skew toward direct computation problems and underrepresent proof-based or abstract reasoning.
2. **µ-MATH size and representativeness**: With only 340 samples, the meta-evaluation set may not adequately capture variability in problem-solving strategies and reasoning nuances. The dataset could be skewed toward certain problem types (e.g., direct computation over proof-based reasoning), leading to an incomplete picture of LLM evaluator capabilities.
3. **LLM-as-judge bias**: The reliance on LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) to evaluate free-form answers can introduce biases and inconsistencies. LLMs may favor solutions that align with their training data rather than strictly mathematical correctness, especially for complex, multi-step derivations where slight deviations might be overlooked. No mitigation measures are described.
4. **LLM-generated solutions in µ-MATH**: The use of LLM-generated solutions may limit diversity and challenge due to inherent model tendencies, risking circularity where the evaluation set reflects the biases of the models being evaluated rather than an objective benchmark.
5. **Missing visual examples**: No examples of problems requiring visual input are provided in the paper, making it difficult to assess the nature of multimodal tasks.
6. **Insufficient evaluation metrics**: Only accuracy is reported in Table 4. Additional metrics like perplexity or metrics to assess handling of ambiguous problems (e.g., WinoGrande-style ACC) are recommended to better gauge true understanding.
7. **Lack of dataset analysis**: Details on question diversity, difficulty distribution, reasoning steps required, and topic coverage within each subject are missing. For example, it is unclear whether problems require recall, multi-step algebraic manipulation, calculus, or proof techniques.
8. **Access link**: The paper states the dataset is released but does not provide an access link (though open-source is mentioned in the abstract).
9. **Undefined “complex”**: The paper states U-MATH aims to promote research on “complex” math problems, but this term is not defined (higher grade, more challenging knowledge, or more reasoning steps?).
10. **Generalization unclear**: It is not clear how findings generalize to other levels or types of mathematical reasoning; future work on transferability is needed.
11. **LLM judge accuracy not fully reported**: Accuracy of LLMs as judges (especially for higher mathematics where answers may be equivalent but differently formatted) is not provided. The best F1 is 76%, but the discrepancy between reported numbers (abstract: 80%) and reviewer observations (76%) should be clarified.
12. **Data size discrepancy**: Reviewers consistently report 1,125 problems in U-MATH and 340 in µ-MATH, while the abstract states 1,100 and 80% F1; these inconsistencies need resolution.
