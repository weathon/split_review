## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces 3D-Prover, a method that augments LLM-based tactic generation for automated theorem proving by filtering sampled tactics using Determinantal Point Processes (DPPs) to select a diverse and high-quality subset. Tactic representations are learned via a transition model that encodes the goal and tactic, then decodes the resulting environment (next goals or error) and predicts success likelihood and execution time. 3D-Prover is evaluated on miniF2F-valid and miniF2F-test by augmenting ReProver, showing improved proof success rates and tactic success rates compared to Top-K and Random filtering baselines, with ablations validating the representation learning component. Reviewers note the motivation is sound (pruning the exponential search space, especially since many tactics are semantically similar or lead to errors) and the approach is practical. However, concerns are raised about limited comparisons to state-of-the-art provers, lack of a no-filtering baseline, minimal gains on miniF2F-test, potential data leakage in training the transition model on miniF2F-valid, and insufficient justification for architectural and hyperparameter choices. One reviewer (score 8) is strongly positive, citing well-motivated, effective, and practical design; two reviewers (score 5) and one (score 6) are more critical of evaluation scope and clarity.

### Strengths
- The approach is novel and well-motivated: it directly addresses the problem of semantically similar tactics and the high proportion (75%) of tactics leading to execution errors, which prior work did not factor in. The use of Determinantal Point Processes (DPPs) for diversity-driven filtering is innovative in the theorem proving context.
- 3D-Prover can be layered on top of any underlying LLM tactic generator (here ReProver) without modifying the model, and it successfully prunes a large fraction (87.5%) of sampled tactics while improving proof success rates over Top-K and Random filtering.
- The transition model’s semantic-aware tactic embeddings are shown to be useful via ablation (replacing the encoder with an autoencoder degrades performance), and the representation learning is well-established and analyzed.
- The filtering enables deeper proofs and more efficient search under limited computational resources; improvements are observed even with small K (e.g., K=8). The approach offers a fresh perspective on tree search in theorem proving.
- (Minority positive: Reviewer 4 is particularly positive, emphasizing that the framework is practical, each component’s impact is demonstrated experimentally, and the results advance proof synthesis without requiring model retraining.)

### Weaknesses
- The transition model architecture (encode goal+tactic, then decoder for next goals/error and MLP predictor for error and time) is not justified. Why not a single decoder predicting the entire tuple? No discussion of alternatives considered or experimental comparisons to other architectures.
- Training data for the transition model: 498,236 transitions are collected from a vanilla ReProver attempt on miniF2F-valid and randomly split 95/5. It is unclear whether the same (goal, tactic, next-goal) triplet could appear in other proofs and cause overlap between train and test sets. More critically, the transition model is trained on miniF2F-valid and then evaluated on the same benchmark, potentially explaining the significantly better results on valid vs. test. Results on miniF2F-test show only marginal improvements: pass@1 from 22.4% to 24.4% (K=8) and from 27.8% to 28.2% (K=32).
- The paper does not compare against a no-filtering baseline (i.e., using all 64 sampled tactics) or against other state-of-the-art theorem provers (e.g., DeepSeek-Prover, InternLM-Math). Comparisons are only to Top-K and Random filtering, so it remains unclear whether the diversity filter is more beneficial than simply using those extra computational resources for more evaluations or than other advanced filtering methods.
- Evaluation metrics for the transition model’s output (BLEU, ROUGE-L-F1, top-4 accuracy) measure similarity to ground-truth next state, but high similarity does not guarantee a syntactically and semantically correct subsequent tactic state.
- The hyperparameter sweep (Appendix A.2) shows that λ_s and λ_τ (weights on predicted success and execution time) are both set to zero in the experiments, indicating these terms do not contribute to filtering performance. This lack of impact is not discussed in the main text, and the motivation for including execution time as a factor is questioned: longer tactics like `linarith` can be more effective despite taking longer, and execution time varies with goal state.
- The method filters only after LM sampling, so the computational cost of the LM is unchanged. It is not shown whether using the same resources to run the Lean prover on all LM-generated tactics would yield better overall results. Also, the language model may not generate sufficiently diverse tactics, so the method’s primary advantage may be efficiency (which remains unquantified against a no-filtering baseline).
- The preliminaries and transition model description (Sections 1.2 and 2.1) are hard to follow, with circuitous notations. The decoder’s input concatenates an encoded representation `e` with the goal string `g` again, but `e` already encodes `g` and `t`; the rationale for this duplication and the mixing of encoded and non-encoded strings is unclear.
- Reviewer 1 requested including top-k results for k=1–4 or 5 in Table 1, not just top-4. The paper lacks such granularity.
- Several numerical claims (e.g., “∼36% relative improvement (Table 1)” and “∼6–9% relative improvements (Table 7)”) appear in the text but the quoted values are not located in the paper’s tables as referred.
