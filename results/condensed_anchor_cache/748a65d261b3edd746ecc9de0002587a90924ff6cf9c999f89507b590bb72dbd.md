## Merged Review

### Summary
This empirical study investigates whether investing in advanced imputation methods yields significantly better predictions. Across 19 benchmark datasets, 4 imputation methods (mean, soft impute, MICE with RF, MissForest), 3 predictive models (MLP, XGBoost, linear model), and variations including missingness indicators and semi-synthetic linear outcomes, the authors show that imputation accuracy matters less when using expressive models (e.g., XGBoost), when incorporating missingness indicators (even under MCAR), and matters much more for generated linear outcomes than real-data outcomes. Improvement in prediction is typically ≤10% of the gain in imputation R². The authors conclude that on real data with powerful models, improving imputation has only a minor effect on prediction performance.

### Strengths
- The paper effectively bridges theoretical work on the minimal asymptotic impact of imputation with an empirical evaluation of the "impute-then-predict" pipeline, offering a rigorous assessment of *when* imputation accuracy affects prediction.
- The study is significant as a well-conducted null result: it provides evidence that a common practice (advanced imputation) often brings limited benefit, saving the community from re-inventing square wheels. This fits the notion of originality as recommending a reformulation of the problem.
- The experimental setup is clearly described in detail for reproducibility, and the authors will release code upon publication. Experiments span 19 datasets, multiple imputation methods, prediction models, MCAR and MNAR settings, with extensive analysis including critical difference plots.
- Related work discussion is clear, covering both theoretical and empirical studies on missing value imputation.
- The paper presents novel results even under the simple MCAR setting (e.g., missingness indicators help prediction despite MCAR).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and positioning**: Both key findings—(a) that better imputation yields only marginal prediction gains and (b) that missingness indicators help even in MCAR—are already established. Le Morvan et al. (2021) showed both theoretically and empirically (their Figure 4) that performance of an oracle imputer > MICE > mean imputation, and that missing indicators benefit MCAR. Several earlier benchmarks (Paterakis 2024, Shadbahr 2023, Perez-Lebel 2022, Jäger 2021, Woznica 2020) have shown simple imputation methods are competitive and gains from advanced imputation are marginal. The paper does not sufficiently acknowledge these existing results or justify why they need to be revisited. Reviewer 4 (score 6, confidence 5) questions the novel contribution; Reviewer 1 (score 3, confidence 3) finds the evidence weak.
- **Statistical rigor**: Many conclusions rely on small differences in averages with overlapping confidence intervals, without formal significance tests. For example, Figure 2 shows only mean imputation clearly worse; the other three imputers show no definitive quality difference. The regression slopes in Figure 4 (imputation vs. prediction accuracy) are very small, especially at 20% missing, and correlation coefficients are not reported. The semi-synthetic result (Figure 5) also shows a very small slope. Reviewer 1 notes that more robust statistical testing is needed.
- **Missingness indicator benefit under MCAR**: While interesting, the paper does not provide a theoretical justification or causal explanation beyond citing Le Morvan et al. (2021). Reviewer 3 suggests analyzing the correlation between reconstruction error and gain from the indicator (expecting strong correlation), and performing a control experiment with a random mask appended to rule out the effect of added parameters or regularization.
- **Limited exploration of conditions where advanced imputation helps**: The analysis could be enriched by examining datasets with varying degrees of non-linearity and feature interaction complexity. For example, MissForest might show greater benefit on datasets with complex interactions. Reviewer 5 suggests that while XGBoost may not benefit much from MissForest, less flexible models like MLP show greater improvements with advanced imputation—this contrast is under-discussed.
- **Breakdown by dataset dimensionality**: About one-third of the benchmark datasets have small dimensionality, where 20% missing means only one missing feature. Reviewer 1 expects imputation method differences to be small in such cases and requests a breakdown by dataset dimensionality. The paper should also clarify whether missingness is applied per sample or per feature, which significantly affects interpretation.
- **MNAR experiment (Section 4.4)**: This section feels disconnected from the rest of the paper. As the authors note, most imputation methods without causal structure are invalid under MNAR. The results are not surprising or insightful, and the main takeaway is unclear. (Reviewer 1)
- **Missingness indicator limitations and alternatives**: The paper could discuss potential issues with missingness indicators (over-reliance, added noise) and compare alternatives such as polar encoding (Lenz et al. 2024) or structurally-informed architectures like NeuMiss (Le Morvan et al. 2021). (Reviewer 5)
- **Writing and clarity**: The paper requires substantial stylistic revision. Issues include redundant sentences, unnecessary adverbs (“really”, “interestingly”), unnecessary metadiscourse (e.g., “let me tell you what I’m going to tell you”), acronyms not defined on first occurrence, and inconsistent writing quality between paragraphs. Reviewer 2 (score 8) finds the reading arduous. Specific examples: “missingness indicators” should be defined in the introduction; line 140 “universally consistent” needs clarification; “semi-synthetic” on page 5 needs elaboration; line 341 uses “nuanced” in an incomprehensible way; line 456 sentence “For prediction, imputation matters but marginally.” would be clearer as “Imputation matters for prediction, but only marginally.”; proper casing for figures (e.g., “fig. 2” → “Fig. 2”); line 415 citation for Pereira 2024 missing from references; line 52 missing parentheses; line 85 “mportant” typo; line 223 repeats that hyperparameters were optimized with Optuna.
- **Questions requiring further analysis**:
    - Would larger missingness rates show more significant correlations between imputation accuracy and prediction performance? (Reviewer 1)
    - Does the correlation in Figures 3/4 differ by imputation method? (Reviewer 1)
    - What are the specific novel contributions justifying acceptance, given that both findings are already established? (Reviewer 4)
    - Could explainability techniques on models trained with missingness indicators show whether feature importance drops when missing? (Reviewer 1)
    - The paper states “comparing imputers is not our main objective” twice, yet the imputer comparison is a salient result (e.g., mean imputation performs worse). Should this be downplayed? (Reviewer 2)
