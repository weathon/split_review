## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents LSH-E, a KV cache compression method for LLMs that uses locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to evict tokens that are cosine-dissimilar to the current query. The algorithm computes Hamming distances between binarized Gaussian projections (SimHash) of the query and cached keys, with a projection length much smaller than the embedding dimension, and evicts the tokens with the lowest expected attention. Eviction happens pre-attention, reducing computational cost. The method maintains a lightweight binary structure in GPU memory and dynamically replaces evicted tokens with the current token's key and value at each decoding step. Experiments show 30%–70% cache compression while maintaining performance on reasoning, multiple-choice, long-context retrieval, and summarization tasks.

### Strengths

- The problem of KV cache memory consumption is well-motivated and practically significant.
- The algorithm is simple, clear, and computationally affordable due to greedy pre-attention eviction using lightweight binary operations.
- The use of LSH (SimHash) to approximate attention scores for eviction is an elegant approach; multiple reviewers note this as a key strength.
- The method keeps the compressed cache entirely in GPU memory, eliminating CPU-GPU data transfer latency.
- The static compression of 30%–70% with preserved accuracy is beneficial for LLM serving systems.
- The proposed method outperforms the L2-dropout baseline in the reported experiments.

**Note on disagreement:** Reviewer 4 strongly criticizes novelty, stating that LSH-based attention and memory management has appeared in prior work (KDEformer, Hyperattention, QJL, SubGen) and that these works are not cited. In contrast, Reviewer 6 finds the application of LSH to KV cache problems novel and well-motivated. Other reviewers are mixed (scores 3,5,5) on overall contribution.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing baselines and comparisons:** Only L2-dropout is used as a baseline in the main paper. Missing comparisons with standard KV cache compression methods including H2O, Scissorhands, StreamingLLM, SnapKV, SubGen, Loki, Quest, and attention-based eviction strategies. Supplementary materials include additional baselines but they are not in the main evaluation.
- **Limited evaluation scope:** Benchmarks are limited to two datasets per task, and improvements over L2 are not significant in Needle-in-a-Haystack, Common Words, and MedQA. Tasks requiring very long contexts (e.g., >50k tokens) are not tested; reviewers recommend LongBench with token lengths over 50,000.
- **Missing timing and throughput experiments:** No end-to-end wall-clock speedup, latency, or throughput numbers are reported. Execution time is a key metric for inference acceleration; its absence makes it difficult to assess the practical benefit of pre-attention eviction.
- **Missing ablations:** No ablation studies for the number of hardcoded first tokens (4) and recent tokens (10). The contribution of this design choice to overall performance is unclear. Similarly, no ablation of the LSH projection dimension or quantization parameters.
- **No theoretical guarantee or empirical validation of the greedy assumption:** The eviction decision is based on the current query's attention score, which may not reflect future relevance. Unlike H2O (which uses accumulated attention), no validation is provided that low attention for the current query implies low future importance. Reviewers request empirical results showing consistency of attention scores across query tokens.
- **Unclear aggregation of Hamming distances over time:** The paper does not clarify whether eviction is based only on the most recent Hamming distance or a more complex accumulation rule.
- **Potential for evicted tokens to become relevant later:** The policy does not address locality of reference—tokens evicted may be needed for future queries, especially in tasks with long-range dependencies.
- **No discussion of LSH-induced approximation error:** The error introduced by LSH compression versus exact cosine similarity is not analyzed. A plot of distortion error across compression levels is requested. A direct comparison with cosine-similarity-based eviction (accuracy, latency, memory) is missing.
- **Limited novelty claim and missing related work:** Prior LSH-based attention methods (KDEformer, Hyperattention, QJL, SubGen) and memory-efficient methods (Reformer) are not cited or compared. The distinction from Reformer is inadequately explained.
- **Presentation issues:** Figures have small axis captions; there is a misleading statement on line 145 where "dist" is used for cosine similarity; a possible typo on line 419 ("does" vs "does not"); line 180 has missing quotation marks.
- **Missing experiments on multi-head attention handling:** It is unclear whether LSH compression is applied per head or shared across heads.
- **Missing compatibility experiments:** The method’s interaction with quantization (e.g., KIVI, AWQ) and its impact on first-token latency are not evaluated.
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with weaknesses (included above):** 
  - How does LSH-E perform without keeping the first 4 and recent 10 tokens?
  - Is the L2 baseline configured with the same hardcoded tokens?
  - More evaluations on LongBench at different budgets with standard baselines.
  - Empirical results showing that current token attention is a proxy for future attention.
  - Distortion error plot across compression levels.
  - CUDA-specific optimizations for efficiency.
  - How multi-head attention is handled.
  - Does the method work well with quantization?
