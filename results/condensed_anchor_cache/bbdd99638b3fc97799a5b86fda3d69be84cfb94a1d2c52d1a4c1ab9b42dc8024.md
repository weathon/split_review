## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the emergence and transience of in-context learning (ICL) versus in-weight learning (IWL) in transformers. It introduces a simplified sequential, tabular classification task with a three-part model: an IW predictor, an IC predictor (convex combination of context labels weighted by input similarity), and a gating mechanism that chooses between them. Theoretical analysis derives generalization bounds and regret, predicting that ICL first emerges on rare classes and later gives way to IWL as more data becomes available. Experiments on synthetic data and Omniglot compare a general transformer to separate ICL- and IWL-trained baselines. Additional experiments fine-tune Gemini Nano 1 on natural language prompts to study the balance between in-context and fine-tuning knowledge.

### Strengths
1. **Novel theoretical framework.** The paper provides a new understanding of how a model selects between ICL and IWL, highlighting the role of rare labels in promoting ICL. The three-part model and the overarching story—that ICL arises as a heuristic to reduce loss where IWL lacks experience—are interesting and thought-provoking.
2. **Clear motivation and flow.** The paper connects the observed phenomenon of ICL transience (Singh et al.) with a synthetic model and theory. The intuitive interpretation that new training samples effectively act as test samples under the assumptions is useful.
3. **Experimental validation.** Experiments on synthetic data, Omniglot, and real LLMs (Gemini Nano 1) demonstrate how noise level and the fraction of rare tasks affect ICL vs IWL. The inclusion of real language model experiments is valuable compared to concurrent purely synthetic work (e.g., openreview forums cited by Reviewer 4).
4. **Well-designed experiments (overall).** Reviewer 2 found the experiments well-designed to test theoretical predictions; Reviewer 1 noted validation on both synthetic and natural few-shot learning datasets.

### Weaknesses
1. **Mismatch with Singh et al.'s setting.** In Singh et al., there is no train-loss incentive for ICL over IWL—both are equally capable of the task—yet ICL still emerges and then disappears. In this model, the IC predictor has a performance lower bound that the IW predictor eventually surpasses, creating an inherent advantage for IWL. This difference means the theory does not fully capture the phenomenon studied by Singh et al. (Reviewer 3, W1).
2. **Incomplete characterization of ICL emergence for rare classes.** The claim that "ICL will first emerge on rare classes" is derived by comparing an upper bound on IW risk to an upper bound on IC risk, rather than a lower bound on IW risk versus an upper bound on IC risk. This does not guarantee the IW risk is actually higher than the IC risk for rare classes; it only permits it. The paper does not acknowledge this limitation (Reviewer 3, W2; echoed by Reviewer 2's difficulty seeing evidence for "first emerge" in Fig 2).
3. **Restrictive model of in-context learning.**
   * The IC predictor is modeled as a convex combination of context labels weighted by input similarity (softmax). This overlooks learning of input-output mappings and non-linear relationships, which are core to how LLMs actually do ICL (Reviewer 1, Weakness 2; Reviewer 3, W4).
   * The model assumes a hard switch between ICL and IWL based on error minimization, but real LLMs likely exhibit gradual, nuanced transitions where both mechanisms are simultaneously active (Reviewer 1, Weakness 1).
   * The models used in experiments are small (2 layers, 1 head, context length L=1). It is unclear whether these models are capable of learning the ICL solution the theory describes; the choice of L=1 may be forced by model size rather than theoretical necessity (Reviewer 2, minor issue; Reviewer 2's question about Fig 6).
4. **Unclear experimental support for key claims.**
   * **Fig 2 interpretation.** Reviewer 2 finds it unclear that Fig 2 supports the claim that ICL emerges first on rare classes and is later replaced by IWL. On rare classes, the transformer behaves similarly to the IC predictor from the start and only converges to the IW predictor when the IC predictor does; the OOBD plots show degradation but not clear evidence for emergence on rare classes. Reviewer 2 suggests a simpler explanation: the transformer is trained on a mix of two distributions and thus lies between the IC and IW baselines. (Reviewer 2, main weakness; also questioned by Reviewer 4, W3 about Figure 7.)
   * **Lack of quantitative metric.** Conclusions about ICL vs IWL dominance are drawn by eye-fitting curves rather than using an explicit ICL-vs-IWL ratio. This introduces subjectivity (Reviewer 4, W4).
5. **Gap between theory and experiments.**
   * The experiments compare a general transformer to separate ICL- and IWL-trained transformers as baselines. These baselines may not correspond to the theoretical IC and IW models (e.g., the "ICL transformer" might use very different mechanisms). The paper could have directly implemented the theoretical models for a cleaner test (Reviewer 3, W5).
   * The theory assumes a tabular input space and i.i.d. data. Footnote 1 claims easy extension to continuous inputs, but the bounds and proofs are tightly coupled to the tabular setting; the ease of extension is not justified (Reviewer 3, W3). Furthermore, in realistic ICL scenarios, context examples are often carefully curated and correlated, not i.i.d. (Reviewer 4, W1). The theory's i.i.d. assumption limits generalizability to few-shot learning and other practical settings.
6. **Limited LLM experiments.**
   * The study uses only one model (Gemini Nano 1), tests a small number of examples (listed in appendices), and lacks statistical analysis (Reviewer 1, Weakness 3; Reviewer 3, W6).
   * The LLM experiments may show that fine-tuning can suppress ICL superficially while the ability remains (as in Jain et al., 2023), rather than demonstrating the pretraining-stage transience discussed by Singh et al. The paper does not address this distinction (Reviewer 4, W6).
   * The connection between the LLM experiments and the theoretical framework is weak; it is unclear what the LLM results say about the theory or vice versa (Reviewer 3, W6).
   * The paper does not contextualize the LLM experiments within prior work on competition between fine-tuning knowledge and in-context knowledge (e.g., Wei et al., 2023) (Reviewer 3, W6).
7. **Presentation issues.**
   * Many acronyms and experimental variables (p_relevant, p_high, OOBD, IBD, IC model, IW model) are introduced without a schematic diagram, making the experiments hard to follow (Reviewer 4, W5; Reviewer 2 also noted difficulty processing Fig 2).
   * Figures (especially Fig 2, 3, 7, 8) have inconsistent labeling and could be improved (Reviewer 4, suggestions; Reviewer 2 minor issues).
   * Minor typos: "cdot" (line 148), "Transient" → "transience" (line 303), H defined twice differently (lines 130, 158) (Reviewer 2). Also duplicate reference entries for Edelman et al. and Zhang et al., "attention attention" (line 90), "transient of ICL" (line 304), and possibly misplaced notation (Reviewer 3, minor).
8. **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms (included as weaknesses).**
   * How does the dual operating mode of ICL (task retrieval vs task learning, as in arxiv 2402.18819) relate to this work? The paper does not address this, which would require more complex dynamics (Reviewer 1, question).
   * In the LLM experiment, why can't the model memorize invented (name, city) pairs? This suggests the model's behavior is not fully explained (Reviewer 4, question).
   * How applicable is the theory to real LLMs, given the restrictive assumptions? (Reviewer 4, question; also Reviewer 3, W4; Reviewer 4, W2).
   * Theory questions (Reviewer 3): What parameterization of α is used? Over which distribution are expectations taken in Proposition 1? Are IC bounds for a particular sample? Assumption of one-hot labels in Corollary 1 may be redundant. Lower bound in Corollary 1 does not match the exact value for k=L. What is the "Big-O constant" in Figure 1 caption?
   * Reviewer 3 notation suggestion: Use roman font for cross-entropy to avoid confusion.
