## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses density ratio estimation via binary classification, characterizing loss functions that minimize specified Bregman divergences in the asymptotic limit. The authors derive necessary properties (Theorem 1) for loss functions given a convex potential and a probability link, propose a canonical link yielding convex losses, and introduce new loss functions (Exponential Weight, polynomial weight) that prioritize accurate estimation of large density ratio values – a property missing from classical losses like logistic or boosting. Experiments on synthetic data and real-world domain adaptation tasks (including Amazon Reviews) demonstrate practical benefits. The paper is self-contained and extensive for a theoretical work, but several reviewers (scoring 6) raised concerns about novelty, presentation, and missing analyses; one reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, viewing the characterization as a valuable completion of prior work.

### Strengths

- The paper is self-contained, introducing all necessary tools and notation (Reviewer 1).
- Theoretical results, especially Lemma 4 in the appendix, involve non-trivial applications of previously established results (R1).
- References to existing research are sufficiently provided (R2).
- The paper introduces a new framework for constructing loss functions that target accurate estimation of large density ratio values, a novel direction compared to classical losses (R3).
- The mathematical foundation is thorough, characterizing loss functions that align with Bregman divergences, with good comparison to related literature (R3, R4).
- The empirical evaluation is extensive for a theoretical paper, covering synthetic simulations and several real-world datasets, with applications in deep domain adaptation (R1, R3, R4, R5).
- The theoretical analysis is solid (R4).
- One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive: they found the characterization a valuable contribution that significantly completes the picture laid out in prior work by Menon and Ong 2016, and by Reid and Williamson (see Remark 1). They considered the motivation convincing and the empirical results strong, enhancing the toolbox for principled density ratio estimation (R5). Other reviewers expressed reservations about novelty (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty concerns**: Several reviewers (R1, R2, R4) found the theoretical contribution unclear or incremental. R1 stated that the connection between Theorem 1 and existing results is not clearly delineated, making it difficult to assess the true advancement. R2 provided a detailed derivation showing that Equation (8) in Theorem 1 can be derived from Theorems 4 in [2] and [1], and that the canonical form (10) follows from Corollary 3 in [1] (Section 6.1). R4 noted that the paper did not elaborate how the proof differs from prior analyses, leaving the challenges and novelty of the proof unclear. In contrast, R5 considered the characterization a significant completion of prior work, indicating disagreement among reviewers on this point.

- **Lack of justification for focusing on minimizers of Equation (1)**: R1 questioned why one should limit attention to this specific class, calling the term "many" in Section 2 vague and lacking quantitative support. A clearer rationale for the chosen objective function is needed.

- **Insufficient analysis of trade-offs between small and large ratio estimation**: R3 noted that the paper focuses on improving large ratio estimation but does not thoroughly explore the impact on smaller values. It is unclear whether improved accuracy for large ratios comes at the cost of reduced accuracy for smaller ratios, which could be problematic in applications where both are important. R1 further asked why one would prefer the proposed estimate over KuLSIF for \(\alpha=0.01\) in the lower row of Figure 2, and suggested that applying standard methods to the inverse ratio \(dQ/dP\) could also achieve large-ratio estimation – a comparison not discussed.

- **Missing sample complexity analysis**: R3 pointed out that the paper provides no theoretical bounds on sample requirements, especially in high-dimensional settings, making it difficult to assess practical applicability. R5 also raised a question in the conclusion about finite-sample error bounds similar to PAC-learning complexity terms; the authors indicate this is not known.

- **Experimental shortcomings**:
  - R1 noted that Section 6.2 contains excessive detail, obscuring main findings; some material should be moved to appendices.
  - R3 criticized the lack of detailed experimental setup: specific hyperparameter settings, neural network architecture, and data preprocessing steps are missing, hindering reproducibility.
  - R2 pointed out that Figures 2 and 3 lack axis titles, and Figure 3 lacks explanatory labels, making interpretation difficult.
  - In Table 1, EW consistently performs best for Amazon Reviews under Importance Weighted Aggregation, but intuition for this outcome is not explained (R4).

- **Presentation and notation issues**: R1 found the notation heavy (e.g., \(B_{-\underline{L}^\circ}(\beta,g\circ f)\)) and Remark 1 incomprehensible without prior knowledge; the paper lacks intuitive explanations for key concepts. R4 suggested adding more high-level explanations to make the results easier to follow. R5 noted confusion about the introduction of \(g\) in Algorithm 1 without prior mention; it should be clarified that \(g(x)=\Psi^{-1}(x)/(1-\Psi^{-1}(x))\) at that point.

- **Unaddressed theoretical and practical questions (from R3)**: Is the existence of the density ratio guaranteed when distributions have non-overlapping support? How does the method perform when one distribution has a light tail and the other a heavy tail? The experiments consider only the one-dimensional case; multidimensional cases (common in covariate shift) are not tested.

- **Minor issues**:
  - R1 reported a typo in footnote 2: probabilities should be conditioned on \(x\) (should be \(\rho(y=1\mid x)\rho(x)\) instead of \(\rho(x,y=1)\rho(x)\)).
  - R5 noted a typo in line 78: the loss for an arbitrary \(g\) is not convex but only strictly proper composite (convexity holds only for the canonical \(g\)). R5 also noted a typo in line 273: the denominator should be \(g_{can}\) instead of \(g\).
