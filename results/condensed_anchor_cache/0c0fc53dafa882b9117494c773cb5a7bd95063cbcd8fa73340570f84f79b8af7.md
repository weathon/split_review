## Merged Review

### Summary
Three reviewers rated this paper 5, 6, and 6. They agree that ContraDiff makes a novel combination of contrastive learning and diffusion-based trajectory planning to better leverage low‑return trajectories in offline RL, and that the empirical evaluation is extensive (27 sub‑optimal datasets, ablations, visualizations, hyper‑parameter analysis). The method is simple to implement with few additional hyperparameters. However, all reviewers see key weaknesses: insufficient theoretical justification for the contrastive loss relative to the RL loss and to simpler alternatives (e.g., upweighting high‑return states), heavy dependence on hyperparameters (ξ, ζ, σ) without principled guidance, state‑level contrast breaking temporal correlations, marginal performance gains on many tasks, missing experiments (e.g., AntMaze, mixture datasets, comparisons to SAW/A2PR/LAPO), and unclear writing and presentation. One reviewer (score 5) expressed stronger reservations about potential redundancy of the contrastive loss and the need for a numerical analysis comparing loss trends across returns.

### Strengths
- Novel and well‑motivated combination of contrastive learning with diffusion‑based trajectory planning; applying contrastive learning to distinguish high‑ vs. low‑return trajectories is an innovative application within offline RL.
- Uses low‑return trajectories as a repulsive force to guide the agent away from suboptimal regions, expanding the utility of underutilized data beyond simple reweighting.
- Thorough empirical evaluation: 27 sub‑optimal D4RL datasets, extensive ablation studies, hyper‑parameter analysis, and visualizations.
- Implementation is relatively simple, requiring only a few additional hyperparameters compared to the base diffusion model.
- Main figure (Figure 1) is clear and well‑organized, making the method easy to understand.
- Provides a new perspective on offline RL by explicitly handling low‑return trajectories rather than focusing only on high‑return ones.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical foundation of contrastive loss.** The relationship between contrastive loss and the RL loss is insufficiently justified. Intuitively, the losses appear highly correlated – the RL loss in diffusion‑based methods already biases toward high‑return trajectories via value‑guided sampling. This raises concern that the contrastive loss may be redundant rather than a meaningful regularizer. A numerical analysis comparing the two losses across trajectories with different returns would help quantify the degree of redundancy. The paper also does not explain why explicitly pushing away from low‑return states is superior to simpler alternatives such as upweighting high‑return trajectories or using imitation‑based filtering (e.g., SAW, A2PR, LAPO). More theoretical justification is needed.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and lack of principled thresholds.** The determination of positive/negative samples relies heavily on hyperparameters ξ, ζ, and σ (return thresholds) without theoretical justification for their value ranges or settings across environments. A systematic study of how varying these thresholds affects the distribution of samples and learning dynamics is missing. The approach appears ad‑hoc.
- **State‑level contrast loses temporal structure.** The current design samples positive/negative states purely based on return or clustering results, breaking the temporal correlation within trajectories. This may discard valuable sequential information. A trajectory‑level contrastive mechanism or trajectory‑aligned state sampling should be explored, and comparative experiments between state‑level and trajectory‑level approaches would provide stronger empirical support for the design choice. The clustering step to enforce “dynamic consistency” is also not clearly explained – it is unclear whether it captures reachability or state transitions beyond grouping next states, especially in environments with complex nonlinear dynamics.
- **Marginal performance gains.** Improvements on many tasks fall within one standard deviation of baselines. Methods like CDE and HD achieve comparable results without any special handling of low‑return trajectories, suggesting the claimed advantages of the contrastive mechanism may be less significant than stated. A more thorough analysis of unique benefits is needed.
- **Missing experiments and comparisons.** 
  - No results on sparse‑reward tasks like AntMaze, a common offline RL benchmark.
  - The mixture dataset evaluations (Table 3) introduce expert trajectories but methods like CQL are not tested on these settings. Additional experiments are required to fully demonstrate model performance.
  - The paper mentions comparing ContraDiff with “other regular methods” but does not clearly indicate where these results are (they appear only in the appendix); more explicit presentation is needed.
  - Comparisons to related offline RL methods that also use state‑value weighting (SAW, A2PR, LAPO) are missing from the related work and experiments.
- **Complexity and runtime.** The use of k‑means clustering is not discussed in terms of complexity or runtime implications, especially for large offline RL datasets. The diffusion model’s training cost (minimizing MSE between ground truth and neat trajectory) appears expensive; an experiment comparing training cost would be informative.
- **Unexpected results requiring explanation.** 
  - Table 3 shows that introducing expert data causes a *drop* in performance for DT (from 36.6 on HalfCheetah‑MR to 7.5, 6.7, 6.1). The authors should explain whether the same codebase was used and why such a drastic decrease occurs.
  - In most tasks ContraDiff‑SR outperforms ContraDiff‑SRD, which contradicts the authors’ intuition that SRD better captures transition dynamics. Further analysis is needed.
- **Unclear baseline source.** It is not stated whether baseline results were taken from existing code, reimplemented, or from other papers; this should be clarified.
- **Writing issues and typos.** Several sentences are unclear or contain typos, e.g., L290: “high‑return sample sparsity situations”, L301: “which are focus on addressing”, L364: “declines with the ratio declines”, Table3: “Walekr2d”. The experiments section in particular needs better clarity.
