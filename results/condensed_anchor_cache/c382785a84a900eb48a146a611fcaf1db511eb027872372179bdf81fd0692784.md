## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper compares two attention-head mechanisms proposed for in-context learning (ICL)—induction heads and function vector (FV) heads—across 12 language models of varying sizes and families (Pythia, GPT-2, Llama 2). The authors report that FV heads are largely distinct from induction heads, appear in deeper layers on average, and have a stronger causal effect on few-shot ICL accuracy when ablated. They also find that some FV heads evolve from induction heads during training. The majority of reviewers (scores 3,3,3) find the results interesting but raise significant concerns about methodology, statistical rigor, novelty, and clarity of claims. A minority (scores 8,6) view the work as a valuable fresh perspective that challenges prior beliefs and opens new directions.

### Strengths
- Fresh perspective on ICL mechanisms, highlighting the underappreciated role of FV heads. The idea that induction heads may serve as a precursor to FV heads opens new research directions. (R1)
- Experiments are thorough across model sizes and families (Pythia, GPT-2, Llama 2) with controlled ablation studies and careful handling of overlapping effects. (R1, R4)
- Well-organized and clearly written; comprehensive background on induction and FV heads. (R1, R3, R5)
- Addresses a fundamental question in LLM interpretability and challenges existing beliefs about the primacy of induction heads. (R1)
- Appreciates the disambiguation between few-shot ICL and token-loss-difference ICL, which helps disentangle the contributions of the two head types. (R2)
- Controlled ablation approach to separate effects is liked. (R2)
- First to explore how induction/FV heads are learned and formed during pre-training—a concrete contribution. (R3)
- Detailed empirical analysis linking two influential ICL mechanisms. (R4)
- Investigation of induction heads in few-shot ICL and larger models is interesting; the consistent trend across models suggests induction heads are not the sole contributor. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Ablation methodology**: Replacing output vectors with mean values (Sec. 4.1) is ad-hoc; no justification given for mean over other statistics, and robustness is unclear. (R2) Furthermore, mean ablation may trigger adaptive computation (McGrath et al.), so additional verification methods (e.g., direct contribution measurement, information flow analysis) are needed. (R5)
- **Confounding by head location**: The paper does not control for the fact that induction and FV heads appear at different layers. A baseline ablating heads at varying locations is missing. (R2)
- **Statistics on layer depth**: The claim that FV heads appear in deeper layers is weakly supported; average differences are only 1–2 layers, and models like GPT-2 Medium, Large, XL, and Llama 2-7B show identical averages. Standard deviations and a statistical test (e.g., t-test) are needed. (R3)
- **Metric for distinctness**: The metric for head overlap (exact head identity) is potentially misleading because neighboring heads can have similar functionality. The paper should also measure overlap by layers (e.g., Pythia 6.9B has both types in layers 8 and 17). (R3)
- **Unsupported claims**: Several conclusions are presented as proof when they are really conjectures. For example, Line 252 speculates that FV heads implement more complex computations without direct evidence. (R2)
- **Inaccurate literature claim**: The statement that prior work on induction heads focused on small models (line 187) is inaccurate; works [2][3] have extended induction heads to models up to 20B/66B and discussed scale effects. These should be acknowledged, weakening the claimed novelty. (R3)
- **Limited novelty**: The paper merely compares existing indicators (induction score, FV score) without providing technical improvements or a more effective explanation of ICL. (R4)
- **Unclear practical impact**: It is not obvious how these findings can improve ICL performance or guide future work. (R4, echoed by R2’s question on actionable implications)
- **Misleading phrasing**: Claims such as “contrary to the prevailing belief that induction heads are a primary mechanism of ICL” (lines 51–52, 473–474) should specify that this refers to few-shot ICL, because the paper still finds induction heads important for token-loss-difference ICL. (R5)
- **Weak task performance**: Many small models achieve only 20–30% few-shot accuracy across the tasks; if the models cannot perform the task well, the mechanistic conclusions are questionable. Additionally, the breakdown of accuracy by individual task is missing (Appendix A.2 does not contain it). (R5)
- **Insufficient engagement with prior work**: Olsson et al. [2] already noted that induction heads do not tell the whole story (calling some behavior “spiritually similar” to copying). The paper does not adequately acknowledge this. (R5)
- **Questions that highlight gaps**:
  - If induction and FV heads are distinct, how can some heads score high on both metrics? Does this indicate overlapping functionality? (R2)
  - Are the heads analyzed in Section 5 taken from each checkpoint separately or only from the final checkpoint? (R3)
  - Why would ablating monosemantic FV heads not hurt ICL performance (Conjecture C2)? These heads could be performing important task-specific operations beyond copying. (R3)
  - Some FV heads are also induction heads (per Todd et al., Appendix H); does this strengthen or contradict the claim that induction heads are unimportant for few-shot ICL? (R5)
  - In Figures 5 and 6, which specific heads drive the correlation? For instance, does the sharp spike in Pythia 6.9B correspond to the same FV head that has an induction score spike around step \(2^{9}\)? (R5)
  - Why does Llama 2 exhibit lower FV scores, and how might its architecture or training explain this? (R1)
  - Why does the gap between FV-head and induction-head effects increase with scale? (R1)
