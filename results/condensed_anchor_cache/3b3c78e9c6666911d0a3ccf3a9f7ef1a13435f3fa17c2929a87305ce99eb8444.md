## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes TSBO, a Bayesian optimization (BO) algorithm for high-dimensional black-box functions that uses a semi-supervised teacher-student framework to leverage unlabeled data and improve surrogate model generalization. The teacher is an MLP outputting (mean, variance); the student is a GP trained on synthetic unlabeled data with teacher pseudo-labels. A bilevel optimization updates teacher parameters using labeled data loss and feedback loss from the student GP (evaluated on a validation set). Two optimized unlabeled data sampling strategies are introduced. The trained teacher-student model generates pseudo-labels for the main BO surrogate GP. Experiments on three tasks (arithmetic expression and two chemical designs) show improved sample efficiency over baselines. Reviewer ratings are 6, 5, 5.

### Strengths

- **Novelty of the semi-supervised direction in BO** (R3): Self-supervised learning has not been thoroughly explored in BO, and the paper explores a novel niche. The overall idea of leveraging unlabeled data is nice and offers a broader insight that can be instantiated in multiple ways (R2).
- **Addressing surrogate model accuracy** (R3): Improving BO efficiency by producing a more accurate surrogate is a good approach that deserves attention.
- **Promising empirical results** (R1, R2): TSBO outperforms existing latent-space BO baselines (e.g., LBO methods) significantly. Notably, one reviewer (score 6, confidence 2) found the method robust to parameter changes and highlighted that models trained with limited data even outperform baseline models with unlimited access to data, paving the way for novel applications where labeled and unlabeled data are scarce.
- **Multiple relevant ablations** (R3): With many algorithm components, substantial effort is devoted to ablations (Section 6.3 and elsewhere).
- **Clear pedagogical figure** (R3): Figure 1 communicates the proposed method effectively.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of theoretical convergence analysis** (R1): The paper does not discuss for which function classes the method converges or provide any convergence rates. This weakens the presentation of asymptotic behavior. (Reviewer 1 asked directly: “Is there a class of functions for which the method converges? Can we expect any rate of convergence?”)
- **Missing performance characterization with larger datasets** (R1): The paper does not show how TSBO behaves when given access to larger parts of the dataset.
- **Unjustified complexity and insufficient intuition** (R3): The method has three learnable components (teacher, student, synthetic data sampler) in a nested bi-level scheme. It is difficult to discern why synthetic data are sensible, correctly pseudo-labeled, and what drives performance. No plots demonstrate the fit of the student, the synthetic data distribution, or other insight that would make the process digestible. The paper lacks a clear explanation of why adding synthetic data improves the GP surrogate—is it because the teacher MLP aggressively extrapolates, because the vanilla GP is under-confident and synthetic data reduce uncertainty, or some combination? (Reviewer 3 asked for qualitative differences between SGP and BO GP predictions, and whether synthetic data are added with extra noise, replaced, or substituted.)
- **Superiority over other semi-supervised methods not established** (R2): If semi-supervised learning is key, why would other approaches (e.g., manifold/Laplacian regularization) not work equally well? The paper does not justify why the specific teacher-student framework is superior for BO.
- **Questionable role of the teacher** (R3): The teacher generates pseudo-labels for synthetic data. Comments in the paper (e.g., about student feedback being relevant for tuning the teacher toward the global optimum) suggest the teacher is indirectly conducting the optimization. If the teacher can generate accurate synthetic data, why not use it directly as a surrogate instead of the conventional GP?
- **Questionable role of the student** (R3): The student is trained only on synthetic data and validated on a (promising) subset of true data, with its loss coupled to the teacher’s. It appears to act merely as a regularizer (incorporating GP-like smoothness into the MLP). The paper does not clarify what additional value the student provides beyond conventional regularization.
- **Ablation evaluation on synthetic latent-space points, not real inputs** (R2): The ablation in Section 6.3 evaluates surrogate performance using points sampled from the latent space rather than original data (e.g., actual molecules with computed latent embeddings). Since the goal is to improve the surrogate’s generalization to points resembling the original input space, the current synthetic evaluation does not directly address this. The reviewer strongly suggests running the ablation on real inputs.
- **Incomplete discussion of relevant prior work** (R2, R3): The paper does not discuss several important references on high-dimensional BO and latent-space BO (e.g., Eriksson et al. 2019/2021, Papenmeier et al. 2022, Letham et al. 2020, Nayebi et al. 2019, Maus et al. 2022, Deshwal & Doppa 2021, Notin et al. 2021, Eissman et al. 2018). Reference [9] in R2’s list even considers semi-supervised learning via an auxiliary network. Additionally, the key idea in Section 5.1 is very similar to MES (Wang & Jegelka 2017) and should be cited (R3). It is not necessary to compare experimentally, but these works deserve proper discussion.
- **Sparse evaluation** (R3): Only three benchmarks (all chemical design tasks) are presented. Adding conventional test functions would strengthen the evaluation. (Contrast: R1 found the experiments sufficiently convincing to give a score of 6.)
- **Minor presentation issues** (R3): Typos in Appendix B1 (“proposes”→“purposes”, “remove, however”) and Section 5.0 (“systematically sample”→“systematically sampling”). Legend and Figure 1 font sizes are too small.

**Reviewer confidence and agreement note:** Reviewer 1 (score 6, confidence 2) was notably more positive about the method’s robustness and practical value, while Reviewers 2 and 3 (scores 5, confidence 4) raised more fundamental concerns about complexity, justification, and evaluation. The weaknesses above reflect the majority view but preserve the minority’s stronger endorsement in the strengths.
