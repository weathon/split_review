## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Mixture-of-Prompts (MoP), which applies a Mixture-of-Expert paradigm to prompt optimization. It clusters training demos (using K-Means-Auto), then performs region-based joint search (RBJS) to produce a specialized instruction per cluster. During inference, a test point is routed to its nearest cluster and uses that expert's instruction + demos. The method outperforms APE on the APE benchmark. Reviewer opinions span from positive (R1: 6; R4: 5) to critical (R3: 3). All reviewers find the motivation interesting and the divide-and-conquer approach reasonable; several note the method is clear and the experiments extensive. However, concerns center on clustering validity, limited evaluation (only discriminative tasks, only APE benchmark), lack of clarity in implementation details (Eq.9, router, initial sets), limited novelty, and insufficient analysis of cluster quality and heterogeneity of instructions. The most negative reviewer (R3) disputes the clustering assumption and sees little novelty; the most positive (R1) encourages broader benchmarking and embedding diversity but considers the work solid.

### Strengths
- The motivation is interesting and reasonable: a single fixed instruction is unlikely to be optimal for all samples in a dataset; tasks can often be subdivided into subtasks requiring different prompts. (R1, R2, R4)
- The method is clear, easy to follow, and automates prompt engineering in a systematic two-phase approach (demo assignment + instruction assignment). (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Experiments and analysis are extensive and well-designed, with impressive performance gains over APE on the APE benchmark. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The use of semantic similarity for clustering is intuitive, and the case studies (e.g., APE subtasks) provide qualitative insight into expert behavior. (R1, R2)
- The Mixture-of-Experts format for prompt optimization is novel compared to prior work (e.g., PromptBoosting) and could be extended in interesting directions (soft MOE, multiple embeddings). (R2)
- The analysis of different experts' performance justifies the motivation and can offer insights to the community. (R4)
- Notation and equations are easy to follow. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope:** The experiments are conducted only on the APE benchmark, which consists of discriminative, close-ended tasks. The method’s performance on generative tasks (e.g., long-form QA, creative writing, complex reasoning, code generation) is unclear. (R1, R4) A reviewer suggests using real-scene user logs like ShareGPT to demonstrate broader applicability. (R1)
- **Generalizability of embeddings:** The method relies on GPT-based embeddings; results with other embedding models are needed to assess generalizability. (R1) Also, comparison with other prompt optimization techniques is lacking. (R1)
- **Clustering validity (major point from R3):** The approach assumes that training demos can be decomposed into homogeneous clusters that cover the entire problem space. This raises several unaddressed issues:
  - How to ensure training demos are representative enough to cover all test cases? (R3)
  - K-means is sensitive to initialization and may converge to local optima; the paper does not discuss the impact of different clustering algorithms or initialization strategies. (R3)
  - There is no clear metric for cluster quality beyond final task performance; it is unclear if clusters are semantically meaningful or artifacts of the algorithm. (R3)
  - The method for choosing the optimal number of clusters (K-Means-Auto) is not explained, nor is the balance between expert number and efficiency. (R3) A reviewer suggests an alternative: ignore clustering and treat demos as a K-dimensional latent space. (R3)
- **Heterogeneity of instructions:** The instruction assignment (Independent Search + Joint Search) is seen as straightforward and does not explicitly enforce diversity among cluster instructions (e.g., maximizing KL divergence). As a result, in practice some instructions are incoherent (Table 6) or do not complement their demos (Table 4: some demos are geographic while instruction asks about apparel). This contradicts the intended synergy. (R2, R3)
- **Clarity issues (R2, R4):**
  - APE (both the method and the tasks) is not introduced when first mentioned, causing confusion. (R2)
  - The implementation of Eq.9 is unclear: Section 4.4 describes generating 20 instruction proposals and picking the top 4, which seems inconsistent with the cluster-specific optimization objective. (R2)
  - The routing algorithm at test time is not discussed; it is unclear if each test case is run through all prompts or has a router. Running multiple prompts would raise efficiency concerns. (R4)
  - The origin of the initial demonstration set and instruction set (based on APE) is not self-contained; how APE’s proposals are reused in RBJS (“we save the results …”) is not explained clearly. (R4)
  - The derivation from Eq.5 to Eq.6 is not justified. (R4)
- **Lack of dependencies across experts:** Each cluster is optimized independently; a boosting-style approach or conditioning on other clusters’ instructions could improve complementarity. (R2)
- **When task instruction and input are not easily split**, it is unclear how MoP would work. (R1)
- **Novelty concerns (R3):** The overall approach is seen as not surprising; the two-step search is a straightforward combination of existing ideas. (R3) R3 also argues that the paper overclaims by stating a limitation is searching for “demo-free instruction” when the real limitation is that searching an optimal instruction is hard. (R3, Q1)
- **Ensemble interpretation (R4):** The method could be viewed as a classic ensemble; it would be interesting to fix one phase (e.g., only vary instructions) and ensemble outputs rather than using a router. (R4)

*Minority contrast:* R3 is considerably more negative on novelty and clustering assumptions, while R1 is more positive overall and encourages broader validation without questioning the core idea. R2 and R4 are moderately positive but raise non-trivial clarity and technical concerns.
