## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers find the paper tackles a relevant problem (joint fairness and privacy) and is well written with a clear algorithmic idea—incorporating fairness constraints into PATE’s query‑rejection mechanism. However, all three question the significance of the contribution: experimental gains are minimal (<1% in many cases), FairPATE underperforms baselines on some datasets (e.g., Adult, UTK Face at ε=5), and results on several datasets are hidden in the appendix without proper comparison (CheXpert, CelebA, FairFace, Retired‑Adults). Novelty is disputed (one reviewer sees little new beyond prior rejection‑based fairness work), and comparisons may be unfair because FairPATE uses public unlabeled data and a rejection option that baselines lack. Theoretical claims (Theorem 2) are challenged, and the handling of human decisions after rejection is considered flawed. Missing ablations, missing baselines, and a need for clearer discussion of when FairPATE is preferable are noted.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a highly relevant issue in ML, addressing both theoretical and practical implications of fairness and privacy.
- Simple adaptation of existing PATE; simplicity is a plus.
- Fairly well written and easy to follow; the algorithmic idea is clearly presented.
- The points of intervention give a nice overview of the PATE approach and ways to independently inject additional mechanisms.
- Rejection for fairness does give additional options for achieving fairness.
- The main algorithmic idea—choosing which points to label based on both privacy and fairness constraints—is neat and could be useful in other contexts.

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental significance is weak:** Improvements over baselines are rarely larger than 1% (e.g., Credit card and Parkinson’s datasets). FairPATE underperforms on Demographic Parity for Adult and UTK Face (ε=5). Results on CheXpert, CelebA, FairFace, and Retired‑Adults are absent from the main text and lack comparison; there are no FairPATE results on CheXpert at all. Provide comparisons on these datasets for demographic parity and equalized odds with Loewy et al. and state‑of‑the‑art DP methods.
- **Unfair comparisons:** FairPATE uses public unlabelled data, while Loewy et al. uses a stricter notion of DP without public data; this gives FairPATE an advantage. Also, FairPATE uses a fairness‑based rejection mechanism that baselines do not have, potentially inflating its Pareto frontier. Either incorporate a rejection option into existing baselines or account for the additional privacy of public data in the comparison.
- **Theoretical claims are problematic:**
  - The interpretation of Theorem 2 is incorrect: it only proves a privacy guarantee for the composed mechanism, not that the guarantee is necessarily degraded (as claimed above the theorem).
  - Theorem 1 argues against pre‑processors, but PATE/FairPATE itself fits the definition of a pre‑processor, making the theorem potentially self‑undermining.
  - The claim that adding privacy after fairness breaks fairness (point C1) may not hold in expectation if demographic statistics are considered at the population level.
- **Flawed handling of rejection for fairness:**
  - If a query is rejected and delegated to a human judge, the human’s decision must be incorporated into subsequent fairness statistics; ignoring this may artificially boost apparent fairness. Suggestion: update counts m(z,k) based on human decisions and allow queries that improve fairness even when the threshold is already violated.
  - The abstention argument is flawed: artificially refusing to predict on majority groups can trivially satisfy a fairness metric without improving minority performance. The correct solution should aim to improve performance on the minority group.
- **Missing ablations and unclear experimental design:**
  - No ablation study showing the impact of the inference‑time rejection mechanism (IPP) across datasets with different majority/minority distributions.
  - Rejection rates are not shown in any experiment; include them or argue that misclassifications can be viewed as rejections.
  - The derivation of error bars in experimental figures is not explained.
  - The distribution whose fairness is to be protected is unclear (public vs. private data); this ambiguity extends to intervention points 1,2,3 and Theorem 1.
- **Missing baselines:** No comparison with Zhang et al. (2021), Kulynuych et al. (2022), Berrada et al. (2023), or the arxiv paper “Learning with Impartiality to Walk on the Pareto Frontier of Fairness, Privacy, and Utility.” The term “FairDP‑SGD” is not defined.
- **Novelty and positioning:**
  - The fairness enforcement via rejection sampling was proposed in several earlier works (including those cited). An explicit statement of novelty is needed.
  - No discussion of when to use FairPATE vs. alternative frameworks.
  - The paper lacks an argument for why a trade‑off between privacy and fairness is necessary (e.g., as opposed to simply improving accuracy which may also improve fairness).
- **Missing consistency and discussion:** Inconsistencies across datasets (e.g., DP‑Fermi outperforms FairPATE on Adult) are not discussed in the main text; factors causing these differences should be explained.
- **Figure and presentation issues:**
  - Figure 2 and 3 reuse the same orange color for two different algorithms (Tran et al. in Fig 2, Jagielski et al. in Fig 3). Report all algorithms in all figures or justify their absence.
  - Why are Tran et al. and Jagielski et al. not reported for the UTK‑dataset experiment?
  - Minor: update arxiv references to conference versions. Small typos (e.g., double “violations”) and mischaracterizations (“DP protects a given sensitive feature”).
