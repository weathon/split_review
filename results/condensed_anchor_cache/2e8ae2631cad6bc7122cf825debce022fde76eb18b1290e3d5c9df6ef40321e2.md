## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates whether sparse autoencoders (SAEs) of different sizes learn a canonical, atomic set of features from language model activations. It introduces two analysis techniques: SAE stitching (comparing latents across SAE sizes by inserting or swapping latents from a larger SAE into a smaller one) and meta-SAEs (training an SAE on the decoder matrix of another SAE to decompose latents into more atomic components). It also proposes BatchTopK SAEs, a variant of TopK SAEs that enforces fixed average sparsity. The empirical results suggest that SAEs do not find unique, complete, atomic feature sets; larger SAEs capture novel information missed by smaller ones, and larger-SAE latents often decompose into combinations of smaller-SAE latents.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and timely question about whether SAE features are canonical or atomic, with implications for scaling SAEs and using them for model interventions and circuit analysis.
- The experiments are original, well-executed, and thorough, including solid comparisons with baselines and detailed experimental information (R1, R3, R4).
- Figure 5 (showing continuous improvement in MSE when interpolating between SAEs of different sizes via stitching) and Figure 6 (showing similarity between meta-SAE latents and similarly-sized base SAE latents) are compelling demonstrations (R1).
- The motivation and methods are explained clearly and intuitively, with helpful examples (R3). However, one reviewer found the presentation presumptive and hard to follow in places (R2).
- The paper contextualizes its approach by discussing relevant state-of-the-art methods (R3).
- An interactive dashboard is provided to explore meta-SAEs (R3).
- The conclusion that SAEs do not form a complete set of explanations and that larger SAEs contain more fine-grained information is interesting and relevant (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing reconstruction loss for meta-SAEs:** The paper does not report the reconstruction loss or accuracy when meta-SAEs reconstruct the base 49k-latent SAE latents. If meta-SAEs only provide a weak approximation, this undermines the claim that large-SAE latents are linear combinations of more atomic latents (R1).
- **Clarity and motivation issues with stitching:** The reasoning behind stitching is not clearly motivated. Features and decoders are optimized separately for each SAE; it is unclear why suboptimally combining them (e.g., fixing encoder features from both SAEs and learning optimal decoder weights for the combination) provides insight. The significance of MSE increases or decreases during stitching is not justified (R2). One reviewer also questioned the asymmetry in Equation 5 (using bias only from decoder 0, not decoder 1) (R2, R3).
- **Incomplete explanation of meta-SAEs:** The paper lacks clarity on how decoder directions (vectors) are used as training data for meta-SAEs. The notation \( W_i^{dec} \) is ambiguous (scalar vs. vector). The reviewer suggests treating feature vectors \( f(x) \) as entities for meta-SAEs rather than just decoder directions, because directions alone do not convey co-activation patterns (R2).
- **BatchTopK SAE is not well justified or connected to the main contributions:** The BatchTopK method is introduced but its motivation is unclear, and its connection to the paper’s core arguments about canonical units is not explained. The method uses a different nonlinearity during training (batch top-k) vs. inference (per-example top-k), which seems ad-hoc and may cause entire sequences to have zero activation. The claim that BatchTopK “outperforms” TopK is questionable because it imposes fewer constraints (R2). One reviewer asks how varying \( k \) impacts semantics of learned latents (R3).
- **Lack of discussion on limitations and representation quality:** The paper does not discuss limitations of its proposed approaches (R3). There is also no expanded discussion on how to assess the quality of representations or how to adapt SAE dimensionality to suit different analyses (R3).
- **Positioning and scope not clearly stated:** The abstract and introduction present the research as a general feature analysis of neural networks, but experiments are exclusively on LLMs. The paper should either clearly state it is about LLM probing or demonstrate applicability beyond language models (R4).
- **No evaluation of SAE probing capability:** The paper evaluates SAE goodness solely via reconstruction MSE, not by probing capability (e.g., localizing specific model behaviors like Indirect Object Identification as in Cunningham et al.). It would strengthen the work to show how the new tools improve causal feature discovery or counterfactual behavior analysis (R4).
- **Missing training details for SAEs:** No information is given on the optimizer, hyperparameters (e.g., \(\lambda\)), or how SAEs are trained (R2).
- **Choice of layer 8 of GPT2 is not justified** (R2).
- **Figure 5: unclear what “average L0” means and what it is averaged over** (R2).
- **Figure 11 in supplementary is not well explained** (R2).
- **Minor grammatical issues:** “vertexes” should be “vertices”; left quotation marks need fixing; inconsistent spelling (color/colour); incorrect opening parentheses (lines 369 onward) (R1, R2).
- **Threshold for novel/reconstruction latents is unclear:** In Figure 4, the 0.7 cosine similarity threshold is mentioned, but the trend is less clear in Figure 18 (GemmaScope32k). The claim that stitching “works well” on GemmaScope32k needs elaboration (R4).
- **Definition of “active latents” is missing** (R4).
- **Meta-SAE experiments: it is unclear on which base SAE the meta-SAEs were trained** (e.g., what size SAE yields the reported 2304 latent meta-SAE) (R4).
- **Non-convexity of SAE objective:** Different SAEs may find different local minima, so observed differences could be due to optimization rather than fundamental non-canonicity (R2).
- **A reviewer questions whether it is already obvious that larger SAEs find features missed by smaller ones** (R2).
