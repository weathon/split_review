## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes ShapeConv, a CNN layer whose kernels act as shapelets for time-series modeling. It claims theoretical equivalence between shapelets and a specific CNN kernel with squared norm and pooling. The method uses shaping regularization to enforce similarity/diversity and allows human-knowledge initialization. Experiments on UCR datasets for classification and clustering are reported.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, and easy to follow.
- It establishes an interesting theoretical link between convolutional operations and the shapelet transform, offering a fresh perspective for combining shapelets with deep learning.
- The proposed initialization scheme and regularization terms (shaping regularization) are novel in the context of shapelets and lead to improved performance.
- Comprehensive experiments are conducted on both supervised (classification) and unsupervised (clustering) tasks.
- Empirical results show good performance, achieving state-of-the-art on several benchmarks.
- The method is versatile, applicable to both supervised and unsupervised settings.

### Weaknesses

1. **Theorem 3.1 and claimed equivalence are not novel and lack rigor**:
   - The observation that the shapelet distance profile is equivalent to convolution was previously made in (Lods et al. 2017), yet the paper does not adequately distinguish its contribution from that prior work.
   - The proof in Theorem 3.1 disregards the fact that the bias term in a standard convolution is input-independent, unlike the \(-\mathcal{N}(s_i, X_{j:j+l_s-1})\) term.
   - The core claim of equivalence to a **standard** CNN kernel is not demonstrated: the \(\|X\|^2_2\) term cannot be obtained from a standard convolution operation, so ShapeConv is essentially a re-writing of the shapelet transform, not an equivalence proof.

2. **Insufficient comparisons to state-of-the-art methods**:
   - The experimental evaluation omits many strong baselines: ROCKET (Dempster et al. 2020), COTE and its variants, ShapeletTransform, MUSE, CIF, ensemble methods from the extensive review of Ruiz et al. (2021), and deep learning approaches like RNNs and Transformers.
   - Without these comparisons, it is difficult to assess the true performance of ShapeConv relative to established methods.

3. **Interpretability evaluation is weak and unconvincing**:
   - Only toy examples are shown (Fig 4: only 2 shapelets) and limited datasets (GunPoint, Herring, ECG200). (Reviewers 3,4,5)
   - No effort was made to obtain original images/videos to causally verify shapelet interpretations (e.g., modifying herring images or reenacting gun-point video). (Reviewer 3)
   - Pairing ShapeConv with MLP/SVM is insufficient for interpretability; the authors should have used tree-based or linear models or explainers like SHAP to determine shapelet importance. (Reviewer 5)
   - The claim that shapelets cover “turning points” is tautological. (Reviewer 3)
   - Human initialization used in interpretability examples is tricky to evaluate (which humans, how trained). (Reviewer 3)

4. **Limited dataset selection and unclear criteria**:
   - The supervised evaluation uses only 25 UCR datasets (not the full 125) and unsupervised uses 36 datasets, without justification for these subsets. (Reviewers 3,4,5)
   - The text says “25 UCR” – is this correct or a typo for “125 UCR”? (Reviewer 3)

5. **Experimental details and clarity issues**:
   - It is unclear whether the initialization phase cost is included in runtime. (Reviewer 5)
   - Table 1 differences in cd plots across methods are not explained. (Reviewer 5)
   - Baseline hyperparameter tuning procedures are not described. (Reviewer 4)
   - Computational complexity analysis (time/space with respect to input length, shapelet length, and number of shapelets) is missing. (Reviewer 2)
   - The use of average accuracy across heterogeneous datasets is meaningless due to differences in size, class skew, and number of classes. (Reviewer 3)
   - Spurious precision (four significant digits) in Table E.5. (Reviewer 3)

6. **Insufficient related work review**:
   - The paper omits notable shapelet methods (Random Shapelet Forest, standard ShapeletTransform) and relevant dictionary/interval-based approaches. Competitor selection in experiments is unusual. (Reviewers 1,4,5)

7. **Missing ablation experiments**:
   - What happens if the ShapeConv layer is replaced with a standard convolutional layer (same initialization and regularization)? This would isolate the benefit of the squared norm term. (Reviewer 4)
   - Would interpretability remain if multiple ShapeConv layers are stacked? (Reviewer 1)
   - The importance of supervised information at initialization is not assessed, nor are simpler alternatives like k-means++ on subsequences. (Reviewer 4)

8. **Practical implementation challenges not discussed**:
   - Integration into existing deep learning frameworks, potential issues with backpropagation and gradient computation due to shaping regularization and human knowledge constraints. (Reviewer 2)

9. **Presentation and writing issues**:
   - Flowery language (e.g., “resonates with human comprehension”). (Reviewer 3)
   - Typos: “Shaplets” (Fig 5), “This illustrate how”, contractions (“don’t”). (Reviewer 3)
   - Figure 1 legend obscures data. (Reviewer 3)
   - Starting sentences with mathematical symbols is bad practice. (Reviewer 1)
   - Unsupervised clustering method not clearly described in Section 3.4 (it appears later in Section 4.2). (Reviewer 4)
   - No discussion or citation of code implementation. (Reviewer 5)
