## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses scalability of influence functions for large models by proposing LoGra, a gradient projection strategy that leverages the Kronecker structure in backpropagation to avoid materializing large gradient or projection matrices. LoGra computes projected training gradients via low-rank projections on input/output activations, and the method is theoretically linked to the damping effect in influence functions. The authors also introduce LogIX, a plug-and-play Python package using PyTorch hooks to easily integrate TDA into existing training code. Experiments on models including MLP, ResNet, GPT-2, GPT2-XL, Pythia-1.4B, and Llama3-8B-Instruct with 1B-token dataset show LoGra achieves comparable attribution accuracy to EKFAC while delivering up to ~6,500–7,000× throughput improvement and 5× GPU memory reduction, at the cost of significantly higher storage (3.5 TB for LoGra vs 89 GB for EKFAC). The method performs worse on Pythia-1.4B in qualitative analysis.

### Strengths
- Addresses a central practical challenge in TDA: scaling influence functions to large models and datasets.
- The technical innovation (LoGra) is well-motivated: using the gradient structure in backpropagation to perform efficient Kronecker-factored projection without materializing full gradients, and theoretically connecting projection to the damping effect in influence functions.
- Impressive empirical efficiency gains: up to ~7,000× throughput improvement and 5× GPU memory reduction over EKFAC on Llama3-8B-Instruct (Table 1), while maintaining competitive attribution accuracy.
- Strong practical focus: LogIX software package allows easy integration into existing training pipelines via forward/backward hooks, lowering the barrier for adoption (reproducible codebase).
- Comprehensive experiments across multiple model scales (MLP, ResNet, GPT-2, GPT2-XL, Pythia-1.4B, Llama3-8B-Instruct) including both quantitative (correlation with leave-one-out retraining, where applicable) and qualitative analysis.
- Paper is well-written and provides sufficient background for gradient-based TDA methods (noted by two reviewers; one reviewer disagrees on clarity).

### Weaknesses
- **Notation and clarity for non-expert readers**: The paper is not self-contained; an average reader needs to refer to prior papers for Kronecker product, Kronecker product of vectors, K-FAC approximation of the Hessian, and Gauss-Newton Hessian definition. This is a criticism (Reviewer 3, low confidence in presentation).
- **Details of Hessian calculation omitted**: It is unclear how the calculation is performed, in particular how token-wise gradients are obtained and used. Reviewer 3 states “I do not understand how this calculation was performed”.
- **Equation issues**: In Equation (5) and (6), \(x_t \otimes D x_o\) is a matrix, but the left-hand side is a vector; removing the vec(·) operation might fix the dimensionality mismatch. Reviewer 3 raises this as a technical inaccuracy.
- **Theoretical limitations of Lemma 1** (Reviewer 3, high confidence):
    - Assumption 1 (training and test token distributions are the same) is impractical for language modeling; training text and generated text typically differ.
    - Variance is computed per token, but \(g_{tr}\) and \(g_{te}\) are averages over multiple dependent tokens.
    - The claimed “cut-off” arises from normalizing eigenvectors by \(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\); without this normalization, top eigenvectors would receive smaller coefficients. The interpretation is not uniquely justified.
    - The argument that normalization ensures bounded \(c_j\) ignores heavy-tailed distributions; it would imply gradients lie in a low-dimensional subspace, which is not always true and when true indicates slow training (citing [1] An Investigation into Neural Net Optimization via Hessian Eigenvalue Density and [2] Efficient Sketches for Training Data Attribution).
- **Choice of projection dimension (k) not addressed**: The paper does not discuss how to select k in practice or what value was used in experiments. Reviewer 3 and Reviewer 4 both request this information.
- **Analysis of failure cases on Pythia-1.4B is insufficient** (Reviewer 2): The qualitative analysis shows worse performance, but no investigation into whether this is due to the projection step or inherent limitations of influence functions for that model/architecture. The authors should analyze gradient components before/after projection to quantify information loss, and characterize which types of examples fail (e.g., rare tokens, specific sequences).
- **Practical implications of the damping connection are unclear** (Reviewer 2): How does the projection dimension affect the effective damping? Is the damping effect uniform across layers or more pronounced in certain parameter groups? More explicit guidance is needed.
- **Storage requirement trade-off insufficiently discussed** (Reviewer 2, also mentioned by Reviewer 1 indirectly): 3.5 TB for LoGra vs 89 GB for EKFAC is a 40× increase. The paper should discuss practical implications for researchers with limited storage, how storage scales with model size, and the cost-benefit analysis including storage, data transfer, and accessibility. Reviewer 2 specifically asks for this.
- **Compute/memory complexity not fully reported** (Reviewer 1): Order complexities hide constants; a breakdown in terms of model parameters, batch size, and sequence length would help comparison. Additionally, compute and memory (FLOPs, forward/backward overhead) for methods compared in Figure 4 should be explicitly reported.
- **Quantitative evaluation impossibility not justified** (Reviewer 1): Section 4.2 states quantitative accuracy measures are not possible without retraining. The paper should clarify why alternative evaluation strategies (e.g., held-out sets, proxy tasks) are not used.
- **Limited applicability to non-linear layer types** (Reviewer 4): LoGra’s low-rank projection is currently designed for linear and QKV layers. The paper should discuss extension to convolutional layers (sparse connections, shared weights), normalization layers (LayerNorm, BatchNorm with small parameter sizes), recurrent layers (complex interdependence), and sparse fully connected layers. A more detailed explanation of how the method handles these is needed.
- **Impact of low-rank projection on attribution accuracy not quantified** (Reviewer 4): The paper lacks a quantitative analysis of approximation errors under different low-rank dimensions, and how the trade-off between efficiency and accuracy (e.g., comparing Figure 4 metrics vs Table 1) changes with varying rank.
- **Computational efficiency of dimensionality reduction methods not discussed** (Reviewer 4): The paper uses PCA and random projection without analyzing their runtime trade-offs. PCA involves covariance matrix computation and eigen-decomposition, which can be expensive in high dimensions; random projection is faster but may require ensembling, adding overhead. These efficiency considerations should be included.
