## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes enriching preference datasets with machine-generated rationales that explain choice reasons, and introduces Rationale-DPO (RDPO), which adds a rationale likelihood loss to the DPO objective. Experiments on Orca and UltraFeedback datasets with models including Mistral-7B-v0.1, Zephyr-7B-Beta, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct show improved data efficiency, faster convergence, reduced verbosity bias, and comparable or better win rates vs. DPO. However, reviewers are split: one is very positive (score 8, confidence 5), while three are critical (scores 5,5,3, confidence 3,3,4) citing limited novelty, unclear theoretical motivation, weak experimental rigor, and missing comparisons with related work in synthetic preference generation and generative reward modeling.

### Strengths
* This study is well motivated and adds to the literature of incorporating richer feedback (additional rationales) to do more efficient RLHF alignment.
* The paper proposes a new approach to integrate model-generated rationales in preference tuning, avoiding the need for additional human labels.
* The area of generative reward modeling is important and gaining traction.
* (Reviewer 1) The paper is well written with clear notations and provides up-to-date literature on RLHF techniques. It underscores the potential of rationale-based data augmentation, paving ways for more effective language model alignment.
* (Reviewer 1) The experiments on Orca and UltraFeedback are convincing, with rational theoretical analysis using mutual information and in-depth ablation discussion in appendix B.2.
* (Reviewer 1) Mentions relevant mechanism design references (Zhang & Duan, 2024; Matsushima & Noda, 2023) that support the economic utility of dataset design.
* (Reviewers 2,3,4) Experimental results show that optimizing rationale likelihood alongside preference loss boosts model performance, reduces annotation needs and training data volume, and performs comparably or better than DPO in multiple settings.

### Weaknesses
* **Limited novelty and poor positioning w.r.t. related work.** The methodology (maximizing likelihood of preference and rationale) is very intuitive and simple and is not significantly novel (Reviewer 2). The paper lacks positioning against growing literature on synthetic preference generation and generative reward modeling, including missing references: RLCD (Yang et al., 2024), West-of-N (Pace et al., 2024), Judging LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2024), Self-Taught Evaluators (Wang et al., 2024). It also focuses entirely on direct preference optimization and does not discuss translation to the reward modeling + RL paradigm (Reviewer 4).
* **Unclear theoretical motivation and analysis.**
  * Equation 2 decomposition: why does the preference label not depend on the rationale when Section 5 uses “preference inferred from the rationale” (opposite formulation)? (Reviewer 4)
  * The claim of “closed-form relationship between rationale informativeness and its alignment with true preferences” appears in the appendix without explanation – what is the form of this relationship? (Reviewer 4)
  * Information-theoretic results are completely relegated to the appendix and cannot be counted as a contribution (Reviewer 4).
* **Insufficient ablation and component analysis.**
  * In the ablation on γ, the scale from 1.0 to 10.0 does not matter; did the authors try smaller or extremely large γ? (Reviewer 3)
  * How does tuning solely on rationale likelihood without DPO loss affect performance? (Reviewer 3)
  * Justification is needed for using a variable γ given the theoretical suggestion of γ=1 (Reviewer 3).
* **Experimental design lacks rigor and thoroughness.**
  * Reporting win-rate against DPO alone does not capture absolute improvement; should report against a fixed opponent (e.g., GPT-4 on AlpacaEval 2.0) to ensure the baseline DPO model is properly optimized (Reviewer 3).
  * There is no evidence that the DPO model is fully optimized. For example, Llama3-8b-instruct + Ultrafeedback annotated by PairRM can achieve 40% LC win-rate, but the appendix reports below 25% – so one cannot conclude the rationale loss significantly improves performance (Reviewer 3).
  * Preference dataset sizes are typically >>11K; why focus on small, non-representative sizes (e.g., 1K, 12K)? Why is there no improvement with DPO beyond 1K preferences? (Reviewer 4)
  * In L353, why is the DPO model trained with 12K Ultrafeedback used as baseline if its SFT performance is lower than that of models trained on less data? (Reviewer 4)
  * Why not evaluate on established benchmarks like RewardBench or AlpacaEval (Reviewer 4)?
  * How does RDPO with poor quality rationales (e.g., permuted or opposite) perform against standard DPO? How can practitioners ensure rationales quality is sufficient? (Reviewer 4)
  * Why does RDPO perform similarly to DPO when trained on Llama-3-8B in Figure 5? (Reviewer 4)
  * Length bias: RDPO produces much shorter responses and maintains a higher win-rate against DPO, which is surprising and deserves analysis. But in section B.4, length on AlpacaEval 2.0 remains close to DPO or the original model – inconsistency needs explanation (Reviewer 3).
* **Statistical significance and data efficiency concerns.**
  * Fig 2 suggests DPO > RDPO with 1K Ultrafeedback data, but Fig 3 shows the opposite – if this is due to statistical uncertainty, error bars should be shown (Reviewer 4).
  * In Figure 2, DPO win rate lags behind RDPO by only ~5-8% for the same data points, but RDPO requires a lot more text per data point (Reviewer 2).
* **Practical cost and data collection difficulties.**
  * Generating qualitative rationales increases annotation cost in dollar terms; a breakeven analysis and operating guidance for budget-limited projects is missing (Reviewer 1).
  * Procuring rationales can be more expensive than binary feedback; for certain cases (e.g., artwork comparison), humans may not be able to explain their choice (Reviewer 2).
  * Using LLMs to generate rationales risks misaligned responses (e.g., not harmless) and may create an echo chamber (no new perspectives beyond the LLM’s beliefs) (Reviewer 2).
* **Minority contrast:** One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 8, high confidence) finds the theoretical analysis convincing and the work well-positioned, while others (Reviewers 2,3,4, scores 5,5,3, confidences 3,3,4) view theoretical claims as unclear or insufficient and the experimental setup as weak.

**Additional clarifications requested:**
* Instead of plotting data points w.r.t. performance, plot total number of text tokens used for training w.r.t. performance; report average training time for both procedures (Reviewer 2).
* Provide exact formula for win rates and any tie-breaking rules used (Reviewer 2).
* How are draws measured in Fig 2? Clarify the sentence: “the drawing rate for the RDPO model is stable and low across different training data sizes, which shows that RDPO winning rate is higher not due to flipping the draw points but the losing points” (Reviewer 4).
* Fig 2 caption typo: “Winrare” (Reviewer 4).
