## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Free Lunch Adversarial Attack (FLA), a black-box attack for text classification that requires neither knowledge of nor queries to the victim model. The attacker only has access to victim texts. FLA constructs a shadow dataset via clustering and assigns pseudo-labels, then iteratively trains a hierarchy of substitute models to approximate the victim’s decision boundaries. It generates adversarial examples using multiple existing attack methods and selects the best candidate. Experiments on SST2/SST5 and Emotion datasets and on LLMs (Qwen2, GPT) show that FLA achieves up to 45.99% ASR with zero query cost. One reviewer (score 5) praised the novelty and LLM results; the other three reviewers (score 3) raised significant concerns about assumptions, evaluation, presentation, and base attack contributions.

### Strengths

- **Novel threat model & zero-query attacks.** The idea of attacking with no access to the victim model or training data is novel and timely, especially for restricted LLM APIs, and the paper is the first to explore zero-query textual adversarial attacks with strong success rates.
- **Use of pseudo-labels and hierarchical substitution.** Constructing a surrogate model by clustering test texts and iteratively assigning pseudo-labels is interesting and underpins the attack.
- **Demonstration on LLMs.** FLA is evaluated on large language models (Qwen2, GPT family) and achieves up to 45.99% ASR, showing real-world security risks.
- **Empirical support.** Experiments on two datasets (SST2/SST5, Emotion) indicate effectiveness, and the inclusion of LLM evaluation is a positive addition.

### Weaknesses

- **Overstated “free lunch” assumption.** The assumption that the attacker has access to the victim’s test set is as restrictive (if not more) than query access or knowledge of architecture. The attack also requires iterative construction of shadow datasets and training multiple substitute models, which introduces computational cost that is omitted from the cost metric. Defining Cost solely as the number of queries to the victim model is unfair.
- **No fundamental contribution to attack techniques.** The paper does not propose a new adversarial generation method; it reuses existing attack methods directly (PSO, TextBugger, etc.) as “various attack methods”. The specific methods used in FLA for each experiment are not clearly stated, making it unclear whether the superior performance in Table 1 comes from the FLA framework or from an ensemble effect (Table 4 shows that using a single attack method inside FLA can be worse than applying that method directly, e.g., PSO with 7 models achieves 34.12% ASR on SST5-DistilBERT vs. 45.14% in Table 1).
- **Insufficient evaluation breadth.**  
  *Datasets/Tasks*: Only sentiment analysis (SST2, SST5, Emotion). Baselines (CT-GAT, HQA) are evaluated on more tasks; additional tasks (e.g., topic classification, toxicity) should be included.  
  *Adversarial defenses*: Only simple defenses tested; stronger defenses (TMD, RanMASK, InfoBert, AdvFooler, FreeLB, ASCC) are missing. The claim “training-free defense methods are more appropriate for FLA” is unsupported.  
  *Baselines in transfer-based setting*: Table 5 only compares with CT-GAT. Other attacks (TextBugger, PSO, GA, HQA) can be adapted for transfer-based attacks on LLMs; their performance should be reported.
- **Questions about experimental setting and reproducibility.**  
  *Low ASR of baselines*: In the paper’s experiments, baseline attacks (HQA, TextBugger) have much lower success rates and higher query costs than reported in their original papers (e.g., TextBugger achieves >70% ASR on AGNews with ~300 queries). The reason for this discrepancy is not explained.  
  *Clustering and pseudo-labeling*: The intuition for why clustering followed by pseudo-labeling approximates the victim’s decision boundaries is not rigorous, and there are no explicit assumptions about the relationship between cluster structure and victim model. Line 219 claims a 2-class dataset “approximates the distribution of arbitrary original data” without justification.  
  *Effect of embedding model*: T5 is used without specifying version or how embeddings are computed. The sensitivity of attack performance to the embedding model is unexamined.  
  *Zero-shot scenario (Section 5.4.2)*: If a public dataset with ground-truth labels is available, does FLA still use clustering? How does this compare to training substitution models directly on the labeled data?
- **Theorem 1 inaccurate for FLA workflow.** The theorem assumes that adversarial examples are tested against all substitution models and the attack succeeds if any one succeeds. In practice, FLA selects a single adversarial example based on a threshold. Table 4 shows that increasing the number of substitution models does not consistently improve ASR (e.g., PSO-FLA on Emotion with 1,3,7 models), contradicting Theorem 1.
- **Presentation and correctness issues.**  
  *Notations*: Line 184 should use ∈ instead of =; o_{ŷ_i} in Eq (6) is undefined; m is used for both number of labels and number of substitute models.  
  *Missing detail*: Section 4.1 does not contain the analysis that “using two clusters yields optimal attack performance” (claimed at Line 406).  
  *Cost metric*: Definition in the appendix is still confusing.  
  *Citation error*: TextBugger is cited as [1] (should be [2]).  
  *Typos and unclear phrases*: “eliciting strong responses” (Line 210), “transfer attack” (should be *transfer-based*), “Several” and “details” misspelled, Section 5.4.3 title “Attack on LLMs”.  
  *Table 4*: Should be described as 3 models, not 7.
- **Cannot perform targeted attacks.** Since FLA does not have access to true labels, it can only produce untargeted adversarial examples.

- **Training cost.** Training many substitute models (potentially tens per victim text) is expensive and the total training time is not reported.
