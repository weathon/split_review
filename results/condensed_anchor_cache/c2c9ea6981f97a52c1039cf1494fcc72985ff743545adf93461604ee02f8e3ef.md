## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes learning a Bregman divergence similarity metric between images via self-supervised training of convex neural networks, then uses this metric to define threat models and perform adversarial training with mirror descent. The method aims to improve robustness to common image corruptions. Reviews range from very positive (score 8, strong novelty and clear presentation) to very negative (score 3, limited technical contribution and insufficient evaluation). Two reviewers (3,4) rate the paper poorly, citing lack of rigorous analysis, missing comparisons, and straightforward engineering. One reviewer (2) is moderately positive but notes missing baselines and generalization concerns.

### Strengths
- **Mathematical elegance and motivation**: The formulation via Bregman divergence learned with self-supervision and adversarial training via mirror descent is mathematically clean and well-motivated (R1, R2). The use of Bregman divergences as an alternative to \(L^p\) norms for image similarity is a promising direction (R1).
- **Novelty**: The proposed training procedure is considered novel by R1 and R3, and the approach contributes to metric learning and out-of-distribution robustness (R1). *However, R4 strongly disagrees, stating that the technical contributions are limited and the algorithm is a direct application of Bregman divergence within an adversarial learning framework.*
- **Comparative performance**: The method outperforms \(L^2\) adversarial training and RLAT on CIFAR-10-C, particularly on contrast corruption (29% margin) (R1, R2, R3). The comparison to LPIPS shows the proposed metric is effective (R1). R3 notes superior performance across corruption severities.
- **Insights and presentation**: The paper is well written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4). The discussion of learned base functions for different corruptions provides interesting insights (R2). Table 1 and Algorithm 1 are helpful (R1). R4 praises exposition but views the contributions as limited.
- **Novel geometry**: Preliminary evidence is shown that the method learns a “geometry of image corruptions” (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation dataset**: Experiments are only on CIFAR-10-C; results on larger/more complex datasets like ImageNet-C are missing (R1, R3). R1 is concerned the method may not scale (convex architectures for base function), and R3 questions scalability to ImageNet.
- **Missing in-distribution accuracy and data augmentation baselines**: The paper does not report clean accuracy on CIFAR-10, making it hard to assess whether robustness gains come at the cost of standard performance (R2). Comparisons to simple data augmentation strategies that also use the corruption model \(\tau(x)\) are absent (R2). R2 asks for these baselines explicitly.
- **Practical applicability of corruption model**: The corruption model \(\tau(x)\) is simulated (CIFAR-10-C). The method’s generalization to real-world, unseen corruptions is not justified; overfitting to specific simulated corruptions is possible (R2).
- **Technical rigor and theoretical gaps**:
    - The assertion \((\nabla\phi)^{-1} = \nabla\bar{\phi}\) relies on the ICNN being of Legendre type, which is not rigorously proven (R3). The numerical inverse approximation via Eq. (7) using \(\ell_2\) loss is not justified: why \(\ell_2\)? Optimality and accuracy implications are unclear (R3).
    - Convergence of Bregman divergence training is not analyzed; the impact of sampling numbers on approximation quality is unknown (R3).
    - The projection onto the intersection of \(B_\phi\) and \(B\) (algorithm projecting onto \(B\) then \(B_\phi\)) does not guarantee the point lies in the intersection. A more rigorous projection method is needed (R3).
    - Lemma 1 is not important in the main text; the simpler sampling approach in A.2 is used in experiments anyway (R1). The connection between Figure 1 (with \(d=1\)) and Lemma 1 (says \(d\) in \((0,1)\)) is not explained (R3). The role of \(\mu\) as fraction of Gamma functions is unclear (R3).
    - The computation of \(\Gamma\) is approximated, though it should be tractable; this may introduce inaccuracies (R1).
- **Insufficient comparison to state-of-the-art**: R4 argues the paper lacks thorough comparison to existing adversarial training techniques, making it hard to assess practical improvement. R1 notes the proposed method does not perform best on zoom blur corruption.
- **Asymmetry of Bregman divergence**: The asymmetry is not adequately addressed; the symmetrized version is mentioned but not explored, yet many augmentations are symmetric (R1).
- **Projection and practical limitations**: The closed-form projection onto the Bregman ball is unavailable for neural networks; a binary search heuristic is used. R1 questions convergence of this heuristic as \(\eta \rightarrow 0\).
- **Limited technical contribution**: R4 views the paper as lacking technical novelty—only one lemma and an algorithm, no substantial theoretical advances. The algorithm is a direct application of Bregman divergence in an adversarial learning framework.
- **Inverse map training details**: It is not clear whether a `detach` operation is used when training the inverse map (Eq. 7). R1 asks for metrics of inverse map fit on test data and whether it can be fine-tuned at test time.
- **Computational cost unknown**: R3 requests a breakdown of computational cost (forward/backward passes per iteration).
- **Notation inconsistencies**: Fonts for variables like \(\tilde{x}\) and \(u\) are inconsistent (R3).
- **Minor issues**: Equation 3 seems out of place; Table 1 missing +1 in KL mirror map; mirror descent text should say “mapping \(z^{t+1}\) back to the *primal* space”; \(\tau\) used before definition (R1). Various typos listed by R1. R1 also asks about \(L_p\) adversarial robustness of the models, modes in divergence plots (Fig. 2), and choice of distance \(d\) in Fig. 3. R2 asks why the contrast-trained model performs well on both contrast and fog while fog-trained model is worse on both.
