## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Efficient episodic Memory Utilization (EMU) for cooperative MARL. EMU uses a learned encoder–decoder to create semantically coherent memory embeddings and an episodic incentive reward that augments the environment reward to promote desirable transitions. Evaluated on StarCraft II (SMAC) and Google Research Football (GRF), EMU shows superior performance over state-of-the-art methods. The approach aims to accelerate learning and avoid local optima.

### Strengths
- **Novel framework and underexplored area:** The application of episodic memory in MARL is novel, and the theoretical foundation is sound (R2, R3). The idea of semantically coherent memory via an autoencoder structure has not been explored in multi-agent settings before (R3).
- **Clear writing and presentation:** The paper is well-structured, easy to follow, and uses helpful visual illustrations (Figures 2 and 3) (R1, R3, R4).
- **Strong empirical results with extensive ablations:** EMU outperforms existing methods on SMAC and GRF benchmarks. Extensive ablation studies isolate the contributions of different components (R1, R3, R4).
- **Effective episodic incentive and semantic memory:** The episodic incentive mechanism is interesting and appears effective (R3).
- **Comprehensive theoretical analysis:** The paper provides theoretical support, including a proof that the approach helps policies converge to the optimal policy (R4).
- **Related work positioning:** One reviewer (R3, confidence 5) finds the related work review sufficient and well-positioned; however, note disagreement below.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited comparison with single‑agent episodic memory methods:** One reviewer (R2, confidence 4) notes that multiple works ([1–3]) have created similar frameworks in single‑agent settings (e.g., for exploration), and the paper does not compare with these or explain why they cannot be ported. The embedding procedure may be reinventing the wheel from existing procedures. This comparison should not be relegated to the appendix. In contrast, another reviewer (R3, confidence 5) finds the related work review sufficient and well‑positioned. (Disagreement preserved)
- **Threshold‑based desirability definition requires domain knowledge:** The method sets a return threshold (in experiments \(R_{thr}=R_{\max}\)) to classify trajectories as desirable/undesirable. Two reviewers (R3, confidence 5; R4, confidence 3) raise concerns:
  - The threshold may require domain knowledge to set properly; its fixed nature (is it dynamic?) limits applicability when the maximum return is unknown or variable.
  - The binary classification is sensitive and may not capture nuanced desirability.
  - If \(R_{\max}\) is unattainable, the method may be ineffective.
  - A clear discussion on how to adapt the threshold to other environments is lacking.
- **Computational and scalability concerns:**
  - Updating all memory keys when the encoder changes is computationally intensive, potentially a bottleneck in large‑scale or long‑episode environments (R3, confidence 5).
  - Scalability to vision‑based environments is unclear: storing many images, reconstructing them, and the effectiveness of the reward in high‑dimensional spaces are questionable (R1, confidence 3).
  - The embedding dimension is very small (dim=4, Table 4 in appendix), raising doubts about its capacity to reconstruct the global state. The paper does not sufficiently justify this choice or analyze its impact on performance (R4, confidence 3).
- **Evaluation: random projection vs. learned embedding yields minimal gain on standard metric:** The test win rate for random projection is almost equivalent to that of EmbNet/dCAE; improvement on the overall win‑rate (sample efficiency) is less significant. The trade‑off between the added complexity of the learned embedding and the practical gains is not fully analyzed. The paper should show curves for EMU with random projection in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 (R1, confidence 3).
- **Unclear motivation and intuition for episodic incentive:** The episodic incentive reward is set proportional to the difference between the true value and the predicted value; the reasoning is unclear. Is it to incentivize visits to states where the Q network has not converged? (R1, question 4). Also, is the incentive only effective when a desirable trajectory is found, which may be hard to obtain? (R3, question 2). The implications of Theorem 2 are not clearly explained (R1, question 5). Section 3.2 could use more intuition and better writing (R1).
- **Missing comparison with MAPPO:** The method should be compared with MAPPO to strengthen the evaluation (R3, question/weakness).
- **Minor writing issues:** There is an overuse of “the” throughout the text (R2).
