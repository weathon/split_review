## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes "loneliness," a nearest-neighbor-based metric defined on unlabelled training samples, for node and data selection in distributed and federated learning. The loneliness of a sample is its distance to the nearest neighboring sample within the same dataset; the loneliness of a dataset is the minimum over its sample-wise loneliness. The authors introduce a selection procedure called Goldilocks that chooses nodes and datapoints whose loneliness is close to an ideal (presumably known) value. The work claims that loneliness correlates with test accuracy and that using it for selection outperforms balanced-label-distribution approaches, while reducing privacy risks and data acquisition costs. The evaluation includes MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Fashion-MNIST in centralized and federated settings. Reviewers rate the paper low (score 1, 3, 3, 3), with concerns about novelty, privacy justification, reliance on a known optimal τ, and insufficient experiments.

### Strengths

- The paper is easy to follow and well written, with clearly described experiments and evaluation details.
- It tackles the relevant problem of node and dataset selection in federated learning.
- The concept of loneliness as an unlabelled-data quality metric is interesting and innovative in the context of privacy-preserving and resource-efficient distributed learning.
- By focusing on unlabelled data, the approach potentially reduces labeling costs and data transmission requirements, which is valuable for distributed and federated learning.
- The approach can generalize to various machine learning contexts, including centralized and federated frameworks, demonstrating flexibility.

### Weaknesses

- **Privacy-utility gap not justified.** The paper claims that sharing label fractions infringes privacy whereas sharing loneliness (which requires features) does not. This claim needs substantiation (theoretical or empirical) because label fractions typically pose minimal privacy risk, and direct access to features is often restricted in distributed learning (R2, R3, R4).
- **Loneliness is not a novel metric.** Nearest-neighbor distances have been used for decades in clustering, outlier detection, and data quality assessment (e.g., LOF, QI², ECS). The paper offers no significant conceptual advance over these existing distance-based measures (R1, R2, R4).
- **Flawed reliance on knowing the ideal metric value.** The Goldilocks procedure selects nodes and datapoints with loneliness close to an optimal value τ, but determining τ is the core challenge. The paper provides no method to estimate τ in practice, nor does it study sensitivity to mis-specification. One reviewer notes this is trivial once τ is known and makes the approach unrealistic unless τ is apriori known (R2, R4).
- **Problematic definition of dataset-level loneliness.** Using the minimum sample-wise loneliness (Eq. 3) is fragile: a single duplicated datapoint sets loneliness to 0, and for large datasets the minimum is often dominated by noise or duplicates. Using the average or other aggregation would be more stable and representative. No intuitive rationale is given for the min choice (R2, R3).
- **Insufficient experiments and missing baselines.** The evaluation only compares against balanced-label-distribution approaches, not against existing node/data selection methods (e.g., FedDC for small datasets, or other data valuation metrics). No ablation study is provided. MNIST is too simple for evaluating loneliness (k-NN achieves near-perfect accuracy on it). The federated experiments use mild Dirichlet heterogeneity (α ∈ [1,5]) instead of the more challenging α ≈ 0.01 typical in heterogeneous FL papers (R1, R2, R4).
- **Curse of dimensionality and norm choice.** Using L₂ distance on raw high-dimensional inputs (e.g., images) loses discriminatory power as all points become equally lonely. The paper does not justify the choice of L₂ nor analyze metric behavior under feature-space transformations. Loneliness measured in input space may be meaningless if representation learning can map to a better feature space (R2).
- **Computational overhead.** Calculating pairwise distances for all local datasets introduces significant cost. The paper lacks any analysis of computation time or memory requirements, which is critical for resource-constrained FL settings (R3).
- **Risk of overfitting to "easy" subsets.** By discarding nodes or datapoints with low loneliness (i.e., dense regions), the selection may exclude samples that are critical for representing the full distribution, leading to overfitting on selected easy data (R2).
- **Theoretical contribution is weak.** The proof that loneliness correlates with KL divergence is unsurprising (more outliers → harder to fit); more importantly, the submission does not contain the actual proof. One reviewer could not find it in the main text or appendix (R1, R2).
- **Missing comparisons to relevant metrics.** The paper does not benchmark loneliness against well-known outlier detection or data-quality metrics such as Local Outlier Factor (LOF), QI², ECS, or alternative KNN-based measures (R2, R3). The authors should justify why loneliness is more valuable than these existing approaches.
- **Definition of additional metric (μ̂) unclear.** The definition of μ̂ used in the evaluation (line 321) is not clearly explained (R3).
- **Unclear how to collect loneliness in FL with privacy constraints.** Even if sharing loneliness is less onerous than sharing labels, it is not explained how the central server would securely collect per-client loneliness values without violating privacy (R4).
