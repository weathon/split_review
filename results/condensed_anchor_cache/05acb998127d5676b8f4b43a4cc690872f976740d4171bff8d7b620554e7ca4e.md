## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the importance of handling ties in RLHF. The standard Bradley-Terry (BT) model assumes binary preferences and ignores ties. The authors propose using the generalized Bradley-Terry model with ties (BTT) to incorporate tie annotations. They provide theoretical analysis showing that ignoring ties causes bias in preference strength measurement. Experiments on synthetic datasets with ties labeled by open-source LLMs demonstrate improved performance over BT. Reviewers agree the topic is relevant but raise concerns about the practical significance, experimental sufficiency, theoretical assumptions, and necessity of the approach.

### Strengths
1. **Relevance and significance**: Including ties in preference modeling is highly relevant, as real-world human judgments often involve ties or ranking difficulties. The problem is worthy of exploration. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
2. **Clarity and readability**: The paper is well structured, easy to follow, and clearly explains the concepts. (R1, R2, R4)
3. **Theoretical insights**: The paper provides theoretical analysis showing that ignoring ties leads to bias in preference strength measurement, helping to understand the effectiveness of BTT. (R1, R3)
4. **Algorithm proposal**: The BTT model is proposed to address model mismatch in conventional preference datasets that lack tie annotations. (R3)
5. **Experimental demonstration**: Experiments show advantages of BTT over BT on synthetic preference datasets with ties. (R3) _(Note: Other reviewers found experiments insufficient; see Weaknesses.)_

### Weaknesses
1. **Practical significance and necessity of BTT**:
   - The problem requires relabeling preference data to include ties, which is not feasible for most existing public preference datasets (R1).
   - The necessity of explicitly introducing BTT is questioned: Table 1 shows that BT reward models already assign similar rewards to chosen and rejected responses when ties exist, suggesting BT can implicitly recognize ties without BTT. The paper should further justify why BTT is needed (R2).
   - Ties occur infrequently in real annotations (e.g., Anthropic HH-RLHF has over 160k samples with only a small portion labeled as ties), limiting the practical significance of the problem. It is unclear how the impact of ties changes with data scaling (R3).

2. **Theoretical concerns**:
   - The theoretical analysis is based on ground truth labels of ties rather than noisy label modeling. Since real-world tie annotations may be noisy, the superiority of the theoretical results is not meaningful (R1).

3. **Insufficient experimental evaluation**:
   - Only one dataset (HH-RLHF) is used, and only one baseline (DPO) is compared. Preference optimization has been explored for over a year with many datasets and methods available (R2, R4).
   - Evaluation on HH-RLHF is not convincing because that dataset contains very few tie samples. The construction of the main simulation dataset is also unclear (R1).
   - Metrics should include commonly used benchmarks such as AlpacaEval, MT-Bench, and Arena Hard (R1).
   - The method should be compared with more recent advances in preference optimization that consider noise, e.g., Robust Preference Optimization (R1, R2). The paper also lacks direct comparison with the related variant DPO with offset (ODPO, Amini et al., 2024) (R4).
   - Questions remain about how the BTT model parameters θ are determined (R3).
   - It is unclear whether experiments can be conducted on publicly available real-annotated datasets (R3).

4. **Presentation and layout**:
   - Figures (e.g., Figure 1, Figure 2) and tables (e.g., Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5) are overly large and occupy excessive space, detracting from readability and professional appearance. This suggests a rushed submission (R4). _(Note: Contrast with positive readability comments from R1, R2, R4 in Strengths.)_
