## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a dependent leverage score sampling method for active linear regression in the agnostic setting. The method uses pivotal sampling via a binary tree that matches the data geometry to promote spatial coverage. The authors prove that any sampling strategy with marginals proportional to leverage scores and satisfying a weak one-sided ℓ∞ independence condition requires only O(d log d) samples in general, matching i.i.d. leverage score sampling. For the special case of polynomial regression (linear regression over an infinite row set on a low-dimensional surface), they prove an improved O(d) sample complexity. Empirical evaluations on problems from learning PDE solutions and uncertainty quantification show that their pivotal sampling algorithm (Algorithm 2) reduces sample requirements by up to 50% compared to existing methods.

### Strengths

- The paper is extremely well-written, with clear motivation, thorough discussion of prior work, and contextualization of results (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The proposed pivotal sampling method using a binary tree tournament is novel, innovative, easy to implement, and appealing (R1, R3, R4, R5).
- The theoretical results are solid. The main theorem shows that leverage score sampling with limited independence still yields a matching O(d log d) sample complexity bound, which inspires the design of problem-adaptive sampling algorithms (R3, R5). For polynomial regression, they achieve an optimal O(d/ε) bound without resorting to the BSS barrier function argument – a very interesting result (R5).
- The analysis is general enough to be applied to any non-independent leverage score sampling method that obeys a weak one-sided ℓ∞ independence condition; this may be of independent interest (R4).
- The empirical evaluations are compelling and clearly show that pivotal sampling significantly outperforms Bernoulli leverage score sampling, with up to 50% fewer samples needed to reach a given accuracy (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
- Providing approaches with improved empirical performance even when theoretical guarantees are similar is an important contribution (R2).

**Note:** Reviewers disagree on technical novelty. R4 considers the algorithm and its analysis novel to the best of his/her knowledge. R1 and R5, while acknowledging the results are interesting, comment that the techniques are not completely novel: the first result heavily relies on [KKS22] and the adaptation to approximate matrix multiplication follows standard arguments; the second result mainly follows [KKP17]. Despite this, both R1 and R5 rate the contribution “good” (score 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Technical novelty.** The auxiliary results for Theorem 1.1 (Lemma B.1) appear to be direct consequences of existing matrix Bernstein inequalities, e.g. [Vershynin, Section 5.4]. Similarly, the approximate matrix multiplication results seem established in [Tropp, 2012, Section 6.4]. The authors should clarify why standard concentration results cannot be applied directly and what specific challenges necessitate their alternative approach (R1). The core arguments for Theorem 1.2 follow [KKP17]; adaptation to the pivotal sampling scheme, while non-trivial, does not introduce a fundamentally new proof technique (R5). Some reviewers (R1, R5) found the technical novelty limited, though R4 considered the algorithm and analysis novel overall.

- **Empirical evaluation scope.** Only one baseline (Bernoulli leverage score sampling) is included in the main experiments. The authors should compare with:
  - Maximum leverage score sampling (R3).
  - The provably optimal method of Chen and Price (2019), which is only shown in Figure 8c of the appendix. It would be compelling to include it in Figure 4 (main body) (R4). Also, Reviewer 2 suggests adding a random permutation of rows as the leaves of the tree in addition to PCA-based ones.
  - The set of test problems would benefit from higher-dimensional instances to better assess scalability and robustness (R1).
- **Computational complexity.** The paper lacks both theoretical analysis and empirical results regarding the computational complexity of pivotal sampling, particularly the construction of the binary tree. If running time is a concern in large-scale systems, it is not clear that a PCA-based approach is always feasible (R2, R3).
- **Empirical improvement not fully justified theoretically.** The analysis does not explain why the method performs more than 50% better than independent leverage score sampling in practice, except for ℓ₂ polynomial regression. The authors concede this limitation (R4).
- **Lack of clarity on the 50% reduction claim.** The authors should explicitly state this claimed improvement in the technical sections (R2).
- **Impact of tree selection.** The choice of the tree in Algorithm 1 significantly affects the performance; its impact is not analyzed (R2).
- **Constants are important.** Since the motivation partly relies on constants being better than the theoretically optimal method of Chen and Price (which performs poorly in practice), it would be useful to note the constants explicitly where possible (R2).
- **One-sided ℓ∞ independence condition.** The main theorem could be stated in terms of the parameter D_inf; it would be interesting to note its value for independent uniform sampling, and whether D_inf is the same for any independent sampling approach (R2).
- **Minor typos.** “Theoretic” should be “theoretical” (p.3); “Bernouilli” should be “Bernoulli” (p.6) (R2).
- **Extension questions.** Can the “tournament” style technique be applied to other norms and their optimal sampling strategies? (R2). Can the argument be generalized to BSS-type sampling to achieve optimal sample complexity while leveraging problem structure? (R5).
