## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces few-shot generative Hybrid Domain Adaptation (HDA), adapting a pre-trained GAN generator to integrate attributes from multiple target domains using only a few images per domain, while preserving source domain characteristics. The proposed discriminator-free method uses a CLIP-based directional subspace loss (distance loss to blend target attributes, direction loss to preserve source features). Experiments on FFHQ with attributes like age, smile, sketch compare favorably to baselines (e.g., DoRM) on semantic similarity, fidelity, and cross-domain consistency, with faster training.

### Strengths
- The proposed HDA task is interesting and well-motivated: collecting data for independent target domains is often easier than for every combination thereof.
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and the method is simple, intuitive, and clearly described.
- The discriminator-free approach reduces computational cost compared to adversarial alternatives.
- Results on FFHQ are convincing and surpass strong/recent baselines (e.g., DoRM) on all metrics; qualitative results are compelling.
- Quantitative results show faster training than competing methods (though details are contested, see Weaknesses).
- The method handles large domain gaps (e.g., face to sketch) and ablation studies confirm component contributions.
- **Contrast**: Two reviewers (score 8) found the problem well-motivated and results convincing; a third reviewer (score 5) was less convinced, citing limited domain diversity and over-reliance on face datasets, which they argued weakens evidence for generalizability.

### Weaknesses
1. **Motivation and novelty**: The paper claims to introduce hybrid generative DA, but prior work (e.g., DoRM) already addresses similar settings (Fig. 1 of DoRM). The distinction from existing work (beyond requiring separate models) needs clearer articulation. One reviewer questioned why existing domain adaptation or generalization techniques cannot handle composite attribute generation; another asked why HDA is needed when attributes like smile/age are already editable via conventional StyleGAN editing methods.
2. **Limited domain and dataset scope**: Experiments focus only on face datasets (FFHQ) and a narrow set of attributes (age, smile, sketch). This does not demonstrate capacity for more complex domain shifts (texture, lighting, viewpoint) or generalizability to non-face object categories. The method lacks face-specific components, making the evaluation gap more concerning.
3. **Insufficient ablation on number of shots**: Results are reported only for 10 shots. Experiments with 1-shot and 5-shot are necessary to assess robustness and stability in very low-shot regimes, especially for the directional subspace loss.
4. **Unaddressed hyperparameter sensitivity**: The approach requires manual tuning of α_i weights per target domain. No principled method for selection is provided, and sensitivity analysis is missing. Optimal values likely vary across domains and combinations, hindering practical adoption.
5. **Missing comparisons to relevant methods**:
   - No comparison to diffusion model personalization (e.g., Dreambooth), which would contextualize trade-offs between GAN and diffusion fine-tuning.
   - No comparison to few-shot GAN adaptation methods with discriminators, such as DualStyleGAN and 3DAvatarGAN.
   - No comparison to Nitzan et al. (CVPR 2023) on repurposing dormant latent directions, which is conceptually related.
   - No discussion of “Mind the GAP” and “StyleCLIP”, which use similar CLIP-based directional losses and domain constraints.
6. **Lack of qualitative/quantitative evaluation of source domain preservation**: The paper does not evaluate editability (e.g., pose manipulation via 2D generator) before and after adaptation. No quantitative metrics assess whether the original generator’s attributes are retained. The impact on 3D GAN settings is also unexplored.
7. **Lack of discussion on conditional generation**: It is unclear if the method can generate images conditioned on specific target attributes (rather than a single composite style). The paper should discuss limitations and potential extensions for controllable generation.
8. **Incomplete details on training time comparisons**: Table 2 lacks transparency: were baseline numbers obtained from official code? Were hardware/GPU configurations exactly matched for an apples-to-apples comparison? The reported speed advantage is not fully verifiable without this information.
9. **Impact of pre-trained image encoder**: The choice of encoder (architecture, pre-training objective) is not studied. Its effect on image quality and adaptation convergence should be analyzed.
10. **Comparison to large-scale text-to-image models**: One reviewer asked how the method compares to models like DALL·E and Imagen (though direct comparison is difficult, a discussion of relative advantages would be valuable).
