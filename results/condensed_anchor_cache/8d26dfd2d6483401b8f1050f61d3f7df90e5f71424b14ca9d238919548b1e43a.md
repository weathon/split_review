## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies overoptimization in composite reward models (RMs) for LLM alignment, showing that correlation between component RMs significantly affects overoptimization thresholds (“proxy points”). It introduces constrained RL approaches (ξ‑PPO, μ‑PPO, PPO‑SAT) that keep each proxy reward below its threshold, and NM‑PPO, a derivative‑free method (Nelder‑Mead) that identifies proxy points during a single training run. Empirical experiments validate the methods.

### Strengths
- The analysis of overoptimization in composite reward functions is interesting, important for LLM alignment, and novel (to the best of the reviewers’ knowledge).  
- The concept of “proxy points” as thresholds for proxy rewards is a commendable original contribution.  
- The proposed constrained RL solution is simple yet effective, and the interaction between component RMs in influencing proxy points is correctly identified.  
- Experiments with multiple constrained optimization variants (ξ‑PPO, μ‑PPO, PPO‑SAT) and the derivative‑free NM‑PPO are thorough; NM‑PPO is novel and computationally efficient.  
- The paper is generally well written, with sufficient related work and explanation.  
- The topic tackles a critical issue in the popular and rapidly deploying field of LLMs.

### Weaknesses
1. **Heuristic determination of the joint maximizing proxy point (Reviewer 1)**.  
   Proxy points are obtained by fitting a surface over evaluation scores from runs that maximize each reward individually, without accounting for interactions between RMs. The resulting joint point may not align with the true optimal weighting.  

2. **Impractical assumption of a ground‑truth reward model (Reviewers 2, 4; Reviewer 3 asks about scalability)**.  
   The method relies on an oracle/gold reward model to identify proxy points. As the authors acknowledge, this restricts applicability to real‑world RLHF where such an oracle is unavailable or extremely costly. This concern makes the approach more of a theoretical analysis tool than a practical solution.  

3. **Insufficient detail and missing evidence in methodological explanation (Reviewers 1, 2, 3; Reviewer 4 asks about necessity of all constraints)**.  
   - The precise implementation of the constrained RL approaches (how proxy points are incorporated into the optimization objectives) is not adequately detailed.  
   - Specific missing clarifications:  
     - Why is KL regularization not used when determining the proxy points (Fig. 3.1)?  
     - In Equation (4.3), should \(v_\mu(s)\) encompass the second term? (mixed advantages)  
     - For PPO‑SAT, are the RM weightings fixed or adjusted via Lagrangian multipliers? The paper says they are fixed in Section 5, which needs explanation.  
     - In Figure D.2, why is a tanh constraint used for what appears to be an inequality constraint? This seems inconsistent with Section 4.  
     - Does All‑PPO have a KL constraint?  
     - How is line 9 of Algorithm 2 computed?  
   - Important information is relegated to the appendix (e.g., sample outputs in D.2) without highlighting key features in the main text.  
   - Missing evidence: plot of NM‑PPO training curve; plot of ξ‑PPO and μ‑PPO in Figure D.2 for stability comparison; sample outputs in Section D.2 lack corresponding evaluation scores and reward values, making performance comparison hard.  

4. **Cost and scalability concerns (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)**.  
   - The method requires multiple runs to identify proxy points for each RM; NM‑PPO reduces this but still queries the evaluation metric during training (number of queries not reported for Figs. 3.2 and 5.1).  
   - It is unclear how easy it is to identify proxy points in larger or multi‑topic datasets, or how results might differ for more advanced LLMs (e.g., GPT‑4).  

5. **Evaluation metric (Equation A.1) lacks in‑depth justification (Reviewer 2)**.  
   The metric combines METEOR and intent reward alongside a ground‑truth evaluation (respecting Goodhart’s Law), but there is little discussion of its relevance for LLM‑based RLHF. The use of ROUGE‑2 may not capture dialogue‑specific text quality, and the diversity metrics need stronger justification. It would be helpful to explain how the metric was chosen, whether other metrics were considered, and the design principles behind its construction.  

6. **Unexplained behavior of certain methods (Reviewers 1, 4)**.  
   - In Figure 5.1 (right), ξ‑PPO’s performance drops after about 180k training steps – are the constraints still satisfied during this stage?  
   - μ‑PPO performs well even though its proxy point thresholds are exceeded, yet Figure 5.3 shows that using thresholds higher than the proxy point thresholds significantly hurts ξ‑PPO. This apparent contradiction requires explanation.  
   - Is it necessary in practice to satisfy all constraints? An ablation comparing satisfaction of only the intent constraint or only the METEOR constraint would clarify.  

**Reviewer disagreement note**: While Reviewers 1 and 3 found the paper very strong (scores 8), Reviewers 2 and 4 were more critical (scores 6). The most pointed criticisms concern the impractical ground‑truth assumption, insufficient methodological detail, and missing empirical evidence. All reviewers agree the work is well motivated and tackles an important problem.
