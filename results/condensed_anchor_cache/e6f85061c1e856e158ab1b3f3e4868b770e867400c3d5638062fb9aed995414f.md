- Scores: 6, 3, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using minimax expected regret (MMER) level sampling in Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) to mitigate goal misgeneralization in reinforcement learning. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem and the novelty of applying regret-based prioritization, along with a formal theoretical framework distinguishing ambiguating and disambiguating levels. However, significant concerns were raised about the clarity of the setting (reward‑free vs. reward‑based UMDP), limited and inconclusive experiments (only one gridworld domain, 3‑4 seeds, no statistical tests), confusing visualizations and axis labels, an overcomplicated and potentially non‑novel theoretical section, lack of comparison to alternative level‑sampling or learning approaches, and presentation issues (typos, unclear notation). One reviewer (score 6) found the theoretical analysis clear and the empirical evaluation effective, while others (scores 3,5,3) considered the evidence weak, the theory confusing, and the experiments insufficient to support the claims.

### Strengths
- **Important problem and novel idea**: Goal misgeneralization is a critical issue especially in safety‑critical RL; applying minimax regret (MMER) to mitigate it is a novel and promising approach (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Formalization of level ambiguity**: The distinction between “disambiguating” and “ambiguating” levels is a novel and intuitive contribution that helps understand the problem (Reviewer 3).
- **Theory and proofs**: The paper provides a clear, well‑structured theoretical analysis (Reviewer 1) with rigorous proofs that MMER policies avoid goal misgeneralization in the resource‑allocation setting (Reviewer 3). Experiments include several variants of baselines (Reviewer 2).
- **Combined theory and experiments**: The paper includes both theoretical arguments and empirical results (Reviewers 1, 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Ambiguous setting (reward‑free vs. reward‑based)**: The paper introduces reward‑free UMDP (no reward observed) in Section 3, but later assumes a ground‑truth reward (e.g., Definition 3.1). The transition between settings is not explained, leaving unclear whether the learner observes a reward or not (Reviewer 2). This confusion persists through theory and experiments (Reviewer 2).  
  *Related question*: In Definition 4.1, are states part of the trajectory? Clarify that \(s_i,s_j\) belong to the trajectory (Reviewer 2; also noted as typo by Reviewer 4).
- **Overcomplicated and confusing theoretical section**: A large part of the theory is devoted to a “resource‑allocation” setting that is restrictive and has unclear connection to actual RL environments. The proof is unnecessarily complicated and does not provide clear intuition why MMER works beyond “prioritize high‑regret levels”. The presentation makes the logical flow between theory, method, and experiments hard to follow (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 1 also noted structural clarity issues). Reviewers suggest deferring much of the resource‑allocation part to the appendix to allow more space for empirical results and intuitive explanations (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited and potentially non‑novel theory**: The claim that worst‑case (MMER) is better than average case under distribution shift is already implied by covariate‑shift literature (horizon=1, level parameter controls initial states). Thus the paper’s theoretical results may not provide new insights (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient experiments**:  
  - Only one gridworld domain (“Cheese in the corner”) is tested; missing domains used in prior work (e.g., “Keys and Chest”, “Tree Gridworld” with denser rewards) makes it hard to assess generality (Reviewer 3).  
  - Experiments are limited to simple configurations; need more complex gridworlds (varying sizes, obstacles, reward structures) to establish robustness (Reviewers 1, 3).  
  - The absence of robustness results for “Cheese on a dish” (e.g., how ACCEL and PLR scale with different feature numbers) is a gap (Reviewer 3, Question 3).  
  - Only 3–4 seeds per experiment, below typical deep RL standards; lack of statistical significance testing weakens the claims (Reviewer 3).  
  - The empirical evidence is not conclusive: most regret‑based methods track domain randomization (DR) closely, only diverging at very low mixture weights (around 1e‑3 in “Cheese in the corner”). The mutation method for environment generation may be more impactful than MMER itself. The main divergence comes from \(\text{ACCEL}_\text{id}\), not from MMER per se (Reviewer 3).  
- **Confusing visualizations and missing explanations**:  
  - Figures 2C and 2G show “prop of OOD training levels” vs. “mixture weight” without defining either axis; the meaning of \(\alpha\) (proportion of ambiguating or disambiguating levels?) is not explained (Reviewer 3).  
  - The terms “ambiguating” vs. “disambiguating” and “OOD” are not defined in the context of training levels; the connection to goal misgeneralization is not obvious (Reviewer 3).  
  - Inconsistent use of percentages vs. proportions: e.g., line 412‑413 says “3‑10% of training levels are disambiguating” while line 420 says “\(\alpha = 0.03\%\)”; the scale of \(\alpha\) in the plots (max 1) does not match the percentage descriptions (Reviewer 3).  
  - Why is the vertical axis in Figures 2D and 4G on a log scale, given reward is 0‑1? The rationale is not explained (Reviewer 3, Question 2).  
  - The corner size \(c\) in “Cheese in the corner” and how it relates to level ambiguity is not explained (Reviewer 3, Question 1).  
- **Lack of comparisons to alternative approaches**: The paper does not compare MMER level sampling against other plausible strategies, such as maximin reward, novelty‑based level prioritization, or improvements to the inner‑loop (e.g., better exploration via intrinsic curiosity). Without such comparisons, it is unclear whether MMER is uniquely effective or just one of many viable methods (Reviewer 3).  
- **Possible unreliability of MMER in practice**: A reviewer hypothesized that the best performance of max‑latest regret estimator (over GAE‑based regret) may stem from value overestimation on disambiguating levels, combined with learning rate and entropy regularization, rather than from MMER itself. This suggests MMER may not be reliable in isolation and deserves further investigation (Reviewer 3, Question 4).  
- **Poor formulation and modeling**: The paper does not mention constrained RL, even though the resource‑allocation setting is essentially a constrained problem. Associating resource/budget constraints with reward functions (rather than state‑actions) is counter‑intuitive and limits applicability; reformulating with a cost function on state‑action pairs would be more natural and powerful (Reviewer 4).  
- **Presentation issues**: Many typos (e.g., line 190 missing “is”, line 191 double “the”, “allocationof” on line 240); the return of a trajectory should not depend on \(\theta\) (lines 87‑88); notation \(\sum_{s_t \in \tau}\) should also sum over actions and next states (lines 241,248). Definition 4.2: \(G\) has no reward associated with it (Reviewer 4; also noted by Reviewer 2 as a question).  
- **Structure and clarity**: The logical flow between theory, method, and empirical evaluation is not always easy to follow (Reviewer 1). The paper is difficult to read overall (Reviewer 4).
