## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper defines "training on the test task" as a confound in LLM evaluation: models exposed to task-relevant data during pretraining appear superior, but this advantage disappears when all models are fine-tuned on the same task data. The authors argue that performance differences across model families and claims of emergent capabilities are confounded by this practice. They propose fine‑tuning all models on task‑relevant data before evaluation to adjust for the effect, and show that emergent behavior gradually disappears under this protocol.

### Strengths
- Explores an increasingly important area—the "science of evaluations"—addressing a novel and surprising confound (training on the test task, distinct from training on the test data) that has broad implications for benchmarking and emergent‑capability claims. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- Experiments are logical and well thought through, using a large set of base models of varying sizes, allowing scaling comparisons. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- The proposed method (fine‑tuning all models on the same task‑relevant data) is promising, effective, and computationally light. (Reviewer 1)
- Clearly presented with clean, legible figures and persuasive argumentation. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- Creative experiments show that older models do equally well when given more task data, that changing question style (not content) closes the gap, and that model families follow the same scaling law under fine‑tuning. (Reviewer 4)
- Demonstrates that instances of emergent behavior disappear gradually as models are trained on the test task. (Reviewers 1, 3)
- Argues convincingly that training on task‑specific data, not fundamental architectural improvements, is responsible for recent performance boosts when FLOPs are held constant. (Reviewer 2)

### Weaknesses
- Only tests pre‑trained base models, not instruction‑tuned or chat‑finetuned models, which are more commonly evaluated in practice. Unclear whether findings apply to already‑finetuned models. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- The cutoff date of November 2023 for “old” vs “new” models is arbitrary and insufficiently justified. Alternative analyses (e.g., why November 2023 rather than November 2022; evidence that post‑cutoff models include instruction data in pretraining) are needed. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- Fine‑tuning all models on the test task may measure performance on a specific benchmark rather than the general ability the benchmark is meant to measure (“fight fire with fire” concern). (Reviewer 1)
- Does not directly test the hypothesis that newer models have more in‑distribution task data in their pretraining sets, despite arguing for it by implication from the fine‑tuning results. Direct evidence (e.g., sampling unconditionally from base models, checking open‑source datasets) is missing. (Reviewer 3)
- p‑values for significance in Figure 1 overestimate significance because models come from only a few model families (correlation). (Reviewer 3)
- Impact of hyperparameter tuning on fine‑tuning results is unclear; additional clarity on the hyperparameter sweep (Appendix A.2) is needed. (Reviewer 2)
- Only a couple of main evaluation datasets (MMLU, GSM8K) are explored. (Reviewer 2)
- Figure 3 is unclear: the added information relative to Figure 1 is not obvious, and the shared color scheme is misleading since the underlying data differ. (Reviewer 4)
- Minor presentation issues: typos (“These results provides…” line 244; missing period after “Figure 1” line 259); consider turning “What does MMLU test for?” into an active statement; inconsistent formatting of R² (lines 429 vs 430); ambiguous elisions in “of some”/“of others” (line 37). (Reviewer 4)
- Some could argue the finding is obvious given recent training practices, but the reviewer notes that the paper shows no additional additive benefit from recent models beyond fine‑tuning, making the result non‑obvious. (Reviewer 4, minority contrast)
- Questions remain about applicability to LLMs specifically (e.g., whether “LLM” should appear in the title). (Reviewer 2)
- Final paragraph of Section 4.2 was not fully followed; the paper’s own results seem to answer the posed question (task‑training explains improvements outside scaling laws). (Reviewer 4)
- Equation fitted in Figure 8 (bottom row) is not motivated; switching from Equation 1 (top row) to another form is unexplained, and the caption/labels could be clearer (e.g., hint that data are the same in both rows, row labels within the figure). (Reviewer 4)
