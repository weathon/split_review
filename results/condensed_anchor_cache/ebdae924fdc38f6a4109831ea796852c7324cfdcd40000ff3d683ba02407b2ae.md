## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DeltaGNN, which uses an “information flow score” based on first and second delta embeddings ($\Delta_u^t$ and $\mathbb{V}_t[\Delta_u^2]$) to guide graph rewiring that decouples the graph into homophilic and heterophilic subgraphs for separate aggregation. The goal is to mitigate over‑smoothing and over‑squashing while capturing both short‑ and long‑range interactions with linear complexity. Reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the score and the simultaneous treatment of both issues, but raise serious concerns about conceptual clarity, theoretical justification, experimental scope, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Novel connectivity measure**: The information flow score, supported by theoretical lemmas, provides a new way to numerically identify nodes responsible for over‑smoothing and over‑squashing (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5). Reviewer 2 specifically finds Lemma 1’s suggestion—that heterophilic edges can be identified via feature differences—constructive.
- **Simultaneous treatment of over‑smoothing and over‑squashing**: Addressing both problems together is valuable and relatively rare (Reviewer 5). The approach of decoupling into homophilic/heterophilic subgraphs and performing dual aggregation is considered innovative and promising (Reviewer 2).
- **Computational efficiency**: The complexity of the information flow score is superior to other rewiring methods, and the method demonstrates strong scalability and prediction accuracy in the reported experiments (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- **Empirical performance**: DeltaGNN shows consistent improvements across 10 datasets and outperforms all compared baselines (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 1 praises the connections drawn between delta embeddings and the two problems.

### Weaknesses
- **Confusion between over‑smoothing and heterophily**: The paper repeatedly implies that heterophily causes over‑smoothing (e.g., lines 110, 121, 161). Multiple reviewers (4, 5) stress that over‑smoothing is a spectral phenomenon (low‑frequency domination) that can occur on homophilic graphs and is not caused by heterophily. Heterophily causes performance degradation regardless of depth, but the two issues should not be conflated.
- **Unclear motivation for the heterophilic component**: Several reviewers (1, 4, 5) question why introducing heterophilic edges is necessary to solve over‑smoothing/squashing, and whether the “heterophilic aggregation” on long‑range nodes is beneficial given evidence that over‑globalization can harm node classification (Reviewer 4 cites [2]). The paper does not provide direct proof that the decoupling improves node distinguishability (Reviewer 4).
- **Weak theoretical foundation for the score**:  
  * Lemma 1 is seen as deriving not actual homophily (which depends only on labels and topology) but node‑feature separability; the term “homophily” is used inappropriately (Reviewer 5). Reviewers also find the proof hard to follow (Reviewer 2) and note that the dependence on architecture is not explained (Reviewer 5).  
  * Lemma 2 is not a formal lemma; it is a discussion based on prior work (Nguyen et al.) and lacks a mathematical statement (Reviewer 5). The connection between the numerator of the score and over‑squashing is not adequately motivated (Reviewer 5).  
  * The measurement $\mathbb{V}_t[\Delta_u^2] = d(\Delta_u^t - \Delta_u^{t-1})$ uses outputs from different layers (different spaces), making the distance rationale questionable (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient and poorly‑chosen experiments**:  
  * No large‑scale experiments on datasets like ogbn‑arXiv or large non‑homophilous graphs; the claim of scalability is unvalidated (Reviewer 3).  
  * Benchmarks (Cora, Pubmed, Texas, Wisconsin, Cornell) are very small; long‑range interactions are debatable on Cora/Pubmed (Reviewer 5). Texas/Wisconsin/Cornell have known issues and high tuning sensitivity (Reviewer 5).  
  * Missing comparisons: MLP is not included—MLP often outperforms GNNs on Texas, Wisconsin, Cornell (Reviewer 3). State‑of‑the‑art heterophilic methods (e.g., ACM‑GCN+, GPR‑GNN, PPGNN, [4,6,7] from Reviewer 4) are absent. The baseline list (GCN, GAT, GIN) is not current SoTA (Reviewer 3).  
  * No evaluation on the challenging heterophilic datasets Squirrel and Chameleon (Reviewers 3, 4).  
  * The paper does not provide numerical evidence (e.g., change in homophily ratio) that the rewiring actually reduces over‑smoothing or bottlenecks on real data (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:  
  * The concept “aggregation velocity” for $\Delta_u^t$ is confusing and needs more explanation and examples (Reviewer 2).  
  * Implementation details of the Information Flow Control mechanism (score hill ascent framework) are relegated to the appendix, making the main text hard to follow (Reviewer 2).  
  * Figure 2 is difficult to interpret (feature density plot, score‑vs‑node plot) and labels like “enhance the graph score” are unclear (Reviewer 2).  
  * Paragraph 283–291 contains vague terms (“heterophilic graph condensation”, “homophilic GNN”) and is unclear (Reviewer 5).  
  * Notation issues: inconsistent use of $\mathcal{N}(u)$ vs. $N(u)$, and $\bigoplus\limits_{v \in \mathcal{N}(u)}\mathbf{M}_u$ should be $\mathbf{M}_v$ (Reviewer 2).  
  * Equation (1) is not the most general 1‑hop GNN aggregation; a residual term is typically included (Reviewer 5).  
  * Typo in line 181 (“$∆^t_u$ the first” → “$∆^t_u$ be the first”) (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing related‑work discussion**: The paper does not situate itself relative to graph‑filter‑based GNNs (e.g., GPR‑GNN, PPGNN) that also perform implicit rewiring (Reviewer 3). Missing citations on rewiring and Graph Transformers (Reviewer 5).
- **Potential memory and architectural issues**: DeltaGNN variants consume 2–3× more GPU memory than GCN on small graphs, raising concerns for larger graphs (Reviewer 3’s question). The GIN underperformance (Table 1) may stem from sum aggregation making variance dependent on node degrees, suggesting the method is not well‑suited for sum aggregation models (Reviewer 2’s question).
- **Over‑smoothing vs. over‑squashing ambiguity**: The introduction sometimes treats them as interchangeable, but they are distinct phenomena—over‑smoothing is feature convergence independent of topology, over‑squashing is topology‑dependent and affects long‑range interactions (Reviewer 5).
- **Removing bottleneck edges may disconnect components**: The rewiring cannot guarantee reduced over‑squashing unless node labels are correctly identified, which the algorithm does not know in advance (Reviewer 5).
