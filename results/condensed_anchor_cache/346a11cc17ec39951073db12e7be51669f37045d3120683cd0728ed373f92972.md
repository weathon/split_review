## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper investigates how LLMs generalize in reasoning tasks versus factual recall by tracing the influence of pretraining data. Using an EK-FAC influence function, the authors rank documents from a 5-million-token subset of pretraining data based on their impact on model outputs for two model sizes (7B and 35B) across 80 query-completion pairs (three simple mathematical reasoning tasks and several factual questions). They find that factual responses depend heavily on individual documents containing the answer, while reasoning responses draw on a broad, distributed set of documents containing procedural knowledge (e.g., code snippets, mathematical formulae). Reasoning influence scores are correlated across similar queries, suggesting shared strategies. Code documents are overrepresented among the most influential documents for reasoning. The larger model shows less variation in influence across documents, hinting at greater data efficiency. The findings indicate that LLMs approach reasoning by aggregating procedural knowledge rather than retrieving isolated facts.

### Strengths

- The paper tackles an intellectually significant and timely question: how LLMs generalize reasoning abilities from pretraining data. This exploration into the mechanics of LLM reasoning generalization is meaningful given the focus on interpretability and robustness (R1, R2, R4).
- The application of influence functions to trace pretraining data influence is novel and frontier research, as most interpretability work examines parameters/activations rather than tracing back to training samples (R3).
- The findings provide intuitive and insightful distinctions: reasoning relies on distributed procedural knowledge (e.g., code, verbal procedures) rather than direct answer retrieval, in contrast to factual tasks. This is cleanly conveyed through comparisons across task types and model sizes (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Experiments are well-executed; the analysis and explanation are reasonable (R2). The authors honestly qualify claims, caveat narrow scope early, present alternate hypotheses, and conduct sub-experiments, demonstrating thoughtful writing (R3).
- The method is straightforward, well-explained, and includes sufficient detail for reproducibility (R4).
- The refinement and adaptation of EK-FAC influence functions to assess impact on model accuracy is a key technical strength (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited experimental scale:** Only 80 queries are analyzed, further split into sub-groups. The three reasoning tasks are basic arithmetic with small numbers, short reasoning hops, and no fractional answers. This narrow scope severely limits generalizability to broader reasoning (e.g., logical, linguistic, spatial) or real-world tasks (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer (R4) emphasizes that this compromises the reliability of conclusions.
- **Incomplete data coverage:** Only a 5-million-document subset of the total pretraining data is used; less frequent but highly influential documents may be missed (R1, R2).
- **Limited model diversity:** Only two models (7B and 35B) from the same family are studied, with one not publicly available. No reproduction on fully public models like Llama, limiting generalizability across architectures and training regimes (R2, R3). Reviewer 4 questions why these specific models were chosen over more widely used and capable ones.
- **No cross-validation:** Findings are not corroborated with other interpretability methods, such as probing or mechanistic interpretability (R2).
- **Focus on positive influences only:** The paper predominantly examines positively influential documents; negative influences (which could reveal reasoning limitations, biases, or procedural noise) are not studied, potentially biasing conclusions (R1).
- **Methodological clarity and concerns:** The EK-FAC description is brief and lacks clear motivation for its individual components; the formulas are not well motivated (R3). The discussion section (sec 5) is dense and confusing, especially the alternating hypotheses starting L490 (R3). Reviewer 1 also notes the overall style resembles a blog post, with a lengthy conclusion/discussion that could be more succinct and converted into actionable strategies.
- **Potential gradient discrepancy:** Gradients are computed with the base model but query gradients with the fine-tuned model. This discrepancy could cause unreliable influence calculations and is a methodological concern raised by multiple reviewers (R1, R4).
- **Lack of granularity:** Analysis considers each document's influence on the entire completion, not on individual reasoning steps. This may affect conclusions about how models compose intermediate steps (R4).
- **Reliability of influence functions:** Appendix A.1 reports that influence scores for certain documents are only marginally different from random selections, raising questions about whether the influence function reliably indicates actual influence (R4).
- **Insufficient answer detection:** Keyword matching to check if documents contain answers may be inaccurate because LLMs rely on embedded representations; even if no explicit match is found, the document could still be semantically similar to the answer (R4).
- **No exploration of prompt variations:** Different prompt formulations for the same query are not tested, which could affect which documents are influential (R4).
- **Unclear definition of "procedural knowledge":** The concept could be elaborated, especially its relation to learning algorithms or routines in training data (R2).
- **Potential skew from code:** The high influence of code documents may over-specialize reasoning capabilities and could hinder performance in non-coding contexts (R2).
- **Missing examples of retrieved documents:** Providing examples would give insights into how documents influence the model's reasoning approach (R4). However, one reviewer (R3) had no serious questions and was more positive overall, acknowledging the caveats. In contrast, reviewer 4 is the most critical, questioning the fundamental reliability of the influence function conclusions given the small scale and unvalidated methodology. Reviewer 1, while still positive, notes the lack of prescriptive takeaways. The two strongest reviewers (R2, R3) are more supportive but still note limitations. This disagreement should be considered when weighing the evidence.
