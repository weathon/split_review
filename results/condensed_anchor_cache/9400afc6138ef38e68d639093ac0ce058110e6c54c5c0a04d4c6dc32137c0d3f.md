## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents NeRFuser, a language-conditioned multi-task policy framework that integrates neural rendering pre-training and diffusion training. It pre-trains a 3D encoder via NeRF to distill 2D semantic features from Stable Diffusion into a 3D representation, then uses Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) to learn multi-modal action sequence representations. Experiments on 10 RLBench tasks and 5 real robot tasks show over 30% improvement in success rate over GNFactor and PerAct.

### Strengths
- The proposed architecture is reasonable, intuitive, and well-designed, with clear reasoning for each component. (R1, R2, R4)
- The denoising objective is well-known to be effective for representation learning. (R2)
- Distilling features from Stable Diffusion into a 3D encoder is a clear motivation for improving out-of-distribution generalization. (R2)
- The two-phase framework (pre-training then policy learning) is well combined. (R2)
- Extensive analysis is provided: generalization experiments, ablation on components (e.g., swapping Stable Diffusion for CLIP/DINO), results in both simulation and real world. (R2, R1, R4)
- Performance gains over GNFactor and PerAct are statistically significant: over 16% higher success rate across 10 tasks (R3) and over 30% improvement overall (R1).
- Benchmarking is extensive with 5 real-world tasks, 10 in-distribution simulated tasks, and 5 unseen simulated tasks. (R3)
- The method figure clearly illustrates all components. (R3)
- The paper is clearly presented with a nice project page, good figures, and code release. (R4)
- Interesting insights: Stable Diffusion is a superior 2D teacher compared to CLIP or DINO; the 3D encoder can be pre-trained on out-of-distribution data and still yield good performance. (R4)
- Solid experimental design with thorough baseline comparisons and ablations. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and novelty concerns**:  
  - The motivation and contributions are not clearly articulated; the writing is confusing (e.g., the first paragraph of the introduction does not explain why the raised issues are important or if they are a consensus in the field). (R1)  
  - The two methods (neural rendering pre-training with Stable Diffusion and diffusion-based action reconstruction) appear cleverly combined but lack novelty. (R1)  
  - The key technical contributions are very similar to prior works: distilling 2D semantic features from foundation models into 3D representations was already proposed by GNFactor, and using diffusion for learning vision-language features for action sequences resembles Janner et al. and Huang et al. (R3)  
  - One reviewer points out that without an experiment isolating which component provides the improvement over GNFactor, it is unclear whether the gains are due to the NeRF pre-training or something else. The ablation study only swaps the 2D teacher (Stable Diffusion vs. others), not addressing the similarity to GNFactor. (R3)  
  - *Contrast:* While Reviewer 3 finds the contributions already proposed by prior works, Reviewer 4 considers the results a meaningful improvement to SOTA. (R3, R4)

- **Missing explanations and unclear details**:  
  - The integration of Stable Diffusion in Section 2.2 is insufficiently described: What is F(r)? Are per-pixel features extracted? What language condition is used? What timestep and which layer of the U-Net are used? How sensitive are these choices to hyperparameter changes? (R2, also R4 asks a similar question)  
  - Table 4 is ambiguous and needs better explanation. (R2)  
  - Table 3 contains unclear natural language commands (e.g., “drawer”, “meat”, “safe”). (R3)  
  - The paper does not mention how point clouds are obtained from the frames. (R3)  
  - The exact procedure for extracting Stable Diffusion features is missing: is it done by encoding the input image, adding noise (to which timestep?), then denoising in one step? (R4)

- **Inconsistencies in comparisons and baselines**:  
  - The PerAct performance in Table 1 significantly differs from the original paper’s reported numbers; is this due to metric differences or incomplete convergence of the compared PerAct? (R1)  
  - The GNFactor numbers reported in NeRFuser differ significantly (up to 20%) from the numbers in the GNFactor paper; why? (R3)

- **Inference time and speed**:  
  - The paper claims diffusion model inference time is a drawback, but does not explain how adding a policy network solves this, and no speed/inference time results are provided. (R1)

- **Limitations of the approach**:  
  - The method relies on point cloud voxelization, which requires the entire scene to be observed up front and limits applicability to large scenes (e.g., autonomous driving/navigation). The voxelization also introduces discretization that may limit precision for fine-grained manipulation. (R4)  
  - Generalization is shown only on weak forms of OOD tasks (“new position”, “larger object”, “distractors”); it is questionable whether the method would generalize to new environments or objects with significantly different appearance, even with Stable Diffusion features. The reliance on Stable Diffusion features creates a potential bottleneck dependent on its pre-training data and architecture. (R4)  
  - The neural rendering (NeRF) objective may be redundant: the voxel grid already stores RGB and occupancy, so the radiance field essentially learns an identity function plus view dependence; the RGB objective likely contributes little to learning. (R4)  
  - The gap between the final checkpoint and the best checkpoint suggests training instability, possibly due to conflicting objectives among the 3D encoder, policy model, and diffusion model. (R4)  
  - The method requires a specialized setup with multiple calibrated cameras during training. (R3)  
  - The method is only tested on constrained environments/agents; more diverse settings would be desirable. (R4)  
  - The name “NeRFuser” is misleading (sounds like a method for fusing NeRFs); the neural rendering component is not the most important part. (R4)  
  - The real robot experiment: it is unclear how the pre-training/training/test sets are separated; it would be interesting to train only on RLBench and test on real tasks, but that combination was apparently not tried. Also, the real robot results lack standard deviations because they were not repeated multiple times. (R4)  
  - Figure 5 is mentioned but no explanation is provided in the text; more visualization results in other settings (unseen, out-of-distribution, without distillation) would be useful. (R2)
