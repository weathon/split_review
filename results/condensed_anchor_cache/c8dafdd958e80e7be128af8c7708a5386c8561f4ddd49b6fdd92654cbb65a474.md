## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses the problem of selection bias in interventional causal discovery, where subjects are selectively enrolled into experiments. The authors show that existing interventional causal discovery methods fail under such bias because interventions and selection may occur at different times, leading to inconsistent conditional independence relations. They introduce an “interventional twin graph” that models both the observed world (where interventions are applied) and a counterfactual basal world (where selection occurs before interventions), characterize its Markov properties, and propose a provably sound algorithm (CDIS) for identifying causal relations and selection mechanisms up to an equivalence class from data with soft interventions and unknown targets. The paper is well-motivated with clear illustrative examples, provides a solid theoretical foundation, and validates the method on synthetic and real-world data. However, reviewers noted significant issues with exposition clarity, completeness of the algorithm and its evaluation, and the need for more detailed empirical analysis and identifiability discussion.

### Strengths
- The problem of selection bias in interventional experiments is under-explored yet crucial; the authors use clear examples to illustrate why it matters and why existing methods and simple DAG augmentations fail (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5).
- The paper provides a solid theoretical foundation: rigorous definition of the interventional twin graph, characterization of its Markov properties, and a proof of soundness for the proposed algorithm (Reviewers 1,2,3,5).
- The paper is well-motivated, with clear examples that help readers understand the concepts, though some examples could be further clarified (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- Synthetic experiments show that CDIS outperforms baselines under selection bias and remains robust as the number of variables increases; real-world applications in biology and education demonstrate practical utility (Reviewers 2,3,5).
- The paper tackles a philosophically interesting question about the meaning of selection bias and provides a sound answer with the necessary mathematical framework (Reviewer 5).
- One reviewer (Reviewer 5) considered the algorithm to be “sound and complete”, though this is contested by another reviewer (see Weaknesses) and is not claimed by the paper itself.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and exposition**:  
  * Section 3 is dense and lacks intuitive contextualization; examples are cryptic and fail to provide accessible understanding (Reviewers 1,5).  
  * The adaptation of d-separation to the twin graph lacks high-level intuition; the paper assumes strong familiarity with graphical models (Reviewer 2).  
  * Abbreviations like “CI”, “MAG”, and “PAG” are not defined before their first use; a table of notations would help (Reviewers 3,5).  
  * Example 2 would be more convincing with a numerical example (like Example 1) showing why selection implies $X_1$ and $X_3$ are dependent when conditioning on $X_2$ (Reviewer 1).  
  * Theorem 1 is not contrasted with the regular Markov property, leaving the extra condition unmotivated relative to the examples (Reviewer 1).  
  * The claim that Lemma 1 “misses key distributional information” is not made explicit (Reviewer 1).  
  * The claim that the twin graph has fewer degrees of freedom with the invariance constraint, leading to CIs not implied by d-separations, is unclear (Reviewer 1).  
  * Section 3.3 lacks intuitive motivation for the lemmas; the dense notation makes them difficult to follow (Reviewer 1).  
  * There appears to be a contradiction between statements in Section 3.2 regarding conditioning on $X_2$ and $X_2^*$ (Example 4 vs. earlier claim) (Reviewer 1).  

- **Completeness of algorithm and theory**:  
  * The characterization of Markov equivalence classes may not be as fine-grained as possible because the equivalence class is defined with respect to the left-hand side of the implications in Theorem 1, not the right-hand side (Reviewer 4).  
  * The algorithm is only proven sound, not complete. The simulation study does not evaluate completeness (e.g., fraction of true causal edges recovered). Without completeness, the algorithm’s usefulness is questionable; a trivial algorithm that says no causal relationships is also sound but not useful (Reviewer 4).  
  * Reviewer 5 states the algorithm is “sound and complete”, which contradicts the paper’s own claims and Reviewer 4’s assessment; this discrepancy highlights the need for a clear statement of what the algorithm guarantees (contrast).  

- **Empirical evaluation**:  
  * Simulation results should report the proportion of true causal edges estimated as directed, precision and recall of edge direction, and the effect of sample size, graph sparsity, and selection strength (Reviewer 3).  
  * The gene application lacks comprehensive analysis: specific regulatory relationships are not clearly identified, the results are not validated against existing biological knowledge, and the choice of genes and experimental setup is not justified (Reviewer 3).  
  * The education dataset analysis is vague; it is not explained why pre-intervention selection bias is a potential issue in that context, and the resulting graphs and variable names are hard to interpret (Reviewer 3).  
  * Algorithm step 2 (testing equality of conditional distributions) is not specified; what method is used? (Reviewer 1)  
  * Computational cost is not discussed; an analysis of the number of conditional independence tests and how it scales with the number of variables and interventions is needed (Reviewers 2,3).  
  * The paper does not evaluate CDIS in scenarios without selection bias to compare its performance with standard methods and understand if the added complexity degrades performance (Reviewer 2).  

- **Identifiability and limitations**:  
  * Identifiability guarantees for selection variables are not addressed; conditions under which the true interventional twin graph can be identified, and limitations when selection mechanisms are unidentifiable, should be discussed (Reviewer 2).  
  * It is unclear whether the approach generalizes to arbitrary latent variables or is specific to selection bias. If it does generalize, the framing is too narrow; if not, the paper should explain why (Reviewer 4).  

- **Minor issues**: Typos: line 48 “existing existing”, line 169 “models a completely different scenario” needs revision, line 295 “false” (Reviewers 2,3). Also, line 93 “CI” is likely undefined before use (Reviewer 3).
