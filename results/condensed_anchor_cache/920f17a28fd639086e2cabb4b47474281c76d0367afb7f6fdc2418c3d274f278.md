## Merged Review

### Summary

A bilingual, publicly available stereotactic EEG (sEEG) dataset (CerebroVoice) recorded from two participants reading Chinese Mandarin words, English words, and Chinese Mandarin digits. The paper benchmarks two tasks: brain-to-speech synthesis and voice activity detection (VAD). For synthesis, it proposes the Mixture of Bilingual Synergy Experts (MoBSE) framework, which uses language-aware dynamic weighting of low-rank expert weights. MoBSE is compared against a FastSpeech2 baseline. For VAD, three existing architectures (EEGNet, STANet, EEGChannelNet) are evaluated; the authors find low-frequency signals outperform high-gamma activity. All data and preprocessing code are made publicly available.

### Strengths

* The paper introduces a publicly accessible sEEG dataset for bilingual brain-to-speech synthesis and VAD, a highly under-explored area with scarce curated datasets, thus lowering the entry threshold for future research.
* By incorporating bilingual data (Chinese Mandarin – a tonal language – and English), the dataset opens avenues for studying tonal language complexities in speech decoding.
* The methodology for data acquisition is thoroughly and clearly explained, ensuring transparency.
* The proposed MoBSE framework (a mixture-of-experts approach with a language-aware gating module) outperforms the FastSpeech2 baseline, demonstrating improved bilingual decoding.
* The analysis of how different sEEG features (low-frequency signals LFS, high-gamma activity HGA, broadband BBS) affect speech synthesis and VAD provides insights that may guide future work.
* The paper addresses important ethical concerns regarding patient privacy and invasive neural recordings, demonstrating a commitment to responsible research.
* The text is well-structured, and the figures aid understanding of the method.

### Weaknesses

* **Very small participant pool:** The dataset comprises only two subjects, severely limiting generalizability and raising concerns about overfitting; reviewers note this is insufficient to support claims of the dataset as a benchmark. One reviewer (Rating 3) emphasized the lack of generalizability from two participants and unilateral electrode placement (Subject 1 right hemisphere, Subject 2 left hemisphere, while both hemispheres may contribute to speech).
* **Limited vocabulary and task novelty:** The stimulus set includes only 50 words/digits, repeated many times, leading to a narrow, repetitive vocabulary. The simple speech synthesis task lacks originality, as similar objectives have been addressed in prior work (e.g., [1–3]); many existing invasive datasets can be requested and non-invasive ones are publicly available.
* **Insufficient baselines and comparisons:** Only FastSpeech2 (a text-to-speech architecture) is used as a baseline, whereas existing brain-to-speech models for invasive and non-invasive EEG signals [1–3] were omitted, preventing thorough benchmarking of the dataset and MoBSE. Missing comparisons to non-invasive surface EEG or to other sEEG/ECoG datasets (e.g., Du-IN, Brain Treebank, Feng et al.).
* **Lack of architectural clarity:** It is unclear how FastSpeech2 was adapted to produce audio from sEEG signals; no details are given on how multi-channel sEEG embeddings are derived, the architecture of the Universal Expert module, the number of experts used, or the training procedures, hyperparameters, and loss functions. Figure 3 is unclear and lacks explanation for the sEEG embedding layer.
* **Incomplete evaluation:** Only Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) is reported for speech synthesis; missing standard metrics such as mean opinion score (MOS), mel cepstral distortion (MCD), root mean square error (RMSE), and ESTOI (Extended Short-Time Objective Intelligibility). No samples of reconstructed speech are provided for qualitative assessment. For VAD, the definition of accuracy (window length, silence vs. speech) is not clearly stated.
* **Reproducibility concerns:** The GitHub repository does not include model implementations, training scripts, or detailed documentation, hindering reproducibility.
* **No statistical significance testing:** The authors report improvements of MoBSE over FastSpeech2 but do not provide any statistical significance tests to confirm these improvements are meaningful.
* **Limited dataset analysis:** Missing evaluations such as word classification (e.g., 30-way Chinese word classification, given at least 24 trials per word), cross-dataset testing, or detailed distribution analysis to show the value added by the new dataset compared to existing ones. Combining electrode data from both subjects into a "super subject" was not considered.
* **Reference selection:** Relevant prior works are missing from the Related Works and Table 1, including Du-IN (arXiv:2405.11459), Feng et al. (biorxiv 2023, speech decoding in tonal language), and Brain Treebank (NeurIPS 2024).
* **Referencing methodology:** Common average referencing is used without justification; reviewers requested brain-to-speech results using laplacian or bipolar referencing (as in BrainBERT, Du-IN) to better understand the dataset’s behavior.
* **VAD analysis confusion:** Results across frequency bands (LFS, HGA, BBS) and subjects are inconsistent, and the relevance of these experiments as a benchmark is questionable given the small sample size. The meaning of LFS, HGA, and BBS features and their evaluation relevance are introduced late (Section 5).
* **Practicality concern:** The paper argues advantages of sEEG over ECoG but does not address why non-invasive surface EEG was not used, given sEEG’s invasive nature and collection complexity.
* **Data availability:** Only processed data is provided; raw sEEG data is not shared, preventing researchers from exploring custom preprocessing or other frequency bands.
* **Missing details:** Audio recording equipment specifications are not included, which is relevant for analysis and future data fusion. The process for assessing audio quality during curation (subjective? by whom?) is not described.
