## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper argues that simple edge perturbations (random edge dropping for node-level, random edge adding for graph-level) are as effective or more effective than spectral augmentation methods for contrast-based graph self-supervised learning (CG-SSL). Based on empirical comparisons, the authors question the necessity of spectral augmentations and claim that these simple strategies yield superior performance with significantly lower computational costs. Reviewers acknowledge the comprehensive empirical scope but raise concerns about novelty, theoretical depth, missing baselines, and the strength of the conclusions.

### Strengths
- Comprehensive empirical comparison across multiple datasets, GCL methods, GNN architectures, and node/graph classification benchmarks (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5). The paper systematically shows that random edge perturbations consistently perform as well or outperform more complex spectral augmentation methods such as SPAN (Reviewers 1,4,5).
- Brings focus to shortcomings in the theoretical understanding of spectral augmentation-based graph SSL (Reviewer 1).
- Well-motivated and clearly written; the paper provides a detailed explanation of the discussion about the necessity of spectral augmentations (Reviewers 2,3).
- Demonstrates broad applicability and versatility across different CG-SSL frameworks (Reviewer 5).
- For the large scope of the empirical study, the paper is well-organized (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and theoretical depth**: The core method (simple edge perturbation) is not novel; DropEdge and related approaches have been well studied (Reviewers 1,5). The paper does not provide a new methodological or theoretical insight; the motivation relies heavily on empirical studies rather than a well-discussed theoretical perspective (Reviewers 1,4,5). Specifically, Reviewer 1 states there is "no sufficiently novel methodological or theoretical insight that could be leveraged" and that the paper does not provide conclusive theoretical evidence to warrant a categorical dismissal of spectral augmentation methods, noting that spectral augmentations are effective in some experiments (Table 3). Reviewer 5 notes the relationship between spectral augmentations and edge perturbation is not analyzed theoretically, and the main motivation is empirical.
- **Missing baseline comparisons and insufficient coverage**: The paper claims only three relevant spectral augmentation studies exist (SPAN, SpCo, GASSER) but ignores other relevant works [1-4] (Yang et al. 2023, HaoChen et al. 2021, Shou et al. 2024, Zhang et al. 2023); why were these not included as baselines? (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 5 asks why the paper did not compare to CCA-SSA and M-ILBO, and how the work differs from GRACE and other edge augmentation works by Zhu et al. Reviewer 3 notes that the backbone models used are not state-of-the-art and suggests evaluating on cutting-edge baselines.
- **Questions about experimental design and interpretation**:
  * The superior performance of shallow GNNs is interpreted as evidence that spectral information can hurt representation quality, but this claim requires additional justification due to known issues in training deeper GNNs (over-smoothing, difficulty in preserving locality, noise propagation; see "Bag of Tricks for Training Deeper GNNs", TPAMI 2022) (Reviewer 4).
  * Why is DROPEDGE significantly superior to ADDEDGE in node classification while they are comparable (or ADDEDGE sometimes superior) in graph classification? (Reviewer 4)
  * Figure 2 shows DROPEDGE leads to degenerate graph spectra; why does GCL with such samples lead to superior representations? How does this relate to existing theoretical frameworks (e.g., Provable guarantees for self-supervised deep learning with spectral contrastive loss, NeurIPS 2021)? (Reviewer 4)
  * Does ADDEDGE also lead to similar degenerate spectra? If so, what explains the performance difference? (Reviewer 4)
  * How does one ensure that generic augmentations like DROPEDGE are safe in practice (e.g., removing or adding an edge changing molecule properties)? Can spectral augmentations provide complementary benefits, e.g., by using both edge perturbations and spectral augmentations simultaneously? (Reviewer 4)
  * Reviewer 3 asks if similar conclusions hold on OGB datasets (node, edge, graph-level tasks) and suggests ablation studies on more datasets.
  * Reviewer 5 questions whether the main paper relies too heavily on empirical studies and asks for elaboration on theoretical analysis.
- **Missing details and code**: Table 1 lacks definitions of n and m (Reviewer 2). Code is not available (Reviewer 3).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 2 (rating 6) is more positive, commending the motivation and potential impact for future graph SSL implementation, but flags baseline concerns. In contrast, Reviewers 1 and 5 (ratings 3) find insufficient novelty and theoretical justification, noting that spectral augmentations are effective in some settings (e.g., Table 3). The paper’s strong claim against spectral augmentations is not uniformly supported by the evidence presented.
