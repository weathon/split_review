## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes to reduce the number of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) needed for LLM interpretability by grouping contiguous layers into clusters based on angular distance of residual stream activations and training one SAE per cluster. Applied to Pythia 160M (12 layers), the method is evaluated on reconstruction metrics (L0, L2, R², CE loss), downstream tasks (Indirect Object Identification, Greater-Than, Subject-Verb Agreement), circuit faithfulness/completeness, and a human interpretability study (96 features). Reviewers are split: two give 6 (positive) and three give 3 (negative). While the idea is simple and evaluations are comprehensive, major concerns center on the validity of the claimed efficiency gain, limited novelty, missing experimental details, small model scale, and problematic evaluation metrics.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and timely problem: improving the efficiency of SAE training for LLM interpretability. (R1, R2, R4)
- The proposed approach (grouping similar consecutive layers to train fewer SAEs) is straightforward and easy to understand. (R1, R3, R5)
- The evaluation is comprehensive, covering standard SAE metrics (L0, L2, R², CE loss), circuit-based analyses (faithfulness and completeness via attribution patching), human interpretability, and downstream task performance. (R2, R3, R4, R5) The circuit analysis evaluation is particularly commended for its rigor. (R3, R5)
- Results on circuit analysis evals show minimal degradation for moderate numbers of groups (e.g., K=4,5). (R3)
- The use of agglomerative clustering on angular distance to find optimal layer groups is an interesting design choice. (R4)
- The paper acknowledges some key limitations in a dedicated limitations section. (R5)

### Weaknesses
1. **Disputed efficiency claim.** The claimed speedup is based on training fewer SAEs, but if each grouped SAE is trained on the same number of tokens (1B) as a per-layer SAE, then the grouped SAE processes activations from all layers in the group (e.g., 3× activations for a 3-layer group), resulting in the same total FLOPs as training one SAE per layer. (R3, R5) This makes the core thesis of “efficient training” questionable. (R3) Minor reviewers (R1) note that any speedup comes with a linear quality drop, further weakening the claim.
2. **Limited novelty and contribution.** The idea of training one SAE for a group of adjacent layers is obvious and expected; it does not constitute a significant technical contribution. (R1, R2, R4)
3. **Only evaluated on a small model (Pythia 160M, 12 layers) with simple tasks.** Generalization to larger LLMs and more realistic interpretability scenarios is unestablished. (R1, R2, R4, R5)
4. **Missing crucial experimental details, making results hard to interpret:**  
   - It is not clearly stated whether the grouped SAE is trained on the same *number of tokens* (and thus sees more activations from multiple layers) or the same *number of activations* (e.g., by sampling one layer per token). This directly affects whether the comparison to per-layer baselines is fair. (R3, R5)  
   - For the human interpretability study, “96 features” is ambiguous: per SAE, per SAE family, or total? (R5)  
   - Circuit evaluation methodology is underspecified: are mean ablations done one layer at a time (with separate forward passes) or all at once? Are error terms (original activation minus reconstruction) included? (R5) This matters because including error terms inflates faithfulness for low-quality SAEs. (R5)  
   - The paper uses JumpReLU activations but an L1 loss; proper JumpReLU training requires an L0 loss and straight-through estimators, implying the threshold never updates and the SAE is effectively a ReLU. (R5)  
   - How the grouped SAE handles activations from different layers (concatenation, proportion, prediction task) is not explained. (R4)
5. **Faithfulness metric is problematic.** Including the error term in reconstruction when computing faithfulness means that a poor SAE that outputs zeros (large error) will appear highly faithful, making the metric unsuitable for comparing SAE quality. (R5)
6. **Presentation issues:**  
   - Figure 3 averages metrics (L2, R², etc.) across all layers despite large per-layer variation, which can be misleading; per-layer breakdown should be highlighted. (R5) Recommended to show raw change in cross-entropy instead of normalized CE scores. (R5)  
   - Bar plots lack numerical values and error bars. (R4)  
   - Several typos and rushed writing. (R4)  
   - JumpReLU definition is incorrect (uses ReLU instead of Heaviside). (R5)  
   - Many design choices lack justification: relationship between \(d\) and \(d\_model\), use of JumpReLU, definition of “residual stream”, whether clustering allows non-consecutive layers. (R2)  
   - Figure 2 caption should clarify that the diagonal (adjacent layers) indicates closeness and 0.5 means perpendicular. (R5)
7. **Non-monotonic human interpretability scores with k** (Figure 7) are not explained. (R2)
8. **Differences from prior work unclear** – specifically, how this relates to multi-layer SAEs (e.g., Residual Stream Analysis with Multi-Layer SAEs, arXiv:2409.04185). (R4)
9. **Only examines residual stream**, not MLP or attention components; residual stream is the easiest setting for this idea. (R4)
10. **Aggregation of faithfulness/completeness over layers** by simple averaging may conflate layer-specific properties; harder tasks at later layers could bias results. (R5)
11. **Overstatement of speedup:** The claimed “up to 6× speedup” corresponds to K=1, which performs poorly; practical useful k (e.g., K=3) yields at most 3× if compute-matched. (R5)
