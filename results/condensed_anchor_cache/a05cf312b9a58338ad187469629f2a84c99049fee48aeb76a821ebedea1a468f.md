## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) in the linear-width (P/N constant) and sublinear-width (P/(N N_0) constant) regimes. The authors operate under a spectral universality assumption, which treats kernel eigenfunctions as random with a covariance determined by the posterior kernel, and argue this is logically equivalent to a kernel renormalization theory (a scale shift solved self-consistently) originally derived for deep linear networks. In the sublinear regime, kernels are rank-deficient and the pseudo-inverse is used. Theoretical contributions include novel integral formulas for BNN predictors in both regimes, and an extension of renormalization theory to nonlinear BNNs with ReLU activations. Experiments on simple datasets with MLPs support the claims.

### Strengths
- Very strong theoretical contributions (Reviewer 2: “extremely strong”), especially the novel integral formulas describing BNN outputs in linear and sublinear width regimes.
- Original idea to apply a spectral universality assumption to characterize feature learning in nonlinear BNNs (Reviewer 1); showing equivalence between spectral universality and kernel renormalization is an interesting contribution.
- Combining Marchenko–Pastur from random matrix theory with Mercer’s eigenvalues to evaluate infinite-width limits with diverging data and input dimension is a good, underutilized approach (Reviewer 3).
- The paper bridges kernel-theoretic and statistical mechanics approaches, and the ideas from random matrix theory are valuable for the ML community.

### Weaknesses
- **Spectral universality assumption is not directly proven**; the distribution of kernel eigenfunctions in either the proportional or sublinear regime has not been characterized outside of this assumption (Reviewer 1, confidence 3). Reviewer 1 questions whether the assumption breaks down for the N ≫ P regime with μP scaling (where kernels are non-random but preactivations become non-Gaussian), and for cases where matrices pick up low-rank spikes (e.g., learning single/multi-index models) – both missing experiments/ablations.
- **Experimental results are limited and poorly presented** (all reviewers):
  - Restricted to MLPs on very simple datasets; no experiments on more complex tasks (Reviewers 1, 2). Reviewer 2 acknowledges a possible rebuttal about generality or difficulty, but the lack is still a weakness.
  - Plots lack labeled x- and y-axes (Reviewer 2, 3). Legends are confusing, e.g., “on the left (respectively, on the right)” (Reviewer 2).
  - Section 4 is incomplete; it is not clear which figures correspond to the two examples mentioned at the start (Reviewer 3).
  - For Figures 1 and 2, the number of simulations used to obtain error bars is not stated, and uncertainty around the blue (theory) lines is not reported (Reviewer 3).
- **Notation and definitions are incomplete or undefined** (Reviewer 3):
  - Φ* in Theorem 3.4 is never defined.
  - In Theorem 3.3, $\mathbf{x}\sim \lim_{N_0\to\infty} \mathbb{P}_{N_0}$ is used without defining $\mathbb{P}_{N_0}$.
  - In the first experiment (“linear teacher”), β is not specified (Reviewer 3).
- **Clarity on computational feasibility is contradictory** (Reviewer 3): Between lines 300–303 the authors claim computations are not intensive for small datasets, yet footnote 3 (p. 6) says the limit can be approximated using large objects – “large” vs. “small” is unclear.
- **It is not immediately clear how the integrals in Theorem 3.4 can be explicitly evaluated** (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing related work**: Deep kernel processes (arXiv:2010.01590, arXiv:2108.13097) address similar conceptual issues and should be discussed (Reviewer 2).
- **Minority reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 3) is notably more critical, particularly about the unproven assumption and limited experiments, while Reviewer 2 (score 8, confidence 2) is very positive about the theory but also critical of the experiments. Reviewer 3 (score 6, confidence 4) is moderately positive but flags multiple missing details.
