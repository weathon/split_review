## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ARGMINN, a mechanistically interpretable neural network for regulatory genomics designed to directly encode motifs and their syntax in weights and activations. Reviewers generally acknowledge the importance of interpretable models for this domain and note the architecture's novelty, theoretical grounding, and thorough assessment across multiple tasks (motif discovery, instance calling, robustness, generative sequences). However, significant concerns exist regarding insufficient novelty compared to existing deep learning approaches (e.g., DNABERT, HyenaDNA, EVO), inadequate experimental validation (missing specific numeric results, ablation studies, comprehensive baselines), restrictive assumptions that ignore epigenetics and variant data, lack of clarity in contributions and experimental settings, questionable evaluation splits, and potential overstatement of claims. Two reviewers are strongly negative (scores 3), while three are moderately positive (scores 5–6), reflecting a substantial split in assessment of novelty, rigor, and practical utility.

### Strengths
- The proposed ARGMINN architecture brings mechanistically interpretable design to regulatory genomics, directly encoding motifs in weights and activations to address the interpretability challenge in deep learning for genomics. [R1, R3, R5]
- Authors provide theoretical proofs showing the model can recognize all motif configurations, based on first-order logic. [R1, R3]
- ARGMINN shows superior performance across all tasks compared to baselines (e.g., CNNs, ExplaiNN) and showcases the ability to generate novel functional sequences from learned motifs. [R1]
- The paper gives many case-by-case analyses for each evaluated task, making the results straightforward. [R2]
- The methodological part is well written and easy to follow. [R5]
- The architecture is novel and thought-through, successfully applying mechanistic interpretability methods to the regulatory genomics domain. [R3, R5]
- The authors provide a detailed related work section and thorough assessment for the usage of ARGMINN. [R3]
- The research area (regulatory genomics) is timely and addresses a critical need for interpretable models; potential applications in understanding gene regulation are valuable. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation split introduces bias**: Using chr8 and chr10 for validation and chr1 for testing is concerning because chromosomes vary in genomic features (e.g., enhancer/promoter distribution); chr1 is one of the largest chromosomes and may contain unique regulatory elements not well represented in training data, potentially underestimating generalization. Leave-one-out strategy across chromosomes was suggested. [R1]
- **Missing computational cost analysis**: The introduction of a memory stream into the attention mechanism may significantly increase training time and memory usage, but the paper provides no discussion or analysis of computational resources required. This is critical for practical applicability on large genomic datasets. [R1]
- **Paper is hard to follow; contributions unclear**: The detailed contributions of the proposed architecture compared to previous methods are not clearly articulated; it is difficult to distinguish what is new beyond combining regularizations, separate convolutions, and attention. [R2, R3]
- **Experimental settings and results incomplete**: Specific numeric performance values are lacking; all results are presented only in figures. The paper does not provide specific values for hyperparameters (e.g., n_f, n_L), nor clarify if standard CNNs have the same number of layers as ARGMINN. The species genome sequences used for each task are not stated. An ablation study is missing. [R2]
- **Lack of novelty compared to existing models**: The approach does not convincingly advance the state of the art over recent large-scale models (DNABERT, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA, EVO) or existing interpretable architectures. The techniques used (regularizations, separate convolutions, attention) are common mechanistic interpretation tricks, not grounded in biological mechanisms. [R4, R3]
- **Inadequate experimental validation and baseline comparisons**: Comprehensive comparisons with established baseline methods (both deep learning and non-deep learning) are missing, making it difficult to assess practical utility. The quality of prediction is not properly evaluated against existing TF-binding prediction tools (e.g., those from ENCODE-DREAM, methods using LRP or SmoothGrad for motif extraction). It is unclear whether interpretability is traded off for prediction quality. [R4, R5]
- **Overstated conclusions**: The paper claims significant interpretability and performance improvements without sufficient evidence from data or robust benchmarks. [R4]
- **Restrictive assumptions**: The model ignores essential biological factors that are known to be decisive for transcription factor binding—namely epigenetics (e.g., chromatin openness) and individual genome variants that alter motifs. These are critical for accurate, genome-specific prediction, as shown by ENCODE-DREAM benchmarking and variant-aware tools. [R5]
- **Why learn motifs when databases exist?**: Extensive motif databases (TRANSFAC, JASPAR) already exist from rigorous experiments. The paper would benefit from a clear use-case where inferred motifs are necessary (e.g., TFs without known motifs, or species without databases). It is not justified why learned (possibly inaccurate) motifs are better than detecting known motif occurrences and building a classifier. [R5]
- **Relevance to ICLR audience**: The work may be more suited to a biological or statistical genomics journal rather than a machine learning conference like ICLR. [R3]
