## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces COREP, a method that tackles non-stationarity in reinforcement learning by learning a stable “causal-origin” graph representation of the state. It uses a guided updating mechanism with dual graph attention networks (GATs) to model core and general graphs, aiming to identify the causal origin of changes without explicit prior knowledge of the environment. The approach is evaluated on several non-stationary benchmarks and compared against FNVAE, VariBAD, and PPO. Reviewers are sharply divided: Reviewer 2 (score 8) is positive, praising the motivation, clarity, and experimental breadth, while the other four reviewers (all score 3) raise serious concerns about novelty, theoretical grounding, missing baselines, and practicality.

### Strengths

*   **Problem importance** – Addressing non-stationarity in RL is a relevant and challenging problem (R1, R2).
*   **Causal modeling perspective** – The use of causal ideas to model environmental changes is technically sound and offers a fresh viewpoint (R2). Reviewer 3 also finds the causal interpretation (attributing non-stationarity to latent discrete variable *e* and using mixture models) reasonable.
*   **Algorithm design** – The core algorithm is simple and easy to follow (R2).
*   **Writing** – Overall writing is clear and the related‑work section is comprehensive and excellent (R2, R5).
*   **Experiments** – The experimental design is comprehensive, covering a variety of non‑stationary environments and diverse factors of change (R2). Reported performance surpasses baseline methods (R3).
*   **Integration of fields** – The paper brings together causality, deep RL, and causal structure discovery (R4).
*   **Minority positive opinion** – Reviewer 2 (score 8) found the motivation, algorithm, and experiments convincing enough to recommend acceptance, in contrast to the other four negative reviews.

### Weaknesses

1.  **Unclear motivation for technical choices**
    *   The paper integrates GATs, causal discovery, and non‑stationary RL, but the justification for each choice is weak. Using GAT (attention mechanism) for causal graphs – which are directed acyclic graphs – is not adequately explained; other GNNs may be more suitable for DAGs (R1). The choice of only bidirected edges to indicate changes in the MAG is questioned – edges with circles could also be influenced by the latent variable *e* (R3).
    *   The causal‑origin representation itself lacks a clear explanation of why it is superior to other methods for handling non‑stationarity (R1). How the latent state dimensions *h* are identified (crucial for learning the MAG over {s, h}) is not explained (R3).
    *   The method learns a graph topology to model non‑stationarity, but non‑stationarity can also arise from changing mechanisms within a static topology; the paper does not address how the approach handles mechanism drift (R5).

2.  **Insufficient theoretical analysis**
    *   No performance guarantees (regret bound, convergence rate) are provided, making it difficult to assess theoretical robustness compared to existing approaches that offer such analysis (R1).
    *   There is no guarantee that amalgamating the two graphs successfully extracts the true causal origin of changes. Theoretical insights or references to support this design are missing (R2). Empirical validation on simulated MDPs with ground‑truth causal origins is recommended (R2).
    *   The assumption that the environment is a “non‑stationary mixture of various stationary environments” is strong and essentially identical to the setting already solved by multiple‑model adaptive control (MMAC) – a known approach that does not require causality (R4).

3.  **Limited novelty and contribution**
    *   The combination of GATs, causal discovery, and non‑stationary RL is not sufficiently novel; these techniques are well studied individually, and their integration does not represent a substantial methodological advance (R1).
    *   The paper is unaware that simpler and well‑established solutions (e.g., multiple‑model adaptive control) already address the same setting, making the proposed causal approach appear unnecessarily complex and its contribution questionable (R4).

4.  **Missing comparisons and related work**
    *   Baselines are limited to RL methods (FNVAE, VariBAD, PPO). No comparison is made against mixture‑based MMAC solutions, which are directly relevant to the problem formulation (R4). The related work section completely omits the control‑theory literature on MMAC (R4 provides references: Murray‑Smith & Johansen, 2020; Zhang & Li, 2020; Provan et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2022).
    *   Without this comparison, it is impossible to judge whether COREP offers any practical advantage over existing, simpler methods (R4).

5.  **Experimental shortcomings**
    *   The non‑stationarity emulated in the experiments is naive and too simplistic to reflect real‑world complexity (R1). The algorithm’s efficacy under more typical conditions remains unverified (R1).
    *   The paper claims that existing methods require impractical prior knowledge, but no experiment directly tests whether COREP is more practical than using strong prior knowledge (R5). The approach is instead evaluated only against other RL methods (R5).
    *   The number of distinct stationary environments *K* is not bounded; the impact of *K* → ∞ is not discussed (R5).

6.  **Computational and training efficiency**
    *   No analysis of model complexity, computation time, or sample efficiency is provided, making it hard to assess practical applicability (R1). The algorithm mixes several components (GAT, causal discovery, RL), raising concerns about training difficulty and efficiency (R1).
    *   The total loss contains multiple tuning parameters (>3). Sensitivity analysis is performed only on a single task (Cartpole Swingup), which is insufficient to demonstrate robustness across tasks and parameter ranges (R1).
    *   Training procedure details are incomplete: learning rates, initial values, parameter‑tuning values for each task are not reported (R1).

7.  **Presentation clarity**
    *   Notation is non‑standard and inconsistent with conventions in RL, causal inference, and GNN literature, burdening readers (R1). Equation 3.1 is hard to understand; a standard state‑space or probabilistic representation would be clearer (R4).
    *   The logical flow is unclear and the central claim is vague (R1). Some parts of the introduction are too dense and could be deferred (R5).
    *   There are typos and errors, e.g., repeated author names in citations (R1).
    *   The phrasing “tackle non‑stationarity” is a misnomer; the goal should be to model it, not treat it as an adversary (R5).

8.  **Specific reviewer questions that reflect criticisms**
    *   How are the latent state dimensions *h* identified? (R3)
    *   Why only bidirected edges to indicate changes? Should edges with circles also be considered? (R3)
    *   What happens when *K* (the number of stationary environments) goes to infinity? (R5)
    *   How does the method account for non‑stationarity arising from changing mechanisms (drift) rather than changing topology? (R5)
    *   What justification can be provided for such a complex approach when mixture‑based MMAC already solves the problem? (R4)
