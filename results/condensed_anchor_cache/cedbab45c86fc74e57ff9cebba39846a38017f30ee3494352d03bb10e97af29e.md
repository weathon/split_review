## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FLATTEN, a training-free method for text-to-video editing that uses optical flow to guide spatio-temporal attention. It enforces that patches on the same flow trajectory across frames attend to each other, aiming to improve visual consistency. The method is evaluated on two standard benchmarks against five baselines and shows favorable performance in terms of visual quality, alignment, and consistency metrics, supported by a user study. The reviewers are split: two rate it 8 (positive, confident; one with confidence 5, one with 4) and two rate it 5 (more critical, one with confidence 4, one with low confidence 2). While the idea is considered intuitive and the approach simple/plug-and-play, several weaknesses regarding robustness, scope, evaluation thoroughness, computational cost, and comparison fairness are raised.

### Strengths
- **Sound and novel idea**: The flow-guided attention mechanism is considered original, intuitive, and makes sense for improving temporal consistency. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Practical benefits**: The method is simple, training-free, and can be plugged into existing text-to-video editing architectures without retraining. (R1, R4)
- **Effectiveness demonstrated**: The method achieves state-of-the-art results on benchmarks, with extensive experiments and ablation studies. Qualitative results (e.g., the “racing trucks” example in the supplementary video) show clear improvements over baselines in maintaining visual consistency when motion is present. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Generality**: Flow-guided attention can be applied to other base models to further improve temporal consistency. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Heavy reliance on optical flow as a black box**: The method assumes high-quality optical flow from a pre-trained network. The paper does not analyze robustness to flow errors, such as large/abrupt motion, simultaneous global (camera) and local motion, or cases where flow is inaccurate. Specific questions (R1, R3, R4) include:  
  - How well does the method handle large displacement or abrupt motion? (R1)  
  - How does it handle videos with both global and local motion? (R1)  
  - Can the method do more harm than good when flow is inaccurate? Are there failure examples where the result is worse than the baseline? (R1, R3)  
  - What is the impact of different flow model accuracy, or of gradually corrupting the flow field? (R1)  
  - For occlusion, objects appearing/disappearing, the flow trajectory may be invalid or misleading; why should the method be better than previous temporal attention that does not rely on flow? (R3)
- **Mismatch when editing changes object shape/appearance**: The method only works when every pixel in the original video aligns spatially with the generated video. For misaligned regions (e.g., editing a cat to a tiger with a larger face), the original video’s flow path is no longer correct for the generated content, leading to incorrect key/query assignments. (R2)  
  - Reviewer 2 further questions: if optical flow tracking is accurate, why not simply select a keyframe and copy pixels along the flow path (e.g., via bilinear interpolation) rather than rely on attention? This would seem more accurate for consistent videos. (R2)
- **Limited qualitative comparisons**: Only one visual comparison is provided in the supplementary MP4 (R2, R4). The main paper shows few multiple-frame displays (Figure 5 is insufficient) to judge temporal consistency. For example, the “wolf” example was compared with the TokenFlow website version, and the results of FLATTEN are not as good (R4). The “truck” example appears to edit better than TokenFlow, which contradicts expectations given TokenFlow uses PnP-Diffusion; a direct comparison under identical settings with examples from the TokenFlow website is needed. (R4)
- **Editing capability limited by using only cross-attention**: The method uses only cross-attention with the editing prompt for text guidance; it does not adopt techniques like prompt-to-prompt to precisely constrain edits. This can cause inaccurate background editing (e.g., grass turning yellow in the “tiger” example), while TokenFlow and FateZero better preserve the background. (R4)
- **High computational cost**: Dense spatio-temporal attention (DSTA) is applied across all 32 frames, leading to substantial VRAM usage and inference time. The paper does not compare VRAM requirements or runtime against methods that use keyframes (TokenFlow) or limited reference frames (Pix2Video, ControlVideo). This is a practical concern for users without high-end GPUs (e.g., 80GB A100s). (R4)
- **Missing related work**: Several existing works that use optical flow trajectories for video generation are not discussed in the related work section. (R4)
- **Lack of detailed trajectory validation**: The paper does not provide qualitative or quantitative results showing that the tracked patches truly follow the flow of objects in the video, especially for the challenging cases mentioned (occlusion, appearance/disappearance). (R3)
- **Dataset and sampling details vague**: It is unclear which specific videos from the benchmarks were used for evaluation. (R3)
