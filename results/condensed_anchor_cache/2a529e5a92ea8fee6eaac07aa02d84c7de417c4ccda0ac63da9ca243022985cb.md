## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Readout-Decomposition of Activation Change (RDAC) framework to analyze the stability-plasticity trade-off in continual learning (CL). It decomposes learning-induced activation changes into components in the range and null space of the readout weights, hypothesizing that range changes affect stability and null space changes affect plasticity. The framework is applied to analyze existing CL algorithms (SI, EWC, LwF, GEM, Data Replay) on Split-CIFAR-110. For one-hidden-layer linear neural networks, a gradient decomposition algorithm is derived to explicitly restrict updates to the null space to maintain stability without sacrificing plasticity. Reviewers note the framework provides a novel perspective and the gradient decomposition is a promising application, but raise concerns about validation, generalizability to nonlinear networks, and limited experimental scope.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow, with good visualization of key ideas (R1, R2, R4).
- The RDAC framework offers a novel and neat perspective for analyzing the stability-plasticity trade-off, providing a new tool to disentangle stability and plasticity in CL (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The gradient decomposition algorithm (for one-hidden-layer linear networks) is a concrete application that demonstrates how to maintain stability without significant plasticity loss; this is seen as a potentially significant contribution (R2, R4). R3, however, notes the paper focuses mainly on evaluating existing algorithms rather than proposing new techniques, highlighting a disagreement: R2 and R4 consider the gradient decomposition a strength, while R3 sees the paper as lacking new algorithms/techniques. (Minority contrast: R3 is more negative, R4 more positive.)
- The experimental analysis shows that GEM and data replay preserve both stability and plasticity, while SI, EWC, and LwF trade plasticity for stability (R3, R4).
- The representation decomposition (Fig 1) is clearly explained and useful (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Validation of core hypothesis:** The framework interprets range/null space projections as directly measuring stability/plasticity, but this is not verified. No experiments correlate changes in these subspaces with actual performance on CL tasks. The analysis only examines gradient projections, not how tuning regularization or memory size affects the trade-off and subsequent performance (R1, R2, R3). R1 asks: "Do the changes in the range / null space truly correlate with plasticity / stability in training the networks?"; R2 asks: "How can you formally quantify or guarantee the capacity for plasticity?"
- **Limited applicability to nonlinear networks:** The RDAC framework's insights are derived from linear approximations (SVD of readout weights), but it is unclear how they extrapolate to deep nonlinear networks. The analysis in nonlinear experiments is limited: only the final layer is analyzed, not intermediate layers; null spaces are approximated; no intuition is given on how inaccurate the linear approximation might be. This limits the practical relevance of the findings (R1, R2). R1 asks: "How do we interpret insights from a linear approximation of gradient projections from a nonlinear network?" and "How bad are these linear approximations, and how can we correct them?" R2 asks: "Do you have ideas for extending gradient decomposition algorithms to deep nonlinear networks?"
- **Insufficient empirical evaluation:** The continual learning scenarios used (Split CIFAR-110, MNIST) are relatively simple and do not fully capture the challenges of complex, real-world CL benchmarks. There are no comparisons against the latest state-of-the-art methods, and the paper lacks extensive empirical results and practical implications (R2, R3). R3 asks: "Have you considered evaluating the RDAC framework on other CL datasets or tasks?" and "Are there any plans to conduct such experiments in the future?" R3 also notes the lack of code and hyper-parameter configuration.
- **Theoretical analysis limited to simple network:** The gradient decomposition algorithm is derived only for one-hidden-layer linear neural networks, and the theoretical analysis is not scalable or sufficiently instructive for more complex architectures (R2, R3). R2 asks: "Any performance guarantees of your proposed optimization algorithm?" R3 asks: "How generalizable is the gradient decomposition algorithm for linear neural networks? Can it be extended to more complex network architectures, such as deep neural networks, and still maintain stability without sacrificing plasticity?"
- **Clarity of gradient decomposition method:** The proposed gradient decomposition algorithm is not presented clearly enough; a clear algorithmic description is needed to show how to explicitly control the stability-plasticity trade-off (R4).
- **Connection to related theories not explored:** The paper does not discuss connections to PCA, information theory, rate-distortion theory, or information bottleneck theory, which could provide additional justification or insight (R2). R2 also requests more details on biological connections to representational drift.
- **Minority contrast on novelty:** R3 states the paper does not propose new algorithms or techniques (focusing only on evaluating existing methods), while R2 and R4 view the gradient decomposition itself as a novel algorithm. (This disagreement is noted above in Strengths and also reflected here.) This contrast highlights differing assessments of the paper's contribution.
