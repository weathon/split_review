## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a benchmark to evaluate how well LLMs can simulate human personalities and behaviors for potential use in psychological and sociological experiments. It includes: (1) the “Human Simulacra” dataset of 11 virtual characters with detailed life stories constructed via human feedback and psychology expert review; (2) a Multi-Agent Cognitive Mechanism (MACM) that models human memory, emotion, and reasoning; and (3) a psychology-guided evaluation framework using self-reports (questionnaires answered by simulated characters) and observer reports (human judges rating character responses to 55 situational-judgment-test scenarios). Experiments test 14 LLMs across four simulation methods (including MACM, RAG, and baseline from Character.AI) and replicate a classic social conformity (bandwagon effect) experiment. The authors release the code and dataset. Reviewers agree the topic is important and the benchmark is more rigorous than prior work, but they raise major concerns about the scientific validity of Jung’s personality theory, the limited scope of scenarios and characters, unclear presentation, and insufficient justification for experimental designs and baselines. One reviewer (score 8) found no outstanding weaknesses, while others (scores 3, 6, 5, 6) identified significant clarity and validity issues.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and timely research direction: assessing LLMs’ capacity for human simulation, with implications for replacing human subjects in social science experiments. (Reviewers 1, 2, 5)
- The Human Simulacra dataset is carefully constructed with multiple rounds of expert review and human feedback, ensuring quality, accuracy, and ethical considerations. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- The benchmark is built on more robust psychological theory (Jung’s eight-dimension personality model) than prior role-playing benchmarks (e.g., MBTI), providing a fresh perspective on capturing complex human traits. (Reviewers 1, 5; but note disagreement on Jung’s validity – see Weaknesses)
- The Multi-Agent Cognitive Mechanism (MACM) is novel, coordinating memory, emotion, and logical processing to produce more human-like responses. It is validated in psychological experiments. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- Extensive experiments are conducted across a wide variety of LLMs (14 models) and four simulation methods, including self-reports and observer reports. (Reviewers 2, 4, 5)
- The paper includes a replication of a classic psychology experiment (bandwagon effect), comparing human and LLM-driven behavior. (Reviewers 3)
- The evaluation tasks (self-report, observer-report, conformity experiment) are hard and meaningful, and the experiments in Section 5.2 directly compare human behaviors with LLM simulation results. (Reviewers 3)
- The set of experiments and results are comprehensive, and the dataset and codes will be released. (Reviewer 2)
- Clear and detailed tables and figures enhance presentation. (Reviewer 5)

### Weaknesses
- **Scientific validity of Jung’s personality theory**: The authors reject MBTI due to lack of empirical backing but adopt Jung’s theory, which also has little to no empirical/scientific support. Simply stating “on the recommendation of psychologists” is insufficient evidence. (Reviewer 2)
- **Overclaiming use of Jung’s theory**: The paper generates 640 personality descriptions from Jung’s dimensions but only introduces 11 characters, so the large design space is unused. Moreover, Jung’s theory provides continuous scores for each dimension, and the paper uses only 10 descriptions per ranking, which seems insufficient to capture the scale. (Reviewer 4)
- **Inadequate evaluation scope and realism**:
    - Only 55 scenarios from a single situational judgment test (Mussel et al., 2016) are used for observer reports; this limits the evaluation to a constrained set of situations and may not capture the nuances of real-world social interactions and dynamic contexts. (Reviewers 2, 5)
    - The bandwagon effect experiment is only a single case of psychological/sociological experiments, which cannot support the paper’s broad claim about replacing human subjects. (Reviewer 5)
    - Self-report evaluations resemble question-answering or reading comprehension tests based on character profiles (e.g., “When is your birthday?”) rather than testing complex human behavior. (Reviewer 5)
- **Missing ablations and baselines**:
    - No ablations of MACM components are conducted; only baselines like RAG are compared, and MACM’s gains over RAG appear limited. (Reviewer 2)
    - In the conformity experiments, RAG is not compared against MACM, and the Character.AI baseline is inadequately justified. (Reviewer 2)
    - Ablation on human expert involvement in dataset/evaluation is not performed. (Reviewer 3)
- **Clarity and presentation issues**:
    - Abstract is vague and lacks specifics about empirical experiments and results. (Reviewer 2)
    - Introduction does not clearly explain Jung’s theories, though they are central to the framework. (Reviewer 2)
    - Methods section lacks clarity: generation process, sub-tasks, and rationale for choosing them are not explained (only referenced to a figure). (Reviewer 2)
    - Data collection process: how is human feedback collected? What is the measure of quality? How does it improve with iterations? (Reviewer 2)
    - The phrase “create a comfortable chatting environment … as their best friend” is confusing and not clearly defined. (Reviewer 2)
    - “Cloze” methodology is not defined in the main text. (Reviewer 2)
    - Use of Character.AI models is unclear; how exactly are they employed? (Reviewer 2)
    - In the conformity experiment, it is unclear whether LLMs see the stimuli; if not, the setup is strange. (Reviewer 2)
    - The benchmark’s definition of “what is good/bad evaluation result” is not straightforward; a diagram would help. (Reviewer 3)
    - Terms “psychology support” and “human feedback” in Table 1 are unclear, and “full life story” seems overstated. (Reviewer 5)
    - The title “personification of LLMs” is misleading and overclaimed, as the paper does not explore many aspects of personification. (Reviewer 5)
    - Hallucination comparison between genuine and simulated profiles is unclear; simulated profiles may also cause hallucination and are harder to detect. Why not use storybook characters to avoid ethical issues? (Reviewer 4)
    - The description score of MACM is lower than RAG in Table 3; need explanation. (Reviewer 4)
- **Lack of qualitative analysis**: The paper would benefit from deeper analysis of model failures (e.g., inconsistencies in character behavior, deviations from intended personality), including categorizing failures to identify patterns. (Reviewer 5)
- **Unexplored difficulty variations**: The benchmark does not consider difficulty variations across different kinds of personas (e.g., real-world vs. fictional), which may affect model performance. (Reviewer 3)
- **Theory-driven without application motivation**: The evaluation framework is heavily psychology-theory driven, but it is not clear why certain theories are chosen over others, whether all components are necessary, or whether important aspects are missing. It would be preferable to motivate with real-world applications of persona-driven simulations and design evaluations around components useful in those applications. (Reviewer 3)
- **Data collection requires extensive human effort**, which may limit scalability and reproducibility, though it also ensures quality. (Reviewer 2, 4)
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: One reviewer (score 8) stated “no outstanding weaknesses,” while others raised the significant concerns above. The negative reviewer (score 3) particularly stressed lack of scientific validity and clarity. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
