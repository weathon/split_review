## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper employs a high-dimensional geometric framework (Approximate Kinematic Formula, statistical dimension) to characterize the sharp phase transition point of network pruning, deriving lower and upper bounds on the sparsity level that can be achieved without performance loss. The theoretical pruning ratio threshold, computed via Hessian eigenvalue analysis, is shown to align well with experiments. The paper also proposes an improved spectrum estimation algorithm for large Hessian matrices.

### Strengths
- The theoretical analysis from a high-dimensional geometric perspective provides justification for pruning limits (lower and upper bounds) and aligns well with experimental results, offering valuable insights for the pruning community. The methodology is inspiring: the authors transform the problem into studying Hessian eigenvalues via quadratic approximation of the loss sublevel set, making the problem clearer.
- Extensive experiments across diverse datasets and model architectures demonstrate alignment between theory and practice. The analysis supports commonly observed phenomena such as the superiority of magnitude over random pruning, the effectiveness of iterative magnitude pruning, and the benefit of regularization.
- The authors employ efficient computational tools, including Hessian matrix sampling for spectrum estimation, to handle large-scale Hessian matrices.
- The paper is well-structured with clear notations and concept plots that illustrate key ideas. The derived limit is verified by induction and extensive experiments.

### Weaknesses
- Several reviewers note a lack of clarity in key areas. More intuitive descriptions are needed, especially for the Approximate Kinematics Formula, which is a central component (R2, R4). The terminology “upper bound” for the pruning limit is confusing: the authors illustrate a limit beyond which no sparser network exists without sacrifice, but also refer to a sparsity where a sparse network can be found; the use of “upper bound” may be unsuitable (R4).
- Reviewer 3 identifies a potential mismatch between the pruning objective in Eq.3 (task loss + L1 regularization) and the theoretical analysis in Section 3.3, which considers a well-trained network with weights w* without reference to that regularization. This raises doubt about whether the theoretical justification applies to networks trained under the L1-regularized objective. Consequently, the practical usage is questioned: if L1 regularization controls sparsity, a simpler approach is to count zeros after training or use L0 regularization, rather than relying on Hessian-based theoretical pruning rate calculation. Moreover, the theoretical analysis does not incorporate the hyperparameter λ, so the predicted pruning rate cannot be known before training; if it does not meet expectations, the model must be retrained with adjusted λ, limiting guidance for training with a target pruning rate.
- The pruning method is considered not novel, being simply L1-regularized training followed by magnitude pruning (R3).
- Several reviewers request validation on large-scale datasets (e.g., ImageNet/Place365) and note potential computational bottlenecks for Hessian matrix computation at such scales (R1, R3, R4). Applicability to dense models for predicting pruning rates is also questioned (R3).
- Specific presentation issues: Table 3’s “Sparsity” column numbers should be subtracted by 100 (R1); the margin of improvement for GOP over LTH is not clearly explained, especially since using L1 or L2 norm for magnitude pruning does not alter weight rank (R4). Figure 3 would benefit from a log-scale x-axis to better visualize the sharp drop-off (R1).
- Section 5.3 on regularization-based pruning algorithms appears somewhat disconnected from the paper’s core focus; better alignment with contributions would be helpful (R1).
- It is unclear whether the analysis implicitly assumes specific network architectures or loss functions (R2).
- For the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (LTH), a key point is maintaining the same initialization; the theory currently does not provide an explanation for this phenomenon (R4).
- The paper does not address alignment of theoretical results with pruning of Large Language Models (LLM), despite the era’s relevance (R1).
