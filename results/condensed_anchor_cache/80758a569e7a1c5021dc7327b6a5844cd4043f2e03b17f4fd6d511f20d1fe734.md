## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LLM-Codebook, a codebook-based compression method using Hessian-aware K-means to cluster weights into KB-scale codebooks, followed by LoRA-based recovery. All reviewers agree the paper is well-written. There is disagreement on novelty: one reviewer finds the perspective of treating pruning/quantization as information compression novel and the clustering approach unique; another views it as a straightforward application of product quantization with Hessian-aware K-means already used in prior works, and LoRA recovery is not new. Experimental comparisons are limited to LLaMA-7B and Vicuna-7B (no results on 13B or larger models) and lack comparisons with recent low-bit quantization methods (e.g., omniquant, low_bit_llama, QA-LoRA). Key technical choices (salience estimation sensitivity, random layer selection, necessity of Hessian-aware vs vanilla K-means) are under-explained. A minority reviewer (score 6) is more positive about effectiveness; others (scores 5, 5, 3) note significant clarity and verification issues.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow (all reviewers).
- Solid improvement over naive quantization (R1).
- Achieves higher compression ratios for LLMs (R2, R4). Specifically, good performance compared to GPTQ, SparseGPT, and LLM-Pruner (R4).
- Novel perspective: reframes pruning and quantization as information compression; proposes a unique technique of compressing weights by clustering into kilobyte-scale codebooks (R2).
- Proposed Hessian-aware K-means for importance-aware clustering (R3, R4).
- Overall method is simple and effective (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The method is essentially an application of product quantization to LLMs; Hessian-aware K-means has been used in prior works [1][2] (R1). The combination with LoRA is a simple combination of two mature techniques (R2). One reviewer found the perspective novel (R2), but another considered the overall novelty limited (R1).
- **Missing comparisons**: Does not compare with state-of-the-art quantization methods, especially low-bit compression works such as omniquant, low_bit_llama, and QA-LoRA, which show promising 2-bit results on LLaMA families (R3, R4). Evaluation is only on LLaMA-7B/Vicuna-7B; lacking results on larger models, e.g., LLaMA-13B (R1). Experimental results are not clearly fair in comparison to baselines (R2).
- **Salience estimation sensitivity (Eq 5)**: Only 15 randomly selected samples from Bookcorpus are used. How sensitive is the estimation to data source (e.g., Wiki) and number of samples? What is the influence of estimation error on the recovery stage? (R2, R3)
- **Role of LoRA vs Hessian-aware K-means**: Table 3 shows LoRA tuning is critical for avoiding unacceptable degradation. With only vanilla K-means and more recovery tuning, can the best performance be matched? What is the must-be reason to use Hessian-aware K-means given its extra computation and secondary role in recovery? (R2, R3)
- **Baselines without LoRA tuning**: The baselines in Table 1 (GPTQ, SparseGPT, etc.) do not use a LoRA recovery stage. If such a stage were added, would those baselines catch up with the proposed method? (R2)
- **Table 3 anomaly**: The salience-adopted clustering leads to *more* performance degradation than vanilla K-means (-6.3 vs -3.5). Please explain (R2).
- **Downstream fine-tuning**: After compression, each linear layer consists of a codebook and an index map. How can the model be further fine-tuned for downstream tasks? (R3, R4)
- **Memory cost for larger models**: Using LoRA for full model recovery—would memory cost be huge for a model like LLaMA-70B? (R3, R4)
- **Random layer selection**: It is unclear why a layer is randomly selected for compression during the LoRA fine-tuning procedure (R3, R4).
- **Figure 2 visualization**: The compressed weight tensor looks too similar to the original; please explain (R3, R4).
- **Unverified claims**: Some statements about compression methods lack sufficient verification; latest works (e.g., low-bit quantization) are not discussed (R3, R4). More insightful explanations connecting the components are needed to avoid the impression of a simple combination (R2).
