## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces an information-theoretic analysis of multimodal interactions (Redundancy, Uniqueness, Synergy) and argues that joint learning and modality ensemble paradigms cannot capture all interaction types, leading to generalization gaps. It proposes the Decomposition-based Multimodal Interaction learning (DMI) paradigm, which uses variation-based modules to disentangle these interactions and a three-phase training strategy. Experimental results on CREMA-D, AV-MNIST, Kinetics Sounds, and CMU MOSEI show improvements over baselines. Reviewers found the theoretical motivation novel and the method design interesting, but raised substantial concerns about the clarity and completeness of the method description, the limited scope and rigor of the experiments, marginal gains, missing ablations/comparisons, and insufficient documentation of the synthetic validation. The overall consensus is that while the core idea is promising, the paper in its current form lacks the experimental thoroughness and methodological detail to support its strong claims.

### Strengths

- **Novel theoretical analysis**: The paper is the first to establish a theoretical connection between multimodal interaction types and multimodal learning performance, providing sound proofs (Lemma 3.3, Proposition 3.1) that joint and ensemble learning fail to capture all necessary interactions, leading to generalization gaps. The analysis is clear and well-written. [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- **Clear breakdown of interactions**: The decomposition of multimodal information into Redundancy (R), Uniqueness (U), and Synergy (S), and how they can be explicitly optimized via a loss function, is well explained and illustrated (Fig 3, equations). [R1, R2, R3]
- **Strong performance on chosen datasets**: The proposed DMI method outperforms state-of-the-art baselines on CREMA-D, AV-MNIST, Kinetics Sounds, and CMU MOSEI for most metrics. [R1, R2, R3]
- **Well-designed visualizations**: Figures (especially Fig 1 and Fig 3) are clear, informative, and help understand the method. [R1, R3]
- **Relevant baseline selection**: Chosen baselines including joint learning, ensemble learning, unimodal regulation, and multimodal interaction methods make sense. [R2]
- **Clear experimental technical details**: The setup (backbones, datasets, evaluation metrics) is presented in a understandable manner. [R2]

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear model architecture and insufficient method details**: The method section lacks sufficient detail to understand or reproduce the work. Only Figure 3 describes the architecture; the input, output, and objectives of each training stage are not clearly explained. [R1, R3, R4]  
  - Stage 1 (warm-up encoder): what are the inputs, outputs, and objective? Are both encoders warmed up? [R3]  
  - Stage 2 (freeze encoder): are all encoders frozen, with only the decoders being fine-tuned? [R3]  
  - Stage 3 (end-to-end) and how the pretrained model is used for downstream tasks (further fine-tuning or direct application?) are unclear. [R3]  
  - Does the learning objective (Eqs 13, 14) include a reconstruction loss to guide the decoders? [R3]  
  - The several modifications specific to each modality in ResNet18 (Line 416) are not explained. [R3]

- **Confusing theoretical notation and practical meaning**:  
  - Lemma 3.3 uses O(1/√N)|_{N=2n} big-O notation with a condition “N=2n”. The asymptotic interpretation is unclear—what difference does N=n vs. N=2n make? [R1]  
  - The proof constructs new datasets S(1) and S(2) by separating X(1) and X(2), effectively doubling the sample size, but the underlying data is the same. It is unclear what this result means in practice. [R1]

- **Limited experimental scope and lack of comprehensiveness**: The paper claims to be a general contribution to multimodal learning, but experiments are limited to audiovisual datasets (CREMA-D, AV-MNIST, Kinetics Sounds) and a limited subset of CMU MOSEI (audio-text, visual-text). No results on vision+text only, three-modality (audio+vision+text), or other sensor modalities (LIDAR, EEG, physiological sensors) are provided. This does not match the claim of “comprehensive” and “holistic” experiments. [R2, R4]

- **Weak experimental design within audiovisual datasets**:  
  - Kinetics Sounds: using only 1 frame per second (10 frames total) instead of all frames at 25-30 FPS is suboptimal for capturing temporal information. [R2]  
  - CREMA-D: taking a single frame from the entire video neglects temporal dynamics (onset-apex-offset) critical for affect recognition; the choice is only reasonable because it is an acted dataset where the expression centers near the middle frame. [R2]  
  - The scale of datasets is limited; more challenging audiovisual datasets (e.g., AVSpeech, LRS, LRW, Audioset) are available. [R2]

- **Marginal improvements and missing statistical significance**: DMI’s improvements over the best baseline on AV-MNIST and CMU-MOSEI (V+T) are ≤2% accuracy and not statistically significant. [R3, R4]  
  - The overall performance gain over existing methods is marginal, which weakens the claim of importance of interaction learning. [R4]

- **Insufficient ablation and comparison analyses**:  
  - Ablation study (Table 4) shows that single decomposition methods (DMI-TC, DMI-CD) perform worse than DMI-FC (no decomposition). This is not explained. [R3]  
  - No direct comparison/analysis between DMI and other interaction methods beyond stating that improvements come from decomposition and holistic learning—the reviewer is unsure if comparisons are fair (e.g., modality-specific encoders and model size standardized). [R4]  
  - Uni-modal performance baselines are missing from Tables 2 and 3. [R3]  
  - No analysis of why DMI works beyond the ablation—e.g., what specific interaction types are better captured? [R4]

- **Synthetic data generation and validation lack details**: The synthetic setup (Section 3.3, Figure 1) is interesting but poorly documented. Missing: how informative dimensions “maintain statistical relationships with the label” for each interaction type, how different interaction combinations (e.g., ¼ U + ¾ R) are achieved, number of synthetic data used, DMI architecture in this setting. Only one complex setting (AND+XOR) is evaluated; other logical combinations (OR+XOR, AND+XOR+OR) would strengthen the claim. [R4]

- **Conceptual concerns about DMI assumptions**:  
  - Why is synergistic information only considered task-irrelevant (Line 322)? In pure-synergy tasks like XOR, synergy is task-relevant. [R3, R4]  
  - DMI assumes a complete separation of synergistic features from redundancy/uniqueness, but overlaps can exist (e.g., sarcasm detection: positive sentiment in facial expression may be both unique and necessary for the synergistic inconsistency). Can DMI handle such cases? How would it perform on sarcasm detection? [R4]  
  - Equation 13 uses V and M as symmetric, but the method later makes one task-relevant and the other task-irrelevant (Line 352). How is this controlled? [R3]

- **Concerns about reproducibility and baselines**:  
  - Comparison to Wu et al. (2024): the numbers reported for this baseline are much lower than in the original paper—what experimental differences exist? [R2]  
  - The method uses relatively simple backbones (ResNet18, LeNet) which are sufficient for basic experiments but not for drawing holistic conclusions. [R2]  
  - It is unclear if the method can be easily extended to more than two modalities (e.g., audio+vision+text, multiple visual streams in robotics). [R2]

- **Minor but notable issues**:  
  - Typo: “CRAME” should be “CREMA”. [R2]  
  - Broken hyperref links in Figure 1 and citations in tables. [R2]  
  - Notation errors in appendix: Equation 17/18 should use fraction form, not nested logs (Proposition 3.1 proof); inconsistent notation \(u,v\) for first independent features in A.5. [R4]
