## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies reinforcement learning under unknown reward perturbations, focusing on a class of confusion-matrix-based perturbations that discretize and shuffle reward space. The key assumption is that the true reward is the most frequently observed class after perturbation (mode-preserving). The proposed Distributional Reward Critic (DRC) learns a classifier to predict the bin index of the true reward; extensions handle unknown number of intervals and reward range (GDRC). Theoretical guarantees and experiments on continuous control tasks are provided, claiming wins/tie in 40/57 settings.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important and timely problem (RL with corrupted rewards, e.g., finetuning LLMs). (R1, R2)
- The idea of predicting rewards via a categorical distribution is novel and clever. (R1, R2)
- Generalizes prior work by handling non-order-preserving perturbations without directly estimating the confusion matrix. (R1)
- The adaptive mechanism for an unknown number of intervals (GDRC) and the supporting analysis are nice. (R2, R3)
- Comprehensive study: algorithms for known/unknown intervals and reward range, theoretical guarantees (asymptotic recovery, voting mechanism), and extra analyses (e.g., mode collapse). The authors are upfront about shortcomings. (R2, R3)
- Studying unknown interval/partition settings is more practical. (R4)
- Experimental evaluation is extensive; superior performance is shown on many tasks. (R1 – but see weaknesses about significance and fairness.)

### Weaknesses
- **Restrictive mode-preserving assumption:**  
  The assumption that the true reward is the most frequently observed class after perturbation is artificial and lacks real-world justification (e.g., sensor noise, human feedback). (R1, R2, R3)  
  Reviewer 1 notes it may be more restrictive than order-preserving assumptions and that methods directly estimating the confusion matrix could be more widely applicable.  
  Reviewer 3 finds the RE assumption more natural and asks how robust the method is when the confusion matrix is not mode-preserving.  
  Reviewer 2 gives a concrete counterexample: a betting game with rewards -1 (prob 0.9) and +10 (0.1); Romoff et al. learns expected reward 0.1 and takes the bet, while the proposed method learns mode -1 and avoids it. Thus the approach can decrease performance on unperturbed tasks when rewards are stochastic. If clean rewards are deterministic and the perturbation is mode-preserving then it works, but this is a strong assumption – e.g., human feedback is inherently stochastic. (R2)

- **Fairness and interpretability of experimental comparisons:**  
  Comparisons in Figs. 5 and 6 may be unfair to RE because tasks are not order-preserving; fairer comparisons on settings where all methods apply are needed. (R1)  
  The proposed methods underperform on many instances without analysis of why (e.g., environment characteristics, failure modes). (R1)  
  Learning curves are not significantly better than baselines; conclusions rely only on final return. Lack of Atari experiments (standard for value-based RL) raises generalizability concerns. (R4)  
  It is not clearly established that better reward recovery directly translates to better final performance. (R4)

- **Missing ablations and methodological questions:**  
  Ablation needed: compare the same categorical distribution but train the agent from the **mean** (instead of mode) to disentangle the effect of the categorical assumption from the mode-preserving assumption. (R2)  
  Computational cost of training the classifier vs. estimating the confusion matrix is unclear. (R1)  
  Using **argmax** to select the most probable reward label (Alg. 1, step 7) discards distribution information and reduces the problem to regression, undermining the benefit of learning a full distribution. (R4)  
  Theoretical statements: Theorem 1 is descriptive and lacks a rigorous mathematical statement; the proof is overcomplicated (says essentially “under perfect conditions cross-entropy goes to zero”). (R2, R4)  
  Proposition 1 appears incorrect (e.g., r = r_min corrupted to exceed r_max) and its proof (Appendix B.1) incomplete. (R4)  
  The impact of n_o (number of intervals in GDRC) is studied but Fig. 3 is difficult to interpret, leaving the choice of n_r open. (R1) Determining n_o via training loss is normally intractable in large environments. (R4)  
  Reliance on fixed discretization (like C51) without justification over quantile-based methods; C51 is typically inferior due to unknown range/partitions. (R4)

- **Conceptual issues:**  
  The connection to distributional RL is superficial: distributional RL focuses on return distribution, not reward distribution. Borrowing categorical parameterization from C51 does not fully engage with the theory. The term “distributional reward critic” may be misleading if it does not correspond to a standard action-value critic. (R1, R4)  
  The claim on page 2 that “affine transformations preserve optimal policies” is only true for non-episodic tasks, contradicting the later discussion on Hopper/Walker2d. (R2)

- **Minor presentation and technical issues:**  
  Discretization of continuous perturbations always induces reconstruction error, conflicting with some statements. (R4)  
  Grammar errors and typos; inconsistent reference style. (R3, R4)  
  Notation: “β: Δ(S)” should be “β ∈ Δ(S)”; what is SR_W? (R3)  
  Clarify “10 seeds and 20 random trials” – independent trials are usually associated with one seed each. (R1)  
  In Fig. 6, why does DRC performance improve as noise increases? (R1)  
  Could the proposed approach handle state- and action-dependent perturbations? (R2)  

*Note:* Reviewer 4 is significantly more negative (score 3) than the other reviewers (scores 5–6), particularly regarding theoretical rigor, contribution novelty, and practical significance. This contrast is preserved throughout.
