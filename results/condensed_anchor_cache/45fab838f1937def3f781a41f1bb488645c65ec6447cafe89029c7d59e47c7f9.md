## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ∞-benchmarks, a dynamic evaluation paradigm where sample-level test data from multiple sources are aggregated into a pool, allowing users to select subsets for custom benchmarks. The authors address heterogeneity (different metrics) by converting scores to rankings and using the Plackett-Luce model for ranking, and incompleteness (models evaluated on different subsets) by leveraging identifiability conditions. They introduce ∞-LLMBench and ∞-LMMBench, and claim high sample efficiency (up to 20× cost reduction) and recovery of ground-truth rankings with high Kendall τ even with 95% missing data.

### Strengths
- The question of how to evaluate and understand foundation models is timely and important (R1, confidence 3).
- Personalized evaluation that allows practitioners to focus on capabilities of interest is a useful concept (R2, confidence 3).
- The idea of dynamically constructing custom benchmarks is interesting; it can give fine-grained insights into model behavior and reduce statistical artifacts from individual datasets (R3, confidence 3).
- The Plackett-Luce model for computing rankings is described with theoretical identifiability conditions and shows high sample efficiency, a strong advantage given the cost of human labeling (R3).
- Grammar, styling, and attention to detail are extremely high quality (R4, confidence 4).
- Figure 1 is very well done and communicates the paper’s contributions clearly (R4).
- Table 1 provides a reasonable way to assess different ranking mechanisms (R4).
- Figure 3 is compelling (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear core contribution and poor presentation.** The paper is difficult to follow; it is unclear whether ∞-benchmarks is a new dataset, an evaluation pipeline, or a ranking tool (R1). The main methodology is relegated to Appendix A, with no reference in the main text, yet it is a significant pillar (R4). Section 2.1 is titled “Why This Works” but the reader does not yet know what “this” is, making the section premature (R4). The notation “P1, P2, P3…” is unexplained (R4). Section 2.1 reads as a literature review of Plackett-Luce rather than an author contribution (R4). The paper relies heavily on Plackett-Luce but does not summarize it in the main text, leaving readers unfamiliar with it lost (R4). Section 2.2 is especially difficult to follow (R4). Overall, the technical contribution beyond merging existing datasets is unclear (R1, R2).
- **Ground truth ranking issues.** Rankings are to some extent subjective; e.g., Elo score defines ground truth by many battles (R1). The paper uses existing leaderboard rankings as “ground truth” in Table 1, but those benchmarks (e.g., HELM) also aggregate across subtasks, so it is unclear whether they represent a ground truth or just an alternative aggregation (R3). No metric achieves 1.0 Kendall τ even with full data; it is unclear where the ground truth ranking comes from (R2). For LLMs-Eval and VHELM, the proposed method’s Kendall τ is comparatively low (R4). Line 236 claims the method “preserves the ranking of the top-10 models,” but the figure appears to reorder them; clarification needed (R4).
- **Assumption that ordinal rankings are sufficient.** The paper assumes rankings are all practitioners care about, but in practice the magnitude of gaps matters: e.g., OpenAI o1’s AIME score was significantly higher than competitors, and GDM’s 1M/10M context length was orders of magnitude longer (R4). If Model B is epsilon-better than Model A, but Model A is already implemented and cheaper, switching may not be justified (R4). Also, cost is an important factor; a model that is epsilon-better is not worth 10× or 100× inference costs (R4). The paper should discuss whether dynamic benchmarks can reveal when ranking differences are significant (R3).
- **Personalized evaluation appears straightforward.** The idea of selecting a subset of samples based on a query can be seen as retrieval + evaluation; it has already been explored at a higher level (e.g., using GSM8K, MATH for mathematical reasoning) (R2). The paper offers no substantial technical contribution beyond merging datasets (R1, R2).
- **Trivial or self-contradictory observations.** Section 2.3 notes that a dataset contains many easy/hard problems where all or no models succeed, so evaluation can be efficient by focusing on the central difficulty bin. This is obvious, and to identify that bin one must already evaluate models, defeating the purpose; also, the bin is dynamic as model capabilities change (R2). Furthermore, Insight 2 (“Active Sampling Improves Data Aggregation Efficiency”) appears to contradict Insight 3 (“Random Sampling Matches Informative Sampling”) in Section 2.3 (R4). Figure 4 suggests ~40% of data examples are low-signal, which should let informative sampling use only ~60% as many examples as random, but the paper shows no such benefit; the authors should explain why it does not materialize (R3).
- **Broader concerns about dynamic benchmarks unaddressed.** (1) Do custom benchmarks actually measure the intended capability? Retrieval precision ranges from 28.5% to 100% (Section 3.2.1). Does filtering out erroneously retrieved examples change the top-5 ordering? (R3). Also, how specific can custom benchmarks be (e.g., “Kirchoff’s Law” vs. “electricity and magnetism”), and does narrower specificity face worse retrieval precision? (R3). (2) Dynamic benchmarks could incentivize researchers to invent new narrow benchmarks to claim SOTA, or to try many custom benchmarks until finding one that flatters their model (R3). (3) Re-using data examples across custom benchmarks creates correlations; how can research papers transparently present these dependencies? (R3).
- **Missing citations.** The paper mentions efficient evaluation but does not cite relevant work on multi-arm bandits for efficient evaluation, e.g., [1] Shi et al. (2024) and [2] Zhou et al. (2024) (R2).
- **Methodological assumptions.** The Plackett-Luce method assumes sample-level rankings are available, but model creators often release only aggregate metrics (e.g., 5-shot accuracy on MMLU) (R4). The claim that ordinal measurements can paradoxically outperform cardinal ones (Appendix A) needs empirical proof in this setting (R4).
- **Figures lacking substance.** Figure 2 gives no sense of whether the permutations shown are minor or major (R4). Figure 5 is aesthetically nice but does not convey much (R4).
