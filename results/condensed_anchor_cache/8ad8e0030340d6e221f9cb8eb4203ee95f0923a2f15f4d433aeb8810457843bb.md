## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers find the paper’s investigation of self-debugging with self-generated tests important and clearly presented, praising the definition of post-execution vs. in-execution paradigms, the choice of models (GPT-4o, LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct) and datasets (HumanEval+, MBPP+, LiveCodeBench), and the observation that self-generated test accuracy is only 59.15% even with GPT-4o. The in-execution approach is seen as potentially mitigating bias from faulty tests. However, reviewers raise major concerns about limited model and setting coverage (only two models, greedy sampling, no variation of test count or oracle source), inconsistent prompts between methods, insufficient detail on the in-execution trace mechanism (especially handling loops), the lack of a same‑budget baseline for cost comparison, and weak novelty. Reviewer 4 (score 8) is notably more positive, emphasizing that the experiments are robust and the community will benefit, while Reviewer 1 (score 5) finds the evaluation limited and novelty questionable; the other two reviewers (scores 6) are in the middle.

### Strengths
- **Clear structure and motivation:** The paper is well‑written and systematically compares post‑execution vs. in‑execution self‑debugging, providing a useful framework for future research.
- **Relevant problem:** The focus on self‑generated test bias is timely and important; the finding that tests are only 59.15% accurate (GPT-4o) explains why post‑execution debugging can hurt performance.
- **Analysis of limitations:** The paper dives into why post‑execution fails on basic problems and shows that in‑execution can offset the bias from wrong tests by recognizing faulty feedback.
- **Strong experiment design:** Use of two complementary models (one open, one API‑based), plus the “Plus” versions of HumanEval/MBPP and the newer LiveCodeBench, adds credibility and recency.
- **Interesting nuances:** Despite test inaccuracy, post‑execution still helps on contest‑level tasks; the paper notes that simply adding more post‑execution rounds can help even with wrong test labels.
- **Potential to inspire future work:** The findings highlight the need for better test generation and open up questions about how to leverage intermediate execution states.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited model scope:** Only two LLMs (GPT‑4o and LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct) are evaluated. The findings may not transfer to non‑instruction‑tuned models, code‑specialized models, or other strong baselines (e.g., Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek).
- **Insufficient ablations and settings:** The experiments use only greedy sampling and do not vary the number of self‑generated tests, the source of tests (e.g., using a larger LLM to debug a smaller one), or the debugging iteration count systematically—many of these are known to impact self‑repair results (e.g., Olausson et al. 2024).
- **Inconsistent evaluation prompts:** Post‑execution and in‑execution methods use different prompts (input‑output analysis vs. trace analysis), making a fair comparison difficult; reviewers request the same prompt structure for both.
- **Lack of detail on in‑execution mechanism:** The paper is vague about how intermediate traces are generated and truncated, especially for loops. Loops with unknown iteration counts are treated as atomic blocks—this limitation and its potential impact on bug detection (e.g., bugs inside loops) are not discussed.
- **Cost vs. performance not properly contextualized:** The reported +X% pass rate after 3 debugging iterations is compared to a baseline of a single sample, not to a same‑budget baseline with independent samples. The paper’s claim “Self‑debugging does not require increasing the sample budget” is misleading, as each debugging step uses additional model calls.
- **Benchmark realism:** All benchmarks are self‑contained Python problems (HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench). The findings may not generalize to repository‑level coding tasks, which involve larger codebases and complex dependencies.
- **Weak technical contribution and future direction:** The paper is primarily an empirical study with no new method; the discussion section is thin and offers few concrete suggestions for improving test generation or handling loop traces.
- **Questionable table trend:** In Table 1, pass rate drops from 2 to 3 iterations despite using oracle tests; this is inconsistent because passing programs should be a superset—requires explanation.
- **Reference error:** The self‑debugging paper by Chen (2023) is cited as ICLR 2023 but is actually ICLR 2024; other references should be double‑checked.
- **Data contamination concern:** Reviewer 4 suspects that the large difference in results on LiveCodeBench vs. HumanEval/MBPP may be due to the latter being in the training data of most models—this should be discussed explicitly.
- **Missing case studies and comparisons:** A concrete case study showing how in‑execution offsets test bias is requested, as well as a figure directly comparing post‑ and in‑execution performance across problem difficulty levels and over more iterations.
- **Trace handling for long loops:** The paper does not describe how extremely long traces (e.g., deeply nested loops) are truncated or summarized while preserving essential information.
