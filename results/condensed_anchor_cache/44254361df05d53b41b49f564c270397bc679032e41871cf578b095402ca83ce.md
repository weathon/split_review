## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper provides a new theoretical understanding of the symmetric InfoNCE loss used in CLIP, showing that the optimal similarity measure minimizing this loss is the pointwise mutual information (PMI). The authors derive an upper bound on the excess risk for downstream classification when optimal similarity is achieved, and propose a weighted point cloud (WPC) similarity (a weighted sum of kernel evaluations over sets of feature vectors) to approximate the PMI. Experiments on image-text pretraining and classification benchmarks demonstrate the method’s effectiveness. One reviewer (Score 6) found limited innovation and the point‑cloud framing overstated, while two reviewers (Scores 8) appreciated the theoretical motivation but noted several practical concerns and missing ablations.

### Strengths

1. The paper offers a novel theoretical understanding of the CLIP contrastive loss, deriving that the optimal similarity is PMI and providing an excess‑risk bound – theoretically well‑founded (R1, R3).
2. The introduction is well‑written, clearly motivating why a single CLIP embedding is insufficient for many‑to‑many correspondences (R3).
3. The proposed similarity uses weighted sums of kernel evaluations, benefiting from the kernel trick: it can approximate the optimal PMI more closely than inner‑product similarity for the same computational budget (R2).
4. The combination of theory with practice is demonstrated through experiments that show effectiveness (R1).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited innovation / overstated framing**  
   - One reviewer (Score 6) found the innovation limited, noting that “suboptimality decomposition” is referenced from existing work and that the method essentially uses weighted sums of feature vectors – a common technique – while the connection to point clouds (neighborhoods, connectivity) is tenuous (R1).  
   - Another reviewer (Score 8) agreed that, practically speaking, similarity metrics over pairs of sets of features are well‑established (e.g., ColBERT: arXiv 2004.12832), and extending from NLP to image‑text is straightforward; hence there is not much methodological innovation (R3).  
   - The distinction from prior dense contrastive learning methods (e.g., *Dense Contrastive Learning for Self-Supervised Visual Pre-Training*, CVPR 2021) is unclear (R3).

2. **Assumptions and theoretical justification**  
   - The argument that inner‑product similarity cannot approximate the optimal similarity matrix \(G\) when \(N > d+1\) (due to rank deficiency, lines 309‑310) is not rigorously justified: the effective rank of \(G\) might be small, and the success of CLIP suggests a lower‑rank approximation may suffice. The paper does not show that the full rank of \(G\) is necessary (R2).  
   - The explanation of why a higher rank of \(G\) leads to approximation error is insufficiently detailed (R1).  
   - Assumption C.2 (existence of low‑dimensional latent variables with continuous bijective decoders) seems strong; the paper lacks practical examples or justification for its relevance (R1).  
   - The notion of “similarity structure” (line 41) is not clearly defined, nor is its connection to the theoretical analysis (R1).  
   - Equation (3) contains a typo (empty space before ||), noted by R1 and R3.

3. **Missing ablations and experiments**  
   - The hyperparameter options for \((\alpha_1, \alpha_2)\) do not include a linear‑only \((1,0)\) or nonlinear‑only \((0,1)\) setting. Given that the ablation study shows linear‑only kernel achieves comparable performance (especially in zero‑shot classification), these choices are desirable to assess the contribution of the nonlinear component (R2).  
   - An analysis of the singular‑value spectrum (effective rank) of \(G\) would help clarify whether the rank deficiency of inner‑product is actually deleterious; this would directly relate rank disparity to the L2 error (R2).  
   - No comparison is made to existing dense contrastive losses that also use multiple feature vectors per sample (R3).

4. **Computational cost**  
   - The contrastive loss already scales as \(O(B^2)\) (batch size squared). The proposed method multiplies this cost by the square of the number of feature vectors per sample, making it substantially more expensive (R3).
