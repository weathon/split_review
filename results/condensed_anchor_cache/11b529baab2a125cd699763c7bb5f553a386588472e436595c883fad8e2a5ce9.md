## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TimeRAG, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework for stock movement prediction using LLMs. It retrieves relevant historical time-series sequences (represented as JSON) and trains a retriever with LLM feedback via KL-divergence and contrastive learning (in-batch negative sampling). Experiments on four real-world stock datasets (ACL18, BIGDATA22, CIKM18, Stock23) show improved accuracy (ACC) and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) over selected LLM-based baselines.

### Strengths
- First work to explicitly address RAG for continuous, temporal signals. The challenges of applying RAG to time-series (lack of ground truth, need to handle continuous data) are well motivated and explained. [R4, R1]
- The method of converting time-series data into JSON format is tailored to LLM processing, making sequential dependencies more accessible. This structured representation is not emphasized in prior retrieval models like LLM-Embedder and is essential for enabling LLMs to handle continuous data. [R1]
- Leveraging LLM feedback to train the retriever (using KL-divergence to align candidate distributions with the LLM’s preferences) provides an outcome-oriented way to filter relevant historical data. [R3, R4]
- Experiments on four real-world datasets demonstrate effectiveness, outperforming the compared baselines. [R2, R3, R4]
- The paper is well-organized and easy to read. [R3]

### Weaknesses
1. **Problem formulation and scope mismatch.**  
   - Framing time-series forecasting as a binary classification (rise/fall) is unconventional and discards valuable magnitude information critical for risk management. The model also ignores “freeze” or neutral movements, forcing misclassification of stationary periods. This limits practical utility. [R1, R4]  
   - The title “TimeRAG: It’s Time for Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Time-Series Forecasting” is misleading because the experiments are purely on stock trend prediction. The term “forecasting” typically implies value prediction, not just trend classification. [R2, R3, R4]  
   - The scope is narrow: only stock movement prediction is evaluated, and the method requires re-training the model for each stock, limiting generalization to other time-series domains. [R3, R4]

2. **Lack of novelty and insufficient distinction from prior work.**  
   - The core methodology (reward formulation from LLM feedback, KL-divergence knowledge distillation, in-batch negative sampling) closely replicates LLM-Embedder with minimal adaptation. The only modification is the input data format, which does not constitute a significant methodological advancement. [R1]  
   - The paper omits comparisons with many recent LLM-based time-series methods (e.g., Time-LLM, LLMTime, GPT4TS) and financial RAG models (e.g., AlphaFin, FinGPT). These should be included to better situate contributions. [R3]  
   - No non-LLM baselines such as PatchTST, DLinear, or classic time-series classification models are evaluated. Without these, it is unclear whether benefits stem from the RAG approach or the underlying LLM. [R1, R2]  
   - The paper focuses only on the retrieval side; there is no discussion of learnable elements in the prompt side or LLM side to further couple RAG with prediction. [R3]

3. **Experimental setup and reproducibility concerns.**  
   - Experimental settings are vague: training/validation/testing splits, hyperparameters, and tuning procedures are not clearly stated. [R2]  
   - The selection of positive (top‑1) and negative (last 15) candidate sequences is not well justified. Using the *last* 15 sequences as negatives is especially concerning because they are temporally correlated with the query, making them poor negatives for contrastive learning. A broader range of negatives (e.g., from different stocks or random periods) would be more robust. [R4]  
   - The process for deriving \(P(c)\) in Section 3.2 is not detailed. [R2]  
   - The description of preliminary “trials” that led to removing movement features from candidate representations is not quantified (how much data, metric used). [R4]

4. **Presentation and clarity issues.**  
   - The motivation for using an LLM for numerical stock data is not clear; only a little text (price/volume names) is used. An example showing what information the LLM extracts from the RAG would help. [R2]  
   - Figure 1 is difficult to understand: there are two almost identical task-definition prompts, and the grey text is not explained. The paper only mentions naming conventions but does not clarify why grey prompts appear or whether they were discarded. This obscures the experimental setup. [R2, R4]  
   - The term “LLM’s rewards” is not clarified. [R2]  
   - Table 4 (t-SNE visualization of embeddings) appears graphical; it may be better presented as a figure. Also, confirm whether t-SNE was used for dimensionality reduction. [R1]

5. **Doubts about retrieval value and generalizability.**  
   - The candidate sequences are from the *same* stock’s distant past. The intuition that recent data from other stocks would be more influential is not addressed. [R4]  
   - The case study (Figure 4) shows the retriever retrieving the closest datapoint, which resembles a KNN model (k=1). It is unclear whether the retrieval provides complementary information beyond proximity. [R4]  
   - The outcome-oriented model may lack generalization to other applications beyond stock trend prediction. [R4]  
   - Reviewers suggest switching from classification to regression to capture continuous trends (R1) and renaming the task “time-series trend forecasting” for accuracy (R2).
