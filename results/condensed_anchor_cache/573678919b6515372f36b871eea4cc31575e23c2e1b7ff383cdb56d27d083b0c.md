## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies risk-sensitive RL in low-rank MDPs with non-linear function approximation, aiming to maximize the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) with fixed risk tolerance $\tau$. The authors propose two algorithms: ELA (REpresentation Learning for CVaR) and ELLA (REpresentation Learning with LSVI for CVaR). ELA uses a UCB-based bonus and an MLE oracle for exploration and representation learning. ELLA improves computational efficiency by incorporating a discretized Least-Squares Value Iteration (LSVI) planning oracle. The main theoretical result is a sample complexity bound of $\tilde{O}(H^7 A^2 d^4/(\tau^2 \epsilon^2))$ for obtaining an $\epsilon$-optimal CVaR, where $H$ is episode length, $A$ action space size, $d$ representation dimension. The authors claim this is the first provably efficient CVaR RL algorithm in low-rank MDPs.

### Strengths
1. **Problem significance and timeliness.** The study of risk-sensitive RL (CVaR) in low-rank MDPs with nonlinear function approximation is interesting and relevant, partially addressing an open problem posed by Wang et al. (2023) about extending minimax CVaR guarantees from tabular to low-rank MDPs. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
2. **Clear presentation and structure.** The paper is well-written, with clear notation tables and easy-to-follow exposition of the setting and algorithms. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
3. **Novel computationally efficient planning oracle.** The ELLA algorithm, which employs a discretized LSVI approach for planning under the CVaR objective, is a nice addition and appears to be a novel contribution (Section 5, no prior citations). (Reviewer 3, also noted by Reviewers 1 and 4 as positive)
4. **First sample complexity bound for CVaR in low-rank MDPs.** The paper provides a rigorous PAC guarantee, which is new for this specific combination of risk measure and function approximation class. (Reviewer 4; though Reviewer 3 disputes the "first" claim, see Weaknesses)
5. **Sound technical exposition.** The technical details are generally correct and the analysis is logically structured. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Questionable novelty and reliance on prior techniques.** Several reviewers note that ELA closely resembles REP-UCB (Uehara et al., 2021) applied to an augmented MDP, with the CVaR bonus taken from Wang et al. (2023). The analysis largely adapts existing techniques, converting the CVaR objective into a value function (Eq. 13). The incremental nature of the contribution is a concern, and the authors should explicitly highlight which aspects are novel. (Reviewers 2, 4)
2. **Incorrect claim of "first provably efficient CVaR RL algorithm".** Reviewer 3 states that there have been regret bounds for CVaR RL in function approximation settings prior to this work. The authors should correct or qualify this claim. (Reviewer 3)
3. **Missing lower bound and tightness analysis.** The paper provides only upper bounds. Without lower bounds for CVaR in low-rank MDPs, it is unclear whether the $\tilde{O}(H^7 A^2 d^4/(\tau^2 \epsilon^2))$ bound is reasonable or tight. Questions remain about the dependence on $H$ and $d$ (see also point 6). (Reviewer 2)
4. **Critical missing assumption: requirement of a simulator.** The algorithm described in Section 4.1 collects two sets of trajectories, which appears to require the ability to draw samples from the true transition probability $P^*_h$ (i.e., a simulator). This is a major assumption that is not disclosed in the abstract or formal statement. If a simulator is indeed required, the significance of the result is substantially reduced, as with realizability (Assumption 3.2) and a finite model class, any algorithm would achieve no-regret given enough samples. The authors must clarify whether a simulator is needed and discuss the implication. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Computational inefficiency despite claimed improvement.** Both ELA and ELLA still rely on an MLE oracle for representation learning, which is computationally intractable in general. While ELLA improves planning, the overall algorithm remains practically inefficient. (Reviewer 1)
6. **Poor dependence on horizon $H$.** The bound has $H^7$, whereas prior work on low-rank MDPs with time-dependent transitions (Uehara et al., 2021) achieves $H^3$. The authors attribute this to non-stationarity, but Reviewer 4 points out that prior work also uses non-stationary transitions and obtains $H^3$. The worse dependence likely stems from the reward scale being $[0,H]$ instead of $[0,1]$. Applying similar value clipping techniques might improve the bound. A lower bound or discussion of tightness with respect to $H$ is needed. (Reviewer 4)
7. **Unclear algorithmic description in Section 4.1.** The description of collecting trajectories is confusing: the agent follows policy $\pi_k$ to state $s_h$ then takes a uniform action $a_h \sim U(\mathcal{A})$, which contradicts following $\pi_k$. The rationale for using different policies to sample the two actions (as asked by Reviewer 1) is also not explained. This lack of clarity may hide assumptions. (Reviewers 1, 2)
8. **Missing discussion of dynamic vs. static CVaR.** The paper does not distinguish between dynamic and static CVaR, which has non-trivial implications for algorithm design and theoretical analysis. The authors should discuss this choice and its impact. (Reviewer 3)
9. **Lack of real-world motivation.** The paper would benefit from concrete examples or applications where CVaR RL in low-rank MDPs is relevant, to help readers understand the significance of the setting. (Reviewer 4)

*Note on reviewer confidence:* Scores are 6 across all four reviewers, but confidence levels range from 2 (Reviewer 1) to 4 (Reviewer 4), indicating varying degrees of certainty in the assessment. The positive assessment of novelty is not unanimous: while some reviewers accept the contribution as reasonable (Reviewer 1), others find it incremental (Reviewers 2, 4), and the "first" claim is explicitly challenged (Reviewer 3).
