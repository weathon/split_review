## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically investigates the use of the \(q\)-exponential family of policy parameterizations as an alternative to the commonly used Gaussian for continuous action spaces in reinforcement learning policy optimization. The authors incorporate these policies (including heavy-tailed Student's \(t\) and \(q\)-Gaussian, and light-tailed forms) into several actor-critic algorithms and compare their performance across online (Acrobot, Cartpole) and offline (MuJoCo) benchmarks. Extensive numerical simulations are provided, showing that the Gaussian parameterization is not always optimal and that heavier-tailed distributions, such as the Student's \(t\)-distribution, often improve stability and performance. However, the paper lacks a clear explanation or conclusive experiment about which specific parameterization should be used under which conditions, and offers limited actionable guidance for practitioners.

### Strengths
- The motivation for using the \(q\)-exponential family to encourage exploration is intuitive (Reviewer 1).
- The numerical simulations are extensive and successfully demonstrate that the Gaussian policy is not always the best choice (Reviewers 1, 3).
- There is no similar empirical study in the literature; proposing alternatives to the Gaussian policy for continuous state-action spaces is interesting and useful for RL practice (Reviewer 2 – score 8, strongest positive).
- The execution of the study is solid: experiments are methodologically rigorous, figures are professional, and the presentation is pleasant (Reviewer 2). This effort is much appreciated for a practical paper.
- It is useful to have a paper that clearly states that the Gaussian parameterization might not be the best performing in practice (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- The paper does not provide a clear explanation or conclusive experiment about which parameterization should be used in which cases. The main takeaway is that the Gaussian should be avoided, but it is not clear with which distribution it should be replaced (Reviewer 3).
- It is hard to extrapolate the findings beyond the interesting but expected fact that Gaussian is not always best; the experiments cover only 4 algorithms × 3 environments, making it difficult to derive actionable practical advice about which parameterization to use in general (Reviewer 2).
- The paper could benefit from designing and/or considering simple synthetic or low-dimensional environments with different exploration requirements to clearly demonstrate the intuitive advantages and shortcomings of each policy (e.g., exploration vs. exploitation trade-off) (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Related: is it possible to design an index of exploration hardness or a heuristic to guide the choice of parameterization without myopically testing all candidates? (Reviewer 2).
- The paper does not discuss the computational overhead or sampling complexity of the more sophisticated parameterizations compared to the simple Gaussian, especially as dimensionality grows (Reviewer 2).
- The paper should also discuss and cite relevant existing work on heavy-tailed policies in RL, e.g., Bedi et al. (JMLR 2024) on the sample complexity and metastability of heavy-tailed policy search, and Chakraborty et al. (2023 IEEE ICRA) on dealing with sparse rewards via heavy-tailed policy optimization (Reviewer 2).
- Minor clarifications requested (Reviewer 2): 
  - Do all policy neural networks have a similar number of parameters/weights across experiments for fairness?
  - Are the implementations readily available in existing RL packages or libraries?
  - Is GBMM only valid for \(1 < q < 3\)?
  - l.41: what is meant by "robustness" of heavy-tailed distributions? Elaborate.
  - Clarify the difference between the data settings in Fig. 8 (Medium-Expert, Medium, Medium-Replay).
- The paper does not explore whether distributions could be switched during training to balance exploration and exploitation (Reviewer 1) or whether policies could be combined in an ensemble to get the best of each (Reviewer 2).
- Additional questions were raised about the Levy alpha-stable distribution (Simsekli et al. 2019) and about the interplay between SAC’s entropy regularization and heavy-tailed policies (Reviewer 2). These are not addressed.
- The paper does not help a practitioner understand which environmental characteristics (e.g., sparsity, reward structure) make one distribution better than another (Reviewer 3).
