## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a lightweight uncertainty estimation method using a single multi-headed neural network trained with particle-based variational inference in function space. The key design is a shared backbone with multiple prediction heads (particles) regularised by a repelling loss, combined with spectral normalization to preserve bi-Lipschitz conditions. The approach aims to reduce computational cost and memory footprint compared to full deep ensembles while maintaining competitive uncertainty quality. Experiments on active learning, OOD detection, and calibration under distribution shift (Dirty MNIST, CIFAR10/100, various OOD datasets) are reported, with metrics including accuracy, expected calibration error, and AUROC. The method is compared against deep ensembles (5 members) and DDU, and ablations on context point selection and corrupted CIFAR are provided.

### Strengths
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and has strong related work and background (reviewers 2,4,5). Prior work is properly credited and the method is contextualised in the literature (reviewer 5).
- **Efficiency and practical motivation**: Sharing a backbone across multiple heads and using particle-based diversity is a sound idea that reduces compute and memory relative to full ensembles (reviewers 1,2,3,4,5). This addresses a practical barrier to adopting uncertainty estimation methods (reviewer 4).
- **Experimental coverage and results**: The evaluation spans multiple tasks (active learning, OOD detection, calibration under shift) with appropriate metrics (accuracy, ECE, AUROC) (reviewers 2,4). The method often performs close to ensembles and outperforms DDU, coming in second place behind ensembles (reviewers 1,4). Results indicate that particle optimisation for shared-network ensembling is viable (reviewer 4). The paper also provides ablation on context point selection and corruption robustness (reviewer 4).
- **Pragmatic combination of techniques**: The combination of known components (shared backbones, spectral normalisation, particle-based function-space inference) works and achieves its goal of providing lightweight uncertainty estimates (reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution**: The method is extremely close to prior work (D'Angelo and Fortuin) and directly combines existing techniques (shared backbones, spectral normalisation from DDU) without new technical material (reviewers 1,2,3,5). The core components (particle VI, multi-head architecture) are taken from elsewhere, and the paper lacks a clear explanation of how the proposed mechanisms lead to improved UQ beyond existing methods (reviewer 2). Claims of disentangling aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty and preserving bi-Lipschitz conditions are not supported theoretically (reviewer 2).
- **Inadequate baseline comparisons**: The comparison is unfair: MH-POVI uses 20–30 particles while the ensemble uses only 5 members; a fairer approach would equalise the count or evaluate both with varying numbers (reviewer 4). Moreover, the paper lacks competitive baselines for efficient uncertainty estimation. DDU underperforms even the single net on CIFAR100 (Table 2, Fig. 4), yet alternatives such as Liu et al. (NeurIPS 2020) and Kristiadi et al. (2022) are not compared (reviewer 5). One reviewer (R4) considered the baseline selection appropriate, but others (R5) strongly disagree and list missing methods.
- **Insufficient ablations**: Key components are not ablated: the role of spectral normalisation is not empirically justified – it may not be needed when the method does not rely on distance-aware heads (reviewers 1,5). The depth/width of the ensemble heads is treated as a magic hyperparameter without sensitivity analysis (reviewer 5). The influence of context point selection and the amount of context data on performance and memory is not systematically studied (reviewer 1). The effect of varying the number of particles is not evaluated (reviewer 4). One reviewer (R4) considered the existing ablations good, but others find them insufficient.
- **Missing computational cost analysis**: Despite the emphasis on "lightweight", the paper does not provide quantitative measures (parameter count, FLOPs, memory footprint) to substantiate the claim. The trade-off between the reduced parametric complexity and the added cost of context data (semi-parametric representation) is not analysed – e.g., the memory overhead of storing context points and the Pareto front of memory vs. performance (reviewers 1,3). A breakdown of computational cost for base network, multi-head, and particle inference, and scaling with number of particles and input size, is absent (reviewer 3).
- **Lack of reproducibility and method detail**: The training procedure is unclear – it is not specified whether the model is trained end-to-end, how Eq. 2 is evaluated, or how particles are initialised (reviewer 2). The choice of context points (e.g., Gaussian noise) is not motivated with respect to the data distribution (reviewer 4). Negative data augmentation is introduced without explanation or citation (reviewer 4). The preservation of bi-Lipschitz conditions and avoidance of feature collapse are not explained (reviewer 2).
- **Benchmark task justification and interpretation**: The selection of benchmark tasks is not discussed, and their relevance to real-world uncertainty quantification is unclear (reviewer 3). Figure 3 lacks guidance on interpretation – e.g., in OOD settings aleatoric uncertainty should be low and epistemic high, while the opposite holds in-distribution (reviewer 4).
