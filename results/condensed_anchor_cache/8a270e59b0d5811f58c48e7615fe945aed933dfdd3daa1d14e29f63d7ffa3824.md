## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper builds an open-source automated pipeline using LLMs to generate and evaluate natural language explanations for sparse autoencoder (SAE) features. It introduces five new scoring techniques (detection, fuzzing, surprisal, embedding, intervention scoring) and evaluates them across different SAE sizes, activation functions, losses, and LLMs. The work also finds that SAEs trained on nearby residual stream layers are highly similar, that SAE latents are more interpretable than neurons, and that larger latent SAEs learn more dataset-specific latents. The authors propose guidelines for generating interpretations valid for broader contexts and discuss pitfalls in existing scoring.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely problem**: Automatically generating and evaluating explanations for SAE latents is critical given the large scale of modern SAEs and the need for model explainability. (R1, R3)
- **Comprehensive open-source framework**: The pipeline is a valuable tool for large-scale SAE analysis and is designed to be reusable. (R2)
- **Novel metrics provide new dimensions for evaluation**: The five proposed scoring techniques offer more ways to assess explanation quality beyond prior simulation-based methods. (R2, R3)
- **Scale improvement**: The work scales interpretation to millions of features, improving over previous state-of-the-art. (R4)
- **Interesting and actionable findings**: 
  - Larger latent SAEs learn more dataset-specific latents. (R2)
  - The sampling approach used to generate explanations affects scores; showing examples to the explainer can inflate scores, highlighting a problem with current auto-interpretability evaluations. (R3)
  - Results suggest a priority for training wider SAEs on a smaller subset of residual stream layers to be efficient under compute constraints. (R1, R3)
  - High correlations between latents at adjacent layers are observed. (R2)
- **Well-written with clear ablations** (R1); though other reviewers (R3, R4) strongly disagree on presentation clarity (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient validation of new scoring metrics**:
  - Low correlations between the different evaluation methods (Table 1) are concerning; new methods should show strong rank correlation (>0.7) with the established simulation method to prove they work. Authors should run more rigorous tests, possibly using ground-truth explanations (e.g., SAE latents with known explanations from prior work) and reporting rank correlations. (R1)
  - The paper does not compare the new metrics to the previous evaluation metric from OpenAI’s “Language Models Can Explain Neurons in Language Models” in a solid, quantitative form; having similar conclusions is not sufficient evidence that the new metrics are as good. (R4)
  - The claim that new methods are more efficient is not quantified; runtime comparisons should be provided. (R1, R4)
  - The effectiveness of automatic evaluations in reflecting true explanation quality remains unclear; a human evaluation (even small-scale) is needed to validate the metrics. (R3)
- **Missing comparisons and relevant prior work**:
  - Missing relevant work: “Explaining black box text modules in natural language with language models”; how does its scoring compare to methods proposed here? (R1)
  - Prior work (Transformer Circuits / “Monosemantic Features”) already showed SAEs have more interpretable features than neurons; the purpose of validating this result again is unclear. (R4)
- **Adjacent-layer comparison lacks motivation and support**:
  - The motivation for comparing SAEs in adjacent layers is missing; why is this interesting or novel? (R4)
  - The connection between this comparison and the paper’s main contribution (evaluation metrics) is unclear. (R4)
  - Figure 5 shows statistical alignment across layers, not semantic similarity; the authors should compute explanation similarity (e.g., via explanation text) to support the claim. (R1)
  - No prior work supports the method for evaluating SAEs in adjacent layers. (R4)
  - The claim that “if the explanation for latent α at layer j is very different from the same latent at layer j+1, this would suggest inconsistency and noise” needs elaboration. (R3)
- **Poor presentation and clarity** (disagreement: R1 found the paper well-written, but R3 and R4 strongly critique clarity):
  - Readers unfamiliar with SAEs will find the method hard to follow; the relation between Sections 3.1 and 3.2 is unclear. A figure showing the full pipeline would help. (R2)
  - The paper lacks a simple example of explaining a latent and walking through the entire workflow. (R2)
  - The contribution appears concentrated on the new evaluation metric, but Section 5 abruptly discusses behaviors of SAEs; the paper should be reorganized around a single claim with supporting evidence. (R4)
  - Many observations are presented without in-depth analysis (e.g., impact of context length and latent space size, different sampling strategies). (R3)
  - The rationale behind key parameter choices (context length 256 tokens, activating examples of only 32 tokens) is not adequately justified; these choices seem contradictory given the claim that short contexts hinder identification of complex latents. (R3)
  - The statement “The imperfect correlations hint at either shortcomings of the scoring metrics or the fact that these metrics can measure different qualities of explanations” lacks clarity; the paper should explain what specific qualities each metric measures. (R3)
  - Inconsistency: abstract says “five new techniques”, conclusion says “four new scoring techniques”. (R1)
- **Reproducibility**: Code is not provided as a supplementary file; only a plan to open-source exists. (R1)
- **Sensitivity to prompts and explainer models**: The paper does not address potential biases introduced by specific prompts and limitations of chosen explainer models; how sensitive are explanations to prompt design? Are there inherent limitations in the explainer models? (R3)
- **Minor formatting issues**: No line numbers; header shows “Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2024” instead of 2025. (R1)
- **Unclear results**:
  - Negative correlation between fuzzing score and intervention score in Figure 4 needs explanation; if both are useful, why negative? (R1)
  - Differences between “randomly sampling” and “uniformly sampling” are noted but not explained. (R3)
