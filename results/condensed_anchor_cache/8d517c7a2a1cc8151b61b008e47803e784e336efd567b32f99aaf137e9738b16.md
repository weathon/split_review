## Merged Review

### Summary

Four reviewers rated the paper 8/8/8/8. They agree on the main contributions: Prospect is a novel stochastic algorithm for distributionally robust optimization (DRO) with spectral risk measures and \(f\)-divergence penalty, using bias and variance reduction to achieve linear convergence for smooth regularized losses, requiring only a single learning-rate hyperparameter. Experiments on tabular, vision, and language benchmarks demonstrate 2–3× speedup over baselines (e.g., SGD, stochastic saddle-point). Reviewers describe the theory as sound and the empirical study as comprehensive, but raise concerns about clarity, the restrictiveness of certain assumptions, baseline selection, computational cost, and missing discussions.

### Strengths

- **Originality & novelty:** The paper develops a new iterative algorithm for DRO with spectral risk measures, which appears to be the first to combine spectral risk with \(f\)-divergence in this manner (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The strong duality result (Proposition 3) for this setting is considered new (Reviewer 2).
- **Theoretical contributions:** The algorithm (Prospect) is justified with convergence guarantees (Theorem 1) under reasonable regularity conditions (positive regularization \(\mu>0\)). The analysis covers bias and variance reduction, and the authors discuss cases where assumptions are violated, arguing the algorithm still converges (Reviewers 1, 2). The proof is considered sound with nice properties in Appendix B (Reviewer 2).
- **Significance:** The work expands the class of DRO problems solvable with iterative algorithms, addressing a key limitation of scale in DRO (Reviewer 3).
- **Empirical evaluation:** Comprehensive experiments cover three important problems (classification and regression) and are run to convergence; hyperparameter selection is fair across methods (Reviewers 1, 2). The 2–3× speedup is demonstrated convincingly (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Ease of use:** The algorithm is easy to understand and implement, with intuition provided via texts and figures (Reviewer 4). It requires tuning only one hyperparameter (Reviewers 1, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and presentation:** Several key concepts are not adequately explained. CVaR, 0.5-CVaR, 2-extremile, and 1-ESRM are not formally defined (Reviewer 1). The notion of “spectral risk-based” needs a much clearer introduction (Reviewers 1, 2). Figure 6 is unclear and figure captions could be more descriptive (Reviewer 1). The abstract and introduction are judged as overly jargony (Reviewer 1). In the appendix (pages 28–32, 35–37, 39–40), some equations are highlighted in color but without explanation of their meaning (Reviewer 2). In contrast, Reviewers 3 and 4 found the paper well-presented, so there is disagreement on clarity.
- **Motivation for spectral risk measure:** The paper uses \(\mathcal{P} = \text{ConvexHull}(\text{Permutations of }(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n))\) but provides insufficient justification for why such an ambiguity set is practically relevant or what scenarios it targets (Reviewer 2). Similarly, the connection to DRO is limited; the ambiguity set in Eq. (2) is the entire space of distributions, which is very large and not directly useful—the penalty term likely shapes a tighter set, but this is not discussed (Reviewer 3).
- **Restrictive theoretical assumptions:** The strong duality result (Proposition 3) assumes the conjugate function \(f^*\) satisfies \(|f^*(y)|<\infty\) for all \(y\in\mathbb{R}\); standard \(f\)-divergence DRO with a simplex (Shapiro, 2017) does not need this condition, and the authors should justify why it is needed (Reviewer 2). Proposition 4 assumes strong convexity \(\alpha_n\) of \(f\); can this be relaxed to strict convexity? (Reviewer 2). Proposition 5 lacks a complete proof, and Eq. (15) implicitly assumes \(f^*\) is differentiable—is this assumption made or derived? (Reviewer 2). Also, theoretical results are provided only for \(\mu>0\); it is unclear if this is necessary or merely for convenience, and numerical examples with \(\mu=0\) would help clarify whether the objective induces smoothness on its own (Reviewer 4).
- **Computational cost:** Each iteration of Algorithm 1 requires solving an optimization problem with \(n\) variables (line 10), which is expensive for large \(n\). It would be useful to know if this step can be replaced by a one-step stochastic gradient update, e.g., as in two-time-scale methods in distributionally robust RL (Wang et al., 2023) (Reviewer 4). General scalability (problem size and runtime) is not reported (Reviewer 3).
- **Baselines:** The set of baselines is considered too restrictive. Since some experimental problems violate Prospect’s theoretical assumptions, comparison to algorithms without those guarantees (e.g., Adam, SGD with momentum) would be fairer (Reviewer 1). The advantage of tuning only one hyperparameter is downplayed by noting that many practical algorithms have good default hyperparameter values (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited coverage of \(f\)-divergences:** The experiments test only three types of divergences, while \(f\)-divergences are broad (e.g., KL-divergence is not included). The authors should discuss other popular divergences and compare their algorithm to existing DRO approaches for those cases (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing empirical details:** It is not reported how large the problems are or how long the algorithm takes to run (Reviewer 3). It is also not tested whether the algorithm works with a projection step for constrained optimization (Reviewer 3). An investigation of unbiased estimation alternatives (e.g., Wang et al., 2023) is suggested (Reviewer 4).
- **Miscellaneous:** The paper (including appendix) is long, raising the question of suitability for a conference format (Reviewer 4). The reference to Shapiro (2017) is missing (Reviewer 2).
