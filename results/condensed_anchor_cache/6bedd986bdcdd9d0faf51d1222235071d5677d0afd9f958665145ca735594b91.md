## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses whether knowledge editing methods can truly correct hallucinations in LLMs, arguing that existing evaluation datasets fail to verify that LLMs actually hallucinate before editing. The authors propose HalluEditBench, a benchmark that constructs a dataset of over 6,000 verified hallucinations across 9 domains and 26 topics from three LLMs (Llama2-7B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-7B) and evaluates seven knowledge editing methods on five dimensions: Efficacy, Generalization, Portability, Locality, and Robustness. Results show that no method consistently corrects hallucinations across models and domains, and that current editing techniques have significant limitations. The reviewers generally acknowledge the benchmark’s contribution and the importance of the question, but express concerns about dataset construction details, depth of analysis, missing formulas, potential data leakage from GPT-4o generation, and the benchmark’s long-term practicality.

### Strengths
- Foundational contribution: poses the critical, overlooked question “Can knowledge editing really correct hallucinations?” (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- New benchmark HalluEditBench with 6,000+ verified hallucinations across 9 domains and 26 topics, ensuring LLMs actually hallucinated before editing (R1, R3, R4).
- Five evaluation dimensions (Efficacy, Generalization, Portability, Locality, Robustness) are a significant innovation that extends beyond conventional measures and could become standard practice (R1, R4).
- Novel, impactful insights: current editing assessment is unreliable; hallucination manifestation depends on question design; portability scores are not improved by editing (suggesting LLMs do not reason with edited knowledge in multi-hop questions); efficacy does not correlate with locality; parameter-preserving methods (ICE, GRACE) have low robustness (R3).
- Results convincingly show that effectiveness varies greatly by domain and model, and none of the tested techniques is a satisfactory solution across all aspects (R2).
- Well-designed, substantial set of experiments that effectively validate the proposed metrics (R1). Results are clearly presented and analyzed throughout (R2). Paper is well-organized and readable (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset construction lacks sufficient detail**: criteria for selecting domains and topics are not provided; entity popularity distribution (e.g., long-tail vs. popular entities) is not analyzed (R1). The paper should clarify whether the dataset primarily comes from Wikipedia or Wikidata, and report the version of the Wikidata dump used (R1).
- **Temporal mismatch not quantified**: Wikipedia/Wikidata are frequently updated, and some LLMs may have been trained on earlier versions. The extent of outdated/inconsistent information across LLMs is not estimated or discussed (R1).
- **Benchmark practicality limited**: the benchmark only evaluates specific models; when models are updated or replaced, the data must be reconstructed. This is a major flaw that may make the benchmark impractical (R4). One reviewer (R1) similarly notes that focusing on the dataset construction pipeline might hold greater long-term value than the static dataset.
- **GPT-4o generation of question-answer pairs not validated**: the prompt is documented in the appendix, but there is no mention of manual checks for correctness or discussion of whether GPT-4o could itself hallucinate or produce incorrect results (R2).
- **Missing example showing why existing benchmarks fail**: the paper should provide at least one example or statistics illustrating the shortcomings of other benchmarks in verifying pre-edit hallucinations, to underscore the innovation of HalluEditBench (R3).
- **Insufficient depth of analysis**: the paper summarizes experimental results but lacks speculation on underlying causes. Specific examples: why FT-M performs differently on two datasets (R3), why GRACE’s generalization score drops significantly (R3), why locality scores are satisfactory only for FT-M and ICE, and why ICE and GRACE have low robustness (R4).
- **Formulas missing for the five scores**: evaluation metrics are described only textually, making them difficult to understand (R4). Also, it is unclear whether all five scores are newly proposed; references to prior evaluation work are missing (R4, Q3).
- **Title question not directly answered in conclusion**: the paper does not clearly address “Can knowledge editing really correct hallucinations?” based on its results, and lacks a forward-looking outlook (R2).
- **Related work section feels like an appendix**: could be moved earlier to improve flow; Figure 2 (dataset statistics) could be moved to the appendix as it contains fine-grained details not critical for understanding (R2).
- **Minor issues**: typos: “overfiting” (p4, §2.3), “an knowledge editing operation” should be “a knowledge editing operation” (p132); MIT license file not properly anonymized (R2). Citation formatting errors: \citet should be used instead of \citep for sentences including authors (e.g., Line 52, Line 148) (R4). For FT-L, both Meng et al., 2022 and the original work (Zhu et al., 2020) should be cited (R1).
- **Missing comparisons and ablations**: including at least one GPT model would strengthen comparability with original studies of editing methods (R1). GRACE’s generalization score is less than 0 for some types (e.g., non-mc for Llama2-7B, Figure 4) — this needs explanation (R4). ICE and GRACE’s accuracy (e.g., in Table 1) is not shown, making it unclear how effective their edits are (R4).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 8) was notably more positive, praising the sobering insights and clear presentation, while three reviewers (scores 5, 5, 6) raised more critical concerns regarding dataset construction, analysis depth, and benchmark practicality. This contrast should be considered when interpreting the strengths and weaknesses.
