## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes RA-LLM to defend against jailbreak attacks by randomly dropping tokens from the prompt and checking whether the aligned LLM rejects the request, using multiple inferences. Experiments on Vicuna-7B and Guanaco-7B show attack success rates drop from nearly 100% to about 10%. While the topic is important and the method is intuitively reasonable and easy to implement, reviewers raise concerns about limited evaluation (only one dataset, small models, no proprietary models), missing baseline comparisons (perplexity-based defense, self-evaluation baseline, concurrent works), insufficient handling of computational cost and time overhead, robustness of the alignment-checking approximation, lack of adaptive attack consideration, and potential false positive errors.

### Strengths
1. Defending against alignment-breaking attacks in LLMs is an important and timely research direction.
2. The underlying principle is intuitively clear: strategically dropping tokens can neutralize adversarial prefixes and reduce attack effectiveness.
3. Reported experimental results show substantial robustness on Vicuna-7B and Guanaco-7B, reducing attack success rates from nearly 100% to about 10% or less.
4. The method is easy to implement and does not require retraining or fine-tuning the original LLM.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited experimental scope.** Only open-source models (Vicuna-7B, Guanaco-7B) are tested; evaluations on proprietary models like GPT-3.5/4 are missing but necessary to demonstrate generalizability. Only one dataset is used for ASR and BAR evaluation; dataset sizes are small. The experiments are presented in only two small tables.
2. **Missing baseline comparisons.** The method is not compared with a perplexity-based defense [2], which has shown strong robustness. Also missing is a highly related baseline that detects harmfulness based on the model’s own output (self-evaluation) [1], which may require less computational cost. Concurrent work on prompt erasing as a defense [1] is acknowledged but not compared or deeply analyzed.
3. **Computational cost is inadequately evaluated.** The paper focuses on financial cost rather than time overhead; time overhead is paramount for practicality. The cost calculation is not convincing: the method requires roughly n*(1-p) = 20*(1-0.3) = 14 times the input cost (token length), but this factor is not revealed or discussed. A full runtime comparison against baseline inference is needed.
4. **Robustness of the alignment-checking function is questionable.** The claim that such alignment checking is “not robust” (page 4) is not well-supported; the relationship with adversarial prompts [2] is unclear. The approximation of AC(·) by inspecting only a pre-collected prefix set (e.g., “I cannot”, “I’m sorry”) is not robust—prefixes vary across models (e.g., “as a helpful and harmless chatbot…”), and enumerating all possible prefixes is impractical.
5. **No adaptive attack evaluation.** The paper does not consider an adaptive adversary who knows the defense mechanism, which is a critical missing scenario for security claims.
6. **Potential false positive errors.** It is unclear whether the method incorrectly flags benign prompts as harmful, and the false positive rate is not evaluated.
7. **Dataset and model variety.** Having only one dataset and two relatively small models limits the strength of the conclusions. Larger and more diverse benchmarks are needed.
