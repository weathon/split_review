## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Double-Codebook Speaker-invariant Clustering (DC-Spin) and SpinHuBERT for speech tokenization in spoken language models (SLMs). DC-Spin uses dual codebooks to separate phonetic and speaker-invariant features, aiming to produce tokens robust to input variations and rich in phonetic content. Evaluations compare against self-supervised and neural audio codec tokenizers across zero-shot and supervised tasks, speech resynthesis, robustness, and inference efficiency. The study also analyzes proxy metrics that correlate with downstream SLM performance.

### Strengths
- Introduces Double-Codebook Spin to capture fine-grained phonetic units, with detail on codebook size selection (R1).
- Thorough experimental setup covering zero-shot, supervised tasks, robustness, and efficiency (R1; echoed by R2: comprehensive evaluation across multiple downstream tasks and metrics).
- Analyzes multiple proxy tasks for speech tokenizers, revealing relationship with SLM performance (R1; echoed by R4: identifies decent proxy candidates).
- Originality: SpinHuBERT and DC-Spin tokenizers demonstrate originality (R2); novel contribution to speech tokenization, which is widely used (R4).
- Clear presentation enables easy re-implementation and use (R2, R4).
- Significance: Disentangling factors within discrete speech tokens addresses a growing area (e.g., FACodec); DC-Spin appears to be one of the first tokenizers to separate speaker information from codebooks (R2).
- Proposed method shows strong performance over baselines across tasks (R3, R4; though margins noted as small by R4).
- Tokens generated are speaker-invariant and preserve more content information compared to open-source tokenizers (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Contributions incremental and advantages marginal**: DC-Spin does not show convincing advantages over Spin (Table 2) despite much larger codebook (R1). Margins over baselines are small while the method is significantly more complex (R4). Chunk-wise streaming simulation is not novel; it has been used in ASR/TTS, and the paper only gives a short paragraph about it (R1).
- **Outdated or missing baselines**: Compared neural audio codecs (Encodec, SpeechTokenizer) are dated; newer powerful codecs like DAC, Hifi-Codec, and WavTokenizer should be included (R1).
- **Unclear motivation**: Claim that auxiliary codebook captures fine-grained phonetic units is not explained; if both codebooks focus on phonetics, acoustic detail is ignored, harming generative tasks (R1). Motivation for discretization is unconvincing: continuous representations can connect speech encoder to LM without quality loss from discretization (R3, citing recent papers). The TTS community already targets voice clone and high naturalness; removing speaker information may hinder usage (R3). ASR-WER of resynthesized speech (~18.x) is far below standard (R3, citing papers).
- **Limited evaluation scope**: No direct evaluation of generating coherent, intelligible speech via a SpeechLM – only proxy tasks (R4). UTMOS is used instead of human raters for subjective evaluation; human MOS should be included (R4). Evaluation only with a 150M parameter model; full-sized SpeechLM evaluation is missing (R4). Speaker invariance claim is not verified experimentally (R4). ASR-WER is very high (Tables 4 and 8) and insufficiently explained; the use of an expressive dataset complicates interpretation (R4).
- **Speech resynthesis comparison limited to low bitrates**: Only <1.5 kbps results are shown, but state-of-the-art synthesis systems (NaturalSpeech2, VoiceCraft, MaskGCT) use higher-bitrate codecs; comparison at higher bitrates would be valuable (R2).
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms**:
  - Ablation on independent use of each codebook (primary vs. auxiliary) is needed to validate their roles (R2). 
  - Robustness testing lacks quantitative details on perturbation types/levels (noise, time stretch, reverberation, pitch shift) and how they compare to real-world conditions (R2).
  - Can DC-Spin adapt to higher bitrates? If so, performance comparison with systems like NaturalSpeech2 on MUSHRA scores is needed (R2).
  - How does the method handle speaking style variations, which are also speaker-variant (beyond F0/formant scaling)? (R3).
  - Explanation needed: in Figure 2, how is the codebook formed and discrete tokens generated? Is K-means still used? (R3).
  - Why use HuBert as the base encoder instead of another? What properties make it best? (R4).
  - Why fine-tune from pre-trained HuBert rather than train from scratch with the SPIN objective? (R4).
  - How to pick the final model when different codebook sizes give best performance for different tasks (e.g., TSC best with 1000-size codebook)? (R4).
- **Minority disagreements**: Reviewer 3 (score 1) is strongly negative, arguing the discretization degrades input quality and that the ASR-WER is unusably high, while Reviewer 2 (score 8) is strongly positive, praising originality and significance. The other reviewers (scores 5,5) are moderately positive but note limitations.
