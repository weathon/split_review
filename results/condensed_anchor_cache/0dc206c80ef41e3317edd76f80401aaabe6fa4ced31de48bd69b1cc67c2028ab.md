## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a unified metric, "Model Performance and Goodness" (MPG), to aggregate LLM performance across benchmarks into two numbers: "Goodness" (answer accuracy, inferred via a hierarchical Bayesian model) and "Fastness" (queries per second, QPS). The authors evaluate 13 closed- and open-source models, define a Pareto frontier between the two components, and compare MPG's correlation to LMSys and MMLU. The work targets resource-constrained users (small companies, university labs) who need a single actionable number for model selection.

### Strengths

- The paper tackles an important lacuna in LLM research: accuracy is being prioritised over efficiency, and a measurement framework that balances both can incentivise efficient architectures.
- The target audience (resource-constrained users) is well-defined, and the practical motivation for a single aggregated metric is clearly stated.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (minority reviewer 1, rating 3, confidence 3; other reviewers rated 1 and found presentation poor).
- The authors cover a number of state-of-the-art LLMs, including closed- and open-source models, which is commendable.
- The work situates itself within the context of other approaches (e.g., LMSys, MMLU) and includes comparisons; the authors report good correlations with LMSys, improving upon the correlation of the MMLU leaderboard to LMSys (reviewer 1 notes this as a strength, but also notes a weakness below).
- If done right, this could be a helpful resource for smaller organisations and rapid internal testing.

### Weaknesses

- **Presentation and coherence**: The paper contains numerous spelling errors, syntactic disfluencies, and semantic ambiguities that make it difficult to follow. The conclusion suddenly refers to "IQ" instead of MPG, suggesting a rushed submission or an older version; the first paragraph of the conclusion appears to be from an earlier version, and the second paragraph repeats what was said before. The y-axis of Figure 1 is labelled "IQ", which seems a relict of an older version. The paper has insufficient content for an ICLR venue: all of page 6 is a heatmap and a table, and the paper would be better suited to a workshop.
- **Conceptual inconsistency**: The starting point (need for a unified score) and the solution (MPG) are not consistent. If a developer wants to compare in-house models, they could simply use offline benchmarks strictly related to the metrics they care about; why would they need a unified score that may include unrelated metrics (e.g., social sensitivity)? Similarly, for publicly available models one can simply use LMSys ratings—why need MPG? Table 1 shows that MMLU has a better Rank Pearson correlation to lmsys_rating than MPG in some cases, casting doubt on MPG's usefulness.
- **QPS / Fastness measurement is problematic**: QPS is defined as the average response rate from timing every prompt sent to external servers. This is incredibly noisy: it depends on the authors’ connection speeds, server traffic, rate limits on proprietary APIs, and load differences between API endpoints and RunPod instances of open-source models. The numbers are unlikely to be reproducible or commensurable across models and APIs. QPS is also subject to change for APIs and varies hugely across quantizations for open-source models. The paper does not normalise QPS by number of tokens in the query, nor does it specify whether arithmetic or geometric mean is used (geometric is recommended for rates). In any case, QPS is not novel, and it is unclear why it is given prominence even in the abstract. The paper also ends by largely ignoring the Fastness component and focusing on the latent capability measure, leaving the Pareto frontier and the question of how to balance Goodness and Fastness entirely unaddressed.
- **Lack of novelty and missing related work**: The latent capability estimation methodology is not novel. There is an extensive literature on psychometrics (Item Response Theory, Bayesian Structural Equation Models) for inferring latent abilities, and these methods have been applied to LLM evaluation (e.g., Burden et al. 2023, Kipnis et al. 2024, Polo et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2023). The introduction misses key related papers: tinybenchmarks (arxiv:2402.14992) and metabench (arxiv:2407.12844)—the latter is a very similar effort measuring abilities via a single number over many more LLMs and from a more principled foundation. The paper also includes pseudo-scientific claims about MPG not being a good measure of gas consumption (cites two references on MPG in fuel economy, which is irrelevant to the AI metric).
- **Methodological issues**: The capability hierarchy in Figure 2 is assumed (not estimated or derived), imposing structure from the beginning when it could have been estimated. The authors note that factuality and problem solving benchmarks formed a cluster, which further undercuts the claimed hierarchy. The methodology for latent capability estimation is difficult to follow: it is unclear whether this is a hierarchical Bayesian network, a structural equation model with priors, or something else. The statement "we propose a form of a Monte-Carlo Markov chain (MCMC) to simulate latent questions from the aggregate beta distributions" is confusing—what are "latent questions"? The approach lacks solid theoretical motivation and appears ad-hoc.
- **Empirical rigor is weak**: The paper provides no accompanying code, no open-source datasets, and no thorough explanation of the measurement's validity. The main result is the correlation in Figure 3 (which is vague and unclear—it likely shows Pearson's r but is not labelled as such). Some of the rank correlations are not particularly high. The experimental methodology seems merely applied to several well-known LLMs without proper justification. The paper does not convincingly demonstrate that MPG is a useful or reliable metric.
- **Minority reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (reviewer 1) gave a rating of 3 and found the paper well-written and the correlations a strength, while the other three reviewers gave a rating of 1 and heavily criticised presentation, novelty, and rigor. This contrast should be noted: the paper's merits are not uniformly perceived.
