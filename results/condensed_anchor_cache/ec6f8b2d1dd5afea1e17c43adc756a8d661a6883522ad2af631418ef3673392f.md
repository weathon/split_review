## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DiffDiv, an ensemble diversification framework that uses Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DPMs) to generate synthetic counterfactuals with novel feature combinations, even when the original training data exhibits correlated features. These generated samples are used to train ensemble classifiers, aiming to reduce dependence on shortcut cues and improve OOD generalization. The paper includes analysis of the trade-off between sample fidelity and diversity, and evaluates the method on several datasets (ColorDSprites, UTKFace, CelebA). Reviewers generally find the idea promising but raise significant concerns about experimental validation, missing comparisons, lack of details on the DPM and training integration, impractical manual tuning, and unaddressed drops in majority-class accuracy.

### Strengths
- The core idea of using DPMs to generate counterfactuals with novel feature combinations for mitigating shortcut learning is promising and sensible.
- The analysis of how DPM sample fidelity relates to OODness/diversity is thorough and insightful.
- The paper shows originality in applying DPMs for ensemble diversification without relying on labeled out-of-distribution data or explicit feature labels.
- The approach is straightforward and leverages the natural capabilities of modern DPMs to produce novel feature combinations.
- Writing is clear, well-cited, and easy to follow for several reviewers.
- The use of challenging datasets (ColorDSprites, UTKFace, CelebA) demonstrates practical relevance.
- Reviewer 4 (score 6, highest) finds the method particularly innovative and of significant practical value for dealing with entangled factors—a more positive assessment than the other reviewers, who scored 3, 5, and 5.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient evidence that ensemble disagreement mitigates shortcut learning.** The only evidence provided is ensemble disagreement itself; there is no direct evaluation using worst-class accuracy or minority-group performance on standard shortcut benchmarks like Waterbirds or CelebA (as done in Lee et al., 2022). It remains unclear why disagreement alone suffices to remove shortcut reliance.
2. **Large drop in majority-class accuracy, especially on ColorDSprites.** Table 1 shows the baseline achieves 100% accuracy but DiffDiv drops to 70–90% depending on objective and generation epoch. The paper does not adequately justify why this trade-off is acceptable or how it reflects learning more robust features rather than losing predictive power.
3. **Missing/cursory comparisons to competing methods.** The paper lacks comparisons with state-of-the-art methods, including those that use auxiliary OOD data, generative approaches (e.g., GANs, optimal transport), or alternative data-generation pipelines (e.g., ALIA, which uses LLM-captioned prompts for text-to-image generation without training a DPM). Also absent is a comparison with standard augmentations or pre-trained diffusion models that edit training images. Without such baselines, it is impossible to assess the relative merit of DiffDiv.
4. **Insufficient details on the DPM and training integration (reproducibility concern).** The diffusion model architecture, noise schedule, number of denoising steps, and precise mechanism for incorporating synthetic data into ensemble training are not specified. The paper only provides visualization results, which is insufficient for reproducibility. Similarly, the rationale for the specific fractions of synthetic data used in Table 1 is unclear.
5. **Manual tuning required for each dataset, limiting practicality.** The choice of the diversification hyperparameter (gamma) and the DPM training epoch used for generation (which controls fidelity/diversity) must be tuned per dataset. For example, maximum diversity occurs at epoch 20 for ColorDSprites but at 800 for UTKFace and 1000 for CelebA. The paper lacks a systematic or automated strategy for these selections, making application to new domains cumbersome.
6. **Finding the optimal DPM generation epoch is unstable and ambiguous.** The “originative” interval can yield samples where the class is ambiguous (e.g., Fig 3). Although the supplementary mentions using validation loss as a stopping cue, this is not employed as the main measure, and Fig 6 is difficult to interpret, with three datasets showing very different behaviors.
7. **Novelty concerns.** The method relies on an observed phenomenon of DPMs (generating OOD feature combinations) without theoretical analysis or a novel mechanism to enhance counterfactual generation. Developing specific regularizers for DPMs or providing theoretical grounding for why early-stopped DPMs produce useful OOD samples would strengthen the contribution.
8. **Lack of discussion on DPM advantages for feature disentanglement and generation flexibility.** The paper could benefit from explaining how DPMs handle feature disentanglement compared to other generative models, and whether the DiffDiv framework could extend to non-visual domains (e.g., text, structured data).
9. **Potential cost of training a DPM.** For larger datasets, training a DPM from scratch may be expensive; cheaper alternatives exist (e.g., ALIA, standard augmentations) and should be compared.
10. **(Minor) Unclear description of L1/L2 baseline objectives.** Line 242–243 describes these objectives as maximizing pairwise distance between any two model outputs, but the loss formulation only measures the average of the ensemble prediction—this mismatch should be clarified.
11. **Presentation issues.** Table 1 is hard to follow (fractions not explained, ablation results not separated clearly). An overview figure of the DiffDiv framework is absent, making it difficult for readers to grasp the complete pipeline at a glance.
