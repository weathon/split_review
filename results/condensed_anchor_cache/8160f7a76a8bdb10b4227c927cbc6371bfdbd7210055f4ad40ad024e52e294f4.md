## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper revisits design choices (architecture, context length) in sequence modeling for offline RL under the max-return paradigm, which replaces manual target returns with maximized returns predicted by the model. It evaluates three architectures (Reinformer, Reinconver, Reimba) and nine datasets (beyond standard Gym, including Maze2d, etc.), systematically analyzing performance across varying data distributions. The key finding is that dataset characteristics heavily influence performance, overshadowing architectural differences, and that conclusions from limited evaluation (e.g., only Gym) can be biased. Reviewers are split: some (R1, R3) rate the paper as interesting and comprehensive (score 5), while others (R2, R4) find it lacks novelty and depth (score 3). All agree the investigation is broad and well-written, but criticism focuses on insufficient mechanistic insight, reproducibility gaps, and reliance on existing observations.

### Strengths
- Systematic and wide evaluation across 9 continuous control offline RL datasets, 3 architectures (Reinformer, Reinconver, Reimba), and multiple context lengths (K=2,5,10,20). This is a notable improvement over prior work limited to simpler datasets, and highlights how data distribution shapes performance.
- The max-return framework effectively addresses the limitation of manually specified target returns, enabling evaluation on more diverse datasets.
- The paper questions past claims (e.g., sequence modeling superiority) and shows no single method consistently wins, which is helpful for the field.
- Detailed insights into context length effects: e.g., shorter context aids trajectory stitching; models trained on long sequences can perform well at inference with short sequences via masking.
- The analysis of architecture and context length provides practical guidelines for practitioners (e.g., when sequence modeling vs. classical RL like IQL may be preferable).
- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and clearly explains prior work.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of depth and mechanistic understanding** (R1, R2, R4): The analysis scratches the surface without deeply investigating *why* certain architectures or context lengths behave differently. R1 suggests focusing on one aspect (e.g., dataset quality) and intervening causally (e.g., corrupting data, adding distractors, testing hypotheses about waypoint information in Maze2d-large via targeted experiments). The speculation that “Reinformer pays more attention to global information” (Figure 2b) is not causally supported.
- **Limited novelty** (R2, R4): The insights are incremental. For example, it was already known that classical RL is better at trajectory stitching compared to sequence modeling. The paper re-evaluates existing methods without introducing significant new algorithms.
- **Reproducibility concerns** (R4): Code, model checkpoints, data preprocessing scripts, and hyperparameter guidelines are not provided, hindering replication and practical adoption.
- **Dataset analysis could be deeper** (R1, R4): The paper does not explore specific dataset factors (e.g., trajectory length distribution, reward sparsity, state/action dimensionality) that might guide architecture or context length choices. R1 notes that “data quality affects models” is obvious; the paper should ask what specifically in the data impacts performance.
- **Methodological issues in analysis** (R3, R1): 
  - Table 4 models a linear relationship between performance and context length K, but K is limited to {2,5,10,20} – a small, discrete set that may not support linearity. Visualizing this relationship or using a different model (e.g., polynomial regression) is recommended.
  - In Section 5.2.1, the “0” token used for masking may not appear during training; R1 asks why not compute the Jacobian instead.
  - The x-axis appears inverted in Figures 3, 4, and 6b relative to Table 2 (e.g., Reinformer superior on Maze2d-large).
- **Contradictory statements** (R4): Line 067 says “The impact of context length on performance is relatively minor,” but the next sentences argue that shorter context is better for trajectory stitching and that inference with short sequences significantly enhances stitching.
- **Speculation without evidence** (R1): E.g., the claim that “Reinformer’s focus on global information is adept at using waypoint-related information” is unsupported speculation; a better experiment would directly intervene (e.g., give waypoint information).
- **Overly general conclusion** (R4): The paper does not discuss whether the observed effects generalize to other RL tasks/domains beyond the studied datasets.
- **Minor writing issues** (R3, R2, R4): Inconsistent dataset abbreviations (Table 1 vs. manuscript), missing citation for IQL baseline (line 59 is incomplete), missing figure number (line 436), word missing (line 59), awkward phrasing (line 67), formatting nit (bold numbers line 186). “Quite” (line 62) should be removed.
- **Unaddressed questions** from reviewers that overlap with criticisms: 
  - How can classical RL and sequence modeling be integrated concretely? (R2)
  - What types of positional embeddings were used (absolute/cosine?) and how might they hinder trajectory stitching? (R2)
  - What are the computational resource implications compared to traditional RL? (R3)
  - What dataset characteristics (e.g., trajectory length, state/action dimensionality, reward sparsity) correlate with optimal context lengths? (R4)
