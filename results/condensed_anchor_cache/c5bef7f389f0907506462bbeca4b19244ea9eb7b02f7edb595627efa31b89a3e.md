## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a personalized Deep Kernel Gaussian Process (DKGP) framework for forecasting longitudinal brain volume changes. It integrates a population model (p‑DKGP) that captures cohort‑level trends and a subject‑specific model (ss‑DKGP) that tailors predictions to individual trajectories. The two predictive distributions are combined via Adaptive Posterior Shrinkage Estimation, which learns a parameter α to balance population‑level and individual‑specific information. The method is evaluated on two training datasets (synthetic? unclear) and validated on three external neuroimaging studies, showing superior performance compared to statistical models (LME, GAM) and traditional ML methods. The work is well‑written, and the results are generally convincing.

### Strengths

- The paper is well‑written, clearly organized, and easy to follow (R1, R4).
- The motivation to develop a precise predictive tool for diagnosing Alzheimer’s disease has strong clinical value (R1).
- The combination of a population model and a subject‑specific model via adaptive shrinkage is a valuable contribution to longitudinal brain trajectory modeling (R1, R3). However, one reviewer (R3) found limited novelty, citing prior work (Eleftheriadis et al., 2017) and noting that learning the shrinkage weight with XGBoost is common practice; this reviewer gave a contribution score 2 (vs. 3 from others) and suggested a Bayesian Neural Network as a more principled alternative. Another reviewer (R4) similarly noted that personalized GPs and weighting schemes have been proposed before but found the adaptive shrinkage interesting, albeit a small contribution.
- The method demonstrates superior predictive performance over several baselines, including LME, GAM, and other deep learning models (R1). One reviewer (R3) noted that only traditional baseline models were compared and recommended comparisons with more advanced deep learning techniques.
- Validation on three external neuroimaging studies confirms generalizability across different clinical contexts (R1, R2, R4). This external evaluation strengthens confidence in the results (R4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty and positioning**
   -Several reviewers questioned the degree of novelty. The idea of personalizing GPs with population and individual components has been proposed earlier (Eleftheriadis et al., 2017) (R3). The use of XGBoost to learn the shrinkage parameter α is a common engineering practice, not a novel machine‑learning method (R3). A Bayesian Neural Network (Blundell et al., 2015) would provide a more principled approach for weight determination (R3). The paper does not provide a comprehensive discussion of the wider disease‑progression modeling literature (R2). References are relatively old; a literature review of the past two years is needed (R2).
   -The separate training of the population and subject‑specific models may be suboptimal; hierarchical modeling (e.g., HLM, hierarchical geodesic models) would naturally capture the relationship between fixed group‑level information and individual variability (R1). The connection between population trends and personalized trajectories is not clearly established (R1).

2. **Missing ablations and experiments**
   -No ablation for the personalization component (e.g., α=1) to show that the subject‑specific adaptation improves over the population model alone. This makes it difficult to assess the true benefit of the added complexity (R4).
   -No comparison with more advanced deep learning methods (R3). The baselines are traditional (LME, GAM) or earlier deep‑learning models.
   -No ablation studies for: fewer ROIs, different biomarkers, or non‑monotonic biomarkers. It is unclear whether the method can handle non‑monotonic progressions (R2).
   -No sensitivity analysis of the shrinkage parameter α; the impact of α on prediction accuracy is not explored (R3). The rationale for choosing XGBoost to learn α is not provided, and alternative models were not tried (R3).
   -No simulation dataset to illustrate the advantages of the approach (R2).
   -No evaluation of missing data handling or imputation strategies used (R2).
   -The latent space is not interpreted or analyzed (e.g., temporal dynamics, subpopulation clustering) (R3).

3. **Methodological concerns**
   -The subject‑specific model ss‑DKGP uses parameters from the population model p‑DKGP; the rationale for freezing the latent MLP parameters is unclear (R3). If ss‑DKGP already shares population parameters, the need to combine predictions is questionable (R2).
   -The combination of GP variances assumes independence; this assumption is not justified (R3).
   -In Eq. (11), the expectation appears conditioned on the latent representation z_p, but this condition is missing in the final formulation – possible typo (R1).
   -Eq. (2): “Φ” is not defined (R4).
   -Algorithm 1, step 12 is not atomic; it needs a clearer explanation (R4).
   -In Eq. (17), if an individual ROI differs greatly from the population mean, the optimization may bias toward minimizing error with the subject‑specific prediction y_st. The model’s balancing behavior in such cases needs theoretical or empirical justification (R1).

4. **Data and results**
   -Fig. 3: the differences between population and personalized trajectories may depend on the training data distribution (e.g., bias toward cognitively normal, MCI, or AD); each experiment should account for this (R1). Summaries of personalized trajectories for all six ROIs should be provided; legend font size should be increased (R1).
   -Bar plots lack confidence intervals, so it is impossible to assess statistical significance of differences between methods. Table 1 presents standard deviations; standard errors (of the means) should be reported instead (R4).
   -How were the six brain ROIs selected and extracted? What information is input (volume, size, full ROI data)? (R1). How much individual data is needed for the personalized GP to be useful? (R4).
   -External dataset validation: preprocessing steps are not clearly described, and their effect on generalization is unknown (R2).
   -The interpretation of results and their real‑world implications for participant‑level monitoring of disease progression is inadequate (R2).

5. **Presentation**
   -Inconsistent use of \citet vs. \citep (R4).
   -LaTeX issues: blank lines after equations causing extra space; missing punctuation after equations; Eq. 2 undefined Φ; lines 82‑83 should be tuples, not sets; line 163 missing wildcard; line 417 missing space (R4).
