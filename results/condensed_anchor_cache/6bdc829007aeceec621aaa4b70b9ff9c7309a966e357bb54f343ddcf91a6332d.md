## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates how LLM embeddings and attention representations change following in-context learning (ICL), using neuroscience-inspired techniques (RSA, parameterized probing, attention quantification). Experiments on Llama-2 70B and Vicuna 13B across three tasks (reading comprehension with distractors, linear regression, adversarial prompt injection) show that ICL improves behavioral performance and that representational changes correlate with these improvements. The paper also proposes an "attention ratio" metric to measure attention weight on relevant vs. irrelevant parts.

### Strengths
- Understanding ICL is a critical and relevant problem for the ICLR community.
- The research is somewhat original in analyzing how embeddings change under ICL, going beyond simple behavioral measures.
- The proposed attention ratio metric is new and interesting.
- A variety of tasks and analysis approaches (RSA, probing, attention metrics) are employed across two open-source models (Llama-2, Vicuna).
- The appendices are detailed and informative.
- The adversarial prompt injection task targeting latent representations is a creative addition.

One reviewer (score 6) found the work largely well done, the paper clear overall, and noted that if focused further on representational measures the significance could be reasonable. Other reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) were more critical of novelty, clarity, and methodological justification, as detailed below.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novel insights**: The main finding is that ICL improves performance and that last-layer representations reflect that improvement. Since behavior is a direct function of last-layer representations, it is not clear what new insight this provides. The paper would benefit from investigating earlier layers to reveal how ICL affects representations throughout the model.
- **Overemphasis on behavioral results**: Many figures (e.g., Fig 3, Fig 4a,b) focus on behavioral changes that are already well-established, rather than on the novel representational metrics. The behavioral benefits of ICL are already documented in prior work.
- **Methodological arbitrariness and lack of justification**:
  - Why max-pooling to compose token embeddings? How does this choice affect conclusions?
  - In Section 2.1, the "most relevant subset" of tokens is selected manually; such hand-picked tokens may not represent LLM behavior.
  - The set of "common components" crucial for tasks (Section 2.2) is not explained; how were they designed, and how do we know they represent solving strategies?
  - Why only the final layer of the LLM for attention analysis (Section 2.4)?
  - For the attention ratio metric, how are subsets a, b, c determined, and why is subset 'a' not included in the computation of A(a,b,c)_x?
- **RSA motivation and novelty**: The rationale for using RSA over other probing methods (e.g., CKA) is shaky. Parameter-free probing already exists in NLP (e.g., https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.383.pdf). The neuroscience motivation is interesting, but RSA has already been applied in NLP, reducing its contribution. The RSA methodology itself is not adequately described.
- **Experiment design concerns**:
  - Why these three tasks (reading comprehension, linear regression, adversarial injection) rather than tasks where ICL is widely used?
  - The adversarial injection task (Section 5): why precede instructions to be deceitful with instructions to be truthful? Would a fourth condition with only deceitful instructions be useful? Also, it is unclear how this experiment demonstrates the impact of ICL.
  - Unexpected result: In the adversarial persona injection, ICL makes representations closer to the hypothesis model for names rather than actions, even though the answer should be an action. The authors should explain this and clarify what is considered a "correct" response (truth-aligned or instruction-aligned?).
- **Interpretability of attention values**: The use of attention for interpretability is questionable without justification, given known issues (e.g., "Attention is not Explanation" by Jain & Wallace).
- **Writing quality**: Many typos ("mebeddings", "has two distinguish", "reading a book.", "Hanna" missing 'h', "to test this, designed", "tested on used open source"), grammatical errors ("RSA avoid this risk", "the models behavior", "to reduces the effect"), LaTeX errors ("Section ?? for t-test results"), citet/citep confusion, and incomplete references (at least 13 papers lack venue names). These issues, while not completely impeding comprehension, cast doubt on attention to detail.
- **Figure presentation**:
  - Violin plots (e.g., Fig 3) extrapolate to negative values for measures that are non-negative by definition, making them initially confusing.
  - Figure 5 a-c lack axis labels; these should be clearly indicated.
  - Figure 12 in the appendix appears erroneous: part c is supposed to represent the average of a and b, but a and b look identical, and axes are unlabeled.
  - Figure 3's y-axis label "Behavioral Error" is not explained; the nature of the plots should be described.
  - General figure text is unreadable due to size.
- **Missing model details**: It is not specified whether Llama-2 and Vicuna are instruction-tuned and, if so, on what datasets.

Note: One reviewer (score 6) found the paper clear and the work largely well done, and considered the attention ratio metric a positive contribution. However, the same reviewer also raised concerns about behavioral focus, attention interpretability, and figure issues. Other reviewers were consistently critical of novelty, methodological justification, and presentation quality.
