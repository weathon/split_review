## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Siamese Masked Interaction Learning (SMILE) for audio-visual speech recognition (AVSR). It combines early fusion and representation alignment via transformer encoders, masked reconstruction, and a Siamese framework with target/student masking. An adaptive dynamic multimodal fusion weights features from different layers. Experiments on LRS2 and LRS3 achieve state-of-the-art performance under both low- and high-resource settings.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clearly structured, and the motivations for each module are easy to follow.
- Open datasets and planned release of code/models support reproducibility.
- Ablation studies (Table 2) demonstrate that all design choices contribute positively to performance.
- The use of masked reconstruction under a supervised setting, and the ablation on training strategies of the Siamese network, are novel contributions to AVSR specifically (though the broader techniques are not new).
- The model is evaluated in both low-resource and high-resource settings, showing state-of-the-art results on both.
- The utility of weighted fusion of layerwise features is extended from acoustic-only to multimodal features.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (multiple reviewers):** Many core components (masked reconstruction, Siamese framework with random masking, optimization functions, adaptive layerwise weighted fusion) have been proposed in previous works. The adaptive fusion via a weighted sum over layers is considered straightforward and common (e.g., SUPERB benchmark). The improvement is marginal and appears saturated.
- **Falls behind state-of-the-art:** The WER on LRS3 (e.g., 0.9% WER in [1]) is better than the proposed model. The reported gains are small.
- **Missing experiments for core motivation:** The paper claims AVSR is designed for noisy/occluded conditions but provides no experiments under noisy settings. Also missing: modality ablation (audio-only, video-only, audiovisual) to quantify visual contribution; missing-modality conditions; audio-only ASR trained with the same pretraining/finetuning setup.
- **Dataset bias:** LRS2/3 were collected via an automated ASR pipeline where the speech-only model can achieve 100% accuracy on the curation set, making the test set biased toward easy examples for AVSR under clean conditions.
- **Inconsistent novelty attribution:** The term “Siamese networks” is cited from ~2020 papers, but the concept dates back to Koch et al. (2015). The work also refers to AVSR as an “emerging research area,” ignoring 30+ years of prior multimodal speech research.
- **Ablation gaps and unanswered reviewer questions:**
  - In Table 3, the gap among training strategies (e.g., masking rates) can reach ~2% WER—a large difference relative to the final gains over baselines—suggesting existing training strategies dominate performance, further weakening the contribution of the proposed modules.
  - In Table 2, the improvement from adaptive multimodal fusion is small; the gain may stem from extra learnable parameters. The authors did not report the learned values of weights \(S\) and \(w_i\).
  - In Equation (9), the weight \(S\) is shared across all layers; why not use a dynamic, layer-dependent weight (as in Equation (10)) since visual contribution likely varies per layer?
  - In Table 3(b), only four combinations of audio/visual masking rates were evaluated; other combinations were not justified.
  - The sensitivity of results to hyperparameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) was not tested.
  - The description of horizontal flipping (Section 4.2) is unclear—is it applied per frame or to the whole sequence?
- **Presentation issues:** The introductory paragraph on multimodal complementarity is imprecise (e.g., phonetic information is also visual; emphasis/stress are also acoustic). Equation (7) and its explanation are redundant with Equation (6).
