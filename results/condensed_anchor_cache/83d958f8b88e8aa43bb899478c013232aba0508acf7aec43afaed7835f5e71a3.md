## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes KNIFE, a distillation method for encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5) that leverages free-text rationales (FTRs) available during training. It first fine-tunes a teacher model on question+FTR to predict the answer, transferring reasoning knowledge into its hidden states. Then it fine-tunes a student model on question only to predict the answer while aligning its hidden states and output distribution with the teacher’s. Experiments on two question-answering datasets show improvements over finetuning and prompting baselines in fully-supervised and low-resource settings, and ablation studies confirm the importance of FTR quality.

### Strengths
- Novel approach to distilling reasoning knowledge from free-text rationales into small encoder-decoder LMs, addressing a scenario where rationale annotations exist for training but not at test time. One reviewer (rating 5) found the method intuitive and effective with a bottleneck architecture that has novelty.
- Thorough experimental analysis across varying model sizes (T5-Base, T5-Large), two QA datasets (including StrategyQA), different input–output compositions, and FTR variants (human-written vs. machine-generated).
- Comprehensive ablation study (Tables 8, 9) investigating the contributions of hidden state alignment (KD-in) and output distribution alignment (KD-out).
- Note: Two reviewers gave a rating of 3, raising concerns about significance and generality, in contrast to the more positive assessment from the third reviewer (rating 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited contribution and marginal gains:** Improvements are not always statistically significant; e.g., on StrategyQA, KNIFE’s performance is comparable to simply fine-tuning T5 with FTR as input and answer as output (FT I→RO). Distilling from T5-Large to T5-Base is less effective than from T5-Base to T5-Base, which is counterintuitive.
- **Restricted scope:** The method is only evaluated on two multi-choice QA datasets and is designed for encoder-decoder models like T5, not applicable to decoder-only models (e.g., Llama). It is unclear whether KNIFE generalizes to other reasoning tasks (arithmetic, commonsense, code generation) or knowledge-intensive tasks.
- **Ambiguous component contributions:** Ablation results (Tables 8, 9) show that using KD-in loss only or KD-out loss only occasionally outperforms the combined objective, questioning the necessity of using both. These single-objective results should have been included in Table 1 to demonstrate the significance of the combined approach.
- **Missing key baselines:** No comparison to retrieval-augmented generation (e.g., finetuning T5 to condition on retrieved (question, rationale, answer) demonstrations) or to recent chain-of-thought distillation methods that distill reasoning from larger LMs (e.g., Llama-2, GPT-3.5-turbo). The authors claim advantage when large LMs lack reasoning ability, a premise that seems out of sync with current trends.
- **Unclear source of improvement:** The small gain from incorporating FTR might be due to extra knowledge rather than reasoning capability, and may not generalize to scenarios with less knowledge overlap or larger rationales. The upper-bound performance of a small model given both input and FTR is not explicitly shown.
- **Large gap to LLMs and no interpretability gain:** The best student model still lags far behind large language models, and the student does not improve model explainability or reasoning beyond task accuracy.
- **Overlapping reviewer questions (which double as criticisms or requests):**
  - How does KNIFE compare to recent CoT distillation works?
  - Why are results with KD-in only and KD-out only not shown in Table 1?
  - What is the upper-bound performance when the small model is given both input and FTR?
  - Why does FTR help—is it due to extra knowledge, and how does this approach compare favorably to retrieval-augmented generation?
