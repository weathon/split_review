- Scores: 5, 6, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DuRND, an extension of Random Network Distillation (RND) that uses two separate random network modules—one for states labeled "successful" and one for "failed"—to derive both a novelty reward and a contribution reward. The aim is to better balance exploration and exploitation by focusing exploration on novel states that correlate with success. The method is integrated into PPO and evaluated on Atari, VizDoom, and MiniWorld, outperforming several baselines. Reviewers agree the idea is clear and simple, but have significant concerns about hyperparameter sensitivity, dependence on success/failure definitions, missing comparisons (especially vanilla PPO), and insufficient analysis of the reward coefficient scheduling. One reviewer (score 6) is more positive, calling the approach elegantly simple and valuable, while the others (scores 3, 3, 5) are more critical, questioning the novelty and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clearly structured, and easy to follow. The DuRND algorithm and figures are clearly explained.
- The approach is elegantly simple, lightweight, and easy to implement from an existing RND implementation. It can be combined with popular RL algorithms (PPO, SAC) without significant overhead.
- The problem of balancing exploration and exploitation in sparse-reward tasks is important and long-standing.
- The experimental evaluation is thorough: over 10 training runs across 10 environments (Atari, VizDoom, MiniWorld) compared against 6 baseline methods.
- Ablations demonstrate the utility of both the novelty and contribution rewards in isolation and their synergy when combined.
- The authors explicitly discuss limitations of their approach, which is a positive aspect.

### Weaknesses
- **Reliance on success/failure labeling and milestones**  
  The method requires a success criterion (e.g., sum of rewards ≥ 1 within \(T_{max}\) steps) to label states. This makes the framework difficult to apply beyond goal-reaching tasks and introduces strong assumptions about what constitutes success, especially in multi-objective or delayed-reward environments. The reliance on manually defined milestones introduces engineering overhead and limits autonomy. \(T_{max}\) is a hyperparameter whose value is not provided in the paper; its suitable value correlates with environment periodicity, making it hard to tune in tasks like legged robotics. Reviewers also question how the Success RN module is learned when success trajectories are very sparse (few positive examples).  
  *Reviewer 1: “The dependence on using a success criterion … makes the applicability … difficult.” Reviewer 2: “In ambiguous or multi-objective tasks, defining success and failure may not be straightforward.” Reviewer 3: “\(T_{max}\) is still a hyperparameter … difficult to tune when the periodicity of the environment is very hard to know.” Reviewer 4: “When the reward is sparse, there are few success trajectories. Does it cause a problem for learning the Success RN module?”*

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and insufficient analysis of λ and ω**  
  DuRND introduces two coefficients λ (novelty reward weight) and ω (contribution reward weight) that are linearly decayed/increased during training (λ from 1 to 0, ω from 0 to 1). This requires prior knowledge about the total number of training steps and assumes learning progress correlates with step count—an assumption that may not hold generally. The paper lacks a sensitivity analysis of how different values of λ and ω affect exploration behavior, how to set them without prior knowledge of environment reward scaling, how to determine the endpoint of linear scaling without expensive tuning, and how robust performance is under suboptimal scheduling. Reviewers note that RL algorithms are highly sensitive to such coefficients, and the performance gains might stem from the choice of schedule rather than the DuRND formulation. The decay also conflates the effect of exploration bonuses with the schedule, making it hard to isolate contributions. Notably, RND uses a fixed coefficient (0.5 from \(2A_{env} + A_{rnd}\)), whereas DuRND’s dynamic coefficients differ drastically.  
  *Reviewer 1: “The approach relies on knowledge about the training time to decay the intrinsic reward coefficients … Fixed coefficient would provide a more consistent baseline.” Reviewer 2: “The rewarding of the shaped reward signals … lacks a sensitivity analysis … Post-rebuttal: the most critical aspect … remains largely unaddressed.” Reviewer 3: “RL algorithms are VERY sensitive to the choice of the coefficient … it is very concerning whether the performance improvement mostly coming from the choice of the hyperparameter … I would potentially lift my rating if … same schedule of coefficients.”*

- **Missing comparisons and insufficient baseline details**  
  Vanilla PPO is not included as a baseline in any of the environments, despite DuRND using PPO as the base algorithm. This omission is repeatedly requested by reviewers. Moreover, details about how baselines (ExploRS, RND, #Explo, ReLara, ROSA) were tuned are missing: it is unclear if all baselines used the same base agent (PPO) and if their intrinsic reward coefficients were held constant (and at what value). The large differences in performance could be due to different base algorithms rather than the exploration method. Reviewers also ask for results of RND and other baselines when using the same linear schedule for coefficient decay to isolate the effect of DuRND’s state-splitting.  
  *Reviewer 1: “Some information about baselines seems missing … the lack of a consistent base agent across all baselines makes it difficult to isolate the effects … Does this mean that PPO was not the base agent for all considered agents?” Reviewer 3: “PPO is missing in the comparison, which is very crucial … What is the performance of RND … when you also decrease their coefficient λ from 1 to 0 linearly?” Reviewer 4: “Can the authors provide scores from other model-free RL algos on the tasks? Like PPO?”*

- **Contradictory findings regarding the motivation**  
  The paper’s motivation states that novelty-based rewards “can divert agents from their main objectives,” yet Figure 6 shows that pure novelty rewards achieve decent scores in several tasks (Freeway, Frogger, TMaze), questioning the necessity of the contribution reward.  
  *Reviewer 4: “Figure 6 shows that novelty reward can actually achieve descent scores … This questions the paper's assumption.”*

- **Unclear challenge level of tasks**  
  It is hard to gauge the contribution because the tasks may not be particularly challenging. For example, the highest known score on Freeway is 34, while DuRND achieves 25.  
  *Reviewer 4: “It is unclear how challenging the evaluated tasks are … the best algo in Figure 6 achieves 25.”*

- **Missing details and alternative formulations**  
  - The learning rate of the predictor network \(f(\cdot; \theta)\) is not reported.  
  - The alternative formulation of using the minimum of \(e_s(s)\) and \(e_f(s)\) for the novelty reward was not tested; the current sum can produce a high novelty bonus even if one network has seen the state many times.  
  - The state representation for the toy task was not described (one-hot encoding?) and code was not provided.  
  *Reviewer 1: “Wouldn’t it be more natural to use the minimum … Were any experiments conducted with alternative formulations?” Reviewer 3: “It is also crucial to control the speed of the converge rate of the intrinsic reward model … not mentioned.” Reviewer 4: “Can the authors provide code (it's not in the current supplementary material)?”*

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 6) found the method elegantly simple and the evaluation thorough, acknowledging that the contribution is incremental but valuable. However, this reviewer also shared the concerns about hyperparameter sensitivity and success/failure definitions, and lowered their score post-rebuttal due to lack of analysis. The three lower-scoring reviewers (3, 3, 5) were more critical overall, questioning the validity of the improvements given the hyperparameter changes and missing comparisons.
