## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces AutoPR, a tool that leverages LLMs with call graphs, routing algorithms, in-memory caching, and collaborative agents to automate pull-request generation for bug fixes. AutoPR starts from a static call graph and optimizes it to capture dependencies for bug localization and patching. It is evaluated on SWE-Bench and compared to SWE-Agent and Devin. Results show that Auto-PR-all outperforms both (reported 65.7% vs Devin 53.2%), while Auto-PR-avg underperforms. One reviewer (score 5) praised the practical problem and readability, but the other three (scores 3) found poor presentation and lack of novelty. All reviewers agree that the problem is relevant, but most see major weaknesses in evaluation rigor, missing comparisons, and presentation.

### Strengths
- The optimized dependence graph captures relevant dependencies while reducing complexity, making it a task-focused representation (R1).
- Practical incorporation of memory caching and routing algorithms for handling large code repositories (R1).
- Solves a useful problem of automating repetitive software tasks, saving budget and time (R2).
- Easy to read and follow (R2). *Note: this is a minority view; other reviewers strongly disagree.*
- Graph-based approach aiding zero-shot code generation is promising (R2).
- Application to the SWE-Bench dataset provides a relevant real-world testbed (R3).
- Combines LLMs with call graphs and ASTs to improve PR generation from issue descriptions (R4).
- Improved task resolution rate on SWE-Bench compared to Devin and SWE-Agent using Auto-PR-all (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons**:
  - No comparison with a baseline using a static call graph (non-optimized) to isolate the benefits of the optimization (R1).
  - No comparison with AutoCodeRover, RepoUnderstander, or CodeXgraph — similar agentic workflows that perform well on SWE-Bench (R1, R3). A detailed comparison table of features/capabilities is needed (R3).
- **Insufficient explanation of key components**:
  - The types of dependencies captured and how non-call dependencies (shared state, event-driven, configuration) are handled are not explained (R1, R1 Q1).
  - Routing algorithms and in-memory caching are claimed but not described in terms of implementation or contribution to performance (R1 Q2, R4).
  - Sections 2.1–2.2 present call-graph construction detail that appears irrelevant; no novelty is claimed there, and the math seems retrofitted (R2, R4). A concrete use-case example is needed to clarify the methodology (R2).
- **Evaluation rigor and fairness**:
  - Evaluation inconsistency: AutoPR results are reported as averages over multiple runs (A-pr-avg, A-pr-all), while baseline tools (SWE-Agent, Devin) are evaluated on a single run. This makes Auto-PR-all's superiority potentially inflated (R1, R4). A comparison of Auto-PR-avg with single-run baselines, or multiple runs for baselines, is needed (R4).
  - No statistical significance testing (e.g., Scott-Knott test) and insufficient repeats (<20) to mitigate randomness of generated patches (R2).
  - The use of pass@3 with SWE-Agent is questionable; pass@1 should be used for fair comparison (R3). Table 1 shows Auto-PR-avg is inferior to SWE-Agent, which is not discussed (R3).
  - Claim that overfitting patches are useful for localization lacks concrete evidence and is tenuous (R4 Q8).
- **Poor presentation**:
  - Very poor writing and formatting: LaTeX symbol errors, double-quote issues, disjoint abstract last lines, and missing/incorrect figure references (Figures 6, 7a, 9 do not exist; Figure 1 unclear; Figure 2 does not match the workflow description; Figure 2 repeats Table 1) (R1, R2 minor, R3, R4). Sections/step references are also wrong (e.g., SWE-Bench referenced to Section 2.2) (R1, R4).
  - “To further complicate the problem…” is unclear and confusing (R2 Q1, R4 Q4).
  - The phrase “(for brevity, we use AutoPR to denote AutoPR in this section)” contains a typo; should be A-PR (R4 Q6).
- **Lack of novelty and research framing**:
  - Paper reads like a tool paper, not research; no central research question that literature failed to answer is posed, and the future of AutoPR is unclear (R2).
  - Related work is too high-level; it does not discuss whether similar tools exist or how AutoPR differs from them (R2, R3).
- **Unexplained practical concerns**:
  - If multiple correction attempts are time-consuming, it could be counterproductive for simple tasks; no metric captures that trade-off (R2 Q3).
  - Scalability and the types of bugs AutoPR can effectively address are not discussed (R1 Q3, R3 Q3).
