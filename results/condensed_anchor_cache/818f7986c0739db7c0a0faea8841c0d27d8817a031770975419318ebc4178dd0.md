## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates world models (WMs) in transformers trained on maze-solving tasks. Using Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) and attention pattern analysis, the authors examine how transformers encode maze edge connections and intervene to confirm causal roles. They compare two models: Stan (learned positional encoding, 8 heads) and Terry (RoPE, 4 heads). The main contributions claimed are the discovery of WM features, a decision-tree method for feature attribution, and analysis of positional encoding effects.

### Strengths
- The paper provides an interesting case study of maze-solving networks, which have received little attention in interpretability work, furthering the study of world models and SAEs (R1, R2).
- The empirical findings of world models in maze-solving transformers are established (R2). One reviewer (R3, score 5) found the claims to be original and well-substantiated, and the analysis of the role of positional encoding to be novel. However, other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) were less convinced about the strength of evidence (see Weaknesses).
- The decision-tree method proposed for distinguishing features relevant to edge connections is novel and useful for comparing the two positional encoding schemes (R2).
- There is some cross-checking between edge features and SAE features in Figure 7a (R2).
- Some observations, such as the attention patterns shown in Figures 3-4, are interesting (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient evidence for “world model” claim**: The central claim is not convincingly demonstrated. The input tokens already explicitly represent each maze edge, so representing edges as features is trivial: a linear probe on the input embeddings can recover the same information, and interventions on features are essentially equivalent to editing the input tokens (R1). There is no formal definition of “world model” in the paper, and it is unclear how such a model is used for predicting the shortest path; the claim lacks quantitative grounding and a clear picture of overall internal structure (R4). Even the more positive reviewer (R3) notes that results in Section 3.1 are qualitative without quantitative support (e.g., average fraction of attention with standard deviation) and that the claim about reasoning with novel features is not fully substantiated.
- **SAE analysis concerns**: The SAE discovery of a separate feature for each possible edge suggests an overcomplete dictionary memorization failure rather than a compressed representation (R1). No details on SAE training architecture, hyperparameters (learning rate, L1 decay), or reconstruction quality (fraction of loss recovered, explained variance) are provided (R2, R3). A taxonomy of other feature types beyond edge connections is missing; the analysis is limited to edge features only (R2). The discussion of SAE decoupling effects in Section 3.2 is vague, and it is unclear how to measure the degree of entanglement in features for Stan and Terry models (R4).
- **Limited circuit analysis**: The circuit analysis is restricted to attention visualizations and magnitudes of OV matrix vectors. There is no use of linear probes or mutual information to quantify information flow; speculation on linear probe failure is unsubstantiated without results (R2). The meaning of “magnitude of vector” in Figure 4 (L0 or L1 norm?) is unclear (R3). The explanation of the alternating pattern between L0H3 and L0H7 is poorly explained and only became clear after reading later sections (R3). The paper does not address whether observations about specific neurons (L0H3, L0H5, L0H7) generalize across different runs (R4). Terminology like W_OV is not defined (R4).
- **Confounded comparison between Stan and Terry**: The two models differ in both positional encoding (learned vs. RoPE) and number of attention heads (8 vs. 4), making it impossible to attribute differences solely to positional encoding. A controlled experiment varying only positional encoding while keeping architecture constant (including number of heads and dimensionality) is needed (R3).
- **Unclear writing and figures**: The decision tree methodology is insufficiently explained; it is unclear how features are deemed “most relevant” for edge connections (R1, R2). Figures 4-5 do not clearly advance the main argument (R1). Figure 1b is confusing as a visual representation (walls look like cells) (R3). All figures have very small font sizes (R3). Figure 7b's bar plots are unclear: the text says “effect of patching attention head on the SAE latent vector,” but what does 100% effect mean? (R3). The explanation of attention pattern switching (from 1/3 to 5/7 preceding tokens after position 100) is insufficient (R1). It is unclear why separate attention heads attend to even vs. odd parity cells (R1). The paper is disorganized and hard to extract the main message (R4).
- **Insufficient support for key claims**: The claim that models can reason with more active features than seen during training is only tested by adding a single connection feature, not multiple simultaneous features (R3). The failure of Stan removal intervention (Figure 9) is confusing: removing connections reduces the sequence length, so why should the model fail? The paper should check if the maze remains solvable after removal (R2, R3). The claim that Stan fails on 6x6 levels with one more connection (Figure 10) is confusing because the model was trained on 7x7 sparse mazes; the failure may be due to increased sequence length rather than number of connections, requiring clarification (R3).
- **Contribution beyond prior work**: The overall contribution does not add much beyond Igorevich et al. 2023 (R2). Reviewers 1 and 4 (both score 3) gave low contribution ratings; Reviewer 3 (score 5) was more positive but also had concerns.
- **Missing details**: The paper does not mention whether “token-detector” features appear in the SAE that activate on maze cell tokens; if so, removal interventions might fail because cell features are not removed (R3). The paper does not specify whether intervention accuracy on removing a connection checks if the maze remains solvable; if not, removal could render the maze unsolvable regardless of the model (R3). Questions about the attention pattern parity and head function remain unanswered (R1, R4).
