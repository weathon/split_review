## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies proxy gaming in neural network proxies under optimization pressure, introducing ProxyBench in the text domain. It evaluates proxy robustness using metrics (final gold reward and RMS divergence) across Best-of-N, reinforcement learning, and white-box optimization. Experiments reveal that proxy gaming occurs even when average proxy reward aligns with gold reward, and that per‑example discrepancies are systematic. Adversarial training provides limited gains. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem and the extensive experiments, but raise concerns about metric justification, novelty, domain restriction, clarity, and completeness of analysis.

### Strengths
- Addresses the crucial and under‑studied problem of proxy gaming, especially in RLHF for LLMs. The broad relevance of proxy models in policy training (when evaluation is costly) is noted. (R1, R2, R4)
- Introduces ProxyBench, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating proxy robustness, with the potential to standardize future evaluations. (R3, R4)
- Conducts extensive experiments that reveal previously unknown phenomena, such as systematic per‑example gaming, relationship between proxy model size and robustness, and phase shifts in reward curves. (R2, R3)
- Provides practical insights into mitigation strategies (e.g., adversarial training) and highlights limitations of current proxy reward models. (R1, R2, R3)
- Proposes novel evaluation metrics (final gold reward, RMS divergence) and provides detailed analysis of baseline methods. (R3)
- Exploration of multiple optimization strategies (BoN, RL, white‑box) indicates a deep understanding of the problem landscape. (R3)
- Writing is overall clear and easy to follow. (R2)

**Minority more positive:** Reviewer 3 (score 6) emphasized the substantial contribution of ProxyBench and the depth of analysis, rating contribution as “excellent”, while other reviewers (scores 5) rated contribution as “fair”.

### Weaknesses
1. **Evaluation metric (RMS divergence) not well justified.**
   - The metric uses derivatives of proxy and gold reward curves; its motivation and mathematical formulation (P' and G') need elaboration. It does not directly quantify per‑instance reward discrepancy, which is the core of proxy gaming. (R1, R2)
   - An alternative loss formulation is suggested: \( ( \frac{1}{T} \sum e_i^\gamma )^{1/\gamma} \) with hyperparameter \(\gamma\) to emphasize instance‑wise gaps. (R1)
   - The paper should articulate why focusing on per‑example discrepancies (beyond expected reward) is crucial for real‑world RLHF. Randomness in data makes worst‑case metrics impractical without further justification. (R2)

2. **Lack of quantitative analysis on consistency of gamed examples.**
   - The claim that gamed examples are consistent across proxy models (Section 5.1) is mentioned but not backed by numbers. Without quantifying this consistency, the importance of per‑example gaming and its non‑random nature remains unclear. (R2, R3)
   - Underlying causes of this correlation are not analyzed; strategies to mitigate it are missing. (R3)

3. **Novelty concerns and reliance on existing work.**
   - Most experimental setup and data are borrowed from existing work [1], weakening originality. (R2)
   - Optimization methods (BoN, RL, white‑box) are pre‑existing; no justification for their selection or comparative analysis of how they induce different types of proxy gaming. (R4)
   - Missing comparison with the relevant robustness evaluation method TextGrad (ICLR 2023). (R3)

4. **Clarity and organization issues.**
   - Title is misleading: investigations are only in the text domain, and the paper covers more than adversarial optimization (BoN, RL). (R1)
   - Connection between the goal (evaluating proxy robustness) and the chosen optimization methods is not clearly established; it is unclear how these methods relate to real‑world proxy gaming scenarios. (R4)
   - The paper would be better organized as a comprehensive analysis rather than a benchmark; more empirical results are needed for soundness. (R2)
   - Related work is lacking; RLHF and reward modeling should be introduced before proxy gaming. (R2)

5. **Assumptions about the gold model and static training setup.**
   - The gold model (ensemble trained on human preferences) is assumed to accurately reflect the true objective, but it may inherit biases or shared vulnerabilities from the training data. Sensitivity to the choice or diversity of gold models is not explored. (R1, R3)
   - Training the proxy on a fixed dataset without iterative interaction with the gold model does not capture real‑world dynamics (continuous updates, divergence over time). (R3)
   - The gap between RLHF practice and the gold model used in the analysis is acknowledged but not quantified. (R1)

6. **Domain restriction and limited validation.**
   - Experiments are confined to the text domain; applicability to other modalities (image, audio) is not demonstrated, raising questions about generalizability. (R1, R4)
   - Benchmark credibility is questioned because validation relies on a single model architecture (DeBERTa‑v3, albeit in different sizes). (R4)

7. **Incomplete analysis of observed phenomena.**
   - Phase shifts in reward curves (RLHF optimization) are not analyzed for their specific mechanisms; the paper may miss subtler forms of proxy gaming. (R3)
   - The claim that a larger policy provides a stronger attack (Section 5.2) lacks intuition or deeper explanation. (R1)

8. **Questions left unanswered.**
   - How “gold” are the gold models? (R1)
   - Does adversarial training hurt clean performance of the proxy? (R1)
   - Why do we need an adversarially robust proxy? What are the specific contributions of this work beyond a benchmark? (R4)
