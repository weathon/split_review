## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents a differentially private algorithm for finding second-order stationary points (SOSP) in stochastic optimization. Building on the SpiderBoost framework, it introduces two key improvements: (1) replacing independent Gaussian noise with a tree mechanism (a correlated noise mechanism from the differentially private continuous observation literature) to reduce cumulative noise across gradient-difference queries, and (2) adaptively selecting batch sizes for gradient-difference queries in proportion to the current iterate’s movement, which reduces sensitivity and sharpens the utility analysis. The algorithm achieves a rate of \(\alpha = \tilde{O}\bigl(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}} + (\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n\epsilon})^{1/2}\bigr)\), matching the state-of-the-art rate for first-order stationary points (FOSP) and improving on the previous SOSP rate of \(\tilde{O}\bigl((\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n\epsilon})^{3/7}\bigr)\). All reviewers recognize the theoretical advance; one reviewer (R3) rates the paper lower (6) due to concerns about notation clarity, privacy of the adaptive batch size, and lack of discussion of the matrix mechanism, while the other three rate it 8.

### Strengths

- **Timely and important problem.** Finding SOSP under differential privacy is a natural and challenging extension of private optimization; prior work left a gap between SOSP and FOSP rates, and this paper closes that gap.
- **Meaningful theoretical improvement.** The algorithm improves the rate from \(\tilde{O}((\sqrt{d}/n\epsilon)^{3/7})\) to \(\tilde{O}((\sqrt{d}/n\epsilon)^{1/2})\) for the private term, while the non-private term \(\tilde{O}(1/n^{1/3})\) is unchanged. This establishes that privately finding an SOSP need not be harder than finding an FOSP.
- **Novel combination of techniques.** The use of the tree mechanism in this private non-convex optimization setting is original, and the adaptive batch-size strategy—adjusting the sample size proportionally to the \(l_2\)-distance between iterate pairs—tightens the sensitivity and utility analysis in a non-trivial way. The analysis is thorough and well-supported.
- **Clear writing and exposition.** (R1, R2). The ideas are presented in a logical, easy-to-follow manner, and the motivation for the algorithmic components is generally clear.
- **Potential for broader applicability.** The techniques (tree mechanism plus adaptive batch sizing) may apply to other private optimization problems, not just SOSP.

### Weaknesses

1. **No empirical evaluation.** All reviewers (R2, R3, R4) note the complete absence of experiments. The theoretical gains need to be validated on benchmark problems of increasing size; it is unclear whether the algorithm is practical or how runtime and the achieved \(\alpha\) compare to prior methods in practice. This also makes it difficult to assess robustness to hyperparameter choices.
2. **Notation and presentation flow.** Multiple reviewers found the notation confusing or the structure abrupt.
   - The oracles \(\mathcal{O}_1\) and \(\mathcal{O}_2\) resemble asymptotic \(O\)-notation. An alternative naming would reduce ambiguity (R4).
   - The paper reads like a sequence of lemmas without enough overview; transitions between sections are abrupt, and motivations for certain choices (e.g., the specific form of the adaptive batch size) are not fully explained (R4). A table of notation was requested (R3).
3. **Privacy of the adaptive batch size.** The expression for the batch size \(b_t\) in Lemma 3.8 depends on \(\tilde{\Delta}_t\). If \(\tilde{\Delta}_t\) is computed from the data, then the batch size itself may leak private information. The authors need to explain how the batch size is made private (R3). This is a fundamental concern about the algorithm’s privacy guarantee.
4. **Regime of improvement and tightness.** The improvement from exponent \(3/7\) to \(1/2\) is modest given the substantial algorithmic changes. Questions raised:
   - Is the bound \(\tilde{O}((\sqrt{d}/n\epsilon)^{1/2})\) known to be tight for FOSP? (R2)
   - The authors claim that for small \(\epsilon\), \(\alpha_F \ll \alpha_S\), but as \(\epsilon \to 0\) the exponent \(1/2\) dominates \(3/7\). The regime where the improvement is most significant should be explicitly characterized, and any limitations as \(\epsilon\) becomes very small clarified (R2).
5. **Tradeoffs between gradient oracles.** The motivation for using two oracles (\(\mathcal{O}_1\) and \(\mathcal{O}_2\)) is unclear. The statement that \(\mathcal{O}_1\) is more accurate but incurs higher cost is vague; exactly why not use \(\mathcal{O}_1\) every time? A clear explanation of the accuracy–privacy–computational tradeoff is needed (R2).
6. **Comparison of assumptions and possible extensions.** The paper does not compare its assumptions (e.g., smoothness, Hessian Lipschitz) to those in related private optimization works. Such a comparison would clarify the scope of the result (R4). Additionally, the current approach uses each data point only once in the inner loop; the possibility of reusing data across outer loops with advanced privacy composition and subsampling amplification (which could improve sample complexity) is not discussed (R4).
7. **Matrix mechanism alternative.** The tree mechanism can be improved by the matrix mechanism, which reduces the error by a constant factor and often performs better in practice. The paper does not discuss whether the matrix mechanism could further sharpen the analysis (R3).
8. **Minor notation question.** The norm used throughout is assumed to be the \(l_2\) norm; this is not stated explicitly (R3).

*Reviewer 1 found no weaknesses, but the other three reviewers raised the concerns listed above, especially the lack of experiments and clarity issues. One reviewer (R3) expressed lower enthusiasm overall (score 6) because of these gaps.*
