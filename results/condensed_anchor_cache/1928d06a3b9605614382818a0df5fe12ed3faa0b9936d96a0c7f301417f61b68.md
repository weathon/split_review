## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a trend/seasonality decomposition-based causal structure for counterfactual outcome prediction in time series. It uses existing plug-in decomposition networks (FEDformer, DLinear, MICN) to learn trend and seasonality representations as confounders and adjustment variables, with an imbalance loss to reduce selection bias. Experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets show improved RMSE over two baselines. Reviewer opinions are sharply split: one reviewer (score 6) found the work original, clear, and significant; the other three (scores 3,3,3) rated it fair to poor, citing major issues with novelty, theoretical justification, experimental rigor, and reproducibility.

### Strengths

- The idea of using trend/seasonality decomposition to reduce selection bias is novel and interesting. (R1, R2, R4)
- The proposed method is overall well presented and easy to follow; the language is clear and the paper is well-structured. (R1, R2)
- Experimental results show improved performance against SOTA baselines (CRN, RMSN) on synthetic and real-world data. (R1, R3)
- The approach addresses a critical problem in time-series causal analysis and could impact domains like finance, healthcare, and meteorology. (R2)
- The model is fairly intuitive and shows promising experimental results. (R3)

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty is severely limited.** The framework is identical to Hassanpour & Greiner (2020) – the same loss function (Eqs. 3,4,5,6 in that work) and the same independent constraints – with the only change being the use of existing plug-in decomposition networks (FEDformer, DLinear, MICN). (R4)
- **No theoretical justification for the central assumption** that trend acts as confounder and seasonality as adjustment variable. Counterexamples are possible (e.g., a seasonal event directly influences trend). No empirical evidence is provided that learned components align with ground truth trends/seasonality. (R2, R3, R4)
- **The claim of reduced selection bias is unsupported and contradictory.** The paper criticizes balanced representation methods for selection bias, yet it uses the same IPM-based imbalance loss (Eq. 4 in Hassanpour & Greiner). It does not explain why the learned trend component, explicitly used to predict treatment, avoids introducing selection bias. (R1, R4)
- **Minimizing discrepancy to make seasonality independent of treatment is not justified.** The mechanism by which Eq. 11–12 ensures independence is unclear, and maximizing discrepancy across interventions seems arbitrary. (R1, R3)
- **Evaluation is inadequate:**
  - Only two baselines (CRN, RMSN) are compared; more causal discovery and time-series forecasting methods are needed, including methods that explicitly model time-varying confounders or use other decomposition approaches. (R2, R3)
  - All evaluations use RMSE only. No metrics for individual treatment effect (e.g., ATT, precision in estimating heterogeneous effects) are reported. (R2)
  - No uncertainty measures (standard deviation, confidence intervals, p-values) are provided, so statistical significance of improvements is unknown. (R1)
  - Very few datasets are used; evaluation on more real-world time series is needed to generalize. (R2)
  - The real dataset evaluation is unclear: counterfactual outcomes are not observable, and “real” columns in Table 2 are not explained. (R1)
  - Experiments focus on predictive accuracy, not on recovering true causal effects; evaluating treatment decisions and timing is critical (cf. Bica et al. 2020). (R3)
  - The ablation study is limited (removing loss components for only one plug-in model) and does not ablate network components (trend/seasonality encoders). (R3)
- **Synthetic data generation lacks reproducibility.** Exact functional forms, parameters, and noise models are not provided; the paper only refers to Melnychuk et al. (2022) for details. (R2, R4)
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity** (e.g., regularization coefficients) is not analyzed, making it hard to assess robustness. (R2)
- **Causality assumptions (positivity, consistency, unconfoundedness) are stated but never justified** for the datasets; violations could invalidate conclusions. (R2)
- **Problem setting is incomplete.** The causal diagram for time series is missing: how do historical outcomes and covariates relate? Does using only past P time steps introduce unmeasured confounding? Do causal relationships between covariates change over time? The impact of trend/seasonality on causality is not clarified. (R4)
- **Missing related works:** Seedat et al. (2022) on continuous-time counterfactuals and Cao et al. (2023) on irregular time series with hidden confounders. (R2)
- **No attribution** is given for traditional decomposition methods, and they are not used as comparison benchmarks. (R3)
- **Societal impacts** of deploying such counterfactual forecasting models are not discussed. (R2)
