## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviews are strikingly disparate: one reviewer is strongly positive (score 8, confidence 4), one strongly negative (score 3, confidence 5), and two moderately positive/negative (scores 5 and 6, confidence 3 and 4). The positive reviewer praises the creative analogy to LDPC codes and random graphs, the unified explanation of compute-optimal scaling, emergence, and plateauing, and the solid theoretical analysis. The negative reviewer finds the theory not rigorous, with missing definitions, a trivial connection to LDPC, no experimental verification for language models (LMs), and the framework so vague that it is impossible to test. The moderate reviewers acknowledge the interesting framework and potential but raise major concerns about missing technical details, a flawed Proposition 1, unjustified assumptions, weak connection to actual LMs, and large gaps between theory and empirical data. There is fundamental disagreement over the paper’s soundness and significance.

### Strengths
- Creative use of low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes and random graph theory to model language model scaling phenomena. (R1, R4)
- The unified framework can simultaneously explain compute-optimal size scaling (Chinchilla rule), emergent capabilities, and performance plateauing. (R1, R4)
- The theoretical analysis is solid and well-presented; the paper is well-written. (R1)
- The trick of modeling concept learning as belief propagation decoding of an LDPC code over an erasure channel is interesting and has potential. (R4)
- The framework is reasonable and diverse enough to model learning and acquisition of new skills; it could incorporate additional factors (e.g., data quality). (R3)
- The topic and theoretical framework are related to recent works and have generated community interest. (R3)
- Using tools from coding theory and random graph theory shows great potential for grounding empirical observations in rigorous mathematics. (R3)
- Ambitious goal of a unifying theory for scaling behavior. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of rigorous definitions:** The basic definitions of “text”, “concept”, and “skill” are missing, making the theory vague and untestable. (R2) For instance, given a text sequence, how does one determine if it is a text, a concept, or a skill? Are they limited by representation and length? (R2) Without these definitions, it is impossible to come up with simple examples to verify the theory. (R2)
- **Weak connection to language models:** The link to ML is superficial; simply renaming variable and factor nodes of LDPC codes to text, concept, and skill nodes makes the claim trivially true, yielding no deeper insight. (R2) The theory seems to apply to any learning machine (e.g., humans) and lacks discussion of learning/education literature. (R3) Inappropriate wording such as “chooses to learn” (line 102) and “The goal of the language model is to learn as many concepts as possible” (line 245) misrepresent actual LM training. (R3)
- **No experimental validation for language models:** The paper contains no relevant experimental results for actual LMs. (R2, R3, R1) The study would benefit from showing measurable quantities like accuracy or number of learned skills side-by-side with those from real LMs. (R3) The claimed peeling process should be empirically tracked during training (e.g., evolution of graphs over epochs). (R1)
- **Unjustified or overly simplistic assumptions:**
    - The assumption that the peeling process stops after training is unverified; modern LMs are trained for few epochs and may not have converged to a state where the process is no longer active. (R1)
    - The assumption of randomly generated graphs with binomial degree distributions may not hold for real-world concept-text and skill-concept graphs. (R1) It is unclear whether the theory holds for other degree distributions. (R1)
    - Modeling concept learning as a simple erasure channel is overly simplistic given the complex nature of semantic understanding and concept formation in LMs. (R4)
- **Technical errors and missing details:**
    - Proposition 1 is flawed: claiming that if neither R/T nor T/R tends to 0 then “R/T must be a constant” is incorrect (counterexample: R = sin(C), T = cos(C)). This proposition is presented as a main contribution. (R3)
    - Equation (4): the left-hand side does not depend on ε, but the approximation on the right-hand side depends on ε. It is unclear how to explain this and whether ε can take arbitrary values as in Appendix A.2. (R4)
    - Many technical elements are not defined or explained: what are the {Pi} (line 124), “matching condition” (line 316), r in Equation 2, ε in Equations 3, 7, and 8, “post-decoding bit erasure rate”, “excess entropy” (and why bounding it from below matters), “compute-optimal scaling” definition, and “decoding process”. (R3)
    - The paper claims to use information theory but actually uses statistical mechanics ideas from graph-based coding (Richardson & Urbanke, 2008) and random graph theory; the term “non-asymptotic information theory” is misleading. (R3)
- **Unclear motivation and rationale:**
    - It is unclear what parts of the framework are novel versus from previous works (e.g., hierarchical skills, message-passing dynamics). (R3)
    - The rationale for N (number of parameters) being proportional to R (number of skills) is not given. (R3)
    - The rationale for studying a limited compute budget (tradeoff N·T < C) and whether such a tradeoff appears in LM training is not explained. (R3)
    - The abstract and introduction should clarify whether “size” means number of parameters or size of training data. (R3)
- **Large gap between theory and empirical data:** The lower bound on excess entropy is too far from the empirical excess entropy reported. (R4)
- **Minor issues:** In Appendix C, equation (29) is only approximately equal to equation (30); the approximation (1-x)^n ≈ 1-nx requires p_b to be small. (R1) A reference for Chernoff’s bound is missing (line 759). (R1)
- **Contrasting opinions:** Reviewer 2 (score 3, confidence 5) states that the theory is so vague it cannot be verified and does not follow standard scientific research – more like “belief/religion”. Reviewer 1 (score 8, confidence 4) finds the theory convincing, solid, and significant for guiding future model training. This extreme disagreement highlights that the paper’s contribution and soundness are highly contested. (R1, R2)
