## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies data mixture optimization for LLM pretraining. It proposes a unified optimization framework (LMO) showing that prior offline and online methods all minimize total loss under a method-specific “mixing law” (linear or log-linear loss-proportion relationship). The authors argue that while these mixing laws can express the true relationship, existing methods estimate the parameters inaccurately, leading to inconsistent performance versus stratified sampling. Based on this insight, they derive a new online method, AIOLI, that dynamically estimates mixing law parameters during training and adjusts proportions via exponentiated gradient descent. Empirically (160M model, 50K steps, 6 data domains), AIOLI outperforms stratified sampling on all 6 datasets (average 0.28 PPL gain), while existing methods can be up to 6.9 PPL worse. In a practical limited-budget setting, AIOLI improves over prior methods by up to 12.01 PPL.

### Strengths
- **Unified framework**: The paper provides a well-written, mathematically clear framework that unifies several existing data mixing methods (e.g., DoReMi, Skill-it, DOREMI, DoGE) under a common formulation, revealing that differences are mainly in how mixing law parameters are estimated. This is a significant conceptual contribution. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Problem importance**: The data mixing problem is crucial for large-scale LM training, and the framework can advance future research in this area. (R1)
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The experiments are necessary and sufficient to support the main claims, including evaluations of both unrestricted (Tab. 2) and practical (Tab. 3) settings. (R1, R3)
- **Novel algorithm without extra runs**: AIOLI is an online method that does not require additional proxy training runs or a separate optimization loop, unlike some prior methods (e.g., DoReMi, Skill-it). It consistently improves over stratified sampling on all datasets. (R1, R4)
- **Robustness**: Compared to baselines, AIOLI shows remarkable robustness and does not fail badly under short, computationally constrained proxy runs. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scale and realism**: All experiments use only a 160M parameter model with at most 50K training steps and up to 7 data domains. This scale is far from real-world pretraining (which often has thousands of domains, much larger models, and different emergent behaviors). The linear/log-linear loss-proportion assumption is only validated empirically on this small scale; its validity for realistic setups is unproven. (R1: “10 different proportions are not enough to find the optimal proportion”; R2: “validation was only conducted at 160M… not convincing at all”; “real pre-training scenarios often contain domains of over 10000 levels”; “small models can be used to predict larger models” is not justified.)
- **Algorithmic novelty questioned**: The core algorithms (LearnParams and AIOLI) are very similar to the Skill-it method (Learngraph and the EGD-based data selection) applied to the continual pretraining setting. The adjacency matrix update via validation loss differences and the exponentiated gradient descent on mixture weights are essentially the same. This weakens the claimed novelty. (R4)
- **LearnParams accuracy and hyperparameter sensitivity**: 
  - The LearnParams algorithm (Alg. 2) estimates the mixing-law matrix \(A^*\) without sweeping, but its accuracy is unclear and there is no guarantee of correctness. (R1, R3)
  - The simulated “mixing” step (line 379–387) is empirical; its generality and effectiveness across wider scales are unproven. (R2)
  - AIOLI introduces many hyperparameters (e.g., \(k\), \(\delta\), learning rates), but insufficient guidance is given on how to set them, hindering practical application. Ablation or sensitivity analyses for these hyperparameters (e.g., \(k\), \(\delta\) in Algorithm 2) are missing. (R3, R4)
- **Not always best on individual datasets**: Although AIOLI improves over stratified sampling overall, it underperforms some baseline methods on specific datasets (e.g., A/B/SE in Tab. 2), raising questions about consistency per domain. (R3)
- **Uniform weights convergence**: The final domain weights from AIOLI are very close to uniform (Table 16). Since uniform sampling is a natural baseline for minimizing average domain loss, it is not clear that AIOLI provides substantial practical advantage over that simple baseline. (R4; also echoed by R2’s question on practical benefit)
- **Missing experiments and analyses**:
  - **Larger models and scaling**: No experiments on larger models or validation that conclusions from 160M transfer to larger scales (e.g., 1B+). (R2)
  - **Downstream tasks**: Only PPL is reported; no evaluation on downstream task performance (e.g., MMLU, QA, coding) under different mixing strategies. (R2)
  - **Out-of-domain generalization**: The paper only targets minimizing average loss over the training domains. It does not evaluate settings where the validation distribution is unknown or different (OoD), as considered in DOGE and Skill-it. (R4)
  - **Figure 2 fitting**: All points were used for fitting the mixing law; no prediction on a held-out proportion is shown, leaving the predictive validity of the assumed form unclear. (R2)
  - **Algorithmic rationale**: The principle behind the effectiveness of Algorithm 2 (lines 379–387) is not explained in layman’s terms, and it is unclear whether the simulation process harms the final model performance. (R2, R3)
  - **Update strategy**: AIOLI performs only one update step per round (Alg. 1, line 5). It is unclear why iterating more steps to approach the optimal mixture given the estimated mixing law would not yield better results. (R3)
  - **Weight trajectory**: No plot of the data mixture weight trajectory over training; it is unknown whether AIOLI learns a curriculum (like DoReMi, DOGE) or stays near uniform. (R4)
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: One reviewer (R1, score 8) found the experiments fully sufficient; others (R2, score 5) insisted the scale and validation are inadequate. This disagreement is reflected in the weaknesses listed here, where the negative assessment is preserved.
