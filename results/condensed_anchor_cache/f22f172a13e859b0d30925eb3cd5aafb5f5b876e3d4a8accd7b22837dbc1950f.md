## Merged Review

### Summary

All four reviewers find the paper’s core idea—exploiting invariance of the effect‑given‑cause distribution under changes in the cause prior—novel, and they consider the writing clear and the experiments extensive. Reviewer scores span 3, 5, 6, 6, indicating a split: Reviewers 1–3 see promising contributions (well‑structured, strong empirical results on large‑scale data, interesting theoretical findings like the DAG basis and minimal downsampling rate), while Reviewer 4 (score 3, confidence 5) raises fundamental concerns about the theoretical validity of the downsampling approximation and about the use of leaf nodes in the basis set. All reviewers mention missing details or missing justifications.

### Strengths

- The proposed method is novel (R3, R4) and the paper is well written with clear descriptions and motivations (R1, R2, R3). The accompanying figure helps understand the algorithm (R1).
- Practical, efficient causal discovery algorithm backed by theoretical findings, e.g., the basis of a DAG and the minimal downsampling rate (R2).
- Extensive experiments across three types of functional causal models, six baselines, and varying graph sizes (R1, R2, R3). The method demonstrates better scalability (up to 25× speedup) on large‑scale datasets (R2, from abstract).

### Weaknesses

#### Conceptual / Theoretical Gaps

- **Ancestor vs. parent invariance (R1, major):** If \(Z\) is an ancestor (not a parent) of \(X\), the invariance test (Eq. 1) may also yield zero variance. The method’s ability to distinguish true parents from ancestors is unclear; this point is central to the correctness of the parent‑identification step.
- **Leaf nodes in basis set (R4, fundamental):** Basis variables include leaf vertices. Changing the prior of a leaf node does not affect the distribution of its ancestors (causal flow is parent→child), so perturbing such basis variables cannot simulate interventions on causes of the leaf. This may prevent the method from detecting true causal relationships.
- **No theoretical guarantee for downsampling (R4, fundamental):** The downsampling technique approximates \(P(\text{effect}|\text{cause})\) under a modified prior, but observational data cannot replace true interventions. The paper provides no justification that the downsampled conditional equals the interventional conditional; the approximation is assumed without principled foundation or error bounds.
- **Necessary but not sufficient condition (R2):** Theorem 2 (and Eq. 1) gives only a necessary condition for a set \(Z\) to be the parent set. The claim that “when \(m\) is infinitely large the implication becomes bi‑directional” lacks rigorous justification. The convergence of variance to zero and the assumptions required (e.g., on the data distribution) are not explained; practical finite‑sample implications are also absent.

#### Algorithmic / Implementation Details Missing

- **Variance threshold (R1, R2):** The paper does not specify how to set a threshold for deciding when the variance is “zero” (Eq. 1). With noisy real data, a threshold is essential; its absence raises robustness concerns.
- **How priors \(P_i(\mathbf{X})\) are learned / constructed (R1, R2):** The method’s description of resampling to obtain datasets \(D_i\) with different source priors \(P_i(\mathbf{B})\) is vague. “Sampling from a simplex” is mentioned but not parameterized; the choice of categorical distributions and how \(P_i\) relate to the original data are unclear. (R1 also notes that the distinction between “we cannot compute \(P_i(X)\)” and “we can resample \(D_i\)” is not made clear.)
- **Downsampling mechanism (R1, R2):** “Downsampling without replacement” is not sufficiently detailed—how are data points selected, and what guarantees that the resulting sample reflects the intended prior \(P_i(\mathbf{X})\)? The “minimal downsampled rate” needs an intuitive explanation of its role in preserving causal relationships.
- **Cost of computing \(\phi(X)\) (R1):** It is not explained how expensive it is to compute \(\phi(X)\) (the invariance test), nor whether the process must be repeated after step (ii) of Theorem 3.

#### Experimental / Validation Limitations

- **Semi‑synthetic only (R2):** The bnlearn networks generate data from known graphs; the results do not validate the method on fully real‑world (purely observational) datasets. Real‑world validation would strengthen the claims.
- **Runtime on small / medium datasets (R2):** In Table 2, running times for small‑scale datasets (and even Munin, a large dataset) are not satisfactory. The authors should explain why the algorithm does not always yield the advertised speed benefit.

#### Presentation / Clarity

- **Delayed definitions (R1, major):** Many key concepts (e.g., source variables, basis set, the construction of the augmented bidirectional graph) are deferred until Section 4.2, making the initial sections hard to follow. A concrete example or visualization of the “maximum clique in an augmented bidirectional graph” is needed (R1).
- **Notation (R1):** In Eq. 2, \(P'\) is used without definition; the notation is ambiguous.
- **Typo (R4):** Theorem 5 refers to “Theorem 2” but should be “Theorem 4”.

#### Open Questions / Further Concerns

- **Applicability to time‑series or heterogeneous data (R2, R3):** The paper does not discuss whether the method extends to time‑series or heterogeneous datasets, nor the specific challenges involved.
- **Thresholding in practice (R1):** Is the variance always zero for true parents? If not, how should a threshold be chosen?
- **Computational cost (R1):** Must the invariance test be run for all possible parent sets?

**Reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 4 (score 3) considers the downsampling approximation and the leaf‑node issue to be fundamental theoretical flaws, while Reviewers 1–3 (scores 5,6,6) see the method as novel and practically promising, though they request the missing details listed above. The split suggests that without rigorous justification of the downsampling step and the basis‑set choice, the method’s validity remains contested.
