## Merged Review

### Summary  
The paper reinterprets preference alignment (RLHF, DPO) through an imitation-learning lens. It derives a new method, Direct Imitation Learning (DIL), whose objective minimizes reverse KL between an “optimal” policy and the current policy. DIL estimates a density-ratio reward via Bregman divergence and a change-of-variables trick to avoid adversarial training or explicit reward models. Experiments on UltraFeedback Binarized, Anthropic HH, and Reddit TL;DR with 7B-scale models show DIL generally outperforming DPO, SimPO, and other baselines on the Open LLM Leaderboard and AlpacaEval 2.0.

Reviewers split on the paper’s core claim: some find the imitation-learning connection novel and valuable, while others argue the derivations are largely known, the framing overstated, and the key approximation flawed.

### Strengths  
- **New mathematical connections** between imitation learning (reverse‑KL, forward‑KL, density‑ratio estimation) and existing RLHF/DPO objectives. Reviewer 3 considers these connections novel and valuable for the community. Reviewer 1 acknowledges the first part of the derivation (up to Eq.12–14) is well‑presented and improves understanding of how existing algorithms relate.  
- **Avoids complex components** – DIL integrates density‑ratio estimation [Sugiyama et al., 2012] and a change‑of‑variables trick [DPO] to directly learn the policy, eliminating adversarial training or separate reward models.  
- **Strong empirical results** on open, relevant benchmarks at a reasonable scale (7B models):  
  - DIL generally performs best against DPO, SimPO, IPO, KTO, and SLiC on the **Open LLM Leaderboard** (UltraFeedback Binarized, Mistral‑7B and Llama3‑8B bases).  
  - Higher win rates (GPT‑4 judged) against SFT, Chosen, and Average baselines on **Anthropic HH** and **Reddit TL;DR**.  
- DIL shows **better training dynamics** than SimPO: likelihood of chosen responses stays roughly constant while likelihood of rejected responses decreases (unlike SimPO where both drop). (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)  
- Paper is **well‑written and easy to follow** (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).  

### Weaknesses  
- **Core framing and derivation are contested.**  
  - Several reviewers argue the “RLHF secretly performs imitation learning” claim is overstated. Reviewer 1 notes that after Eq.12 the objective becomes a reverse‑KL on the current policy’s state distribution, which is **RL not imitation learning** (exploration, premature convergence are concerns). The temperature in Eq.13 appears without explanation. Reviewer 4 states the knowledge‑distillation claim is **incorrect**: Eq.13 is reverse‑KL (mode seeking), whereas knowledge distillation is forward‑KL (mean seeking). The paper should reframe as “imitating an optimal policy based on human preferences” (Reviewer 1).  
  - Reviewer 4 argues that **reward learning is not imitation learning**: training a reward model does not imitate an expert policy; the optimal policy can be parametrized by the reward model (already known from DPO).  
- **Lack of novelty** (Reviewer 4): DIL is “backtracking of the derivation of the DPO objective” – Eq.16 is the same as DPO Eq.14 without the partition function and assuming π_expert = π*. DIL merely redefines the DPO reward function excluding density‑ratio estimation.  
- **Key approximation is questionable** (Reviewer 1): To reach Eq.21/24, the paper assumes the reference policy π_ref can be approximated using only rejected responses y_l ~ π_ref(y|x) and discards terms on chosen examples. The reviewer states “This is simply a wrong assumption” and appears **ad‑hoc**.  
- Many derivations **already known** but not explicitly cited: e.g., the connection in Eq.9 between RL and energy‑based models was derived in DPO and in RL‑as‑probabilistic‑inference literature. (Reviewer 1)  
- **Minor / uncertain statistical significance** (Reviewer 3): Table 2 improvements are often smaller than 1 percentage point; no significance tests, standard errors, or confidence intervals are reported.  
- **Data efficiency unclear** (Reviewer 2): The paper claims DIL is more efficient but does not provide metrics on the number of preference pairs needed to reach a given performance compared to DPO/SimPO.  
- **Limited model scale** – experiments use models <10B parameters; scaling properties for larger models are not investigated (Reviewer 2).  
- **Missing or unexplained empirical details:**  
  - KTO baseline mentioned but not shown in tables; SLiC appears in tables but not discussed in baseline paragraph (Reviewer 3).  
  - Table 3 win rates compare against SFT, Chosen, Average but not directly against DPO or other baselines (Reviewer 3).  
  - Figure 3 (learning dynamics) does not include DPO, despite discussion referencing DPO (Reviewer 4, Q2).  
  - The “Chosen” and “Average” baselines in Table 3 are not defined (Reviewer 3).  
  - The Y-axis labels in Figures 1 and 3 are confusing (Reviewer 3).  
  - The base model (SFT) performances are not shown in Table 2 (Reviewer 3).  
- **Math exposition needs clarity** (Reviewer 3): especially in Section 4 – it is unclear that the final objective is Eq.24; the statement at the end of 4.3 (“can then be solved with any RL algorithms”) initially suggests RL is still needed. A summary box would help.  
- **Safety concern** (Reviewer 2): DIL suppresses dispreferred responses less than SimPO – does this make the model more prone to generating harmful responses?  
- **Preference modeling assumptions** (Reviewer 4, Q1): DIL claims to avoid Bradley‑Terry but introduces new reward objectives (LSIF, UKL, BCE) – does it still rely on preference-modeling assumptions?  
- Additional reviewer questions that overlap with missing comparisons or clarifications are covered above (e.g., MT‑Bench/Arena Hard results, Table 2 label Mistral‑7B should be Zephyr‑7B‑SFT, etc.).  

**Reviewer disagreement highlights:**  
- Reviewer 2 and 3 praise the imitation‑learning connection as novel and insightful; Reviewer 1 and 4 argue many derivations are known and the framing is problematic.  
- Reviewer 4 considers DIL not novel (a restatement of DPO); Reviewer 3 and 1 (partially) see it as a contribution.  
- The significance of empirical gains is viewed positively by Reviewers 2–4, while Reviewer 1 finds improvements minor and asks for qualitative analysis (e.g., KL evolution, examples where DIL excels).
