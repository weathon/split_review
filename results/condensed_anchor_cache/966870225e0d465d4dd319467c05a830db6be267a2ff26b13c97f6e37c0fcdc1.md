## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Noise-guided unsupervised Outlier Detector (NOD), which trains a binary classifier to distinguish data samples from uniform noise, using the classifier’s score as anomaly score. Theoretical results are provided to justify the approach. Experiments on 22 real-world datasets compared to 11 baselines claim a 30.6% average ROC_AUC improvement. The method is simple and easy to deploy, based on sparsity and maximum entropy reasoning.

### Strengths
- The problem is timely and challenging, and the motivation is compelling (R1, R3).
- NOD is simple and straightforward to implement and deploy, with a clear reasoning based on sparsity of outliers, maximum entropy of noise, and using noise as a stable reference (R2, R3).
- Theoretical results (lemmas, propositions) are provided to support the method (R2).
- Experimental results are comprehensive and appear solid, with NOD showing state-of-the-art performance across a diverse set of 22 real-world datasets against 11 baselines (R2, R3; however, one reviewer found that the improvements over REPEN are not statistically significant – see Weaknesses).
- Various analyses (ablation, etc.) are conducted and provide valuable insights (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (R1, R2, R3):** The core idea of using noise comparison is similar to REPEN and other prior work; components like negative example generation and smoothness prior already exist. The paper does not clearly distinguish its new contributions (R1). The theoretical proofs are not sufficiently grounded: Lemma 1 appears applicable to any noise but the proof depends on specifically generated uniform noise; the density function ρ is introduced without explaining its underlying distribution (R2, R3). The theory is hard to follow, lacks consistency, and the proofs in the appendix are not self-contained (R2, R3). Unclear terms such as “scaling factor”, “4√dim”, “dataset space”, and “tag” are used without definition (R3). Multiple typos and inconsistent notation (e.g., Proposition 1 vs Assumption 2, inconsistent use of y for both data point and label, “socre”, “donot”, “Equ.” vs “Eq.”) further detract from clarity (R3).  
- **Fundamental question about the classifier (R2):** Since both inliers and outliers in the original data are used as positive samples (label 1) in training, it is unclear how the binary classifier can distinguish between them. This undermines the logic of assigning higher anomaly scores to outliers.  
- **Experimental clarity and significance (R1, R2):** Baseline parameter settings, choices, and estimation techniques are not provided clearly, hampering reproducibility (R1). The performance improvement over REPEN (the second-best method) is not statistically significant; the results do not clearly show NOD outperforming baselines (R1). The paper does not explain why NOD is run 20 times, leaving uncertainty about result variance (R2).  
- **Figure 1 (R3):** The figure is unclear and its explanation is inadequate. The claimed disadvantage of kNN (assigning the same score to points A, B, C) is not convincingly demonstrated; from the figure, kNN appears to correctly classify those points as outliers.  
- **High-dimensional limitation (R3):** The uniform sampling approach may have limitations when applied to high-dimensional data (e.g., ImageNet), and the paper does not address this.

*Note:* One reviewer (R1, score 5) was more positive overall, finding the idea nicely motivated and the results supportive, while the other two reviewers (R2 and R3, score 3) had substantial concerns about theory clarity, experimental rigor, and novelty. This contrast is reflected in the strengths and weaknesses above.
