## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes HERON, a reinforcement learning framework that uses a ranking of reward factors (weak hierarchical preferences) to generate trajectory comparisons via a hierarchical decision tree with thresholds. These comparisons are then used to train a preference-based reward model (cross-entropy loss from the Bradley–Terry model) for policy learning. The method is evaluated on classic control, robotic control, multi-agent traffic light control, and LLM fine-tuning for code generation. The paper claims improved sample efficiency, robustness, and reduced human annotation cost relative to reward engineering.

### Strengths
- Paper is well structured and easy to follow.
- Extensive evaluation across diverse tasks: classic control, robotic control, multi-agent traffic light control, and large language model fine-tuning for code generation.
- Ranking is more interpretable and easier to design than specifying precise weights for reward factors.
- Empirically, RL trained with the ranking-based reward model outperforms baselines (reward engineering, uniform weighting).
- HERON fills a gap in RLHF by distilling rewards from relative feature rankings, reducing the human labeling burden.
- The method avoids costly human annotations (no need for pairwise trajectory preferences from humans).
- Conducts a number of ablations and analysis to understand strengths and weaknesses.
- Potentially leads to more principled and stable performance compared to ad-hoc reward engineering.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of justification for preference-based reward learning framework.** When a ranking of reward factors is given, it is unclear what the target of the reward learning is (what utility function the Bradley–Terry model is approximating) and why the standard RLHF framework (cross-entropy loss) is applied.
- **Limited personalization.** Humans can only specify the ranking of factors; they cannot control the trade-off between objectives. Two annotators with the same ranking but different desired emphasis on a factor will get the same reward function.
- **Less expressiveness than weighted combinations.** Some reward functions that can be expressed as weighted sums cannot be captured by a fixed ranking of factors. The paper does not discuss this limitation or when ranking is sufficient.
- **Assumptions about comparisons are unrealistic.** The method assumes a fixed precise threshold and that comparisons are always correct (as described on page 4). In realistic RLHF settings, human comparisons are noisy; the paper does not justify this assumption.
- **Method is essentially a form of reward engineering.** The algorithm finds coefficients (via the decision tree and preference learning) that respect the importance order of hand-crafted reward factors. This requires the same hand-crafted factors as reward engineering, which may be unavailable in domains where rewards are learned from raw observations (e.g., images, text).
- **Marginal improvement and inconsistent results.** Reported gains are 7.8% in robotics, 33% in traffic light control, and 3.9–5.2% in coding, with high standard deviation (e.g., Fig 3a and 3c). These improvements are not consistently significant across all tasks, raising doubts about practical impact.
- **Unconvincing comparisons to existing RLHF methods.** The paper does not compare to state-of-the-art preference-based RL methods such as PEBBLE or Meta-RewardNet. The claim that HERON is not an RLHF method does not negate the relevance of such comparisons. A classical baseline would be a (potentially linear) reward learnt from trajectory comparisons provided by human annotators—this would directly test the benefit of using ranking feedback over standard RLHF.
- **Unclear problem setting.** It is not explicitly stated what feedback is provided by humans (order of factors? or comparisons?) and what the assumptions are about the relationship between reward factors and the true reward.
- **Missing experiments and analyses.**
  - Learning curves on robotics environments are not included.
  - In Fig 3(c), the ground-truth reward line performs worse than the other methods; no explanation is given.
  - Hyperparameters for DDPG or Q-learning may need tuning to reduce high variance in Fig 3a and 3c.
  - Section 4.3 discusses flexibility but does not compare to the studied baselines like reward engineering.
- **Clarity issues.**
  - Algorithm 1 has a syntax error in the while condition (`l ≤ n μ = 0`).
  - Figure 3 legend obstructs the plot.
  - Algorithm 1 and Figure 2 are not essential for understanding the methodology and could be skipped in favor of a clearer description.
