## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies how adaptive level sampling affects zero-shot generalization (ZSG) in deep RL, focusing on two failure modes: instance overfitting and over-generalisation. It proposes measuring mutual information (MI) between the agent’s internal representation and training levels as a proxy for overfitting, and claims that adaptive strategies (e.g., prioritising levels by value loss) lower MI. The paper then introduces Self-Supervised Environment Design (SSED), which uses a VAE to generate new training levels, aiming to reduce MI while minimising distribution shift. Experiments are performed on Minigrid (gridworld) tasks; ProcGen is mentioned in conceptual discussion but not used for evaluation. One reviewer (R2) strongly disputed the empirical and theoretical grounding of the MI claims, while R1 and R3 were more positive about the insights and experimental results.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: Measuring environment diversity via mutual information to quantify generalization is novel; the concept of over-generalisation is also novel (R1, R2).
- **Proposed method (SSED)**: SSED is simple, makes intuitive sense, and empirically shows improved generalization on Minigrid; the use of a VAE to generate levels while controlling distribution shift is appreciated (R1, R2, R3).
- **Experimental results**: Results on Minigrid are strong and appear statistically significant; the ablation on the VAE component is informative (R1, R3).
- **Open-source contribution**: The maze environment is made publicly available (R2).
- **Theoretical framing**: The mutual information perspective in Section 4 provides an insightful discussion (R1, R3). R3 notes that the discussion makes the MI criterion natural.
- **Baselines**: The paper compares against state-of-the-art approaches (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Overpromising / unsubstantiated claims**: Reviewer 2 strongly criticizes that the paper repeatedly claims MI is measured, minimized, and correlated with overfitting (e.g., abstract, conclusions) but **no empirical measurement of MI is presented**. All evidence is circumstantial; Figure 2 does not directly measure MI. This is a fundamental lack of support for the paper’s main claim. (R2)
- **Undefined notations and unclear writing**: Both R2 and R3 note that many symbols are introduced without definition, e.g., $\text{MI}(i,\pi)$, $\hat V_t$, $V_t$, $V^\pi(s)$ (which is only defined for a POMDP, not the CMDP), $L$ in Theorem 3.1. The paper also gives inconsistent architecture details (non-recurrent vs LSTM). Section 3 is dense and hard to follow for non-experts; sub-sections would help. (R2, R3)
- **Rigour of MI–generalization connection**: The link between MI, generalization, and value-loss scoring is indirect and not rigorous. The decomposition $V_i^\pi = V^\pi + v_i^\pi$ is flawed because the two error terms could cancel, giving low error but high MI. Why sampling levels with highly negative classifier cross-entropies reduces MI is not explained. The bound in eq.(2) is the only clear connection; the logical leap from “high MI implies poor zero-shot transfer” to “low MI implies good generalization” is not justified. (R2)
- **Figure 2 interpretation is problematic**: First two rows (different scoring functions) are not significantly different, contradicting the claim that $S^V$ is better at maintaining low MI. The third row has smaller generalization gap but much lower performance; the result is confounded (lower performance trivially reduces the gap). The right plot is uninformative without knowing performance. (R2) Additionally, Fig. 2 uses a non-recurrent policy, while the main experiments use LSTM; the reason is not explained (R3).
- **Limited environment complexity**: All main experiments are on Minigrid; complex environments (e.g., ProcGen) are mentioned but not used. This raises concerns about real-world applicability and whether the generalization challenges in Minigrid (grid layouts, object placements) capture diverse real-world dynamics. (R1)
- **Citation and novelty of value-loss prioritization**: The claim that de-prioritising levels with low value loss was “first” proposed in Prioritised Level Replay is overstated. Many prior curriculum learning works use value functions for prioritization (e.g., goal generation, open-ended learning). The paper ignores substantial literature in curriculum learning, multi-task learning, and goal generation that shares the same high-level principle. (R3)
- **Missing analysis / theoretical proof**: The hypothesis in Section 4 is presented as intuition rather than a proven result. A formal proof or more rigorous analysis would strengthen the paper. (R3) R2 also questions the approximation in Lemma 4.1: it assumes levels are constantly sampled with $p(i)$, but the method changes the sampling distribution. (R2)
- **Alternative approach not explored**: The paper only uses MI to guide level sampling, not to directly shape the policy (e.g., by rewarding actions that reduce MI). Why this alternative is not considered is not addressed. (R2)
