## Merged Review

### Summary
MMAU is a new benchmark for evaluating large audio-language models on complex multimodal understanding tasks, covering speech, environmental sounds, and music. It includes 10,000 human-annotated audio-question-response pairs requiring information extraction and reasoning across 27 distinct skills. The authors evaluate 18 open-source and proprietary models, with the best (Gemini Pro v1.5) achieving only 52.97% accuracy, highlighting significant room for improvement.

### Strengths
- The topic is very important: a unified audio benchmark analogous to MMLU/MMMU in text/vision is critically needed, and this paper addresses that gap.
- The benchmark is large-scale and comprehensive, covering 27 distinct skills across three domains (speech, environmental sounds, music) with deeper, more difficult auditory reasoning tasks than previous benchmarks.
- It is the first benchmark to focus on reasoning and expert-level knowledge extraction in audio, setting it apart from prior work limited to foundational processing tasks.
- The authors benchmarked 18 existing LALMs, a non-trivial effort given the difficulty of using some open-source repositories. The resulting performance comparisons and detailed error-type analysis (perceptual, reasoning, etc.) across models and domains provide valuable insights into current model limitations and directions for improvement.
- The benchmark design is reliable: real-world human experts were involved, multiple rounds of human review were conducted, GPT-4 was used to enrich options, and detailed prompts are provided in the appendix.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with extensive information in the appendix.

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation methodology (multiple-choice format bias):** The benchmark relies solely on multiple-choice questions. This may not reflect true model capabilities, especially for models not post-trained on MCQ formats, and could advantage contrastive models. The paper suggests including open-ended questions as an alternative. Moreover, the evaluation itself uses simple string matching to determine correctness, which is overly simplistic for open-ended model outputs. More robust methods (e.g., using an LLM judge) and verification via human annotation on a subset are needed. The response quality of models like SALAMONN and Qwen2-Audio is significantly influenced by prompting, raising concerns about whether the evaluation accurately measures limitations.
- **Data selection and quality concerns:** The data are sourced from 13 datasets, but the paper lacks a clear explanation of how the 10,000 data points were selected from these large collections. The initial screening and quality/diversity assurance process is not described. Although expert annotation is mentioned, there is no detailed description of how bias was controlled during annotation, nor elaboration on quality checks and bias mitigation in Appendix C.2.
- **Claim about multi-skill tasks not supported:** The paper claims tasks may require multiple skills, but a review of Appendix H shows most tasks can be completed using a single skill. This claim lacks precision and is not sufficiently supported. The distinct separation between information extraction and reasoning skills may oversimplify tasks that inherently require a combination.
- **Missing evaluations:** 
  - The benchmark focuses on short audio (~10 seconds); long audio understanding is not tested despite providing more contextual information.
  - Few-shot evaluation is not included. Given that few-shot is common in LLM benchmarking, the authors should run few-shot on representative models and report the zero-shot vs. few-shot gap, especially for models not post-trained on multiple-choice questions.
  - OpenAI’s real-time API and voice mode API are not tested (possibly due to timing or cost). Running on a hard subset to compare with Google’s model would increase the paper’s value.
- **Insufficient analysis of model differences:** More discussion is needed on why different models perform better on specific benchmarks – e.g., whether differences stem from training data (Google’s models vs. academia), architecture/training (early fusion, late fusion, discrete token vs. continuous embedding vs. text input), and how to improve.
- **Training/test data overlap:** Datasets like AudioSet and AudioSet-strong, which are commonly used to train ALMs, are among the sources. This overlap could skew results. Clarification or experiments to rule this out are needed.
- **Missing details on synthetic data generation and difficulty classification:** The paper does not explain what tasks the synthesized data targets, how it is synthesized, or how question difficulty (“easy”, “medium”, “hard”) is determined. Since some skills yield consistently poor performance regardless of difficulty, difficulty may be better classified by skill type rather than by question.
- **Unclear purpose and impact of specific prompts:** The prompt in Appendix L, where the model is required to generate a correct option, lacks clear justification and its impact on evaluation is not discussed.
- **Section 5.2 (noise experiment) details missing:** The authors should provide the breakdown by category and clarify whether ground truth text of speech is provided to the model, as it is unclear how models could perform any meaningful classification with Gaussian noise.

*Note: Reviewer 4 is notably more critical, raising concerns about data selection bias, annotation quality control, evaluation simplicity, and the validity of the multi-skill claim – points that are explicitly preserved above.*
