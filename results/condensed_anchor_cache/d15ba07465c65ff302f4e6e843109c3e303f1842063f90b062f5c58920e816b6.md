## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Continuous Ensemble Forecasting, a method for diffusion-based weather forecasting that replaces the fixed noise variable with a stochastic process having continuous sample trajectories. This allows generating temporally consistent ensemble trajectories in parallel without autoregressive steps, and can be combined with autoregressive rollouts for arbitrary fine temporal resolution. The approach is evaluated on global weather forecasting and achieves competitive probabilistic results. The use of probability flow ODE from diffusion models to achieve smooth, continuous trajectories is the core contribution.

### Strengths
- The proposed method is presented clearly (R1).
- Leveraging the probability flow ODE of the noise in diffusion models to generate smooth and continuous trajectories is an interesting and meaningful improvement compared to autoregressive methods (R3).
- The model eliminates traditional autoregressive steps during sampling, allowing high temporal resolution and improved computational efficiency – a challenging feature in weather prediction (R2).
- The application of diffusion models to meteorological forecasting is exciting (R2).
- The method exhibits a certain level of novelty (R4).
- Authors provide intensive experiments on their method (R3).
- Weather forecasting is an important problem (R4).
- Some of the figures in the paper look good (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing related works and baselines**:
  - No discussion of nonautoregressive sequence forecasting by diffusion models (e.g., CSDI, NeurIPS'21; Non-autoregressive Conditional Diffusion Models, ICML'23) (R1).
  - No reference to fast sampling of diffusion models via operator learning (Zheng et al., 2023); the paper does not clarify how the proposed sampling diverges from such approaches or whether they could be combined (R2).
  - Missing comparisons with recent spatiotemporal models: Dyfusion, Alternator, Mamba, s-mamba (used for weather/sea surface temperature) (R2).
  - Comparisons with previous SOTA ML models are limited; FourCastNet and ClimaX have public code but are not included (R3, R4). According to Table 1, the ARCI-24/6h model (if understood correctly) is not optimal under most settings (R4).
- **Insufficient formal/mathematical description**:
  - The “regularity conditions” (Line 234) are not specified rigorously; a formal mathematical description with proof or empirical validation is needed for claims about trajectory continuity and temporal stability (R2).
  - The assumption behind the customized ODE in Eq. (2) is unclear: why is there a linear noise level \( \sigma \)? What if \( \sigma^2 \)? More explanation needed (R3).
  - Claims about fixed noise across time steps and ODE-based interpolation are under-supported; no ablation study or mathematical evidence to show the impact (R2). Also unclear how ODE solver interpolation compares to alternative methods (R2).
  - No mathematical derivation for the training objective and loss function; a step-by-step derivation with rationales for modifications relative to standard diffusion models would clarify the theoretical foundation (R2).
  - The paper should provide formal proofs or derivations for key claims (e.g., trajectory continuity, temporal stability) rather than relying solely on empirical observations (R2).
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:
  - The term “Continuous” in the title and Section 4 name seems to refer to temporal continuity (arbitrary fine temporal resolution), not “continuous ensemble forecasting”; rephrasing is needed to avoid confusion (R3).
  - Figure 1 lacks sufficient information to clearly highlight differences from previous methods or how the proposed method overcomes limitations; it deserves a more precise description in its caption (R3, R4).
  - The introduction does not clearly articulate the limitations of current methods or how the proposed method addresses them (R4).
  - The Methods section mixes descriptions of existing methods with the new method, making it hard to discern the exact contribution (R4).
  - Figure 2 should provide a clearer depiction of the methodology (R4).
  - Notation issues: the letter ‘k’ is used for different representations (Eq. 1 and Alg. 1); use superscripts or subscripts consistently to denote time points; in Eq. (2) it is unclear why \( z(1) \) corresponds to \( X[k+1] \) (since 1 is a diffusion step, not noise level); \( v_i^k \) in Algorithm 2 is not defined (R3).
- **Experimental and evaluation shortcomings**:
  - Only two of the five forecasting variables are shown in Table 2; results for all five variables should be included for a complete assessment (R2).
  - No error bars or confidence intervals due to computational limitations; at least a subset of key results with error bars would improve robustness (R2).
  - The authors mention diffusion models are computationally expensive, but no computational efficiency analysis is provided (R3).
  - No code is available to help readers understand the paper (R3).
  - The contribution listed in the introduction seems generic, something any weather forecasting model could claim (R4).
- **Methodological questions and missing details**:
  - Why is a stochastic process better than random Gaussian vectors for nonautoregressive generation? (R1)
  - What is the formal definition of ensemble forecasting? (R1)
  - What is the input size (number of variables) for the model? Are univariate forecasting tasks performed for each variable? (R1)
  - How are \( n_{\text{ens}} \) trajectories fused to obtain final predictions? (R1)
  - Does Algorithm 2 generalize to other diffusion models or continuous normalizing flows (flow matching, stochastic interpolants)? Discussion is needed (R2).
  - Is there a significant difference between weather forecasting and general time series forecasting? (R1)
  - What is the rationale for fixing noise across time steps? Any sensitivity analysis? (R2)
