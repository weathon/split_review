## Merged Review

### Summary
The EquiLoPO Network proposes an equivariant neural network for volumetric data that achieves analytical equivariance to continuous SO(3) rotations while allowing unconstrained trainable filters. It uses group convolutions based on irreducible representations (Wigner-D matrices) as Fourier basis and introduces a local activation function—a polynomial approximation of ReLU applied in the harmonic domain—that prevents the introduction of higher-order harmonics and preserves equivariance. The model is evaluated on MedMNIST3D datasets, outperforming several baselines. Reviewers rated the paper 3, 5, 5, 6, 6, indicating mixed opinions: one reviewer (score 3) criticized the experimental rigor and presentation, while others appreciated the theoretical contributions, particularly the novel activation function.

### Strengths
- **Novel activation function**: The polynomial approximation of ReLU that limits higher-order harmonics is a significant contribution. It addresses a long-standing challenge in equivariant networks, where good nonlinearities applicable in harmonic space have been sought (R1, R4, R3). This distinguishes the approach from norm-based activations (gating, global 2-norm) and provides a new path for pointwise operations in the spectral domain (R1, R4).
- **Sound theoretical derivation**: The mathematical framework is rigorous, with correct treatment of equivariance and group convolution in the Fourier basis (R1, R3, R4).
- **Good written quality and clarity** overall; the paper is clear at each step about the authors’ intentions (R4, R3). Detailed analysis of softmax, constant and adaptive nonlinear activations is included (R2).
- **Comprehensive evaluation on MedMNIST3D** with ablation studies showing the effectiveness of individual components, especially the activation function (R3, R5).
- **True rotational equivariance on continuous SO(3)** without discrete approximations or filter constraints, offering a new methodological benchmark for volumetric data analysis (R5).
- **Potential for broad impact** in medical imaging and other domains requiring 3D data processing (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Context and literature gaps**: The introduction and related work lack sufficient background and references; many important connections (e.g., regular vs steerable group convolutions, canonicalization methods, prior 3D→6D→3D works) are missing or confined to the appendix (R1, R2, R3, R4). The distinction between group convolutional and steerable networks is presented as if they are separate, but they are better understood as regular vs steerable representations (R1, R4). Missing citations: Kaba et al. on learned canonicalization; Andrearczyk & Depeursigne for 3D→6D→3D convolution (R1). No citations in the Related Work section (R4). Motivation for combining the two approaches is insufficiently justified (R3). Comparison with the latest equivariant literature beyond MedMNIST is needed (R3).
- **Clarity and exposition issues**: The main body relies heavily on the appendix for equations and explanations, making the paper difficult to follow unless the reader is already acquainted with equivariance (R2, R4). Equations 1 and 2 may have sign inconsistencies; integration measure notation is inconsistent (R1). The variable *h* is used for multiple convolution operations, causing confusion (R2). Equation 5 has formatting issues (R2). Notation for the local (pointwise) operations should be introduced in the main text, not only the supplement (R4). “Orientation pooling” (line 90) is undefined (R4). It is unclear why a direct product R³ × SO(3) is used instead of the semi-direct product SE(3) (R4). The scaling factor in Equation 9 requires explanation and a sensitivity analysis (R5). The claim that linear layers “detect probabilities” (line 155) is unsupported (R4). Minor grammatical errors (lines 34, 280, 284) (R2). Figure 1 is not informative; a more detailed diagram of 6D feature map construction would help (R2).
- **Experimental completeness and fairness**: No direct evaluation of rotational equivariance (e.g., measuring degree of equivariance under arbitrary rotations) (R2, R5). Missing ablation comparing the proposed polynomial activation to standard pointwise activations (ReLU, GELU, Swish) applied after inverse Fourier transform (R4). Also missing comparison to the “inverse Fourier activation” approach [Fourier → ReLU → inverse Fourier], a natural baseline for bypassing norm-based constraints in Fourier space (R1). No ablation of other components (normalization, pooling, convolution parameters) beyond the activation function (R2). Table 1 does not report standard deviations or number of seeds, making significance unclear (R2, R4). Architectures have vastly different parameter counts; it is unclear if results are FLOP-adjusted or controlled for computational cost (R4). Non-equivariant baselines may not have been trained with data augmentation, creating an unfair comparison (R4). Class imbalance handling is not described (R5). Hyperparameter tuning details (learning rate, batch size, optimization) are missing (R5). Only 28×28×28 voxel datasets are tested—a toy problem; generalization to higher-resolution real medical data (CT, MRI) and scaling behavior are not demonstrated (R5). The model is not tested on other 3D datasets (e.g., point clouds) to evaluate broader applicability (R1). No selection guidance is given for the seven configurations in Table 1; end users cannot choose the best model for arbitrary datasets (R2, R5).
- **Computational cost and practical deployment**: No detailed reporting of FLOPs, memory consumption, training/inference time compared to standard 3D CNNs or alternative equivariant models (R3, R5). Memory and time for 6D feature maps are not provided (R2). It is unclear how the model’s complexity scales with input size or rotational resolution (R5). The computational demands of 3D rotational data augmentation are negligible compared to the cost of 6D convolutions, contrary to a motivation in the paper (R4). Batch normalization adaptations for 6D data are not sufficiently explained (R5). Limitations of the continuous convolution relative to discrete methods (e.g., handling anisotropic features, sparse data) are not discussed (R5). The polynomial degree choice for the activation function is not justified; whether constant vs adaptive coefficients is more suitable is not explored (R5). The terms “pointwise” and “local” are used without clear differentiation (R4).
