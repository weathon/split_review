## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies why extreme dataset distillation (fewer synthetic samples than classes) enables effective one-step gradient learning, by analyzing distilled data instances and the cost surfaces they produce. Experiments span MNIST, CIFAR-10, cart-pole, and Atari Centipede. The authors argue that distilled datasets mimic features of the real data and shape cost surfaces that guide models from initialization into local minima of the true task’s cost surface. Two reviewers (scores 5) found the problem interesting and the presentation clear; three reviewers (scores 3) found the analysis lacking in rigor and the performance insufficient.

### Strengths
+ The paper is easy to follow, with a clear introduction, well-laid-out contributions, and a pleasant writing style (R1, R5).
+ It covers multiple domains (vision: MNIST, CIFAR-10; reinforcement learning: cart-pole, Centipede), providing a task-agnostic distillation algorithm (Algorithm 1) with detailed parameter settings and loss functions (R1, R2, R3, R5).
+ It is the first to investigate dataset distillation in a reinforcement learning scenario (R3).
+ The in-depth examination of cost surfaces offers a valuable perspective on how distilled data guides models to local minima; for simpler tasks the analysis improves interpretability (R3).
+ The inner workings of dataset distillation are understudied; this work provides important insights for better algorithm design (R4).
+ Demonstrates that distilling with one gradient step can achieve 28% accuracy on CIFAR-10 using fewer than 1 image per class via soft-labeling (R4).
+ The idea of exploring why single-batch distillation works is interesting and novel (R5).

### Weaknesses
* **Missing related work and alternative methods:** The paper does not compare with or discuss important prior work. Specifically, [1] (Yang et al.) analyzes dataset distillation in depth; [2] makes multi-step BPTT work; and loss-curvature matching (Shin et al.) provides a more detailed analysis of loss curvature (R1, R2, R4). Only one distillation method is studied; alternatives such as DATM, SDC, IDC, trajectory matching, or other meta-learning algorithms are not considered, limiting understanding of how different distillation objectives impact cost surfaces and generalizability (R2, R3, R4, R5). Without comparisons, it is unclear whether the findings translate to other distillation algorithms (R4).
* **Insufficient analysis of local minima justification:** The method for claiming that distilled data leads to a local minimum is not sound. Visualization via random vector projections was originally designed to capture non-convexity and is insufficient for understanding the optimization trajectory; PCA direction (section 7.2 of Li et al.) or quantitative measures of sharpness (second derivative/Hessian) are needed to distinguish local minima from saddle points or flat regions (R4, R5). No metric is provided to compare loss landscapes; color maps are shared but ranges differ, making visual comparison unreliable (R5). The claim of “minima-matching” is not adequately explained (R5).
* **Subpar performance relative to existing methods:** One-step distillation achieves accuracy far below full-dataset training: MNIST 14.1% worse, CIFAR-10 35.7% worse, Centipede reward gap 1084, cart-pole gap 134.1 (R4, R5). These differences contradict the introduction’s statement that “the distillation-trained model should perform comparably to the model trained on the original task” (R5). Compared to other methods like BPTT (49% with 10 examples) or Trajectory Matching (46% with 10 examples), one-step performance is substantially lower (R4).
* **Lack of theoretical depth:** The paper relies heavily on empirical observations and does not provide a theoretical framework explaining why an appropriate cost surface emerges during distillation (R2). Scalability issues for larger models/datasets are not discussed; computational complexity of generating and analyzing cost surfaces as dimensionality grows is not addressed (R3).
* **Limited experimental validation:** Experiments use only simple datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, cart-pole, Centipede); more complex tasks (high-resolution images, NLP, time-series) and larger datasets are needed to assess robustness and scalability (R2, R3). For CIFAR-10, the same architecture as MNIST is used, which does not produce good accuracy even when trained conventionally; more common architectures (ResNet, VGG) should be considered (R5). Only the average of 1000 models is reported without standard deviation; conventional models are trained once instead of averaged; train accuracy is not reported (R5). The CIFAR-10 single-batch distilled images are not shown (R5).
* **Notation and presentation errors:** The paper does not use the correct ICLR template (missing “Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2025”) (R3). Figure 1 mixes notations (T0, T_0, TO) (R3). Line 161 starts with a stray “+” (R3, R5). Figure 2 has incorrect directions (both images show “3.1% left, 96.9% right” regardless of pole direction) (R5). Figure 3a softmax probabilities are hard to read via color gradient; values should be reported (R5). Figure 4 direction (rows vs. columns) is unclear (R5). Figure 8b makes it difficult to distinguish initialization from distil-trained; distil-trained appears on a higher part of the landscape (R5).
* **Unsubstantiated claims:** The abstract states “conventional wisdom suggests that single-step learning is not generalizable,” but prior work [1] shows full-batch gradient descent with explicit regularization can achieve comparable accuracy on CIFAR-10, so this claim is unsupported (R5). Lines 48–49 lack a citation (R5). Line 167 claims “even poor distillations function similarly to well-converged ones” but evidence is weak given large accuracy drops (R5).
* **Missing comparison between one-step and multi-step distillations:** The paper does not explore how properties like sample diversity or loss landscape sharpness differ when the number of gradient steps is increased (R1, R4). It is unclear whether one-step learning changes the property that distilled data captures early trajectories rather than a specific local minimum (R4). Smaller step sizes for CIFAR-10 landscapes are not shown (R4). No combination with existing loss-curvature analysis is attempted (R2).
* **Unaddressed extension questions:** The paper does not discuss how the approach handles high-dimensional data (e.g., complex images) or whether it could be extended to NLP or time-series (R3).
* **Note on reviewer disagreement:** Two reviewers (scores 5) were more positive about the paper’s clarity and exploration of RL, but still raised significant concerns about missing comparisons and theoretical rigor. Three other reviewers (scores 3) found the work insufficient in soundness, experimental validation, and practical relevance.
