## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses the problem of selecting from multiple teachers in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). It introduces the Hidden Utility Bandit (HUB) framework, formalizing differences in teacher rationality, expertise, and cost. The authors propose the Active Teacher Selection (ATS) algorithm, which maintains a belief over utilities to decide when and which teacher to query. Experimental validation is performed on two domains: paper recommendation and COVID-19 vaccine testing, with comparisons to baseline algorithms. Overall, the problem is considered timely and important, but the work has notable weaknesses in theoretical guarantees, clarity, and practical applicability of assumptions.

### Strengths
- **Originality and relevance**: The problem of actively selecting among multiple teachers in RLHF is novel and underexplored; existing work typically assumes a single teacher. The paper makes a valuable contribution by formalizing this setting.
- **Interesting empirical findings**: The observation that less reliable teachers can sometimes provide richer information (e.g., via noisier comparisons that help estimate utility differences) is insightful and aligns with known intuitions.
- **Problem formulation**: The HUB framework and the use of a POMDP to model teacher dynamics provides a principled mathematical foundation; the soundness of the overall approach is appreciated (Reviewer 1 rated quality as 3/4).
- **Empirical demonstrations**: Experiments on paper recommendation and COVID-19 vaccine testing show that ATS outperforms baselines; the paper also discusses how to infer teacher noise parameters when item utilities are known.
- **Clarity of exposition**: Several reviewers found the paper generally well written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient theoretical guarantees**: No regret bounds are provided for ATS. The analysis presented consists of only two lemmas that feel preliminary; a proper comparison (e.g., to a fixed single teacher, uniform random selection, or highest-competence teacher) with explicit assumptions and convergence properties is needed. (Reviewer 1, 2, 3)
- **Notation and presentation issues**:
  - Sections 2 and 3 are ambiguous and overloaded with notation; e.g., the distinction between \(\mathcal{D}\) and \(\mathbb{D}\) is unclear, and definitions for \(\mathcal{U}^*\) and \(\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{C}*}\) are missing. (Reviewer 1, 2)
  - Rewards and costs are mixed; the teacher query cost should decrease utility (possibly using \(-f^{\beta_t}\) or \(1/f^a\)), and \(f^{\beta_t}\) is ill-defined. (Reviewer 2; also noted by Reviewer 1)
  - Figures 3.b and 3.c lack legends. (Reviewer 2)
  - Table/figures readability could be improved (Figures 2 and 7). (Reviewer 4)
- **POMDP framing questioned**:
  - The use of a discounted cumulative value in the POMDP is not well justified; the problem appears to be a simpler partially observable bandit with preference feedback. (Reviewer 2)
  - The advantage of the POMDP reformulation over a contextual bandit is unclear, and related POMDP literature (e.g., Boots–Siddiqi–Gordon, arxiv.org/abs/2006.12484, arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13081.pdf) and solution techniques (value iteration, policy iteration, Monte Carlo tree search) are not discussed. (Reviewer 1, 4)
- **Lack of exploration–exploitation and cost trade-off analysis**: There is no discussion of how the algorithm balances immediate arm rewards vs. information gain from teacher queries. If query costs are low, the algorithm might over-query the teacher and hinder learning about arm utilities, leading to suboptimal long-term performance. (Reviewer 2)
- **Insufficient detail on the proposed method**: The ATS algorithm is described very briefly; it is unclear whether the policy is learned on the fly or requires prior simulations. (Reviewer 4)
- **Applicability of assumptions**:
  - The assumption that all teachers share the same utility function is not explicitly stated; this is questionable for real applications (e.g., paper recommendations where preferences differ). (Reviewer 4)
  - The space in which the reward is learned is assumed discrete; the formulation lacks clear connections to real-world RLHF (e.g., what constitutes an “arm” when optimizing language model responses?). (Reviewer 1, 4)
  - The experimental setups feel artificial; e.g., waiting a day after a preference query to recommend an article, or immediate vaccine results. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**:
  - No comparison to a simple contextual bandit variant that adds a binary “query or not” action based on state. (Reviewer 3)
  - No two-stage baseline: first perform active teacher selection to learn the reward, then run a bandit to maximize cumulative reward. (Reviewer 4)
  - The number of teacher queries used in experiments should be reported to demonstrate learning efficiency. (Reviewer 3)
- **Related work omissions**: The paper does not cite literature on active annotation/teacher selection in crowdsourcing and active learning (e.g., Rodrigues et al. 2014, Long et al. 2013, Murugesan & Carbonell 2017, *Human-in-the-Loop Machine Learning* 2021). (Reviewer 4)
- **Minor issues**:
  - On page 3, \(\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}^1\times\ldots\) should likely be \(\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^1\times\ldots\). (Reviewer 1)
  - A stray question mark appears on page 3. (Reviewer 1)
  - The term RLHF in the title may be misleading, as the focus is reward learning rather than full RLHF. (Reviewer 4)
  - If \(\Delta_{ij}\) or \(\mathcal{U}\) is known (as assumed in Section 3.2), the learner could simply estimate multinomial parameters and find the optimal arm; the paper does not clarify what learning remains. (Reviewer 2)
  - In real-world settings both \(\beta\) (teacher noise) and \(\mathcal{U}\) (arm utilities) are unknown; the paper does not explain how to jointly handle this. (Reviewer 2)
  - The idea of estimating \(\beta\) from a preference dataset is noted, but if such data exists the reward could be learned directly. (Reviewer 4)
