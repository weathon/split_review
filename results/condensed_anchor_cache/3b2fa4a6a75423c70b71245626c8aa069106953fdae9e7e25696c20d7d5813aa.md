## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the impact of Theory of Mind (ToM) on cooperation among LLM agents and proposes a coalition matching algorithm based on belief alignment to improve cooperative performance. Experiments are conducted in iterative programming (HumanEval, MBPP), debate, and logical/general reasoning (AQUA-RAT, MMLU) environments using various LLMs.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and related work**: The paper is well motivated, with clear and concise related work (R1, R3). The introduction effectively motivates the research problem (R3).
- **Novelty of idea**: The idea of guiding multi-agent collaboration through ToM and belief alignment is novel (R3). The underlying questions—whether higher ToM might hinder cooperation and how to form coalitions based on belief alignment—are interesting and likely to have real-world relevance (R4). (R4 caveated that similar ideas may have been studied in game theory outside LLMs.)
- **Empirical results**: The matching mechanism shows promising improvements in Pass@1 rates on HumanEval (R1, R2). Clear increases in accuracy are observed in logic and reasoning problems compared to existing methods (R2). The empirical results for problem solving and general reasoning are similarly promising (R1).
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The authors conduct comprehensive evaluations across diverse task scenarios and base LLMs, presenting both quantitative and qualitative results (R3). The paper studies a wide variety of tasks, models, and frameworks for multi-LLM problem-solving (R4).
- **Clarity**: The paper is easy to follow, with the appendix providing helpful detail (R2). The presentation of results is largely clear (R4). (R3, however, found the paper less comprehensive; see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
- **ToM formulation and alignment measurement** (severe from R3, R4; moderate from R1, R2):
    - The ToM formulation (Section 4.1) deviates from common higher-order ToM: agents at level-k only receive their own level-(k-1) belief, not beliefs of other agents, limiting nested mental state reasoning (R3). Level-2 ToM responses in the appendix appear based on a misunderstanding of other agents’ actions, undermining the validity of the comparison—level-2 ToM is designed to best respond to level-1, not level-0 agents (R4). R4 suggests benchmarking against a 0-level PM and settings where agents are higher-level reasoners.
    - The alignment measurement (Section 4.2) fails to distinguish different types of higher-order ToM inferences, e.g., “what I think A is thinking about B’s action” vs. “what I think C is thinking about B’s action” yield the same score (R3). The use of subtraction notation is misleading; the distance measure should be explicitly defined (R4). The belief model is vague and alignment scores from LLMs appear arbitrary—e.g., “object-oriented” is highly valued despite not appearing in the belief model (R1). The reliability of LLM-based alignment measurements is unaddressed (R3 question). The method is not generalizable to non-LLM environments (R2 question; R2 suggests title clarify LLM-specific scope).
- **Scalability and generality** (R1, R2, R3):
    - Coalition formation is NP-hard; no ideas for future scalability are offered (R1). Generating beliefs about all other agents creates additional overhead (R1). The algorithm is highly dependent on prompt design, making generalization to different tasks ambiguous (R3).
- **Matching algorithm underspecification** (R3, R4):
    - The algorithm implicitly assumes each agent is matched to a single partner (singleton μ(i) in Equation 2) but does not define preferences over sets of partners for larger coalitions; stability under set preferences is not addressed (R4). Algorithm 1 line 9 does not specify what happens when no partner within tolerance ε exists (does the agent become singleton?). No proof of convergence is provided; with skill-based preferences, cyclic matchings are possible (R4). The skill levels α_i in Equation 4 are not specified—where they come from, and matching on a single task is overly simplistic for complementary team creation; this is critical for reproducibility (R4). What happens when an agent is assigned to cooperate with a given partner (Algorithm 1 line 8) is not clearly defined per scenario, potentially introducing evaluation bias (R3).
- **Experimental design and evaluation metrics** (R2, R3, R4):
    - Multi-agent setups are not clearly described: number of agents, action/observation spaces, collaboration mechanism (R3). The interactive programming scenario appears centralized—only the PM uses ToM and makes decisions, limiting applicability to decentralized multi-agent cooperation; the value of ToM is less salient here (R3). ToM levels of negative debating agents are assumed 0 but not stated (R4). How prompts change after coalition formation is not explained (R4). Coalition sizes and agent counts are missing (R4). No error bars or standard errors are reported (R4).
    - In the debate environment, the baseline (both sides no-ToM) yields a 65.45% affirmative win rate, raising concern that the advantage may stem from this bias rather than the method; results with the proposed method on the negative side should be shown (R2). The ToM-with-matching condition uses two LLM agents to form arguments while other conditions use one, so performance gains could come from having more agents (self-reflection) rather than the matching algorithm; this lack of control isolates the effect poorly (R3).
    - The metrics “Fraction of Trust Members” (FTM) and coalition stability are optimization objectives of the algorithm, not independent measures of cooperation—using them to quantify “cooperation trend” introduces circularity (R3). The claim that higher FTM indicates a more cooperative agent is not generally true (belief alignment ≠ cooperation in zero-sum games) (R4). The claimed conclusion that higher ToM may not improve cooperation conflicts with Tables 3 and 4 where ToM improves performance (R3). The paper should use task completion rate or efficiency instead (R3). The acronym FTM is confusing and overused (R2).
- **Scope of cooperation** (R4):
    - The paper primarily studies a single PM agent with ToM managing others, not multi-agent cooperation among agents with ToM. Phrasing should reflect that ToM improves facilitation/management skills of a single agent rather than multi-agent cooperation (R4). The claim that “agents with higher ToM may not necessarily exhibit better cooperative behavior” is derived from a setting where only the PM uses ToM (R4). The multi-agent programming environment may not require much ToM, so low ToM being more successful could be due to lower complexity; the authors are asked whether this trend holds in other environments (R1 question).

**Reviewer disagreement**: Positive reviewers (R1 score 6, confidence 2; R2 score 6, confidence 3) found the empirical results promising and the presentation clear, but raised concerns about scalability and belief model robustness. Negative reviewers (R3 score 3, confidence 4; R4 score 3, confidence 4) considered the ToM formulation misaligned, the algorithm underspecified, the experimental design lacking control, and the claims unsupported by the metrics. The negative reviewers had higher confidence and raised more fundamental issues.
