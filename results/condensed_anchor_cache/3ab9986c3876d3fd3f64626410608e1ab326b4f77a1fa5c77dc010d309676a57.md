## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how to adapt large-scale pretrained video generative models (specifically AnimateDiff) to enable novel text-conditioned robotic tasks in specific environments using small amounts of in-domain data. Three adaptation techniques are investigated: direct fine-tuning on expert video demonstrations, subject customization using only a few static images, and probabilistic adaptation (score composition with a small in-domain model). Additionally, inverse probabilistic adaptation is introduced. The adapted video models are used as either visual planners (planning future frames) or policy supervisors (providing rewards). Evaluation is conducted in simulation on MetaWorld-v2 (16 robot arm tasks), DeepMind Control Suite (2 tasks), and a dog/humanoid environment. The best performing method differs per environment: subject customization/direct fine-tuning for dog/humanoid and inverse probabilistic adaptation for MetaWorld. One reviewer (score 5) found the scope limited and results unsurprising, while others (score 6) were more positive about the problem framing and experimental scope.

### Strengths
- The problem of efficiently integrating large-scale video generation into robot policy learning is timely and important, especially with the rise of world models in robotics.
- The paper is well motivated and tackles a nuanced question (how to adapt video models with limited in-domain data) that is often glossed over.
- The writing is clear, easy to follow, and implementation details are thoroughly stated.
- The experimental design is thorough, covering both policy learning and video planning across seen and unseen tasks.
- The study is transparent about where methods underperform (e.g., 0% success rates for several tasks).
- The limitation section is thoughtfully constructed.
- Subject customization (adapting using only static images) and inverse probabilistic adaptation are novel contributions.
- Interesting observations are made about the relationship between visual appearance and task success rate, motivating the need for real-world evaluation.
- The discussion of pros and cons of the policy supervision evaluation approach (Sec. 3.2.2) is insightful.

### Weaknesses
- The scope of the paper is limited; the approach to generating visual targets is relatively straightforward, and the results are not surprising given that a web-scale pretrained generator naturally produces better images/videos (one reviewer’s perspective).
- The paper does not sufficiently explore the underlying mechanisms of how adaptation works for robotic control (e.g., how the pretrained model’s latent space is manipulated and whether that is beneficial for downstream tasks).
- There is no direct evidence that improvements in video quality (e.g., FVD) translate to better control performance. Inverse probabilistic adaptation has the highest success rate on MetaWorld (Table 2) but a worse FVD (Table 3), raising questions about the quality–control relationship.
- Generalization tests for control tasks are missing; the MetaWorld tasks are relatively simple and do not require complex reasoning/planning, limiting the impact.
- All experiments use a single video generation model (AnimateDiff), so the generalizability of the findings to other models (e.g., StableDiffusionVideo) is unclear.
- The paper assumes that obtaining in-domain video demonstrations is highly challenging, but recent imitation learning works (e.g., Diffusion Policy) often use ~100 demonstrations. The low data budgets (25 for MetaWorld, 6/17 for DMC) are not well justified, and the impact of varying the number of demonstrations is not explored.
- No real-world robot experiments are included, limiting practical relevance. In simulation, adaptation may focus on appearance rather than physics, whereas real-world dynamics modeling is the core challenge.
- Standard errors are reported only for the first column of Table 1; they should be included across all results to assess statistical significance.
- Probabilistic adaptation performs very poorly in the DeepMind domain (Table 1), attributed to the small capacity of the in-domain model, but this is not explored further (e.g., by increasing capacity or using different architectures).
- The qualitative results for dog jumping (Figure 3) are not convincing: the generated sequence is only partially reasonable, and the simulated behavior appears to be a degenerated version of suboptimal supervision.
- Showing results where all methods achieve 0% (or near 0%) success rate for about half the tasks is not very informative; additional expert demonstrations might help, and the limitations of these challenging tasks should be addressed.
- Several adaptation methods completely fail on certain tasks when used for video planning (Table 4), especially the Soccer task. It is unclear whether the failure is due to a poor inverse dynamics model or poor video adaptation. An analysis of failure cases is needed.
- Subject customization uses only static images and may fail to capture dynamics differences in new environments (e.g., if gravity changes). A controlled experiment varying dynamics would help validate this method.
- The authors conclude that the best method differs between environments (fine-tuning for dog/humanoid, inverse probabilistic for MetaWorld), but no guidance is given for how to choose a method for a new environment based on its characteristics (e.g., dynamics complexity, visual input nature).
- Missing quantitative results for the out-of-domain Humanoid/Dog environments (only qualitative in Figure 3). Success metrics for the jump task are not defined.
- The video quality evaluation (Table 3) only considers two tasks (coffee push, button press), which may introduce evaluation bias; quality metrics across all tasks should be provided.
- The in-domain-only model (trained from scratch on demonstrations) appears to perform similarly in task-level motion to probabilistic/inverse probabilistic adaptation in the qualitative results, with differences only at the pixel level. Additionally, the reproduced results of the in-domain-only model look worse than those in the original AVDC paper, suggesting possible reproduction issues that should be clarified.
- The following specific questions and missing details should be addressed: (1) Clarify if inverse probabilistic adaptation has been previously explored in robotics; (2) Define variables in Eq. 1 & 2 (theta, t, tao, e_theta); (3) Explain “out of domain” constraints for each adaptation method (e.g., does subject customization fix the robot arm?); (4) Provide a brief description of reward computation using the adapted video model in the main text (currently only in Appendix B); (5) State the exact size of the “small dataset” (line 312) in the main paper; (6) Show a qualitative example from Subject Customization in Figure 3; (7) Clarify what “high variance” refers to in line 285; (8) Report the number of closed-loop steps to complete tasks given the short horizon of AnimateDiff; (9) Consider batch-balanced co-training as an alternative adaptation technique; (10) Explain why inverse probabilistic adaptation works well for MetaWorld but much worse for DeepMind Control Suite; (11) Provide average returns alongside success rates in Table 2; (12) Tune hyper-parameters for probabilistic adaptation in policy discrimination to account for distribution shift; (13) Include details of the “suboptimal dataset” in the main paper for the data quality section.
