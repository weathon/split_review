## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper surveys the spectral dynamics of weights (singular values and vectors during optimization) across diverse settings—grokking, image classification with ConvNets, image generation with UNets, speech recognition with LSTMs, and language modeling with Transformers—to unify deep learning phenomena. Key findings include: grokking is linked to rank minimization; rank minimization is a general phenomenon; weight decay implicitly promotes a low-rank bias beyond norm regularization; generalizing solutions have lower rank than memorizing ones; top singular vectors are preserved in lottery ticket pruning and linear mode connectivity. The paper also examines alignment between consecutive layers, finding weaker alignment than theory suggests.

### Strengths

- Clearly written, easy to follow; phrasing polished, introduction insightful, literature review mostly comprehensive.
- Connects simple settings (grokking) to complex real-world tasks (ConvNets, UNets, LSTMs, Transformers) across domains (image, speech, language), demonstrating rank minimization as a common property.
- Spectral analysis approach is informative and has yielded prior theoretical insights (e.g., low-rank adaptation); the paper’s attempt to extend these insights into a more general, wide-ranging empirical study is novel.
- Comprehensive experiments touching upon a wide array of deep learning phenomena (grokking, generalization vs memorization, lottery tickets, linear mode connectivity); findings support the importance of the top singular subspace and include surprising observations (e.g., Figure 2d shows alignment increasing up the ranks over training).
- Differences between random-label and true-label learning effectively highlight how task structure shapes spectral dynamics.

### Weaknesses

- The paper is largely an exercise in plotting singular value dynamics without extracting conclusive or striking insights. It lacks concrete mechanistic explanations for the observed phenomena (e.g., why grokking is correlated with a drop in effective rank). The core message is vague; the abstract mentions a “consistent bias” without specifying it, and the introduction is long but unspecific about the main question.
- More results of different tasks within each domain are needed to claim generality; one task per domain (e.g., one image classification, one language modeling) is insufficient.
- Potential counterexamples are not addressed: could a generalizing solution have higher rank than a memorizing one? If so, the conclusion in line 140 should be revised or stated with more caveats. Are there cases where spectral-dynamics-based arguments alone fail to explain generalization?
- The analysis of weight decay (lines 410-420) is interesting but lacks quantitative depth. There is no study of how the magnitude of weight decay affects spectral properties and generalization performance; “varies across architectures” is insufficient. The paper does not provide a concrete guideline or metric for choosing the weight decay value based on spectral analysis.
- The theoretical foundation is weak. Visual observations in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 are descriptive but lack formal analysis or explanation of why certain spectral patterns emerge (e.g., relating spectral changes to the optimization landscape or learning dynamics). The paper remains descriptive rather than explanatory.
- The connections made in Section 6 (lottery tickets, linear mode connectivity) are not sufficiently novel or rigorously demonstrated; they largely verify prior work with additional examples and visualizations. The conclusions are considered too simple and not actionable.
- For architectures beyond MLPs, the definition of alignment is not standard; the authors tried several definitions (Appendix) and results may vary with different choices. The absence of strong alignment (Figure 5) is noted, but it is unclear whether this is due to large step sizes (as in Mulayoff & Michaeli) and could be recovered with smaller steps. The whitened-input assumption mentioned in related work may not be necessary for alignment in linear networks.
- Several specific details are unclear or inconsistent: what the blue shaded region in Figure 2(a) represents (caption missing); why a one-layer transformer in Figure 2 top row has 6 layers in Figure 2(c) and 50 layers for a 12-layer transformer in Figure 3(d); why the last layer cannot be pruned (line 359); and why alignment appears and then disappears during training (Figure 2d).
- The paper does not explain how spectral dynamics could be leveraged to distinguish memorization vs generalization between different networks trained on the same task (e.g., would higher rank always imply more memorization?).
