## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tests the "mirroring hypothesis" by examining how the geometric structure of neural manifolds in a digital twin of macaque V4 and in convolutional neural networks reflects latent structure in the visual world, focusing on in-plane rotation. For objects, the ring-like geometry of rotation propagates from image space to feature space in both trained and untrained networks. For textures, the circular structure is absent in pixel space but emerges only in networks trained to predict neural data or on ImageNet (robust ResNet50); untrained networks fail. Additionally, an orientation decoder trained on one texture generalizes to novel textures when using features from trained networks, but not from random networks. The authors argue these results support the mirroring hypothesis as a universal property of intelligent vision systems.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and logically structured.
- The study uses monkey physiological data to fit a digital twin model of V4, grounding the analysis in real neural activity.
- The framework distinguishing "simple processing" (structure easily recoverable from input) from "intelligent processing" (structure recovered by learning) is conceptually interesting and clearly demonstrated with the object vs. texture contrast.
- The methods and results are sound, suggesting that training a model on neural data or on classification tasks can recover latent rotation structure, likely reflecting what occurs in the brain.
- The motivation to study universal properties of data processing across biological and artificial systems is valuable for both machine learning and neuroscience.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns:** Several reviewers noted substantial overlap with prior work. The rotation equivariance of CNN representations has been extensively studied (e.g., Lenc & Vedaldi, 2015; Higgins et al., 2017; Jahanian et al., 2019). The mirroring hypothesis appears closely related to representational similarity (Kriegeskorte & Wei, 2012). One reviewer considered the overall question trivial: if a network performs recognition robustly, similar stimuli will necessarily yield similar representations, so the result is unsurprising. Another reviewer found the distinction between simple and intelligent processing novel, but the other two considered the findings essentially already known.
- **Claims are too broad given the evidence:** The paper makes general claims about "neural representations reflecting geometric structures of latent variables in the visual world" but only tests in-plane 2D rotation. It is unknown whether the findings extend to translation, 3D rotation, color, weight, or other latent factors. The definition of "geometric structure of the latent world" remains vague.
- **Insufficient experimental controls and alternative explanations:** 
  - Experiment 1 uses only layer 3 of an untrained ResNet50. It is unclear whether the structured manifold survives in deeper layers or in an untrained ResNet110 (more nonlinearities may break it).
  - For objects (high-frequency variance), a standard ResNet50 was used; for textures (low-frequency variance), a robust ResNet50 was used. This mismatch is problematic because robust networks are known to be sensitive to low frequencies. The alternative explanation that the results simply reflect frequency sensitivity, not mirroring, was not ruled out. The effect of using a robust vs. standard ResNet50 on the object manifold was not tested.
  - The texture experiment did not test a standard (non-robust) ResNet50. It is possible that a simple low-frequency filter with mild nonlinearities (e.g., Henaff, Goris & Simoncelli, 2019) could recover the ring manifold, challenging the claim that large-scale learning is required.
  - The influence of image background on the object manifold was not assessed (e.g., no background or random backgrounds).
- **Model details and ambiguity:**
  - Section 4.1: The difference between the "Digital Twin – Untrained" and "Random Core" conditions is unclear; both appear to be randomly initialized networks with the same architecture.
  - Figure 3: It is unclear whether the manifold analysis uses data from ResNet Layer 3 or directly from the 1244 digital twin neurons.
  - The term "DDM" in Section 4.4 is not defined.
  - The "ImageNet-Trained Core" is inconsistently referred to; experiments use both robust and standard ResNet50 but the paper does not clearly distinguish which is used where.
- **Lack of evidence linking to actual V4 activity:** The conclusion claims that V4 representations reflect geometric structures, but the digital twin's performance on the specific test images (objects and textures) is not reported. It is unknown whether the digital twin accurately predicts V4 responses to these images, undermining the claim about visual cortex.
- **Missing experiments requested by reviewers:**
  - Test the structured data claim with deeper untrained networks and untrained ResNet110.
  - Test the texture manifold with standard (non-robust) ResNet50 and with the Henaff early-vision model.
  - Test orientation decoder generalization (Experiment 3) with standard ResNet50 and multiple layers.
  - For the object set, test with no background or random backgrounds.
  - Clarify the choice of background in Figure 3.
- **Presentation issues:** The abstract is vague and lacks concrete information. Figure 3c is missing its middle panel. The paper spends paragraphs on digital twins yet reports very limited experiments. The first result figure appears only on page 7 after a long introduction; restructuring may improve clarity. The use of "Finding" instead of "Experiment" is unusual.
- **Disagreement among reviewers:** One reviewer (score 6) found the framework novel and the experiments sound, while the other two (scores 3 each) were highly critical, questioning novelty, the sufficiency of evidence, and the presence of simpler alternative explanations.
