## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a method that combines reinforcement learning (policy network) with differentiable surrogate models to perform black-box optimization of simulation parameters. The policy decides when to retrain a local surrogate using simulator calls; the surrogate provides gradients for gradient descent on the parameters. The approach is tested on benchmarks and real-world tasks, including an antenna placement problem. Training the policy is intended to be amortized across multiple parameterized input distributions.

### Strengths
- The problem of amortizing an active learning strategy over multiple optimization instances is interesting and under-explored.
- The approach is straightforward and intuitively appealing.
- The presentation and structure are generally clear and informative (with some noted exceptions).
- The method is tested on multiple real-world experiments (antenna placement, physics) and can handle both stochastic and non-stochastic simulators, suggesting potential practical impact.
- The antenna placement experiment is especially interesting and could form the basis of an applications paper if expanded.
- The motivation for black-box optimization and gradient-free methods is well stated, and the introduction is well written.
- The comparison includes an ensemble of L-GSO models (local surrogate-based optimization), which is a relevant baseline.

### Weaknesses
- **Total simulator cost not clearly reported or accounted for.** The main paper and figures give the false impression that each “simulator call” is a single evaluation; in fact, calling the simulator involves thousands of simulations (e.g., training surrogates). The total number of simulator calls required to train the policy network is not reported in the main text. Algorithm 1 (appendix) reveals a triple nested for-loop, implying a large upfront cost before any black-box optimization. This cost is not shown in the results plots (e.g., Figure 3), so the claimed ~90% reduction in calls is misleading without including training. All three reviewers flagged this as a critical omission. One reviewer (R2) was particularly critical, noting that the paper’s main weakness is that policy training cost is hidden. Another reviewer (R3) asked explicitly whether the plots incorporate training cost.
- **Missing comparison to simulation-based inference (SBI) methods.** The paper does not compare to amortized SBI techniques (e.g., SNPE, SNLE) which can perform inference on the full posterior without additional simulator calls at test time. This comparison is straightforward using available toolkits and benchmarks (Tejero-Cantero 2020; Lueckmann 2021). Given the reported total simulation counts, a simple neural density estimation baseline could be more sample-efficient. This omission undermines the claim of improved efficiency. (R1)
- **Discrepancy with previously published results (Shirobokov et al. 2020).** The results in Figure 3 do not match those in the prior paper. For example, at 100 function calls on the synthetic problem, no algorithm in Shirobokov et al. (2020, Fig. 2a) reached -0.8, but here all algorithms except numerical differentiation reach -0.8. Similar mismatches appear for the 10D Rosenbrock, nonlinear submanifold hump, and physics experiment. The authors do not address these discrepancies. (R2)
- **Insufficient evaluation of parameter accuracy.** The paper never shows the joint distribution of true vs. estimated parameters, nor compares simulation outputs from true vs. estimated parameters. It is therefore unclear whether the tasks (especially real-world ones) are actually solved or merely produce lower objective values. The metric “objective function” is ambiguous—does it refer to the RL reward or the loss for surrogate training? Lowering the objective does not guarantee accurate parameter estimation. Plots of parameter accuracy vs. total simulator calls would be more informative. (R1)
- **Unclear or missing technical details.** Several aspects are not explained clearly in the main body:
    - The role of the latent variable \(z\): only the mean output of surrogates is used, so why include \(z\)? Does the ensemble of 100 models already capture uncertainty? Is \(z\) used after training? (R1)
    - The “uncertainty feature” is introduced and then not mentioned again; it is unclear whether it is necessary, especially when using an ensemble. (R2)
    - The definition of “parameterized input distribution” is unclear, making it hard to interpret the difference between columns (a) and (c) in Figure 3. (R2)
    - The precise sequence of steps (including policy training, surrogate training, and optimization) is not laid out in the main text; a simplified algorithm would help. (R1)
    - Training details of the surrogate neural networks (loss function, training procedure) are missing from the main body. (R3)
    - The values of \(K\) and \(G\) (number of inner loops) are not given. (R3)
- **Amortized benefit not demonstrated.** The paper claims that training the policy can be amortized over multiple input distributions, but this use case is mentioned only once (l.326) without clear motivation. No real-world experiment involves parameterized input distributions, so the key advantage of the method is not shown. (R3)
- **Missing baselines and ablations.** A simple alternative—training a network to map observations to parameters using least squares with the same number of prior samples—might achieve better results using zero simulator calls at inference. This baseline is not considered. (R1) Also, the decision to train surrogates only on local samples is not justified: could incorporating older samples help, and when would one strategy be preferred? (R1)
- **Section “Policy-Based Black-Box Optimization” lacks technical content.** It repeatedly refers to the appendix instead of presenting key information (e.g., policy architecture, training loop, reward design). (R2)
- **Failure modes and assumptions not discussed.** The paper does not discuss how to identify if the policy or surrogate training fails, nor what assumptions about the simulator’s local vs. global properties are needed for the method to work. (R1)
- **Design choices unsubstantiated.** The use of an ensemble solely to estimate \(\sigma\) is not compared against simpler alternatives. (R2) Why are only 2-layer networks used for the 42-dimensional parameter estimation task? (R1) Why not provide the observations \(y\) to the policy network? (R1)
- **Terminology.** “Learning active learning” may not be standard; “meta active learning” (Ravi & Larochelle 2018) might be more appropriate. (R2, minor)
- **Missing references.** The paper omits citations for key SBI works (Papamakarios & Murray 2016; Lueckmann et al. 2019, 2021; Tejero-Cantero 2020) and emulator-based gradient estimation (Nonnenmacher & Greenberg 2021). (R1)
- **Typo** (l.403): “receive locations” should be “receiving locations”. (R3, minor)
