## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies reinforcement learning with trajectory feedback, where the learner only observes the cumulative noisy reward at the end of each episode rather than per-step rewards. The authors propose an online algorithm for finite-horizon MDPs (S states, A actions, horizon H) that achieves a minimax optimal regret bound of \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{SAH^3 K})\). The technical contributions include incorporating linear bandit methods to construct tighter confidence regions for the reward function and using a reference transition model to guide exploration. Reviewers generally find the theoretical results strong and the problem motivated, but several raise concerns about practical applicability of the assumptions (linear rewards, trajectory-only feedback), clarity of the algorithm and proofs, dominance of lower-order regret terms, absence of experiments, and presentation issues.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a well-motivated and novel variant of RL – trajectory feedback – which is practical in applications where per-step rewards are expensive to query (several reviewers).
- The proposed algorithm achieves a minimax optimal regret bound of \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{SAH^3 K})\), improving over existing bounds in trajectory-feedback RL and matching the asymptotic rate of standard RL (R1, R2, R3, R4, R6).
- The technical approach is novel: incorporating linear bandit techniques for tighter confidence regions and constructing a reference transition model for guiding exploration (R1, R4).
- The theoretical analysis is rigorous and sound, with clear informal explanations of the proofs (R1, R3, R4, R6). The motivation for maintaining a policy set within a constant range via batched sampling is well presented (R3).
- Up to Section 4, the paper clearly presents motivations, objectives, and proof sketch, making it accessible (R5, R6).
- One reviewer (R2, score 8) is particularly enthusiastic, highlighting that the problem is a practical scenario that lacked a detailed solution and that the bounds are technically sound and improve upon existing literature.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited applicability of assumptions**: Multiple reviewers (R1, R2, R6) question real-world relevance. The trajectory feedback setting (only cumulative reward at episode end) is restrictive; many practical RL problems involve intermediate rewards (R1). The reward is assumed to be a linear function of the trajectory, which severely limits applicability and reduces the problem to linear regression (R2). R2 notes that even the medical care example from the introduction does not fit a linear reward function. The paper does not discuss extensions to nonlinear or more complex reward structures (R2, R6). R1 also notes that the asymptotic bound matches standard RL, making the practical advantage questionable.

- **Dominance of lower-order regret terms**: Both R4 and R6 point out that the last three terms in Theorem 7 dominate the regret bound, contrary to the abstract and introduction which emphasize the first term \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{SAH^3 K})\). R6 asks for clarification on when the first term is actually dominant; if not, why the paper focuses on it. R4 notes these terms render the result less exciting, require precise characterization, and it is unclear if they can be reduced. R4 (Q7) and R6 both want to know the real dependence of these lower-order terms.

- **Lack of empirical validation**: R3, R6 (and implied by R2) note the absence of any experiments or simulations. R3 suggests a toy experiment comparing to standard linear bandit approaches in episodic MDPs, especially with large state/action spaces and long horizons. R2 requests illustrative examples to help appreciate the theoretical results.

- **Clarity and presentation issues**: Multiple reviewers (R4, R5, R6) find the algorithm description and certain proof steps unclear or convoluted.
    * R4 describes the algorithm presentation as “convoluted” and the “Design” step in Algorithm 5 as lacking sufficient detail.
    * R5 states Section 5 lacks logical flow, with insufficient description of pseudocode; many concepts are listed without thorough theoretical explanation. Substituting proofs with more accessible pseudocode explanation would help.
    * R6 finds several derivations unintuitive and insufficiently explained: (a) the derivation of state-action visitation frequencies from trajectory probabilities (page 5, second key observation); (b) the connection between confidence regions for linear bandits and Inequality (3); (c) the definition of \(W\) and its relationship to expected cumulative reward in equation (8). R6 also requests a clearer step-by-step derivation of why the expected trajectory reward is linear in visit frequencies (Section 3).
    * R1 questions the definition of the set in equation (10) and how to compute \(\max W^{\pi'}(\hat{R},P)\). R5 questions line 310 optimization for double-state reward function, and Algorithm 4 line 7 regarding \(\bar{\pi}\) from Eq (6) and its tractability.

- **Typos, undefined notations, and missing references**: Reviewers (R4, R5, R6) note numerous typos and undefined symbols, giving an impression of a hastily composed draft.
    * Typos: “ofte” → “often” (l46, R1,R4,R5), “fro” → “for” (l250, R4), “S^1 1” → “S^{11}” (l483, R5), “we” capitalization (l388, R5), font error in Algorithm 3 (R5), third term of Theorem 7 upper bound (R6).
    * Undefined notations: \(\mathcal{D}_2\) in Algorithm 2 (R5,R6), \(P_1\) in Algorithm 2 (R6), \(\lambda_\pi\) in Algorithm 5 (R5).
    * R4 notes the examples in the introduction have been discussed before and need proper references. R5 states the structure is inspired by [1] but contains undefined notations.

- **Computational complexity and scalability concerns**: R3 and R5 point out that the algorithm likely requires exponential time cost (R3) and lacks discussion of computational complexity compared to existing methods (R3). R3 requests a detailed comparison. R4 (Q6) asks if the computation in Algorithm 5 line 9 would be quite difficult and requests complexity analysis. R5 questions the tractability of obtaining \(\bar{\pi}\) in Algorithm 4 line 7. R2 notes that policy elimination may be suboptimal compared to parameterized policy optimization for large policy spaces, potentially giving tighter bounds.

- **Theoretical gaps and missing justifications**: Several reviewers raise specific theoretical issues:
    * R4 (Q4) points out that the statement \(\pi^* \in \Pi_{\ell+1}\) is claimed as a high-probability event, but the usage of \(\tilde{O}\) notation appears to treat random variables as fixed without proper justification; this seems like a loose usage and requires explanation.
    * R1 (Q1) asks for more detailed explanation of step in lines 261–262 regarding “for some constant \(c>0\)”.
    * R1 (Q3) and R4 (Q1) ask about the “for sufficiently large \(K\)” condition in Theorem 1: what is the precise lower bound on \(K\) for the result to hold? Without this, practical significance is unclear (R4).
    * R4 (Q2) questions the incremental nature relative to Efroni et al (2021); asks what the main contribution is, whether it is just a mix of known techniques, and whether the new techniques are applicable elsewhere.
    * R4 (Q3) asks if the constant \(c\) in l262 depends on the problem.
    * R4 (Q5) asks how \(K_0\) is set in Algorithm 1.
    * R6 (and R3) request an explanation of why trajectory feedback does not lead to a worse regret bound than standard RL and how the algorithm mitigates the increased variance from cumulative rewards.

- **Minority contrast**: One reviewer (R2, score 8) is notably more positive about the novelty and practicality, but still raises concerns about the linear reward assumption and policy elimination. Another reviewer (R5, score 3) is the most negative, citing many presentation and clarity issues, undefined notations, and impractical elements. This contrast indicates significant disagreement among reviewers regarding the paper’s clarity and practical contribution.
