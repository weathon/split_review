## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces TRACER, an explainability method that groups DNN layers via centered kernel alignment (CKA) on perturbed inputs, then applies feature importance to obtain explanations and generates counterfactuals using a GAN model. The authors claim a causal grounding for their approach.

### Strengths
- First steps towards representing DNNs as causal models.
- Explanations are compelling from a human‑visual perspective.
- Visual comparisons with existing methods (LIME, SHAP, LRP, Grad‑CAM) are provided.
- Strong compression results (high compression with minimal accuracy drops), although the mechanism to derive compression from explanations is unclear.
- English is generally good and the text is readable.
- The research question (interpreting DNNs) is interesting, and the attempt to identify causal sources is noted.

### Weaknesses
- The method description is consistently too high‑level and lacks proper formalization. No algorithm, pipeline diagram, or clear inputs/outputs for each step are given, making it difficult to understand exactly how attributions are computed and how the framework works.
- The causal claim is not justified. The procedure (generating perturbed inputs, applying CKA to group layers) does not obviously identify causal dynamics; the resulting layer groups remain largely uninterpretable and the causal graph discovered is merely a regrouping of adjacent layers. One reviewer (R2) found the paper poor in soundness (score 1), while another (R1) rated it good (3), indicating disagreement on technical quality.
- Many claims are made without experimental validation. Examples from the abstract: “existing solutions offer inconsistent or overly simplified explanations” is not supported by comparisons on consistency or simplicity; “optimal blend of realism, data efficiency, and computational performance” for counterfactuals is not backed by experiments; “deep understanding of primary factors leading to misclassification and decision pathways for correct outcome” (Section 4.2) is not substantiated—the appendix only shows feature importances for original and counterfactual at three layers without revealing network internals.
- The paper uses vague, LLM‑style justifications. For instance, the justification for using GANs instead of nearest neighbors mentions data privacy concerns, but the GAN itself requires storing the dataset for training. The proximity aspect of GAN counterfactuals is not emphasized.
- Counterfactual generation via GANs is already well‑established in the literature; the paper does not clarify what is novel about its design or why it is preferable to existing causal counterfactual methods (e.g., Karimi et al. on algorithmic recourse).
- The factual and counterfactual components feel detached—they are presented as essentially independent contributions.
- Baselines are limited to LIME, SHAP, LRP, and Grad‑CAM; more recent explainability methods and causal‑based approaches are missing. Compression results are not compared to any baselines.
- No experimentation on counterfactuals despite counterfactual generation being claimed as a contribution.
- The evaluation is not convincing. Qualitative comparisons (Figures 4 and 7) are not useful because the true causal explanations are unknown. The chosen datasets (MNIST, ImageNet) are not ideal for evaluating layer‑group visualizations; a dataset like CUB‑200 would be more appropriate. The DDoS attack experiments (Appendix) show only a single sample’s feature importance without comparison to other methods or multi‑sample metrics.
- Computational efficiency is not documented or compared to existing methods, despite the many steps involved (CKA, GAN training, intervention).
- It is unclear how the method scales to large networks or high‑resolution inputs.
- Several inconsistencies: Section 3.2 states interventions on internal representations, but the description that follows intervenes on inputs; it is not justified why a baseline value is used rather than marginalization, nor what baseline value is best.
- Section 3.2.1 claims replacing an input variable with a constant “effectively nullify or modify the influence of selected features,” but this reasoning is questionable.
- The introduction’s claim of a “paradigm shift” is overstated, given the existing work on causal XAI (e.g., Geiger et al., “Neural Network Attributions: A Causal Perspective” 2019). References may also contain errors (e.g., the 2022 Geiger paper may be intended as 2021).
- No code is provided, and several experiments lack full implementation details. Reviewer 3 also questioned scalability and the lack of comparisons with causal approaches.
