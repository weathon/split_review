## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Strictly Local Equivariant Message-passing (SLEM), a deep learning model for predicting multiple quantum operator matrices (Hamiltonian, overlap, density) within the DFT framework. SLEM achieves state-of-the-art accuracy while improving computational efficiency through strict locality (effective cutoff does not increase with layers) and an SO(2) convolution technique that reduces complexity of high-order tensor products, enabling handling of \(f\) and \(g\) orbitals (Reviewers 1,2,3). The architecture is heavily inspired by Allegro (Reviewer 3). The model is validated across various 2D and 3D materials, demonstrating better accuracy and reduced computational cost compared to existing methods (Reviewers 1,2,3). However, concerns about evaluation methodology (same-material training/testing, limited baseline comparisons, missing transferability demonstrations) and theoretical justification for strict locality are raised (Reviewers 1,2,3). Reviewer 2 (score 6, confidence 5) is more critical than Reviewers 1 and 3 (both score 8, confidence 3) regarding the experimental validation and dataset transparency.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and design**: The combination of strict locality (inherited from Allegro) with SO(2) convolutions is a novel contribution that reduces computational complexity for high angular momenta, enabling systems requiring \(f\) and \(g\) orbitals (Reviewers 1,2,3). The parameterization of invariant overlap operators allows efficient prediction without many parameters (Reviewer 1). The strictly local structure ensures the effective receptive field does not expand with layers, improving scalability and parallelizability (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical soundness**: The paper provides detailed mathematical justifications for preserving physical symmetries while maintaining strict locality, demonstrating methodological rigor (Reviewer 2). The contributions are clearly explained and technically sound (Reviewer 1).
- **Empirical performance**: SLEM achieves lower errors, better scaling with angular momentum, faster iteration speed, and smaller memory footprint compared to state-of-the-art methods (Reviewer 1). Improved accuracy and reduced computational cost are validated across benchmarks (Reviewers 1,2,3). Better generalization due to restricted model assumptions is noted (Reviewer 1). Creation of new public datasets enhances impact (Reviewer 3).
- **Importance**: The contributions are important for quantum chemistry and materials science, potentially accelerating DFT calculations and enabling simulations of large-scale systems (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient generalization/transferability evaluation**: Training and testing are conducted on the same material type (e.g., Si) using MD trajectories covering many configurations, making good generalization unsurprising (Reviewer 1). This raises a chicken-egg problem: if DFT is already computed for a system, the value of a surrogate model is unclear (Reviewer 1). The paper lacks cross-material transferability evaluations, such as training on AB/BC/AC and evaluating on ABC, or different element proportions (Reviewer 1), or training on light elements and testing on heavy elements, or different crystal structures (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 notes that a transferability task would significantly enhance the submission.
- **Limited baseline comparisons**: Only two models are compared, and not consistently across all experiments (Reviewer 2). Expanding to include more well-established methods and benchmark datasets (e.g., QH9, nablaDFT, QM9 as used in HamGNN) would strengthen validation (Reviewer 2). E3NN is compared for scalability, but its accuracy MAE is not shown; it is unclear whether E3NN achieves better accuracy despite higher computational cost (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing dataset details and potential approximations**: Details about in-house simulations (parameters, configurations) are not provided, hindering reproducibility (Reviewer 2). The use of neural network potentials for MD sampling to generate structures for Si, GaN, and HfO₂ may introduce approximations relative to DFT; the impact on training data quality and generalizability is not clarified (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 asks which cutoff is used and whether different cutoffs are applied for different systems, which is not specified.
- **Theoretical concerns about strict locality**: Only atom pairs within cutoff \(r_{\text{cut}}\) are considered; it is unclear how noninteracting entries of the operator matrices are set (Reviewer 1). The Hartree term in DFT involves four-center integrals \((ij|kl)\) that decay slowly; when \((i|j)\) and \((k|l)\) are both large, \((ij|kl)\) may be non-negligible even for distant groups, challenging the strict locality assumption (Reviewer 1). The paper lacks theoretical justification for when strict locality holds and should discuss limitations (e.g., systems with strong electronic correlations) (Reviewers 1,3). Reviewer 3 also points out that the method may fail for such systems.
- **Parallelization claims not empirically validated**: While Reviewer 1 praises the strictly local design as significant for parallelization and memory efficiency, Reviewer 3 notes that the parallelization benefits are mainly theoretical in the paper; concrete scaling studies on large systems or multi-GPU benchmarks are missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Accessibility and presentation**: The mathematical formulation is complex and may be difficult for practitioners not deeply familiar with quantum mechanics and tensor operations (Reviewer 2). Merging some experiment tables would improve readability (Reviewer 2).
