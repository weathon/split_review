## Merged Review

### Summary
LCoW trains a separate contextualization module (based on Phi-3-instruct) to prune and explain webpage HTML observations before feeding them to LLM agents. The module is trained iteratively using successful trajectories from simulator rollouts; multiple LLMs score candidate contextualizations via action matching. Experiments on WebShop and WorkArena show significant gains: 20% success improvement for closed-source LLMs (Gemini-1.5-flash, GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) and 33.5% for open-source models (Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B); Gemini-1.5-flash+LCoW reaches SOTA on WebShop, outperforming human experts. Overall reception is mixed: reviewers 1 and 4 are strongly positive (scores 8,8; confidence 4 and 2), while reviewers 2 and 3 are more critical (scores 5,5; confidence 4 each). The main consensus is that the idea is clever and promising, but reviewers raise serious concerns about generalization, experimental scope, reproducibility, and limitations of the approach.

### Strengths
- The contextualization idea—decoupling webpage understanding from decision making—is novel and effective. It improves LLM-based web agents across multiple scales and benchmarks. (R1, R4)
- Achieves state-of-the-art results on WebShop, outperforming strong baselines (AgentQ, LASER) and matching human expert performance. (R1)
- Comprehensive experiments on WebShop and WorkArena (partial: 115 of 1000 tasks) with multiple LLM backbones. (R1, R2)
- The contextualization module generalizes to LLMs not seen during training (e.g., Gemini-1.5-flash trained contextualizer works with GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet). (R2)
- The iterative training algorithm improves the module via real-world interaction feedback without requiring human-labeled data. (R2, R3, R4)
- Practical compute cost: training a small model (Phi-3-instruct) for contextualization does not add excessive expense. (R1)
- Reward model uses a LLM-based judge across multiple powerful LLMs, reducing individual model bias. (R4)
- Qualitative results illustrate how the contextualizer removes irrelevant page elements. (R4)
- Paper is well-written and clearly presented. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Generalization concerns:**
  - The contextualization module is trained on a small dataset (<2,000 self-generated samples); this raises doubts about generalization to diverse web pages and long-tail content variations. (R2)
  - The module struggles with unseen UI elements (noted in the limitations). No clear strategy is provided for handling novel layouts or elements. (R1, R2, R3)
  - Training and evaluation focus on shopping tasks (WebShop, WorkArena subset). The method may overfit to these domains and may not transfer to other website types (e.g., LinkedIn, news, productivity). (R1, R3)
  - The approach is compared to a similar but more general WebArena method that does not require task-specific inputs; the paper’s reliance on format-specific training constrains its scope. (R3)
  - No experiments comparing LCoW to code-based HTML parsers for LLM agents. (R1)
- **Experimental limitations:**
  - WorkArena contains 1,000 task instances, but only 115 tasks are evaluated. Selection criteria for these tasks is not adequately justified; performance may be cherry-picked. (R3)
  - No code or playing episodes are provided to verify results. This is a significant reproducibility concern. (R3)
  - Behavior cloning baseline is trained with fewer demonstrations than the contextualization module, potentially biasing comparisons. (R3)
  - Different LLMs were used for training the contextualization module across different benchmarks (e.g., Gemma for WorkArena, Phi-3 for WebShop); this choice is not explained or justified. (R4)
  - Figure 7 (contextualization module diagram) is not intuitive. (R2)
  - Table 3 in the appendix: data and caption mismatch (33×15 = 495, not the stated number). (R1)
  - Line 182: “select the one that provides the most relevant context for the LLM agent to accurately predict the next action at as the target” is unclear. (R1)
- **Methodological concerns:**
  - The approach relies on collecting successful trajectories from LLM agents. For tasks where no LLM can succeed, the method cannot bootstrap. (R1, R3)
  - Action matching for open-ended actions (e.g., filling a text box) is not feasible; the paper uses GPT-4o for matching, but tasks with diverse valid action sequences pose a fundamental limitation. (R1)
  - In real-world rollouts, some individual actions in a trajectory may be incorrect, yet LCoW treats them as correct for training, potentially learning to drop relevant page elements. (R1)
  - The reward only judges whether the current step matches the recorded action, not whether the final task objective is achieved. (R2)
  - Iterative training with self-generated data risks overfitting to seen patterns rather than learning generalizable features. (R2)
  - No experiments on how long contextual observation generation takes; for tasks requiring parsing large amounts of text (e.g., writing a tweet from an article), the process could be expensive and slow. (R1)
- **Missing comparisons and analysis:**
  - No comparison with prior intelligent code analysis work or human oracle rules for shopping websites. (R2, R3)
  - The limitations section is too narrow; it should address generalization, open-ended tasks, and reliance on successful trajectories. (R1)
  - The effect of LCoW on step count (Figure 8) shows little difference; exact numbers are not reported. (R3)
  - Practical efficacy in terms of cost/tokens from reward models and lack of simulation for new websites is not discussed. (R4)
- **Reviewer disagreement note:** While reviewers 1 and 4 are strongly positive about the approach and results, reviewers 2 and 3 find the weaknesses significant enough to question the paper’s impact, pointing to limited generalization, small training data, and insufficient experimental validation. This contrast is not reflected in the weaknesses summary above.
