## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a modified scaling law for sparse pre-training of LLMs, extending the Chinchilla law by using the average number of active parameters during training. It presents extensive experiments over 80 configurations to identify optimal pruning schedules (starting at 25% and ending at 75% of compute) and claims that sparse pre-training matches dense pre-training in loss while yielding a smaller final model for inference savings. Reviewer 1 (score 8, confidence 4) found the theoretical and empirical analysis highly convincing and the contribution strong, while Reviewers 2 and 3 (scores 6, confidence 3 and 4) acknowledged the novelty but raised significant concerns about limited model scale (under 500M parameters), the use of evaluation loss as the sole metric without downstream tasks, lack of demonstrated computational savings, and the practical impact of the scaling law for dense models.

### Strengths
- Novel modification of the Chinchilla scaling law to incorporate average active parameter count, effectively unifying dense and sparse pre-training frameworks. (All reviewers)
- Extensive empirical evaluation over 80 configurations, providing a thorough analysis of optimal sparsity schedules, learning rates, and batch sizes. (Reviewers 1,2,3)
- Theoretical justifications and empirical validation convincingly support the proposed scaling law. (Reviewer 1; also noted by Reviewer 3 as solid)
- The paper is well-written, clearly structured, and easy to follow. (Reviewer 3)
- The findings offer practical lessons for designing sparse pre-training configurations (e.g., pruning from 25% to 75% of compute, optimal hyperparameters matching dense training under same compute budget). (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
- Model scale is limited (under 500M parameters), weakening the claim of a scaling law for LLMs; previous scaling laws examined models at 1B scale. Reviewer 1 requested larger model experiments approaching state-of-the-art size. Reviewer 3 noted the small scale as a concern. (Reviewers 1,3)
- The sole evaluation metric is pretraining evaluation loss/perplexity; no downstream task evaluations are performed. This is a significant limitation: models could forget knowledge due to pruning, and generalizability on reasoning, knowledge, or other tasks is not assessed. All three reviewers raised this concern, with Reviewer 3 stating no compute limitation prohibits such evaluation. (All reviewers)
- The exact pruning method used in the iterative pruning phase is not described in sufficient detail. It is unclear whether pruning is structured or unstructured, whether it targets individual weights, groups, or layers, and how pruning decisions are made. This impacts reproducibility and understanding of how the scaling law depends on the pruning scheme. (Reviewer 1)
- The independence assumption between average active parameters (N̄) and dataset size (D) in Equation (2) is not empirically validated. The paper assumes independent effects on loss, but this may not hold in sparse pre-training where model capacity and data interact differently. (Reviewer 1)
- No empirical demonstration of actual computational savings is provided. While the paper motivates efficiency, the lack of real-world speed or memory measurements due to hardware/software limitations in sparse matrix support weakens the case for practical applicability. (Reviewer 2)
- The framework requires finely tuned phase-specific compute allocations (dense, pruning, recovery), introducing implementation complexity that could be challenging to replicate or scale, especially in resource-constrained environments. (Reviewer 2)
- The usefulness of unifying the scaling law for both dense and sparse models is questioned, given that more data and compute generally yield better models (as evidenced by LLaMA 2 and 3). It is unclear how the proposed law provides actionable benefits over existing dense scaling laws. (Reviewer 3)
- The paper focuses exclusively on LLaMA-style architectures for sparse pre-training and does not discuss other sparse architectures (e.g., Mixture of Experts). This may overclaim the generality of the findings. (Reviewer 3)
