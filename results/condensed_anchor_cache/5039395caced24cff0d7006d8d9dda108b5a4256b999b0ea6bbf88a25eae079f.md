## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper tackles source attribution for LLM-generated text by proposing WASA, a framework that embeds invisible Unicode watermarks into training data to trace which data provider influenced a generated output. Reviewers acknowledge the problem’s importance and the clear articulation of six desiderata (accuracy, robustness, scalability, performance preservation, transferability, adaptability). However, they raise significant concerns about the baseline comparison, scalability beyond hundreds of providers, robustness to simple watermark removal and strong paraphrasing, the real-world evaluation setup, and the novelty distinction from existing watermarking and data provenance work. While two reviewers (scores 6) find the method promising, three (scores 5) see weaker practical applicability, and one (score 5) is particularly critical of attack consideration and multiple-source attribution.

### Strengths

- Novel contribution: tackles a new and important problem (source attribution) for LLM-generated text, with clear real-world application for intellectual property protection.  
- Clear problem definition and enumeration of six key desiderata: accuracy, robustness, performance preservation, scalability, transferability, adaptability.  
- Proposed WASA framework is simple and generally applicable to many existing LLM architectures and training schemes.  
- Extensive empirical evaluation including main experiments and ablation studies covering each desideratum.  
- Strong performance over the BM25 baseline by a 17–29% margin.  
- Presents a negative result where normal LLM performance can degrade when WASA is applied, setting realistic expectations.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient differentiation from prior work**: The paper does not clearly distinguish source attribution from existing text watermarking for copyright protection or data provenance, especially given that methods like Kirchenbauer et al. can assign keys to each provider for identification.  
- **Inadequate baseline**: The only comparison is BM25, a retrieval method not directly suited for a classification task. Stronger baselines such as training a BERT classifier or using Siamese/\(k\)-shot models are missing.  
- **Scalability concerns**: Experiments only evaluate 20 or 500 data providers; performance degrades with growing numbers, and the paper does not explore scalability to thousands or millions of providers as would be needed in practice.  
- **Robustness to attacks is insufficient**:
  - Watermark removal is trivial via standard formatting (e.g., stripping all Unicode characters).  
  - No evaluation against strong semantically equivalent paraphrasing (e.g., DIPPER) – only mild changes to prepositions, tenses, syntax.  
  - Contrary to the author’s claim, perturbations on input word tokens can affect watermark outputs because hidden states of all tokens flow through the watermark head.  
  - A co-opitive attack: provider B could re-watermark provider A’s data with B’s own token to falsely claim authorship.  
  - In an AI-assisted writing scenario, using another provider’s WASA-LLM for simple grammar checks may transfer watermarks, causing IP conflicts.  
- **Limited to single-source attribution**: The method only makes a binary decision (output belongs to one provider or not) and cannot attribute to multiple data sources, which reduces its value for true source attribution.  
- **Unrealistic evaluation setup**: The accuracy test uses isolated generated text samples directly from the data providers’ training data; real-world LLM output is embedded in larger contexts, which could significantly affect performance.  
- **Lack of experiments with recent LLMs**: Only GPT-2 and LLaMA-2 are used; newer models like LLaMA-3-8B should be evaluated.  
- **Missing implementation details**:
  - How are the watermark Unicode token embeddings initialized? Are embedding parameters updated during training?  
  - No analysis of computational overhead from watermark embedding and regeneration in large-scale applications.  
- **Data distribution challenges not addressed**: Data from different providers may overlap (e.g., Arxiv and DBLP) or be highly diverse (social media), yet no experiments study how WASA handles such cases.  
- **Writing clarity and motivation**: The paper does not convincingly explain why enabling a data provider to verify use of their data in an “honest” LLM solves IP issues, weakening the motivation.
