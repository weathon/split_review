## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents RASO, a foundation model for open-set recognition of surgical objects in images and videos, leveraging a weakly-supervised learning framework that generates tag-image-text pairs from surgical lecture videos. The data generation pipeline produces 3.6 million tag annotations across 2,066 tags from 2,200 procedures. The model architecture builds on RAM (Zhang 2024) with an added temporal-attention fusion layer. Experiments show improvements in zero-shot and supervised settings on four benchmarks, including CholecT50, with reported mAP gains of 2.9, 4.5, 10.6, and 7.2 compared to prior methods. All reviewers acknowledge the potential impact on the surgical ML community due to reduced annotation effort, but raise concerns about technical novelty, evaluation clarity, and data quality. Reviewer scores are 8,8,8,6, indicating overall positive but with one more critical assessment.

### Strengths
- Well-presented and easy to follow (R1, R3, R4).
- The weakly-supervised data generation pipeline from surgical lecture videos is a creative and scalable solution to the scarcity of annotated surgical data (R2, R4). The dataset (2,200 procedures, 3.6M annotations, 2,066 tags) is a potentially valuable resource for the community (R1).
- RASO introduces an attention-based temporal-fusion mechanism for video context, which is relevant for surgical video analysis (R4). The model integrates both image and video understanding (R1).
- Extensive evaluation: zero-shot comparisons against 4 state-of-the-art models on 4 datasets (including EndoVis18 RSS) and supervised comparison on CholecT50 against 3 models, plus ablation studies on pretraining, fine-tuning, and video aggregation methods (R1, R3, R4).
- The authors plan to open-source code, model, and dataset, supporting reproducibility (R3).
- The approach addresses a common limitation in medical ML where expert annotation is costly (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty**: The model is heavily based on RAM (Zhang 2024) with the main addition being a temporal-attention fusion layer. The overall contribution is incremental from a general ML perspective, though possibly impactful in the surgical domain (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer (R4, score 6) is more critical, questioning the justification and necessity of the temporal-attention layer.
- **Missing justification and ablation for temporal-attention fusion**: The paper does not rigorously justify why temporal-attention outperforms simpler alternatives (e.g., average pooling, RNNs) for video recognition. In fact, Table 4 shows that average pooling performs better than temporal attention for instrument recognition tasks, which requires explanation (R2, R4). The ablation study does not explore different attention mechanisms (self-attention, cross-attention) or discuss computational cost for real-time applications (R4).
- **Potential redundancy between label decoder and text decoder**: The architecture includes both decoders, but it is unclear whether both are necessary. An ablation study evaluating performance with only one decoder or different combinations is needed (R4).
- **Data quality and annotation noise**: The weakly-supervised dataset generated from surgical lecture videos may introduce misalignments between text and visual content. Captions are not guaranteed to accurately reflect each frame, and the quality of GPT-4o generated annotations is not assessed. A detailed analysis of caption quality, tag distribution per class, and biases (over/under representation of tools/actions) is missing (R1, R4). The effect of GPT-4o accuracy on downstream performance should be evaluated (R1).
- **Incomplete experimental details and inconsistencies**:
  - It is unclear why the temporal-attention fusion (presented as part of RASO architecture in Fig. 2) was not used in the experiments for Tables 1 and 2. Only Table 3c uses it, and both configurations in Table 3c outperform those in Tables 3a/b, but no ablation on pretraining/fine-tuning with video aggregation is provided (R3).
  - Missing details about evaluation: which data was used for which experimental configuration (R3). For example, how are generated tags split into pretraining and finetuning sets? (R3).
  - EndoVis18 (RSS) is listed with 997 images, but the dataset has 19 snippets of 300 frames each – how was it downsampled? (R3)
  - Frame extraction method (keyframes, regular intervals, etc.) is not specified (R4).
  - Table 2 does not highlight P and R values, even though the text claims superiority based on P (R3).
  - Use of F_beta with beta=0.5 instead of common F1 is not justified (R3).
  - Table 3 has inconsistent decimal places (R3).
  - The text describes a frame-wise approach for video recognition (line 417ff) but it is not defined which features are aggregated (R3).
- **Figure and description issues**:
  - Figure 2 shows the encoder taking visual features and encoded tags as input, but the text (line 197) says the text decoder takes visual features and predicted tags; inconsistency needs resolution (R3).
  - Figure 1 is not referenced in the text (R3).
  - Figure 3 has a typo: "WshiperX" should be "WhisperX" (R2).
  - Paragraph heading "Discussion" (line 235) is misleading as no results are discussed (R3).
  - Lines 184–185: N and T both represent numbers of features, leading to confusion in the embedding size N×T×D (R3).
- **Comparison to directly fine-tuned foundation models**: The paper does not compare RASO to standard foundation models (e.g., RAM, CLIP) fine-tuned on the weakly-annotated data, so the additional benefit of the proposed architecture is unclear (R2).
- **Reproducibility concerns**: No link to code, data, or models is provided in the paper (R3).
- **Reference issues**: Many citations are non-peer-reviewed arXiv papers; authors should check for peer-reviewed versions (R3). Some references (lines 608, 623, 630) lack journal/publisher details (R3).
- **Small number of training epochs**: Pretraining and fine-tuning use few epochs (e.g., fine-tuning for only 4 epochs). It is not explained why this was chosen or whether loss saturates (R1).
