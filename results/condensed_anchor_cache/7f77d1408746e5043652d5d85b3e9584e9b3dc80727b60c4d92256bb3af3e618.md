## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a simple method, visual evidence prompting, to mitigate object and relation hallucinations in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs). It uses small visual models (object detection and scene graph generation) to extract contextual visual knowledge, which is then provided as prompts to the LVLM. Experiments on three LVLMs show improvements on both object hallucination benchmarks and a new relation hallucination benchmark. While the motivation is clear and the method shows consistent gains, several reviewers raised concerns about efficiency costs of adding extra models, domain overlap between the small models’ training data and evaluation data, limited evaluation scope (mostly yes/no questions), lack of comparison with fine-tuning and other ensemble methods, and unanswered questions about how the visual evidence interacts with the LVLM.

### Strengths
- Clear motivation: using small visual models to provide explicit object/relation evidence for reducing hallucinations.
- Simple, effective method that improves performance across all tested LVLMs for both object and relation hallucinations.
- Well-organized and easy-to-follow presentation.
- Proposes a new dataset and benchmark for relation hallucinations.
- Provides in-depth analysis of visual evidence prompting (e.g., effect of different detection models, number of prompts).

### Weaknesses
- Efficiency degradation from integrating separate detection and scene graph generation models is not quantified; real-world latency and compute costs are unclear.
- Domain compatibility issue: the training datasets of the small detection/SGG models may overlap with the LVLM hallucination test sets. The paper must clarify this and test on out-of-domain data where the small models may fail. One reviewer remains skeptical despite Figure 3, because false evidence from the small model could degrade otherwise correct LVLM responses in open-world scenarios.
- The small detection models used may not be representative; the paper should also explore larger detection models to make the comparison fairer.
- Evaluations are limited to yes/no VQA questions; the paper should include open-ended questions and tasks requiring more detailed reasoning to fully capture hallucination behavior.
- The paper should extend comparisons to boosting/bagging ensemble methods, as the current setting is essentially a fixed evidence injection.
- Missing comparison with recent state-of-the-art MLLMs such as LLaVA 1.5 (trained on VG data) and other models fine-tuned on domain-specific data. The paper needs to discuss why evidence-based prompting may be superior to sampling-based fine-tuning approaches.
- Lack of detailed trade-off analysis between evidence-based prompting and fine-tuning; a thorough comparison is needed.
- Unanswered questions about object overlap: what fraction of objects in the questions already appear in the evaluation dataset? What happens if the visual evidence prompt contains objects not mentioned in the question, or only objects not in the question? How robust is the method to synonym substitutions (e.g., “cup” vs. “mug”)?
- Pixel location scaling matters: the paper uses absolute coordinates (e.g., `cup <0,284,133,424>`) but does not test normalized coordinates or scaled coordinates. How does the LVLM differentiate object relations when pixel scales are altered?
- Ablation needed: provide performance when only object labels (without bounding boxes or relations) are used as visual evidence. If that also works well, the necessity of the full proposed method must be explained.
- The method should be evaluated on open-vocabulary objects and few-shot relation settings to test generalization.
- Figure 4 shows a decline for the preposition “at” and only slight improvement for “under”; the paper should explain these counterintuitive results.
- Table 9 shows that RelTR (SGG model) has much higher performance than Motifs, yet the hallucination mitigation gains on LVLMs are comparable or even opposite. The paper needs to analyze this discrepancy.
