## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes UniAudio, a single multi-scale Transformer foundation model that tokenizes audio (speech, sounds, music, singing) and condition modalities into discrete codes, then performs next-token prediction. It handles 11 audio generation tasks, is trained on 165K hours of audio (1B parameters), and claims state-of-the-art or competitive results. The architecture decomposes attention into frame-level (global) and token-level (local) to handle long sequences from residual vector quantization. Training covers 7 tasks with additional fine-tuning for 4 more. All data is public, compute is moderate (16xMI200 GPUs).

### Strengths
- **Goal and scope**: The idea of a single model covering many audio generation tasks (speech, music, sounds) is widely supported. Reviewers note this is the first system to tackle so many tasks with one backbone, requiring only fine-tuning for new tasks.  
- **Architecture innovation**: The multi-scale Transformer (frame-level global attention, token-level local attention) is considered an excellent idea for handling long token sequences, offering the community a new design option.  
- **Experimental thoroughness**: The model was compared against specialized baselines using both objective and subjective evaluations (Amazon Turk), with an anonymized demo website. Training on all tasks together is compared against single-task baselines (though only for one comparison).  
- **Speech and enhancement results**: Decent performance on speech tasks and denoising is noted.  
- **Practical aspects**: All data and code are public; compute requirements are not out of reach for academic institutions.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and insight**: Several reviewers note that all components (EnCodec-like tokenizer, Transformer, mixture-of-datasets training) are existing, making the contribution largely an engineering effort. The paper offers little beyond “large data + existing codecs + multi-scale Transformer works”—the architecture contribution is not well ablated.  
- **Insufficient evidence for multi‑domain synergy**: It is not convincingly shown that training on all domains (speech, music, sound) is better than training separate domain‑specific models. The reported improvement over single‑task models is small, and no ablation study isolating each domain’s contribution (e.g., all combinations of domains) is provided. One reviewer questions: “convince me that training on speech helps music, even with access to enormous unlabeled music datasets.”  
- **Weak performance on text‑to‑music/audio**: The model performs worst compared to baselines on text‑to‑music, the task where long‑range dependencies should be most critical. This undermines the claimed advantage of the architecture for long sequences.  
- **Poor evaluation of long‑sequence capability**: Most evaluated tasks (speech enhancement, voice conversion, etc.) are inherently local and do not require modeling dependencies beyond a few seconds. The architecture seems overkill, and the one task that could benefit (text‑to‑music) has limited performance, possibly due to smaller training data. An ablation comparing architectures for long‑sequence tasks is missing.  
- **Presentation and clarity issues**: The paper is dense; details of the audio tokenizer are relegated to the appendix. Figure 1 (architecture) is difficult to understand without annotations. Table 1 is placed without context, and the comparison is considered misleading because cited methods may also be capable of those tasks (they simply did not include them). Table 3 column headers for subjective/objective metrics are swapped.  
- **Input representation limitations**: The model is tightly coupled to its input representations (T5, mHuBERT, and a learned neural codec). Biases and quantization errors from each component compound, and the entire model must be retrained if any representation changes.  
- **No demonstration of fine‑tuning to an entirely new modality**: The paper fine‑tunes only for tasks within the existing modality set; adding a completely new modality would require a new special token and substantial retraining, limiting generalization.  
- **Evaluation coverage and human evaluation**: The wide evaluation is hard to parse in tables; a bar chart would help. Only Amazon Turk was used; subject experts (e.g., musicians, speech therapists) could provide more reliable judgments. The reasons for selecting the four fine‑tuning tasks are not explained.  
- **Missing ablations and analyses**: No ablation on data quantity (e.g., effect of scaling up data), no comparison with alternative codecs (e.g., DAC), and no analysis of why an end‑to‑end approach (learning the codec jointly) was avoided.  
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 1, confidence 5) is highly critical, citing very limited novelty and contribution to the community. Another reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 1) nevertheless praises the work’s scope, careful evaluation, and public resources, illustrating a stark split in opinion. The other two reviewers (scores 5) are moderately positive but raise significant concerns about synergy, evaluation, and practical limitations.
