## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an information-theoretic framework for evaluating Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning in LLMs, quantifying “information gain” at each step to detect errors without requiring step-level annotated data. A supervisor model is trained to predict the final answer given partial CoT steps, and information gain is computed as the difference in log-probability of the correct answer between successive steps. Experiments on a toy arithmetic dataset and GSM-8K show the method outperforms Outcome Reward Models (ORM) and Math-Shepherd in identifying sub-task failures. Reviewers are split: some praise the novel theoretical perspective and clean formulation, while others raise concerns about the restrictive assumptions, limited generalizability to complex real-world tasks, the need for a separate supervisor model, and the validity of the sample-wise information gain measure.

### Strengths
1. **Novel information-theoretic perspective**: Applying information gain to quantify each reasoning step’s contribution is a fresh and rigorous approach, enabling more granular error detection than outcome-only methods (R1, R2, R3, R5). The algebraic formulation and Bayesian network analysis are clean and represent an important step toward theoretical understanding of CoT (R3, R5).
2. **Clear mathematical foundations and practical algorithm**: The paper provides well-structured definitions, systematically translates assumptions into an algorithm, and conducts thorough empirical validation on toy data and GSM-8K with strong results (R1, R5).
3. **Controlled experiments demonstrate advantage over baselines**: Experiments in Figure 3 and Table 1 clearly show the information gain method outperforms ORM and Math-Shepherd in detecting errors in intermediate steps (R1, R2, R5). The improvements are convincing (R2).
4. **Potential to reduce annotation cost**: The framework does not require step-by-step human annotations; it only needs input-output pairs for training the supervisor model, which is a cost-effective alternative to process supervision (R2, R5). (Some reviewers later contradict this claim—see weaknesses.)
5. **Presentation quality**: The paper is well written, easy to follow, and the approach is presented with good structure (R1, R4, R5). R3 explicitly says the presentation is very good and enjoyable to read.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited evaluation to toy and GSM-8K; unclear applicability to real-world reasoning tasks**: Experiments are confined to a synthetic arithmetic toy dataset and GSM-8K, which do not fully represent the complexity of realistic reasoning. More challenging tasks such as MMLU or MATH should be tested to ascertain practical utility (R1, R2, R4). The reliance on controlled settings with clearly defined error types raises concerns about generalizability to naturally occurring, diverse error patterns (R2). **Minority reviewer R3 (score 8) does not raise this concern, indicating a disagreement in perceived severity.**
2. **Restrictive assumptions about primitive tasks and step decomposition**: The framework assumes a one-to-one mapping between each reasoning step and a single primitive task, but in reality a step may involve multiple sub-tasks or no primitive task at all (filler steps) (R1). Terms like “step” and the set of primitive tasks are not clearly defined in the LLM scenario, making it unclear how to define them for realistic tasks (e.g., Blocksworld, commonsense QA) (R4, R5). The method may not handle nonlinear or branching reasoning paths, and assumes a linear sequence of steps (R2, R5). The impact of different primitive task definitions on conclusions is not studied (R5).
3. **Supervisor model training contradicts “no annotation” claim and adds complexity**: The paper claims the framework does not require expensive annotated datasets, yet it trains a separate supervisor model that needs input–output pairs (final answers) for finetuning (R2, R5). This finetuning still requires data that may be scarce or expensive, and the computational cost of training the supervisor is not compared to that of process supervision models like Math-Shepherd (R2). The claim of being annotation-free is thus unsubstantiated (R2). Additionally, there is no guarantee that the learned supervisor accurately models the conditional distribution \(P(Y|Z_{1:j}, Q)\); the training data and sampled trajectories must follow a distribution similar to the final task—this requirement is not addressed (R4).
4. **Sample-wise information gain measure may be invalid**: The sample-wise mutual information (Equation 5) can be negative, yet the paper argues it is non-negative based on an intuitive justification (lines 299–303) that is not convincing. This is a core theoretical weakness that undermines the otherwise elegant development (R3). Reviewer 1 also notes that Proposition 3.4 is problematic because Equation (3) is asymmetric with respect to \(Y\) and \(X_j\), while mutual information should be symmetric.
5. **Handling of irrelevant vs. error steps is unclear**: The paper mentions that irrelevant steps can occur but does not explain how the information gain method distinguishes between steps that are irrelevant (harmless) and those that actively introduce errors (harmful). Treating them equally could lead to inaccurate assessment of the reasoning process (R1).
6. **False negatives and correct answers through faulty reasoning**: The framework may not flag CoT chains where the model arrives at the correct final answer despite having incorrect intermediate steps (false negatives) (R2). The paper evaluates only on samples where the final answer is incorrect (Section 5.1.1), ignoring this common failure mode where the final answer is correct but reasoning is flawed (R5). The framework also does not address cases where questions can be answered directly without CoT; such steps would be incorrectly labeled as important, and the impact on information gain is not analyzed (R2).
7. **Missing detailed error pattern analysis**: There is no in-depth analysis of where errors occur in intermediate steps, how often they happen, or whether stepwise errors form identifiable patterns. The paper should demonstrate how reliably the supervisor model identifies specific error points (R2, R5).
8. **Potential flaws in baseline discussion**: In Section 5.1.1, the paper claims ORM becomes confident of error right after \(\lambda_2\) even though the error occurs at \(\lambda_3\), labeling this a pitfall. However, this behavior may actually be correct error detection; the statement appears to be a misinterpretation (R5).
9. **Minor errors and clarity issues**: The paper contains typos (e.g., repeated “detect” on line 055, “tasks” on line 164, tense inconsistencies on lines 295/429) (R1). The annotation for task \(\lambda_2\) in Appendix B.1.1 appears to be incorrect (R5). The distinction between sample-wise and distribution-wise information gain needs better explanation (R3).
