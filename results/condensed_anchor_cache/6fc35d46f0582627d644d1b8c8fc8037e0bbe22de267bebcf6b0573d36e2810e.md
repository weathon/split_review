## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces WL-trees, a new tree-based representation constructed via a variant of BFS that disallows revisiting parent nodes. The authors claim that WL-trees are equivalent to the colors computed by the 1-WL algorithm and propose an algorithm to identify anchored subgraphs corresponding to a given WL-tree. Experiments analyze two existing GNN models (CLIP and Nested GNN) using metrics like average counts of anchored subgraphs and conditional entropy.

### Strengths

- The motivation of analyzing what structural information is encoded in GNN node representations is important.
- The formulations of theorems are formal and could be potentially useful.
- The paper offers a different perspective for analyzing graph structures underlying message-passing GNNs.
- Connections between WL-trees and anchored subgraphs are discussed, and an algorithm to identify anchored subgraphs for a given WL-tree is provided.
- Two existing GNN models (CLIP and Nested GNN) are considered and analyzed in the experiments.
- The presentation is good.

### Weaknesses

- The WL-tree concept essentially corresponds to the well-known notion of tree unravelling; its equivalence to 1-WL coloring is absolutely folklore and has been used for decades. The only difference from standard rooted subtrees (Figure 1 of [1]) is the exclusion of the parent node from the child list, but the paper does not justify why this difference yields a novel or advantageous perspective. All results are simple exercises and no new conceptual contribution is made.
- The paper shows a poor understanding of the related literature. It misstates the relationship between 1-WL and guarded FO₂^∃ — Cai et al. proved that the distinguishing power of 1-WL equals FO₂^∃ (the guarded version is weaker), while Barceló et al. gave a uniform result (every guarded FO₂^∃ unary formula can be turned into an equivalent GNN over all graphs). The paper confuses these two different notions.
- The claimed equivalence between WL-trees and 1-WL is called into question by a counterexample: two triangles and a cycle of length 6 are indistinguishable by 1-WL but would produce different WL-trees. This discrepancy undermines the central claim that WL-trees faithfully represent the information captured by 1-WL.
- The method is not well-defined. Inconsistencies appear in Equations 3–5: the distance constraints differ (dist(i,j) < ℓ vs. dist(i,k) ≤ ℓ) without justification; the symbol \(\dot{\cup}\) is not clearly defined; the mapping \(id(\cdot)\) from tree nodes to graph nodes is not a function in the strict sense because a single graph node may appear multiple times in the tree.
- The practical usefulness of Algorithm 1 is unclear; no concrete scenarios or use cases are provided to demonstrate its benefit.
- The choice of CLIP and Nested GNN for experimental analysis is not justified. Many GNN variants exist but why these two were selected is unexplained, limiting the generality of the results.
- Theorems 12 and 13 are confusing: the use of max in Equation 8 is unexplained, the notation \(G(j,h)\) is undefined, and the claimed bijective mapping between WL-trees and node embeddings for Nested GNN is inconsistent because Nested GNN has expressiveness beyond 1-WL while WL-trees are claimed equivalent to 1-WL.
- The metrics used in the experiments (average counts of anchored subgraphs and conditional entropy) lack theoretical justification; the analysis appears ad hoc, with no established connection between these metrics and a WL-tree’s ability to identify a node’s surrounding structure.
- The experiments only analyze two existing models and do not lead to any new model design or new insight into GNN behavior. The utility of WL-trees as an analysis tool is not demonstrated.
