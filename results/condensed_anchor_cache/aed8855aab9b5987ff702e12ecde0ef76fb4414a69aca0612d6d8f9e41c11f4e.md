## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a new fairness notion called Equal Treatment (ET), which requires that explanation distributions (e.g., Shapley values) be independent of protected attributes, as opposed to demographic parity’s requirement that predictions be independent. The authors introduce an Equal Treatment Inspector that uses a classifier two-sample test (C2ST) based on AUC to test for ET, provide theoretical analysis (largely for linear models), experiments on synthetic and real data, and release an open-source Python package `explanationspace`.

### Strengths
- Novel connection between explainability and algorithmic fairness via the ET definition; the Inspector can identify which features cause unequal treatment. (R1, R4, R5)
- Well-written and well-structured, with helpful illustrative examples (R3, R4). One reviewer (R2) noted the high-level idea is simple and intuitive.
- Releases an operationalized tool (`explanationspace`) – though one reviewer reported documentation issues (see Weaknesses). (R1)
- Experiments on synthetic and real data provide evidence of the implications (R3, R5). One reviewer (R4) praised the thorough review of related works (though placed in the appendix).

**Minority contrast:** Reviewer 4 (score 8) found the method novel and well-thought-out, while Reviewers 2 and 5 (both score 3) raised significant concerns about motivation, novelty, and practical feasibility. Reviewed 1 (score 6) and 3 (score 5) were moderately positive but listed substantial weaknesses.

### Weaknesses

**Motivation and Conceptual Issues**
- [Major] The paper does not convincingly justify why Equal Treatment (ET) is superior to demographic parity. ET is strictly stronger and harder to achieve, yet may still permit indirect discrimination (e.g., height correlated with gender – the model could use height equally but still be unfair) (R2, R5). A real-world example illustrating the limitations of demographic parity and the necessity of ET is needed (R5).
- [Medium] The paper frames demographic parity as a proxy for equal treatment, but the fair ML literature recognizes they are distinct concepts. A clearer distinction would be “equal treatment (new definition) vs. demographic parity” (R4).
- [Medium] The claim that “equal opportunity could lead to reverse discrimination” is unsupported; citations are needed (R2).
- [Minor] The distinction between protected and sensitive attributes should be clarified (R1). Relying on a binary Z is restrictive, especially for intersectionality (R1).

**Theoretical and Methodological Limitations**
- [Major] Theoretical analysis (Section 4) assumes exact Shapley values are available, which is unrealistic for non-IID data and non-linear models. The paper does not discuss how approximations affect theoretical guarantees (R4). Question: “When we do not have access to exact Shapley values, how will the theoretical results be affected?” (R4)
- [Major] Most theoretical results and examples are limited to linear models and i.i.d. data; synthetic data is simple (logistic model, Gaussian). This limits generalizability (R5). The proof in Example 4.3 relies on linearity; in complex non-linear cases, explaining sources of unequal treatment is unclear (R1, R5). Question: “How can the method be generalized to non-linear cases?” (R5)
- [Medium] Statistical independence of Z from explanations is necessary but not sufficient for independence of the model from Z (R1).
- [Medium] The space of possible explanation functions beyond Shapley and a brief LIME mention is not explored. Choice directly affects the ET definition; insufficient guidance on selection and potential biases (R4). Question: “What are other explanation functions that can be used? … what makes one function better than another?” (R4)
- [Medium] No comparison to related works that use explanation methods for fairness diagnostics: “Model Explanation Disparities as a Fairness Diagnostic” and “Explanability for fair machine learning” (R1). Also missing comparison to individual fairness (R2).
- [Minor] Using AUC rather than accuracy in the C2ST introduces a tradeoff (scale invariance vs. caring about false positives/negatives) not discussed (R1).

**Practical Concerns and Experimental Shortcomings**
- [Major] Practical applicability is questionable. For legal compliance (e.g., Equal Credit Opportunity Act), the focus is on outcome discrimination, not explanation distributions (R3). The C2ST framework introduces uncertainty (type I/II error, classifier choice) instead of a deterministic number, which may not be legally admissible (R3).
- [Medium] Experiments are too simple (3 features) and lack sufficient discussion. No comparison to other fairness notions or methods; results such as Table 5 are only in the appendix (R1).
- [Medium] The paper does not address whether the ET Inspector should be applied to train/val data instead of test data (R1).
- [Minor] The novelty of using AUC as a test statistic for C2ST is not new (cited [1] in R3’s review). Prior work should be acknowledged (R3).

**Writing, Organization, and Code Issues**
- [Medium] Important content (related works, detailed experiments, description of explanation functions) is relegated to the appendix, making it hard to appreciate contributions (R1, R2, R4). Shapley values are introduced late in the appendix (R2).
- [Medium] The `explanationspace` code has documentation issues: installation instructions do not work, function names in tutorials are inconsistent with the API (e.g., `from fairtools.detector import ExplanationAudit` should be `from explanationspace import ExplanationAudit`). The tutorial lacks `requirements.txt` and clear description of results (R1).
- [Minor] The paper does not use the ICLR template (R5). “Natural data” should be “real data” (R3). Figure 2 is too small (R3).
- [Minor] An algorithm or bulleted procedure would improve readability (R1). The approach relies only on the model’s explanation function; features independent of the model might be missed (R1).
