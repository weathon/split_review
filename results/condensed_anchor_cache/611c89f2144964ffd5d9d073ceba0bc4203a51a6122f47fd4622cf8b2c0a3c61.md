## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes GPTBIAS, a framework that uses GPT-4 as a meta-evaluator to assess bias in LLMs across nine bias categories (e.g., gender, race, socioeconomic status). It generates “Bias Attack Instructions,” collects model responses, and computes intersectional bias scores along with qualitative insights (bias types, affected demographics, reasons, suggestions). Experiments are run on models including BLOOM, LLaMA, OPT, and GPT-3.5, and compare with StereoSet and CrowS-Pairs. Reviewers’ overall assessments are mixed (scores 3, 5, 3, 5; two positive, two cautious). They acknowledge the framework’s flexibility and clear motivation but raise concerns about limited novelty, reliance on GPT-4, lack of evidence for the added qualitative insights, weak comparison with baselines, missing ablation studies, and absence of multilingual or resource-efficient extensions. There is disagreement on the writing quality: two reviewers find it well‑structured (R1, R4), while one (R3) cites numerous writing flaws.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation and approach**: The paper addresses an important and timely problem (bias in LLMs) and the framework is easy to follow (R2, R4).  
- **Innovative use of GPT-4 as evaluator**: Using GPT‑4 to conduct bias analysis on other LLMs broadens the scope of bias evaluation and offers a unique angle, including intersectional bias (R1, R2).  
- **Methodological rigor**: The four‑step pipeline (instruction generation, response collection, evaluation, scoring) is well‑structured, with detailed descriptions of bias types and instructions (R1, R2).  
- **Actionable feedback**: The framework provides not only a score but also reasons and suggestions, which is valuable for model developers (R2).  
- **Flexibility**: GPTBIAS requires no labels, sample pairs, or access to model weights, making it more flexible than traditional methods (R4).  
- **Comprehensive experiments**: The paper includes experiments on several models and nine bias types (R1, R3).  

### Weaknesses

- **Reliance on GPT‑4 as evaluator** (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
  - The framework depends exclusively on GPT‑4, raising concerns about accessibility (cost, licensing) and bias propagation from GPT‑4 itself (R2).  
  - Using the same model family for benchmark creation and evaluation biases results toward GPT‑4 models (R1).  
  - *Request*: Test with alternative evaluators (e.g., GPT‑3.5, open‑source LLMs) and discuss performance trade‑offs (R2, R4).  
- **No evidence for the value of qualitative insights** (R3).  
  - The paper claims GPTBIAS outputs reasons and suggestions for improvement, but experiments only report bias scores and types (Tables 3–5). A case study demonstrating how these insights aid bias analysis or lead to important conclusions is missing (R3).  
- **Unclear comparison with baseline methods** (R1, R3, R4).  
  - Table 3 compares GPTBIAS (score range 0–1) with StereoSet and CrowS‑Pairs (scores typically >1), making direct interpretation difficult. Normalization or a unified metric is needed (R3).  
  - The heterogeneous nature of these benchmarks makes the comparison unreliable (R4).  
  - *Request*: Perform an ablation study comparing evaluation frameworks on the same test dataset to isolate whether GPTBIAS’s test set (not the framework itself) drives its superior results (R3).  
- **Limited to English** (R1, R2).  
  - The framework only applies to English, missing linguistic and cultural variations.  
  - *Request*: Discuss plans or necessary modifications for multilingual adaptation (R1, R2).  
- **No ablation or stability analysis** (R3, R4).  
  - The paper does not ablate the evaluator components (e.g., instruction format, scoring function) to show their impact (R4).  
  - The stability of the evaluation across multiple runs is unknown; experiments on variance are needed (R4).  
- **Outdated target models** (R3).  
  - Experiments use LLaMA, OPT, BLOOM, GPT‑3.5 – consider newer models like LLaMA‑3 or GPT‑4, or clarify why they are unsuitable (R3).  
- **Brief analysis of intersectional bias and bias types** (R3).  
  - Sections 5.3 and 5.4 only describe data from Tables 4 and 5 without drawing conclusions or linking to bias mitigation strategies (R3).  
- **Missing comparison with recent bias evaluation work** (R1).  
  - The paper should compare with ROBBIE (Esiobu et al., 2023), which suggests combining multiple benchmarks rather than using a single one.  
- **Lack of concrete guidance for intersectional scores** (R2).  
  - Intersectional bias scores are introduced but no example or case study shows how developers can practically use them to improve models.  
- **Computational intensity** (R2).  
  - The framework appears resource‑heavy, limiting frequent evaluations. *Request*: Propose a streamlined version or compare a lighter version with the full framework (R2).  
- **Limited discussion on bias mitigation** (R1).  
  - The paper lacks experiments or discussion on how the identified biases can be mitigated (R1).  
- **Writing quality (disagreement)**:  
  - Two reviewers (R1, R4) find the paper well‑written. One reviewer (R3) points out multiple logical problems and typos (e.g., missing verb line 68, incomplete sentence lines 13–15, ambiguous phrasing lines 246–247) and recommends thorough proofreading (R3).  
- **Minor**: Citation style should use `\citep` (R4).  
- **Lack of technical novelty** (R4).  
  - The framework largely relies on ChatGPT‑style evaluation, which is already common practice; the formalization is appreciated but the contribution to the LLM community is limited.
