## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Text-as-Parameter (TaP), a prompt optimization method that uses textual feedback from an optimizer LLM to iteratively rewrite prompts. Experiments are conducted on task-oriented dialogue (MultiWOZ) and medical QA (general and traditional Chinese medicine). Most reviewers (4 out of 5) rate the paper as 3, citing limited novelty, insufficient baselines, small evaluation sets, and lack of open-source model tests. One reviewer (score 5) is notably more positive, emphasizing the richness of textual feedback and the comprehensive experimental validation. Across all reviews, the method is seen as effective but not sufficiently novel or rigorously evaluated.

### Strengths
- Prompt optimization is an important and urgent topic for LLMs (R2).
- Using detailed textual feedback instead of numerical scores is intuitively beneficial and provides richer, more nuanced guidance for prompt adjustments (R1, R2, R4). The self-improvement/refinement framework is promising (R2).
- Without additional training or fine-tuning, TaP improves performance across prompting styles (standard and ReAct) under GPT-4o and Gemini (R3). Results on MultiWOZ improve over the numerical baseline by a large margin (R2, R5).
- A comprehensive set of experiments across simulated (task-oriented dialogue) and real-world (medical QA) settings validates the approach’s effectiveness compared to traditional numerical feedback methods (R4, R5). The TaP method demonstrates steady improvement in complete rates over multiple epochs, indicating robustness (R4).
- The paper presents a clear comparison between prompt optimization and other methods like prompt tuning and inference-time self-refinement (R5). The framework diagram is well-crafted (R3).

### Weaknesses
- Limited novelty: The core idea of using textual feedback for prompt optimization is already present in prior works such as APO (which also gives suggestions on how to edit the prompt except for the meta-prompt difference), TextGrad, and self-refine. This is the most frequently raised weakness across all reviewers (R1, R2, R3, R5). The claim of treating text as a “parameter” is overstated; the method mirrors traditional prompt optimization and does not fully leverage text as an integrated parameter of the model (R4). The shift from numerical to textual feedback alone may not constitute a sufficient contribution (R4).
- Insufficient baseline comparisons: Only one baseline (GPO) is compared experimentally, despite related work mentioning APO, OPPO, OPRO. Missing comparisons with recent prompt optimization baselines (R1, R2, R5). One reviewer specifically suggests benchmarking TaP against multi-agent collaboration methods (e.g., MultiPrompter) because the framework effectively functions as an LLM-based multi-agent system when user, rewriter, and system are all LLMs (R4).
- Small and non-standard evaluation datasets: Experiments are conducted on only 100 MultiWOZ instances, 30 general medicine pairs, and 30 traditional Chinese medicine pairs. This contrasts with baselines like GPO that evaluate on multiple standard benchmarks (BBH, GSM8K, MMLU, WSC, WebNLG). Evaluating only small selected samples (e.g., 30) raises cherry-picking concerns (R3). The choice of task-oriented dialogue and medical QA over popular benchmarks is questioned (R3). Lack of evaluation on general benchmarks such as BBH and MMLU (R1).
- Limited model scope: Only closed-source models (GPT-4o, Gemini) are evaluated. No experiments on open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama-2/3, Qwen-2), preventing direct comparison with baselines like GPO that use Llama-2 (R1, R3). It remains unclear whether the approach applies to models of different sizes and types (R3).
- Missing analyses and ablations: No detailed analysis of convergence, generalization, or the impact of the initial prompt (R1). More ablation studies are needed to understand how the framework works (R2). The reliance on a GPT-simulated user is not validated with human annotations; the quality of simulation is unclear, making it difficult to judge the overall system (R2).
- Writing and presentation issues: The introduction does not introduce experimental results (R2). More explanation is needed for trajectories and other design choices rather than just proposing a name (R2). The comparison with gradient-based methods in Table 2 is questionable because TaP relies on discrete prompt rewriting rather than continuous optimization, which may lead to inconsistent improvements (R4).
