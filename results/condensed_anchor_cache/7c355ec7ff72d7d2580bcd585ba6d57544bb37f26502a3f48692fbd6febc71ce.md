## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates class-incremental learning (CIL) using pre-trained models (PTMs). Through a causal analysis, the authors identify an imbalance in causal effects between old and new data, which they term the "confrontation phenomenon," and propose BaCE, a method that rebalances these effects via a modified knowledge distillation loss and replay. Experiments on image classification (CIFAR-100, 5-Datasets), text classification, and NER, using ResNet-18, ViT, and BERT backbones, show gains over existing methods. Reviewer scores vary: R1 (6, confidence 4) finds the perspective interesting but notes limited analysis of the method’s source of gains; R2 (5, confidence 4) appreciates the clear causal story but questions similarity to prior work [1]; R3 (3, confidence 4) strongly criticizes experimental flaws such as pre-training data overlap and selective baseline reporting; R4 (6, confidence 3) endorses the new view and results while raising concerns about pre-training overlap and dataset scale.

### Strengths
- **Novel causal perspective on forgetting**: The paper reframes catastrophic forgetting in CIL as an imbalance of causal effects between new and old data. R1, R2, and R4 all highlight that the causal analysis provides clear insight into the stability-plasticity trade-off. R2 notes the causal graph makes the confrontation phenomenon easy to understand.
- **Proposed BaCE method**: The rebalancing strategy is simple and effective. R1 and R4 consider the method of interest and novel, with R4 specifically praising the use of causal rebalancing. R3 finds the method new and appreciates that it uses a pre‑trained model (a strength in R3’s view).
- **Strong empirical results**: Across multiple tasks and backbones (ResNet-18, ViT, BERT), BaCE outperforms a range of CIL baselines. R1, R2, and R4 note that the experimental results clearly support the method’s validity. R2 states it “achieves higher accuracy than other baselines.”
- **Readability**: R1 finds the paper easy to read and well documented, with the method clearly supported by the causal hypotheses. (Other reviewers disagree on readability – see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
- **Pre‑training data overlap / information leak**: R3 and R4 both raise the issue that ImageNet‑21k (used for supervised pre‑training) already contains many classes from CIFAR‑100 and the 5‑Datasets, so those classes are not truly “new” in continual learning. R3 calls this an information leak and cites Kim et al. (ICML 2023) as the correct protocol (removing overlapping classes). R4 prefers self‑supervised pre‑training to avoid label leakage. R1 and R2 did not comment on this, indicating disagreement among reviewers on the severity of the issue.
- **Unfair comparison with non‑PTM baselines**: R3 argues that comparing BaCE (which uses a strong supervised PTM) against baselines that do not use a PTM inflates performance. R4 also notes that many baselines may not use the same pre‑training, making the comparison unfair.
- **Selective reporting of L2P results**: R3 points out that in Table 1, when L2P performs worse than BaCE, the reported L2P numbers match the L2P paper, but when L2P performs better (in some settings per the L2P paper), those numbers are omitted. This calls into question the fairness of baseline reporting.
- **Missing comparisons**: R1 notes that IL2M (a balancing distillation method) is mentioned but not included in experiments; a direct comparison would be insightful. R1 also asks why only classic rehearsal‑free methods (EWC, LwF) are compared in the buffer‑empty setting and suggests more recent rehearsal‑free works (e.g., FeTrIL, WA, BiC, LUCIR, and several CVPR/NeurIPS papers from 2020‑2023). R1 explicitly lists five recent rehearsal‑free methods that should have been included.
- **Limited dataset scale**: R2 and R4 observe that the experiments use only CIFAR‑100 and 5‑Datasets, which have a limited number of classes. They recommend evaluation on larger datasets such as ImageNet to better capture the challenges of CIL. R4 notes that more diverse visual features in larger datasets would provide a more robust evaluation.
- **Computational cost not discussed**: R1 points out that Table 8 (appendix) shows BaCE can be 2× to 5× computationally more expensive during training, but this limitation is not mentioned in the main paper.
- **Lack of clarity on what the method actually solves**: R1 notes the paper juggles between establishing the confrontation phenomenon and outperforming SOTA, yet does not deeply analyze where performance gains come from (e.g., role of the PTM vs. the causal rebalancing). Interesting insights are relegated to the appendix.
- **Similarity to prior causal work**: R2 remarks that the causal graph construction is very similar to that in [1] (reference not specified), questioning the novelty. The paper does not adequately differentiate its causal analysis from [1].
- **Ambiguity in the notion of “confrontation” vs. class‑imbalance bias**: R2 asks whether prediction bias from class imbalance and the confrontation phenomenon are different, implying the paper’s framing is unclear. In addition, R3 notes that data imbalance in continual learning is not new, and the related work discussion on this topic is relegated to the appendix.
- **Missing definition of evaluation metric**: R3 asks what “average accuracy” means – whether it is the average incremental accuracy or the final accuracy after the last task. This is essential for reproducibility.
- **Presentation and readability issues**: R1 notes Table 1 is split into two parts and reduced, and all figures are too squeezed. R3 states that many parts of the paper are hard to read. R1 also says formatting needs to be fixed and readable.
- **Absence of limitation discussion**: R1 criticizes the paper for not discussing limitations of the proposed method (e.g., the computational cost, and perhaps when the method might fail). The paper focuses on “establishing the phenomenon” but does not fully explore its scope.
