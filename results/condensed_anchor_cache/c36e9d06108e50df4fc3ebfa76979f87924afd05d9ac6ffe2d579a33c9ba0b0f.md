## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents YAIB, a modular framework for reproducible and comparable clinical ML experiments on ICU data. It integrates five datasets (MIMIC III/IV, eICU, HiRID, AUMCdb) and provides an end-to-end pipeline from cohort definition to model evaluation. The benchmark covers five prediction tasks (mortality, acute kidney injury, sepsis, kidney function, length of stay) and includes experiments demonstrating the impact of dataset, cohort definition, and preprocessing on performance. The code is open-source.

### Strengths
- **Reproducibility and comparability**: The paper directly addresses the urgent need for reproducible and comparable ML experiments in healthcare, a critical issue (R1, R4, R5).
- **Multi-dataset unification**: YAIB unifies MIMIC III/IV, eICU, HiRID, and AUMCdb for the first time in a single framework, enabling cross-dataset validation and transfer learning (R1, R2, R5). The inclusion of AUMCdb is novel (R2). The datasets cover US and European populations, promoting broadly applicable research (R5).
- **Modular and extensible design**: The framework decouples cohort definition, preprocessing, feature extraction, model training, and evaluation, allowing users to easily add custom tasks, datasets, or model architectures (R2, R4). This flexibility also holds potential for domain adaptation, time-series generation, and other downstream applications (R4).
- **Thorough experimental investigation**: The paper includes extensive experiments and ablation studies that clearly demonstrate the impact of cohort definitions, preprocessing strategies, and training protocols on performance (R1, R2, R4). The unified pipeline allows controlled comparisons that are often missing in prior work (R2).
- **Clarity and documentation**: The paper is well-written and organized, with informative tables (especially Table 1) and figures (R2, R3, R5). The code is well-structured, and the appendix provides comprehensive guidelines and example scripts (R3, R5).
- **High potential impact**: Once public, YAIB can substantially reduce the overhead of benchmarking and validation in clinical ML, enabling researchers to compare results across multiple real-world ICU datasets and accelerating method development and real-world deployment (R2, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited feature set excludes critical clinical data**: YAIB extracts only 52 features (mainly vital signs and basic lab tests). It excludes detailed diagnostic codes (ICD-9/10), medication administration records, clinical notes, and other rich categorical/intervention data (e.g., ventilation modes, specific interventions). This restricts model complexity and prevents evaluation of tasks like adverse drug event prediction. The focus on continuous time-varying features overlooks informative binary/categorical variables. Performance in Tables 3 and 4 shows classic ML and DL results are very similar, likely due to this limited feature scope (R1, R4). Time-series data is numeric, not waveform (ECG, PPG); incorporating waveform data would capture subtle physiological changes missed by lower-frequency numeric data (R2).
- **Missing definitions and dataset statistics**: Key terms like “harmonization” and “clinical concepts” are not clearly defined. For harmonization, it is unclear whether it is a simple renaming of features or involves complex transformations to account for differences in data distributions or measurement protocols (R2). Basic dataset statistics (number of patients/stays, class prevalence, range of regression targets) are only in the appendix; including them in the main paper would help explain performance discrepancies (e.g., Figure 2 may reflect prevalence differences across datasets) (R2). It is also unclear how train/validation/test splits are stratified (by dataset or outcome) or whether one dataset dominates the pooled training set for any task (R2).
- **Insufficient quantitative evidence for the impact of experiment choices**: The paper claims experiment choices have a major impact but often does not report specific performance gains/losses (e.g., “improved by X%”) or statistical significance. Statements like “differences were bigger for some datasets than others” are not robust. More rigorous ablation studies with consistent preprocessing/model choices showing significant performance changes due to outcome label definitions, exclusion criteria, or cohort selection would better motivate the framework (R5).
- **Tension between flexibility and reproducibility**: The framework allows users to configure preprocessing differently across datasets, which could undermine reproducibility if researchers apply different steps. The reproducibility value hinges on future work precisely citing and following YAIB’s exact pipeline. The authors should recommend specific approaches for citing YAIB usage in future publications to promote consistent methodology (R5).
- **Missing comparison with existing clinical ML toolkits**: The paper does not contrast YAIB with other clinical ML frameworks (e.g., references [1,2] noted by R3). Such a comparison would clarify its novelty and positioning relative to prior toolkits (R3).
- **Venue fit questioned by one reviewer**: One reviewer (R3, score 5) expresses concern that the paper is primarily a benchmark contribution with limited technical innovation, suggesting it would be more suitable for NeurIPS benchmark track or a journal like Scientific Data. In contrast, another reviewer (R4, score 8) considers the work innovative and extremely helpful, highlighting its high contribution. This disagreement is noted.
- **Clarity on “task” variations in Section 4**: The first two variations (to cohort and feature selection) are not changes to the clinical task definition itself; only the third variation (sepsis definition) qualifies. The description should be reworded to avoid confusion (R2).
- **Missing preprocessing details**: For aggregated features, the time windows and update frequencies are not specified (R2). The handling of variations in units and measurement protocols within and across datasets is not explained, nor is the reason why therapeutic interventions and comorbidities (some mentioned in Appendix C) are missing despite their importance for prediction (R4).
- **Interpretability of results in Table 4**: Kidney Function and Length of Stay metrics are reported as absolute MAE without units or ranges; reporting MAE as a percentage relative to the valid range would provide meaningful context (R2).
- **Support for custom models and outcome definitions**: Code examples primarily cover scikit-learn and built-in PyTorch models. More explicit guidance on integrating a novel custom PyTorch model from a separate file is needed (R5). It is unclear whether models beyond scikit-learn are hardcoded or wrapped (R5 question). Additionally, it is unclear if users can define their own outcome definitions across datasets (e.g., threshold-based AKI definitions) or if labels are strictly dataset-internal (R5 question).
- **Typographical errors**: Missing space in “repositoryto” on page 4 (R2, R4).
