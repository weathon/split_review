## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Iterative LLM Supervised CSL (ILS-CSL) framework, which integrates LLM-based causal inference with score-based causal structure learning (CSL) in an iterative loop. Instead of asking LLMs for pairwise ancestral information (as in prior work), the method lets the data-driven algorithm propose edges and then asks the LLM to decide edge existence/direction using a specific prompt (inspired by Kıcıman et al. 2023). These LLM-provided constraints are fed back as soft or hard prior information to update the score-based search. Experiments on eight real-world datasets from the bnlearn repository show reduced error counts and consistent accuracy improvements over baselines (MINOBSx, CaMML, etc.). The paper also provides a semi-quantitative analysis (Section 4) comparing the approach to ancestral estimation.

### Strengths
- **Timely and important problem**: Leveraging LLM commonsense causal knowledge to enhance data-driven CSL is a valid and current direction (Reviewer 1: high confidence in this point; also noted by Reviewers 4 and 5).
- **Novel use of LLM for direct edge constraints**: The core idea of using the data-driven algorithm as a driving force to let the LLM decide on edge existence (rather than ancestral relations) is novel and addresses a crucial gap in prior LLM-based CSL methods (Reviewer 1: “smart way,” effective demonstration; Reviewer 2: “new idea of providing directed edge constraints”; Reviewer 5: “raises the crucial concern about distinguishing direct vs. indirect relations”).
- **Practical algorithm and prompt**: A concrete iterative algorithm (Algorithm 1) is given, along with the specific prompt for eliciting structural constraints (Reviewer 3).
- **Section 4 analysis**: The discussion of important concerns (error count estimation, comparison with ancestral estimation) is interesting and provides semi-quantitative insight (Reviewer 1; also Reviewer 5 found the analyses intriguing).
- **Empirical improvement**: The method consistently improves accuracy across eight real-world datasets; Reviewer 1 (the most positive) highlighted this, though other reviewers had questions about reproducibility and baselines (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical rigor and formal presentation**  
  – Mathematics is not formal; notations are poorly defined (e.g., D defined as natural numbers, then used as nodes). Equations (1) and (2) are incorrect as definitions of Bayesian networks (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  – The proof of Lemma 1 (in appendix) is hard to follow and may contain errors; assumptions on the score function are not stated (Reviewer 3).  
  – Overall, the algorithm is presented in a style between strict math and pseudo-code, making it difficult to evaluate correctness (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  – The paper should adopt standard mathematical language from causality and graph theory (Reviewer 2: high confidence; Reviewer 3: high confidence).

- **Algorithm design flaws and unclear choices**  
  – Why is the LLM queried only for edges predicted by the score-based method, and not for missing edges? Section 4.1 touches this but remains vague (Reviewer 5, high confidence).  
  – When the LLM says “B.changing V2 causes a change in V1,” the algorithm adds a direct edge V2→V1; this should represent an ancestral relation, not necessarily a direct one (Reviewer 5).  
  – When the LLM says “C.changes in V1 and V2 are not correlated,” the algorithm only eliminates direct edges; it should eliminate all ancestral relationships between V1 and V2 (Reviewer 5).  
  – The three prior constraints: the second (path existence) and the implied ancestor relation are equivalent, not just one-way as implied (Reviewer 5).  
  – No discussion of how undirected edges are handled when the score-based method outputs a PDAG (Reviewer 5).  
  – No guarantee that the algorithm terminates (“until no new constraints are added”) nor a procedure for resolving conflicts between LLM answers or between an LLM answer and the data (Reviewer 5).  
  – If the data-driven algorithm outputs an empty graph (e.g., due to conservative hyperparameters), the LLM cannot provide any feedback (Reviewer 1).  
  – The method is essentially a way to encode prior knowledge; existing literature on causal discovery with prior knowledge (e.g., Constraint-based or score-based with forbidden/required edges) has solid theoretical foundations, which this paper does not provide (Reviewer 5). The term “supervised” is misleading and should be replaced with “facilitated/assisted” (Reviewer 5).

- **Concerns about the role of LLM**  
  – Why would a general, untuned LLM always improve performance? It could introduce wrong prior information, acting like a “layperson” (Reviewer 2, high confidence).  
  – LLMs hallucinate; there is no theoretical guarantee that LLM supervision is beneficial (Reviewer 3).  
  – More fundamentally, causal discovery recovers relations among *variables*, not variable names; if the relations are already known to people (and thus answerable by LLM), the need for data-driven discovery is questionable (Reviewer 5, high confidence). This reduces the contribution to a method for prior knowledge elicitation, not a core advance in either LLM or causal discovery (Reviewer 5).

- **Presentation and organization issues**  
  – The paper is poorly organized and hard to follow; unnecessary technical points should be trimmed to clearly present ILS-CSL (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).  
  – Multiple rewrites for clarity are needed (Reviewer 4).  
  – Typo: “causl” in Section 5 (Reviewer 4).

- **Experimental shortcomings**  
  – **LLM details missing**: Which LLM was used? Why that model? How does training data affect results? Not reported, making experiments non-reproducible (Reviewer 3).  
  – Only tested on discrete datasets from bnlearn; not stated upfront that the method is limited to discrete data. Can it handle linear systems, mixed, or general distributions? (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 1 asks about continuous data).  
  – Baselines (MINOBSx, CaMML) are weak; performance on small networks (Cancer, Asia) is low, so the large improvement may simply reflect poor baselines. How does the method perform when stronger score-based algorithms are used? (Reviewer 1).  
  – No comparison to other ways of incorporating background knowledge (e.g., forbidden/required edges in PCALG or Tetrad) (Reviewer 4).  
  – The approach of updating score priors based on known edges is not novel; similar to Sachs et al. (2005) (Reviewer 4).  
  – Missing discussion of related prior work: Kıcıman et al. (2023) already proposed using LLMs for expert knowledge, and discuss the risk of memorization vs. inference; Jin et al. (2023) gave negative results for LLMs inferring causality from data alone. These should be cited and discussed (Reviewer 4).  
  – Computational and memory costs are not reported (Reviewer 3).  
  – The ratio E_ours/E_full in Section 4.2: does it change when baseline performance improves? (Reviewer 1).  
  – The estimation of E_full includes pairs with forking paths, which do not introduce “Extra Causality,” so E_full may be an upper bound (Reviewer 1).  

- **Minor technical questions**  
  – Eq. (4) uses set minus ambiguously; Eq. (5) writes “G in DAG” as a natural-language constraint, not formal optimization (Reviewer 2).  
  – Eq. (7): P(lambda) is a hyper-parameter; why is it reasonable to assume it fixed? (Reviewer 2).
