## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies data selection under weak supervision: given N unlabeled samples and a surrogate model that predicts labels better than random guessing, the goal is to select a subsample of size n<N, acquire true labels for it, and train via regularized ERM. Using asymptotic analysis (low- and high-dimensional) and experiments on synthetic and real data (Figure 1), the paper shows that (i) data selection can be very effective, sometimes beating training on the full sample, and (ii) popular schemes like unbiased subsampling and influence-function-based selection can be substantially suboptimal. The theoretical framework covers linear models and generalized linear models.

### Strengths

- The problem setup is straightforward yet leads to intriguing theoretical and experimental implications. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- Biased vs. unbiased subsampling comparison: Theorem 1 shows that the error coefficient of unbiased schemes can be arbitrarily larger than that of biased ones, demonstrating suboptimality of unbiased subsampling (Figure 1). (Reviewers 1, 2)
- Figure 1 and Theorem 2 provide a concrete setting where ERM on a selected subset outperforms ERM on the full dataset. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- The paper shows that better surrogate models do not always lead to better selection. (Reviewer 2)
- The authors explore a wide range of subsampling schemes (biased and unbiased) and provide asymptotic analysis in both low-dimensional and high-dimensional regimes. (Reviewer 4)
- Remark 4.1 uncovers connections between an almost universal unbiased subsampling scheme and influence-function-based methods. (Reviewer 4)
- The theoretical formulation is rigorous with solid mathematical guarantees; no notable mathematical errors were found. (Reviewer 4)
- The paper reveals properties of data selection unusual in established fields like coresets by formulating a different goal (e.g., Equation 4.3) compared to replicating the full-data ERM loss; it is expected to have broad impact. (Reviewer 1 after discussion; agreed by Reviewer 2)
- The paper is well-written and easy to read (Reviewer 4), though some found it technical (Reviewer 3). Overall presentation is good.

### Weaknesses

- The theoretical analysis beyond Section 4 assumes the surrogate model is equivalent to the true Bayes conditional distribution (perfect knowledge of label distributions). This undermines the theoretical justification of the “magic” improvement in Figure 1, where a weak surrogate is used. (Reviewer 4, high confidence)
- Theorem 1 shows \(\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}/\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}\) can be arbitrarily large for an adversarially chosen feature distribution, but the reverse ratio \(\rho_{\mathrm{nr}}/\rho_{\mathrm{unb}}\) is not studied, nor are generic distributions like Gaussian. Without such analysis, the practical significance of the theorem is limited. (Reviewer 4, high confidence)
- The analysis is entirely asymptotic (\(n,N\to\infty\) with \(n/N\to\gamma\)). Non-asymptotic guarantees and cases where \(n/N\to0\) are not explored, which could affect applicability to finite samples. (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 1 implied similar concerns regarding optimization)
- The high-dimensional analysis is restricted to a linear model with Gaussian features and assumes \(N/p\) converges to a known constant. These assumptions are very specific and limit generalizability. (Reviewer 4; Reviewer 1 also notes only linear/GLM examples are considered)
- The analysis only considers behavior at the optimum (e.g., equation B.3, assumption B.1.A1) and does not address optimization dynamics—convergence of gradient-based methods requires small subsampling error over the whole parameter space, not just at the optimal parameters. This limits applicability to non-convex models (e.g., two-layer networks) with multiple local minima. (Reviewer 1, medium confidence; Reviewer 2 had no such concern)
- Theoretical results (e.g., equation 4.2) depend on the unknown optimal parameters \(\theta^*\), and it is unclear how to use estimates or how robust the results are when assumptions are violated. (Reviewer 1)
- Several presentation issues hinder clarity:
  - Notation problems: \(n\) used before definition (p.1); “cst” not defined; \(\ell_{\text{test}}\) vs \(\ell\) confusion in (1.3); \(dy\) in Section 2 is unclear. (Reviewer 3)
  - The paper is densely packed with mathematical statements that would benefit from more context; a journal submission might allow fuller explanation. (Reviewer 4)
  - Sections 4-5 are particularly hard to follow. (Reviewer 2)
  - Some propositions (e.g., Proposition 4.3: where is \(b(x)\)?) are missing explicit components. (Reviewer 4)
  - No conclusions section. (Reviewer 4)
- There is disagreement among reviewers: one reviewer found no significant weaknesses (Reviewer 2, rating 8), while others raised substantial concerns as listed above. The paper has a wide range of scores (1,8,5,8). (Reviewer 1 initially rated 1 but raised after discussion; Reviewer 3 rated 5; Reviewer 4 rated 8 but with reservations.)
- Open questions that constitute missing analyses or comparisons:
  - How does the setting compare to semi-supervised learning? (Reviewer 2)
  - Is there a quantitative result connecting surrogate model weakness to improvement? (Reviewer 2)
  - For Figure 1, can the authors report the estimation error of the weak and strong surrogate models, and justify why subsampling reduces error below full-data ERM? Theorem 2 only shows existence of non-monotonicity, not that error becomes lower. (Reviewer 4)
  - How applicable is the method to non-convex models with multiple local minima, e.g., two-layer networks? (Reviewer 1)
  - Can gradient-based approximate procedures be used that require low overall parameter-space error? (Reviewer 1)
  - Why is \(\Omega:\mathbb{R}^p\to\mathbb{R}\) (parameter dimension equals input dimension)? Does this restrict generality? (Reviewer 4)
  - In Proposition 4.1, does Assumption A.1 (of Proposition B.1) hold automatically for the optimal subsampling strategy, or must it be imposed? (Reviewer 4)
