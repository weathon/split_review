## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents Cyclic Hierarchical Federated Learning (CHFL), a variant that arranges edge servers in a ring topology for sequential model passing, and provides the first theoretical convergence analysis for strongly convex, general convex, and non-convex objectives. The analysis covers three setups and shows rates of $\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/MNRKT)$, $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{MNRKT})$, and $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{MNRKT})$ respectively. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR, and Shakespeare are included. All five reviewers acknowledge the paper is generally well‑written and addresses an under‑studied problem. However, four of the five reviewers (scoring 3) are highly critical of the novelty, the assumptions, the experimental validation, and the system model, while one reviewer (score 6) finds the contributions significant and the experiments clear.

### Strengths
- First convergence analysis for Cyclic HFL (R3, R1, R4). The analysis covers strongly convex, general convex, and non‑convex objectives (R1, R5).
- The decoupling of the learning rate from data heterogeneity is a notable practical strength (R2).
- Presentation is well‑organized and easy to read (R1, R2, R3, R4). R1 notes the reader can easily identify the difference from existing works.
- Experiments are clear and validate the theoretical findings (R3, R5). R5 finds the simulation results promising.
- R3 (scoring 6) is particularly positive: “the results are meaningful” and “the experiments are clear and prove the theoretical results.”

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and technical contribution** (R1, R2, R4, R5; R3 asks for highlighting technical contributions): The problem setup closely resembles that of Cho et al. (2023) and Li & Lyu (2024); the differences (e.g., R rounds of local updates, multiple clients per edge) are minor and do not alter the core problem (R2). R1 states the sequential training makes the analysis simpler than standard FL/HFL, weakening the contribution. R4 says it is unclear why combining existing cyclic FL and HFL analyses is challenging. R5 questions the entire premise: the cyclic pattern was motivated by client battery limitations (Cho et al. 2023) but here is applied to resource‑abundant edge servers, making the extension questionable.

2. **Assumptions – looseness and errors** (R1, R2, R3, R5):
   - Assumptions 3.3 and 3.4 bound data heterogeneity loosely; many works (e.g., Scaffold, ProxSkip, [R3] HFL with multi‑timescale gradient correction) avoid such bounds (R1). R2 notes Assumption 3.3 contains an error in defining “Inter‑Edge Data Heterogeneity.” R2 and R5 both point out inconsistency: Assumption 3.4 is titled for non‑convex but the text below it says “for the convex case” (also R3 notes confusion at line 174/178). R2 suggests an affine variance assumption (Ajalloeian et al. 2020) would be more realistic than the bounded variance in Assumption 3.1.

3. **Experiments – insufficient validation** (R1, R2, R4; R3 and R5 are positive):
   - Missing time‑consumption comparison (x‑axis of wall time or latency), which is essential because the sequential protocol increases time linearly with the number of edge servers (R1).
   - Needs stronger baselines, e.g., the HFL algorithm with gradient correction from [R3] (R1). The advantage of clustering in CHFL over applying clustering to original HFL is not made clear (R4).
   - Datasets (MNIST, CIFAR, Shakespeare) are standard and small; larger datasets like miniImageNet or tinyImageNet should be used to confirm advantages (R4). R2 notes calling them “real‑world” is misleading.
   - The clustering policy discussion (Section 4.2) is underdeveloped and lacks experimental validation (R2). The paper claims to provide best clustering policies but does not support this empirically.
   - Assumption that all edges have the same number of clients limits realism; the effect of varying client counts per edge is not studied (R2). Even in Section 4.4 subsets are taken with consistent numbers.

4. **“Optimal” convergence rate claim – unjustified** (R2, R5): The paper repeatedly claims the convergence rate is “optimal” (lines 211, 365) without a formal definition or proof of best attainable rate. R5 notes that for strongly convex objectives one would expect linear (exponential) convergence, which is not demonstrated. R5 further points out a fundamental issue at line 270: a lower and upper bound on the step size is given, implying that if the step size is too small convergence cannot be guaranteed – which is counter‑intuitive and signals a flaw in the analysis.

5. **System model and terminology concerns** (R1, R2, R4): The algorithm uses a sequential cyclic pattern, breaking the typical FL parallel setup; true hierarchy implies multiple star‑shaped layers, which CHFL lacks – the term “hierarchical” may be misleading (R2). R1 notes time consumption scales linearly with the number of cells/edge servers. R4 asks for a clearer motivation: why use cyclic aggregation when non‑IID edge data will bias the model after each update?

6. **Clarity and notation issues** (R2, R5): Several errors and inconsistencies:
   - Table 1: “C” undefined; “i” used inconsistently in “M” (maybe m in M, i in I).
   - “N” defined in line 160 but not used in line 162; “all” vs. “\all” in lines 172/175.
   - Assumption 3.4 erroneously referenced as convex many times (lines 192, 214, 215, 268, 269).
   - Line 214 should refer to “CHFL” instead; “mu” in Theorem 4.1 undefined (should clarify if it represents strong convexity); line 261 “convex case” should be plural.
   - Typos: line 40 missing “increases”, line 45 missing “from”, line 52 unclear “On the other hand” (R5).
