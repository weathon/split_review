## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CoWEST, a collaborative weak-strong LLM framework: a fine-tuned weak model generates domain-specific drafts, a strong model refines them, and a preference-optimization feedback loop further adapts the weak model to the strong model’s preferences. Experiments on three datasets (Counterfactual, Medicine/MedMCQA, Ethics/IfQA) show F1 improvements (average +3.24% over weak alone, +12.17% over strong alone). All reviewers acknowledge the real-world relevance and potential of weak–strong collaboration, but disagree on novelty and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- The research topic—collaboration between a specialized weak model and a general strong model—is important and addresses real-world challenges.
- The paper is well-organized and easy to read.
- The interaction design is interesting (e.g., using the weak model for domain adaptation then the strong model for reasoning as a RAG-like method, increasing information for the strong model).
- The approach is practical and has potential for broad application.
- CoWEST shows remarkable improvements over SOTA RAG-based methods in the experiments.
- Experimental results demonstrate significant performance gains on reasoning tasks compared to weak or strong models alone.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The concept of using weak-model drafts then strong-model refinement and feedback correction has been extensively explored (e.g., “Your Weak LLM is Secretly a Strong Teacher for Alignment”, “Synthesizing Text-to-SQL Data from Weak and Strong LLMs”; Pan et al. TACL 2024). Standard refinement resembles prior works (Xu et al. ACL 2024 Findings), and preference enhancement via DPO is not new. Reviewers 1 and 2 found it “old wine in a new bottle”; reviewers 3 and 4 were more positive but still noted the need for clearer differentiation from static cooperation.
- **Missing baselines**: Comparisons are limited to basic weak/strong alone and RAG. Missing critical baselines for model collaboration/ensemble, including Self-Refine (Madaan et al., NeurIPS 2023), multi-model debate (Du et al., 2023), self-consistency, and LLM cascade methods like CAT (Cascade-Aware Training). Furthermore, no comparison with more advanced weak-strong collaboration methods or SOTA techniques is provided.
- **Dataset representativeness**: Only three domains are used; typical mathematical reasoning datasets (GSM8k, MATH) are omitted. For the medical domain, MedMCQA is limited to multiple-choice; longer-form QA (e.g., K-QA) should be included.
- **Unclear experimental settings**:  
  - The retrieval knowledge base used by FLARE in each domain is not specified.  
  - Preference-tuning sampling details are missing (sample distribution, average sample size).  
  - The learning rate 1.41e-5 is uncommon; was it determined by search?  
  - For Llama-3-8B and Llama-3/2-70B evaluations, are few-shot settings used? (IfQA has supervised and few-shot settings.)  
  - The evaluator appears to be a self-critique; score criteria, comparisons with human evaluation, and alternative evaluators (logits, trainable MLP) are not discussed.  
  - Why is GPT-3.5-Turbo used for the Ethics dataset while GPT-4 is used in other domains?  
  - Is the method explained when the weak and strong model are the same?  
  - Writing issues: inconsistent Llama-3 naming (LLama-3-8B, LLaMA3-8B, Llama3-8B), repeated definition of “LLM”, and \citep formatting errors.
- **Questioning the necessity and effectiveness**: In Counterfactual and Medicine, the weak model without SFT (Llama-3-8B, 68.57) outperforms the strong model (GPT-3.5-turbo, 22.62); in Ethics they are comparable. This undermines the core motivation of using a stronger model to assist a weaker one. The proposed collaboration yields only minor improvements over the weak model after SFT (Table 1) while incurring significant API costs for strong-model refinement and evaluation.
- **Lack of generalizability**: Preference pairs are tied to a specific strong model, limiting effectiveness when collaborating with different strong models.
- **Insufficient qualitative analysis**: No in-depth analysis of which aspects the strong model improves, error types detected, or how often the weak model adopts feedback. The example in Figure 6 is unclear; case studies showing improvements in factual accuracy, reasoning chain, or coherence are needed.
- **Scalability and overhead**: The impact of scaling to larger weak models or more complex tasks (e.g., multi-hop questions) is not discussed. The inference stage requires two passes (weak then strong), increasing computational cost, and the resource overhead of the feedback loop is unaddressed.
- **Need for longer-context experiments**: Comparative experiments on transferring domain knowledge to strong models in longer contexts are missing.
