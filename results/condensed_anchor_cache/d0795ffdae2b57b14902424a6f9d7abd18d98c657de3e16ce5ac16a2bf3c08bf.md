## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper evaluates the vulnerability of four open-source synthetic speech detectors (SSDs) to adversarial attacks under white-box, black-box, and agnostic (transferability) settings, using both objective metrics (VisQOL) and human ratings. The findings indicate that current SSDs are highly susceptible, with attacks often succeeding without degrading audio quality. Reviewers agree the problem is timely, but most find the contribution insufficient: three reviewers rated the paper 3 (reject) and one rated it 5 (accept), the latter citing the wide attack coverage and combined objective+subjective evaluation as valuable. However, even the more positive reviewer acknowledges limitations in scope, evaluation soundness, and reproducibility.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive threat model coverage**: The paper evaluates white-box, black-box, and agnostic attacks (PGD, iFGSM, SimBA, etc.), providing a holistic view of SSD vulnerabilities under different attacker access levels. *(Reviewer 2, 4)*
- **Combined objective and human evaluation**: Attack success is measured not only by detection evasion but also by audio quality (VisQOL scores) and human ratings, showing that perturbations can be imperceptible. *(Reviewer 2, 4)*
- **Timely and practically relevant problem**: The unreliability of deepfake detectors is of primary concern for fact-checkers and investigators, and the paper highlights the urgent need for more robust solutions given rapid TTS advances. *(Reviewer 2, 3)*
- **Clear presentation of insights**: The paper is easy to follow, with key takeaways and findings made accessible. *(Reviewer 1, 3)*

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty**: The vulnerability of deep learning-based audio models (including SSDs) to adversarial attacks is already well-established in the literature (e.g., attacks on ASR [1]); the paper merely demonstrates this without proposing new methods, countermeasures, or robustness improvements. *(Reviewer 1, 2, 4)*
- **No technical contribution**: Only classic attack methods are applied; no novel attack or defense approach is introduced. *(Reviewer 1)*
- **Insufficient evaluation scale and diversity**: All analyses are based on 100 samples from each of 3 TTS datasets (300 total). The sample set is not demographically or phonetically diverse, and newer datasets (e.g., DECRO [1] from WWW'23) or speech-to-speech generators (FREEVC, QuickVC, Suno Bark, TorToiSE, CoquiTTS) are not considered. Random sampling may propagate existing dataset biases. *(Reviewer 3)*
- **Limited number of SSDs evaluated**: Only four open-source SSDs are tested. More advanced or differently structured detectors should be included for broader conclusions. *(Reviewer 2)*
- **Soundness concerns in evaluation design**:
  - The claim in Section 3.2 that “deepfake speech from TTS not seen during training is more likely to bypass SSDs” is not properly justified: benign detection performance across datasets (e.g., ASVSpoof2019 vs. In-the-Wild) is not reported, making it impossible to attribute observed attack success to the attack rather than pre-existing poor generalization due to domain shift. This also confounds transferability experiments. *(Reviewer 2)*
  - The discussion of ASSIST-L in Section 3.3 (“being smaller, may form smoother decision boundaries… better generalization and robustness”) is hand-wavy, unsupported by evidence, and appears surprising without further analysis. *(Reviewer 3)*
- **Insufficient details on human evaluation**: The paper does not specify the number of participants, study procedure, IRB approval, null hypothesis, or whether a standard like ITU-T P.808 for Mean Opinion Scores was used. These omissions make the human study unverifiable. *(Reviewer 2, 3, 4)*
- **No evaluation of proprietary/commercial SSDs**: The study only tests open-source models. It is unclear how results would transfer to commercial systems (e.g., resemble.ai, elevenlabs.io), especially under black-box settings where SimBA may not work because confidence scores are not provided. *(Reviewer 2, 3)*
- **Lack of countermeasure discussion**: The paper identifies weaknesses but does not examine or propose potential improvements (e.g., data augmentation with noise to increase robustness) or test any defensive techniques. *(Reviewer 1, 2, 4)*
- **Poor paper organization**: Figures are often placed far from the text that describes them, hindering readability. *(Reviewer 1)*
- **Insufficient reproducibility**: The paper lacks detailed training procedures (e.g., whether data augmentation was used) and does not provide code for reproducibility. *(Reviewer 1, 2)*
- **Unclear or contested conclusions**: The final claim that “restricting public access to SSD models and internal workings” (security by obscurity) is needed requires defense—specifically, how would this be reconciled with the need for community audit? *(Reviewer 3)*
- **Missing multi-lingual evaluation**: The study is limited to a single language; evaluating multilingual deepfakes could yield novel insights. *(Reviewer 3, as a stretch goal)*

**Minority positive opinion (Reviewer 2, score 5)**: This reviewer found the evaluation more comprehensive and the combination of objective and subjective measures a strength. However, they still noted several of the above weaknesses, including scope limitations, evaluation soundness issues, and lack of commercial model testing.
