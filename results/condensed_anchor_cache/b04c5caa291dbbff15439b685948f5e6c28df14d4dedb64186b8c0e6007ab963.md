## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper hypothesizes that LLMs and simpler networks learn universal representations when trained on knowledge graph tasks. It introduces almost-affine transformation equivalence score (AAT-ES) for stitching models and uses CKA to compare representations across model sizes and contexts (MLPs, LLM in-context learning, LLM weights). The authors claim hints of universality, with representations optimizing generalization via symmetry and meta-transitivity, and propose a “starvation hypothesis” to explain emergence of compact representations. However, all four reviewers find major flaws in rigor, experimental detail, clarity of definitions, and novelty.

### Strengths
- Stitching with affine/almost-affine transformations is an interesting tool for understanding representation alignment across models (R1, R2).
- The investigation of universality across model scales and contexts is an important conceptual question, reminiscent of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (R2, R4).
- The paper demonstrates that LLM representations (via weights and in-context) show higher similarity to trained MLPs than to random baselines, a step toward quantifying universality (R2).
- The general research direction—proposing concrete experiments to test universality—is valuable (R2).
- The effort to study how knowledge graphs are represented across networks, and the model stitching experiments, are noteworthy (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of rigorous evaluation of stitching success:** The reported test loss (e.g., 2.5–3 vs. 2) is a large increase; it needs a reference scale (e.g., compared to Hoffman et al.’s training loss curves) and sanity checks (e.g., stitching random embeddings to an LLM). Stitched models’ preserved abilities (e.g., Winograd, ARC, MMLU) are not shown, so it is unclear how successful stitching really is (R1, R2).
- **Unsupported causal/mechanistic claims from CKA plots:** The paper draws conclusions like “representations are more similar between models of similar scale” and “two blocks correspond to forming semantics and next-token prediction” without experiments (e.g., activation patching) that directly justify these (R2). The mechanistic role of CKA patterns is not validated (R2, R3).
- **Inconsistent and ill-defined use of AAT-ES vs. CKA:** The paper introduces AAT-ES as a preferred measure but then mainly uses CKA in Figures 5–7 without explaining when and why each metric is applied. CKA is also mischaracterized as a “nearest-neighbor” measure (R1, R2, R3).
- **Missing quantification and sensitivity analyses:** Nearly all experiments lack error bars, sensitivity checks, or clear reporting of hyperparameters. Appendix A omits model input/output, tokenization, initialization, optimizer, batching, regularization, and convergence sensitivity—critical because wider models train faster (R2, R3).
- **Starvation hypothesis is not novel:** The “starvation” / “goldilocks zone” idea simply recasts well-known overfitting/underfitting and regularization concepts (e.g., bias-variance decomposition, weight decay, dropout) without new insight. The connection to large-scale LLM training (scaling laws, double descent) is not made (R1, R2, R4).
- **No LLM explicitly trained on a knowledge graph task:** The main training experiment uses only small MLPs; LLM evidence comes only from in-context learning or pretrained weights, not from a controlled KG learning study (R1).
- **Definitions and theoretical framework not used:** The formal definition of equivalence score (Def. 1) and the general formulation in Section 3 are unclear, incomplete (e.g., missing domain/codomain, formal conditions for “superfluous nonlinearities,” selection of ε), and never actually employed in the experiments (R1, R3).
- **Figure 8 and related data pruning unclear:** It is unclear why the graph is pruned to only successful judgments, why this part is extracted only for Figure 8 (not Figures 6,7), and what exactly is being measured; the 65–70% accuracy on a yes/no task is not contextualized (R1, R3, R4). The comparison of LLMs to MLPs in this figure is confusing (R4).
- **In-context learning results are not discussed:** Prompts for in-context representations are described but the results are never analyzed, even though the “starvation hypothesis” makes claims about generalization that relate to these settings (R3).
- **Anthropomorphic and imprecise language:** Terms like “dumb,” “smart,” “starvation,” “fierce competition” are used instead of precise technical descriptions, and new terms are coined for already known phenomena (R2, R4).
- **Visualization and caption issues:** Figure 2 caption is hard to follow; Figure 4 lacks specification of visualization method (e.g., PCA); Figure 8 has unclear counting; axes labels, references, and figure captions contain errors (R1, R2, R3).
