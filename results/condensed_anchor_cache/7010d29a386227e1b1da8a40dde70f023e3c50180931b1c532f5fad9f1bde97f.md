## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Soft Preference Optimization (SPO), an offline alignment method that optimizes a preference loss (modeling preference probability as π_θ(y1)/(π_θ(y1)+π_θ(y2)) with a temperature parameter α) plus a KL divergence regularization term applied across the model’s full output distribution (global regularization), rather than only over the preference dataset. SPO handles pairwise, best-of-n, and ranked preference data. Theoretically, under the Bradley-Terry (BT) assumption, the optimal solution is shown to converge to a softmax of scaled rewards; a weighted version (with μ) down-weights poor-quality samples. A biased low-variance KL estimator using token-level subsequences is also introduced. Experiments on AlpacaFarm with Llama-2-7B compare win-rates against SFT and baselines (DPO, SimPO, CPO, R-DPO, IPO) across the three data types. The ablation study validates the choice of regularization and the weighting function.

### Strengths
* The study addresses the important question of which regularization data to use for alignment methods.
* The proposed objective is simple, elegant, and natural; it is surprising it had not been proposed before (acknowledging the related CPO work).
* The framework is well-structured, with a logical development from basic to general algorithm, and handles multiple preference data types (pairwise, best-of-n, ranked), demonstrating versatility.
* Global regularization across the full output distribution (rather than in-dataset) is a novel and well-justified approach, showing better empirical results.
* Theoretical analysis is extensive, including rigorous proofs of convergence to a softmax of scaled rewards under BT/Plackett-Luce assumptions. Detailed derivations are provided in the appendix.
* Experimental results on AlpacaFarm with Llama-2-7B indicate SPO outperforms several baselines (DPO, SimPO, CPO, R-DPO, IPO) in win-rate and in focusing on the best data.
* The approach of integrating preference loss with explicit regularization makes intuitive sense, and the presentation is clear.
* Reviewer 3 (score 8, no weaknesses) found the empirical evaluation sufficiently convincing of competitiveness; that reviewer also noted the theoretical contribution and clarity.

### Weaknesses
* **Limited experimental scope**: Only Llama-2-7B and AlpacaFarm are used. Broader evaluation on models from different families (e.g., Mistral-7B, Llama-3-8B) and larger datasets (e.g., UltraFeedback) is needed to verify architecture-agnostic performance and generalizability across instruction-tuning distributions. (Reviewers 2, 4)
* **Concerning baseline performance**: Several baseline methods show negative length-controlled win-rates against SFT (R-DPO, CPO, SimPO, IPO), suggesting potential issues with the experimental setup or hyperparameter tuning. A fairer comparison would use hyperparameter settings that replicate successful implementations (e.g., from the SimPO paper). (Reviewer 2)
* **Missing comparison with RLHF/PPO**: Since SPO requires on-policy data sampling for KL regularization, a direct empirical comparison with PPO (using an on-policy preference loss term) is needed to demonstrate the claimed advantage of reward-model-free methods. Optimizing off-policy preference data should be compared with on-policy sampled data. (Reviewer 5)
* **Theoretical derivation omits regularization term**: Theorems 1 and later do not account for the KL regularization term in the solution, whereas this term is present in the analytic solutions of PPO and DPO; this omission compromises the theoretical results given that SPO optimizes a combination of two loss terms. (Reviewer 5)
* **Preference loss lacks DPO calibration term**: The preference loss resembles DPO but removes the calibration terms using π₀ (the reference model). It is not convincingly shown that removing calibration provides any advantage; an ablation comparing the two objectives is needed. (Reviewer 1)
* **Unclear benefit of out-of-domain regularization**: Whether and when out-of-domain regularization helps is not justified; further analysis or ablation is required. (Reviewer 1)
* **Win-rate evaluation insufficient without KL consideration**: A method could achieve high win-rate by having a large KL divergence from SFT. The paper should plot KL vs. win-rate to support the results. (Reviewer 1)
* **Claim about avoiding determinism**: The assertion that SPO avoids determinism while DPO/RLHF tend to produce deterministic models ignores the dependence on the β parameter (as shown in Theorem 1 of https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00761). (Reviewer 3, question)
* **Weighting function μ lacks intuition**: Its design (subtracting the batch average) is complicated and not well motivated; the effect of the γ parameter should be clarified through ablation. (Reviewer 5)
* **Missing data in paper**: On the top of page 9, win-rate improvements are reported as "from xx% to xx%" without specifying numbers. (Reviewer 5)
* **Missing references**: Related alignment methods [1,2,3] that match the model’s output distribution are not cited. (Reviewer 5)
* **No hyperparameter ablation study**: The paper lacks an ablation of key hyperparameters (e.g., α, β, γ) to assess their sensitivity and robustness. (Reviewer 4)
* **Trade-off between alignment and other capabilities not examined**: No experiments show whether SPO’s emphasis on diversity and alignment comes at the cost of overall model performance on broader tasks, a point that could be addressed by comparing SPO with DPO and other baselines on additional benchmarks. (Reviewer 4)
* **Questions about preference representation expressivity**: Is the formulation π_θ(y|x)^α effectively equivalent to the BT/PL parametrization exp((1/β) r(y,x)), so that α and β play similar roles? If so, the claimed additional expressivity needs clarification. (Reviewer 2)
* **Selection of α for different data types**: Is there a principled way to choose α for pairwise, best-of-n, or ranked data? (Reviewer 2)
* **Clarification on RLHF out-of-domain prompts**: The paper claims “RLHF uses out-of-domain prompts”; this statement needs verification or correction. (Reviewer 1)

*Note: Reviewer 3 (score 8) identified no weaknesses and was considerably more positive than the other reviewers (scores 5,5,5,6), who raised the above concerns. The majority assessment suggests the paper has significant issues in experimental depth, theoretical completeness, and missing analyses.*
