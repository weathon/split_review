## Merged Review

### Summary
GAMEBOT is a benchmark for evaluating LLM reasoning in competitive gaming environments. It decomposes complex reasoning in eight games into modular subproblems for fine-grained evaluation, uses Chain-of-Thought prompts with domain knowledge, and validates intermediate steps against ground truth. 17 LLMs are benchmarked. The paper claims contributions: mitigation of data contamination, legibility through intermediate reasoning evaluation, difficulty to differentiate top models, and stronger baselines. Reviewer opinions diverge: two reviewers gave 5 (favorable, but with criticisms about simplicity and missing comparisons), one gave 3 (critical of overclaimed contributions and missing experimental details), and one gave 1 (very negative, citing simple games, lack of deep analysis, and missing usability).

### Strengths
1. **Innovative benchmark for reasoning evaluation**: GAMEBOT provides a new perspective for evaluating LLMs in strategic settings, focusing on reasoning and intermediate thinking steps rather than just final outcomes (R1, R2, R4).
2. **Diverse game selection**: Eight games (board, action, card games) capture different reasoning skills — spatial reasoning, mathematical reasoning, information extraction, pattern recognition, collaboration, risk management (R1, R2).
3. **Clear evaluation metrics / detailed prompt templates**: The use of intermediate thought evaluation alongside final outcome assessment enhances interpretability. Detailed prompt templates are provided to facilitate replication (R1, R2, R4).
4. **Comprehensive experimental setup**: 17 LLMs across 8 games, providing a broad evaluation. The subproblem decomposition allows assessing not only accuracy but also quality of reasoning (R1, R2, R3, R4).
5. **Automated validation of reasoning steps**: CoT prompts with ground-truth validation for intermediate steps is a notable design (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Simple games and limited scope**: The games are relatively simple; most environments (e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe, Connect Four) have small state/action spaces. This may not satisfy the paper’s stated goal of evaluating complex real-world reasoning. Overlap with prior work “LLMARENA” is noted; the main difference is unclear (R1, R2, R3, R4). Reviewer 2 specifically notes that some games like Tic-Tac-Toe and Connect Four have very simple pattern recognition tasks (e.g., just checking win conditions) rather than deeper reasoning (e.g., “minimum number of steps to win” or “compare two moves”).
2. **Hand-crafted subproblems with limited generalization**: The sub-tasks appear manually designed and are not exhaustive. This limits generalization to larger, more complex games. No systematic design principles are described (R1, R2). Reviewer 3 adds that there is no interface for users to add their own LLMs or games.
3. **Overclaimed contributions: Mitigation of Data Contamination**:
   - The claim is based solely on dynamic game states, but games have limited state/action spaces, so LLMs could still memorize game logs. The paper provides no experimental evidence (e.g., fine-tuning LLMs on game logs and comparing performance) to substantiate this claim (R4).
4. **Overclaimed contributions: Intermediate Thought Evaluation**:
   - The evaluation is **offline** — generate intermediate or endgame data and ask questions — not during an ongoing match. The paper does not analyze how intermediate thought processes occur in real 1v1 competitions. Missing details: how the LLM-generated response is compared to ground truth, what are the profiles for each reasoning category (spatial, math, etc.) with examples (R1, R2, R4). Reviewer 2 notes the evaluation metric on intermediate thought is not clear.
5. **Missing experimental details & poor result analysis**:
   - The scores in tables (Tab 2, 3) do not support deep analysis; there is no discussion of how LLMs behave differently across game scenarios (e.g., perfect vs. imperfect information, spatial vs. mathematical reasoning) (R3, R4).
   - Table 3 (“Outcome Evaluation”): how values are calculated is unclear (e.g., matches between each pair of LLMs? Number of matches per LLM/game?) (R4).
   - No statistics on search/state/action space per game, average number of turns/actions/tokens. Without these, the claim of “difficulty to differentiate top models” is unverified (R4).
6. **Missing comparisons and baselines**:
   - Only base LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4) are compared; no popular LLM agent algorithms (ReAct, Reflection, fine-tuning-based methods) are included. The well-structured tasks appear learnable via RL (R1).
   - “Stronger Baselines” claim lacks details: only two LLMs over 20 matches are used for validation; assumptions and development process are not described (R4).
   - The claims that existing benchmarks “suffer from data contamination or lack legibility” and that methods are “costly or biased” are not backed by specific surveys or comparisons (R4).
7. **Evaluation accuracy of LLM-based sub-task evaluation**: The sub-task evaluation results come from the LLM itself; how is the accuracy of this evaluation ensured? (R1)
8. **Prompt dependency and adaptability**: The paper uses task-specific CoT prompts. How would models perform with generic prompts? No discussion on prompt adaptability across games (R1).
9. **Limited usability and reproducibility**: No interface description for adding own LLMs/games; not clear if the benchmark is open-source or released online (R3). No clues to help readers interpret results from Tables 2 and 3 (R3).
10. **No multi-agent competition or optimal solvers**: The paper does not specify if multi-agent (3+ players) competition is supported, nor does it consider optimal solvers (e.g., RL/MCTS, regret-based for poker games) for comparison (R4).

**Reviewer disagreement**: Three reviewers gave scores 5,5,3, while Reviewer 3 gave a 1 — strongly negative. Reviewer 3 finds the games too simple, the analysis shallow, and the benchmark not practically usable. This divergence is not reflected in a uniform positive assessment.
