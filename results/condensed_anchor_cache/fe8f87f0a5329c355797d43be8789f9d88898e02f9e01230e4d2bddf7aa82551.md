## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Hypotheses-to-Theories (HtT), a framework with induction and deduction stages that builds a rule library from training examples to improve LLM reasoning. Tested on relational reasoning and numerical reasoning (including arithmetic in different bases and kinship tasks), HtT claims absolute gains of 10–30% over CoT and LtM. Reviewers find the idea novel but strongly disagree on execution: one reviewer (Rating 8) praises the extensive experiments and clear presentation; three reviewers (Ratings 3, 5, 3) consider the setup too simplistic, the technical details insufficiently specified, and the concept of “rules” misleadingly defined.

### Strengths
- The idea of using LLMs as rule learners is novel, distinguishing the work from prior hallucination-mitigation or symbolic approaches (R1).
- The prompting method is easy and intuitive (R2). Several innovative tricks (e.g., XML tagging) address implementation details and enhance practicality (R1).
- Effectiveness is validated with extensive experiments on relational reasoning and numerical reasoning datasets, showing absolute gains of 10–30% over CoT and LtM (R1, R3, R4).
- Demonstrates that LLMs (especially GPT-4) can learn simple rules such as true/false propositions (R2, R3).
- The presentation is articulate and easy to read (R3). The study contributes to the topical and interesting area of LLM reasoning capabilities (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing technical details about the induction stage.** How are rules extracted from the LLM’s output – e.g., via regular expressions? How are occurrence *k* and accuracy *p* calculated when rules are in natural language? How is ground truth used to verify rules – does it require human annotators? Is a confidence score associated with rules, and how is it evaluated? (R1, R3)
- **The concept of “rule” is ambiguously defined.** Examples in Figure 1 (e.g., “3 + 4 = 7”) are concrete facts rather than general rules. Rule application in a deductive sense (using a general statement to derive new facts) is unclear for such statements. The claim of inducing “rules” may be misleading (R4).
- **Experimental setup is too simplistic.** The learned rules are confined to true/false propositions (“A is B”) and the tasks are simple synthetic datasets (arithmetic in different bases, kinship). The method has not been tested on realistic or nuanced settings (R2, R4).
- **The gains may be partly due to XML tagging rather than extracted knowledge.** The paper does not test CoT and LtM with XML tagging, so the improvement over baselines without tagging may overstate the contribution of rule learning (R2).
- **The prompts in the appendix already contain many ground rules explicitly in text.** This raises the question: how is HtT different from knowledge retrieval? The tasks in related work (e.g., [1]) appear more nuanced (R2).
- **The claim that hallucination resembles hypothesis generation in scientific discovery is unsupported.** Scientific hypothesis generation relies on logical induction/abduction, while the logic behind hallucination is unknown (R3).
- **The method’s ability to learn complex rules is not addressed.** Can it learn first-order logic rules that require unification during reasoning? (R1, R2) Can it handle all varieties of hallucination? (R1)
- **Core technical contribution is described in only 1.5 pages** (pp. 3–4), with undue emphasis on “tricks” like XML tagging rather than fundamental principles that others can adopt (R4). The paper is thus closer to a technical report than a full publication (R3).
- **Learned rules are in natural language, which weakens generalization and causes ambiguity.** It would be valuable to test whether LLMs can abstract numerical rules into higher-order forms (e.g., Peano axioms) and use those for reasoning (R3).
- **Citation inconsistencies** (e.g., inconsistent use of page numbers, missing publication venues, varying abbreviation style) lower presentation quality (R1).
