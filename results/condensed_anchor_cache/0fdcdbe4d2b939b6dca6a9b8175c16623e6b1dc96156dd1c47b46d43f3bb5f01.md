## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LLMatic, a Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithm that combines the code-generation capabilities of large language models (LLMs) with Quality‑Diversity (QD) optimization. Architecture variations are introduced directly at the code level, and a dual‑archive mechanism is used to reduce the number of candidate evaluations. LLMatic is tested on CIFAR‑10 and NAS‑bench‑201, and the authors report competitive performance with only 2,000 evaluated candidates. The work is seen as novel in applying LLMs for NAS and in formulating architecture generation as a language/code modelling task. However, several reviewers raise concerns about experimental scope, novelty in light of similar prior work, presentation quality, and insufficient analysis of key algorithmic components.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of LLM‑based NAS** – Using code‑generating LLMs to produce neural network architectures is a novel direction with potential impact on the NAS field (R1, R2, R3, R5). Defining architecture generation as a language/code modelling task is a new formulation of the search problem (R2).
- **Code‑level search** – Performing NAS directly on the architecture code rather than on an abstract representation (e.g., a DAG) makes the approach flexible, removes the need for a compiler, saves time, and can naturally combine NAS with hyperparameter optimization (R4).
- **Quality‑Diversity framework** – Using QD for the evolutionary search encourages diverse solutions that can serve different hardware requirements or be used for ensembling (R4). The ablation study illustrates the contribution of each component (R4; also noted by R1 as partly missing).
- **Reproducibility** – The authors release code and additional details on the prompts used (R2).
- **Overall algorithmic design** – The algorithm is sensible and should be able to produce high‑performing DNNs (R5). *Note: one reviewer (R4) was considerably more positive (score 5 vs. 3 for others) and praised the flexibility of the code‑level approach and the ablation study.*

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope** – Evaluation is restricted to CIFAR‑10 and NAS‑bench‑201, which are cell‑based spaces. Current NAS research often focuses on more practical spaces (e.g., transformer‑based, MobileNet‑style) and larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet). The approach should be evaluated on such spaces to test scaling and generality (R2, R1, R3, R5). A specific request is to test on NAS‑Bench‑101, a search space with different constraints (R4). The lower rank on ImageNet increases concerns about effectiveness on general tasks (R5).
- **Insufficient novelty compared to prior work** – Previous methods (e.g., GENIUS, EvoPrompting) have already proposed similar ideas for using LLMs to generate network architectures and demonstrated prompts. The paper does not adequately differentiate its contribution or provide a clear picture of what is new beyond the combination with QD (R3, R2).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines** – Table 1 does not include a comparison to other black‑box search methods (beyond random search) nor to generative NAS approaches such as DiffusionNAG. A comparison with EvoPrompting is also recommended (R2). Search time (cost) is not reported for the proposed method or the baselines (R3, R2, R5). Optimization trajectories (any‑time performance) against random search are requested (R4).
- **Reliance on LLM prior knowledge** – Performance depends heavily on the training data of the LLM; the model may not generalize to novel or highly specialized search spaces. The paper does not provide evidence that the LLM possesses useful prior knowledge about NAS (R1, R1 question). The input sequence length of current LLMs is limited, yet the search evaluates up to 2,000 networks—how is this handled, and would incorporating the search history into the prompt be beneficial? (R1 question). *One reviewer (R4) found the approach promising despite this limitation, but the issue remains a concern.*
- **Presentation and writing quality** – The paper contains multiple typos and unclear phrasing (e.g., “ta simple neural network” on page 4, “input(s?) with 3 channels” – R2, R4, R5). The pseudocode (Algorithm 1) lacks proper mathematical annotations and is hard to read; key functions (mutation, crossover, temperature mutation) are not sufficiently described (R5). Figures 2 and 3 have excessive white space; Figure 4 causes rendering issues and the green points (LLMatic architectures) are not visible (R2, R4). Overall the presentation is poor (R1: “1 poor”, R4: “1 poor”, R5: “1 poor”).
- **Ablation and analysis depth** – Although the ablation study is appreciated (R4), several reviewers feel that the contributions of individual components are not fully analysed (R1) and that the experimental description lacks detail (R3). The design choice of randomly picking a prompt at each step (rather than letting the LLM choose) is not justified (R1 question).
- **Practical issues with LLM code generation** – Code‑generating LLMs are often imperfect; the paper does not discuss how many generated architectures are compilable, how bugs are handled, or how the prompt ensures the LLM stays within the NAS‑Bench‑201 search space (R2 question). Reproducibility of results using open‑source LLMs is also unclear (R2 question).
- **Missing reference to published versions** – The authors should use the published version of papers where available (e.g., NAS‑Bench‑Suite was published at ICLR 2022) (R4 question).
