## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper empirically investigates the impact of label noise on Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), which require both class labels and concept labels. Experiments on CUB and AwA2 with varied noise levels (symmetric and pairwise) across three training regimes (independent, sequential, joint) show that label noise degrades both concept and target prediction accuracy. Joint training best preserves target accuracy but severely hurts concept accuracy. Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) is proposed as a mitigation and shows some improvement.

### Strengths

- The problem of label noise in CBMs is novel and well-motivated; prior work has not explicitly examined this effect (all reviewers). Reviewer 2 emphasizes that no work has explicitly examined this problem.
- Extensive empirical evaluation across multiple training schemes, noise levels, datasets (CUB, AwA2), architectures (Inception-V3), and CBM variants (all reviewers). Reviewer 1 notes the extensive set of results; Reviewer 4 says the article is extensive w.r.t. evaluation.
- Interesting finding that joint training maintains target classification accuracy best but incurs a large drop in concept prediction accuracy (Reviewer 1).
- Paper structure is intuitive and easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Detailed insights into the underlying causes of decreased prediction accuracy for both intermediate concepts and final targets (Reviewer 3).
- Proposes a first mitigation approach (SAM) for label noise in CBMs (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- The findings are purely empirical and lack theoretical insights; no explanation is provided for why SAM works better than SGD (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Reviewer 2 specifically would have appreciated theoretical intuition.
- Noise models may not be realistic: symmetric noise may not capture real-world correlations; the pairwise noise model’s dependencies are unclear (Reviewer 1). Noise levels up to 40% are unconventional for real-world scenarios; natural label noise is already present in CUB and AWA2 (Reviewer 3).
- The improvement from SAM over joint CBM is incremental, even at high noise levels; the mitigation is only mildly successful (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The linear classifier used may mask the true impact of label noise; results might be an artifact of its insensitivity to noise (Reviewer 1).
- Concept prediction accuracy for joint CBM at 0% noise is reported as 92.4% (Table 1) versus 96.9% reported in the vanilla CBM literature with the same Inception-V3 backbone; this discrepancy is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- Only the vanilla CBM baseline is used; more baselines should be considered to validate the effectiveness of SAM (Reviewer 3).
- Interpretability is equated with concept prediction accuracy; these are not strictly synonymous (Reviewer 3).
- Label smoothing in the appendix is not referenced in the main text (Reviewer 1).
- Some results are pushed to the appendix; a brief summary in the main text would have been helpful (Reviewer 2).
- Ignores previous work on label noise (e.g., overviews by Ata Kaban and Benoit Frenay) (Reviewer 4).
- Questions that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments:
  - Does SAM improve robustness for other CBM variants? (Reviewer 2)
  - What is the impact of interventions on reducing label noise? Does SAM improve it? (Reviewer 2)
  - Can the authors comment on label noise when using LLMs for concept annotation? (Reviewer 2)
  - What is the effect of asymmetries of label noise? (Reviewer 4)
  - Can different cost functions and other methods for handling noise be investigated? (Reviewer 4)
  - How does this relate to technologies that do not assume concept availability for all data? (Reviewer 4)
  - How does the effect relate to the suitability of concepts? (Reviewer 4)
- Minor issues: rephrase "need to be trained with noisy labels" (Reviewer 1); duplicate references in bibliography (Reviewer 1); line 101 should have "We" capitalized (Reviewer 2); Figure 3 does not specify which model (Joint/Ind/Seq) (Reviewer 2); Figure 7 shows a sharp drop in target validation accuracy for SGD training (Reviewer 3); in Figure 7b the performance of SGD and SAM are almost the same, requiring explanation (Reviewer 3).

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 (score 6) is notably more positive than the other three (scores 3, 3, 3), praising the comprehensiveness and structure, but still raises concerns about theoretical depth, dataset limitations, and missing experiments.
