- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 3, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Parrot, a spoken dialogue language model with a two-stage pipeline: pre-training on single-channel audio (next-token prediction on discrete tokens) and supervised fine-tuning on double-channel dialogue data using a “next-token-pair prediction” objective. The goal is to enable more natural and fluid spoken interactions than cascaded or earlier textless models (e.g., dGSLM). Reviews are predominantly critical, with one reviewer (rating 5) more positive about the research direction and presentation, while another reviewer (rating 1) strongly criticises writing, motivation, and evaluation validity. Overall, the work is seen as having limited novelty, insufficiently rigorous evaluation, and unclear or contradictory reporting.

### Strengths
- The paper explores an important yet challenging direction: dual-channel speech input modeling for real-time spoken dialogue, moving beyond simple turn-based interactions (reviewer 5, rating 1, still acknowledges interest).
- The approach simplifies the two-tower architecture of dGSLM by using a single Transformer that predicts two tokens simultaneously (reviewer 1).
- The authors state they will open-source their training and inference framework, which could be a valuable community contribution given that comparable systems (e.g., Moshi, Mini-OMNI) lack open documentation (reviewer 3).
- Evaluation includes turn-taking properties such as reflective pause and interrupt accuracy, addressing aspects often overlooked in prior work (reviewer 3; reviewer 1 notes limited metrics).
- The paper provides adequate diagrams, tables, and mentions limitations (reviewer 2 acknowledges comprehensiveness; but other reviewers note missing details).
- Related work is reasonably reviewed and cited (reviewer 5, despite other criticism).

### Weaknesses
1. **Minimal novelty and weak motivation**: The framework is essentially a decoder-only adaptation of dGSLM combined with a textually pre-trained model (TWIST), offering limited technical innovation (reviewers 2, 4). The “next-token-pair prediction” is described as a contribution but overlaps with the pipeline itself (reviewer 2). The model structure is not well-motivated: there is a significant gap between training (next-speech-token conditioned on previous predicted tokens) and inference (listening tokens sent in chunks; predicting speaking tokens no longer depends on previous predicted speaking tokens), which disrupts causal modeling—a point unaddressed (reviewer 5). The use of token-pair input doubles context length; LSLM uses more efficient embedding fusion, and the authors do not justify their design choice (reviewer 5).

2. **Misrepresentation of related work and insufficient comparisons**:
   - The paper states open-source models use a cascading approach, but Rubenstein et al. (2023) and Nachmani et al. (2024) are not cascaded nor open-sourced (reviewer 1).
   - Claims that prior work uses encoder-decoder architectures for pre-training are incorrect for several cited papers (e.g., Borsos et al., Lakhotia et al., Kharitonov et al.) which are decoder-only LMs (reviewer 1).
   - Unsubstantiated claims are made, e.g., that RQ-transformers reduce model efficiency, without experimental support (reviewer 3).
   - Comparison with cascaded systems is poor: the baseline is a relatively weak system (dGSLM’s cascade); authors should compare with a state-of-the-art cascade (e.g., Hugging Face Speech-to-Speech with parallel threading) (reviewer 3). The paper also does not compare to standard textless benchmarks such as ZeroSpeech 2021 or StoryCloze (reviewer 4). AudioQA evaluation (vs. AudioQWEN-2) is mentioned only in one sentence without metrics description or discussion of why a model trained only on English and S2S tasks performs on non-English languages and diverse datasets (reviewer 1). Figure 8c reports VocalSound accuracy below 80%, while AudioQWEN-2 reports 90%+—no explanation (reviewer 1).

3. **Insufficient and poorly described evaluation**:
   - **No human evaluation**: The paper claims more natural and fluid interaction but provides no human study, which is essential for evaluating conversational quality (reviewers 3, 4, 5). Reviewer 3 notes that text-based dialogue systems commonly include human assessment; the same should be done here.
   - **Turn-taking metrics (Section 4.5.1)**: The metrics (reflective pause, interruption accuracy) are not clearly defined, and the ground truth is generated by GPT-4 without human validation (reviewers 1, 3, 4). The evaluation is on synthetic data rather than a held-out subset of Fisher (reviewer 1). For LLAMA-Omini and SpeechGPT, which lack interrupt/pause ability, how are these metrics calculated? (reviewer 5). Interaction accuracy metric is undefined (reviewer 4).
   - **Response quality**: For open-ended instruction-following tasks, no evaluation of linguistic quality or coherence is reported (reviewer 5). The metrics in Section 4.5.2 do not reflect content quality (reviewer 5).
   - **Speech-to-text evaluation (Section 4.6)**: The model is textless and does not generate text, yet ASR and ST tasks are evaluated—how? (reviewer 5). The paper also reports results on Chinese (aishell), emotion (MELD), and sound (AIRBench-Sound) despite being trained only on English data, which is highly suspicious (reviewer 5). No explanation is given for why larger latency leads to worse performance (Appendix A.4.2) (reviewer 5).
   - **Missing details on the cascaded system used as baseline**: Not described (reviewer 3). AudioQA evaluation lacks metric details and dataset description (reviewer 1).

4. **Poor writing, clarity, and organisation**:
   - Some tables/figures are not referenced in the main text; statements are inconsistent (reviewer 5). Section 4.5.1 is difficult to follow (reviewer 3). The term “layer-wise” and “consistent” channel embeddings appear only once without explanation (reviewer 4). The GPT score (Appendix A.6.3) is not mentioned in the main text, nor is its purpose clarified (reviewers 4, 5). The left side of Figure 7 (two feature spaces) is not explained (reviewer 5).
   - The related work section (Section A.1) contains unsubstantiated claims and does not concisely compare with the most relevant prior methods (reviewers 3, 4). The main related work section lacks sufficient comparison with dGSLM and Moshi (reviewer 1 also asks to mention Moshi https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00037).
   - Code is public but insufficiently documented; `inference.py` appears missing, and the demo could not be run (reviewer 3).

5. **Missing ablations, analyses, and design choices**:
   - Use of a single codebook vs. multiple codebooks is not motivated; Table 4’s synthesized audio quality results lack dataset specification (reviewer 3). Why is the token rate variable (“each second into 30-50 tokens”)? (reviewer 1).
   - No evaluation of catastrophic forgetting: does Parrot retain LLAMA 3.1’s instruction-following and factual knowledge? (reviewer 3).
   - Only human-human conversation data is used for SFT; human-AI interaction data (even synthetic) could be beneficial (reviewer 3). No discussion of streaming length limits or sequence reduction techniques (reviewer 3).
   - The paper should evaluate “speaking-while-listening” capabilities as in LSLM (reviewer 3). The difference in feature spaces (Figure 7 left) is unexplained (reviewer 5).

6. **Ambiguities and missing details**:
   - Training data amounts inconsistent: Section 4.1 lists 14,000 hrs single-channel and 2,200 hrs double-channel, but Table 1 shows >70,000 hrs single-channel and 2,000 hrs double-channel. Also, InstructS2S-200K is single-channel—how is it used in Stage 2? (reviewer 5).
   - How many data were used to train the speech tokenizer? What is its output rate? (reviewer 5).
   - The model’s handling of text input (AIR-bench questions are text) is unclear; the model is audio-based (reviewer 4).
   - The observation in Section 4.5.3 about channel embeddings in different spaces lacks intuitive explanation (reviewer 3; also reviewer 5’s Figure 7 comment).
   - The main text does not explain the significance of the GPT score or its role in evaluation (reviewer 4).

*Note: One reviewer (rating 5) was considerably more positive, praising the paper’s comprehensiveness and the research direction, but acknowledged novelty and clarity weaknesses. Another reviewer (rating 1) was highly critical, especially of writing, model-inference mismatch, and seemingly implausible results (e.g., Chinese ASR from English-only training).*
