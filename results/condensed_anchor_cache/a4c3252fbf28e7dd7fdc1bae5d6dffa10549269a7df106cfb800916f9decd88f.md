## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates using vision pre-trained models (PTMs) for building generalist robot manipulation policies. The authors find that local (spatial) features outperform global features for multi-task policy learning, and that DINOv2 (trained on conventional vision datasets) outperforms robot-specific models like R3M and VC-1. They also study augmentation methods and propose a self-distillation objective for few-shot adaptation. All experiments are conducted in the MetaWorld simulation environment.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses an important direction: understanding how pre-trained visual representations (especially from vision foundation models) perform in robot policy learning, which is valuable to the robotics community.
- The evaluation includes diverse metrics: success rates for in-domain tasks and out-of-domain generalization (unseen colors, unseen environment), allowing nuanced analysis.
- The study compares multiple off-the-shelf PTMs (CLIP, DINOv2, R3M, VC-1) and finds DINOv2 superior, providing guidance for practitioners.
- The writing and organization are good; simulation experiments on MetaWorld are solid.
- The self-distillation objective for few-shot adaptation is interesting and not commonly used in policy learning, despite being common in other fields.
- The paper discusses inductive biases from PTMs’ training objectives, offering insights into how different representations affect generalization.
- Open-source code and datasets are mentioned, enabling replication.

### Weaknesses

- **Sim-only evaluation with very limited scale**: All experiments are conducted solely in the MetaWorld simulation environment, with no real-world validation. The large sim-to-real gap severely limits relevance. Multiple reviewers (R2, R3, R4, R5) note this as a critical flaw.
- **MetaWorld is a simple, low-resolution benchmark**: Images are only 112×112 pixels, which is insufficient for robust evaluation of visual encoders. The diversity of tasks is also limited—Appendix Table 12 suggests only 10 tasks. This is far from a “generalist” policy as claimed.
- **Lack of other benchmarks**: The paper should include additional simulated benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO, CALVIN, RoboCasa, DMC, Habitat, RoboMimic, ManiSkill, RLBench) and real-world data (OXE, DROID) to support the findings.
- **Main finding is not new**: Several reviewers (R3, R4, R5) state that the superiority of local (spatial) features over global features is already well-known in the community; works like RT-2-X, OpenVLA, Octo, and Shang et al. (Theia, CoRL 2024) have already used local features. The comparison is considered unfair because global features were often used only as a simple evaluation baseline.
- **Missing related works**: The paper omits numerous relevant studies, including:
  - [1] Shang et al., “Theia: Distilling Diverse Vision Foundation Models for Robot Learning”, CoRL 2024
  - [2] “On Pre-Training for Visuo-Motor Control: Revisiting a Learning-from-Scratch Baseline”, ICML 2024
  - [3] Xiao et al., “Masked Visual Pre-training for Motor Control”, CoRL 2022 (also MVP)
  - [4] “Robot Learning with Sensorimotor Pre-training”, CoRL
  - [5] “WHAT MAKES PRE-TRAINED VISUAL REPRESENTATIONS SUCCESSFUL FOR ROBUST MANIPULATION?”, CoRL 2024
  - [6] He et al., “Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners”, CVPR 2022
  - [7] Zhai et al., “Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training”, ICCV 2023
  - [8] Ranzinge et al., “AM-RADIO: Agglomerative Vision Foundation Model - Reduce All Domains Into One”, CVPR 2024
  - [9] Dosovitskiy et al., “An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale”, ICLR 2021
  - [10] “Spatiotemporal Predictive Pre-training for Robotic Motor Control”, “Learning Manipulation by Predicting Interaction”, “Language-driven representation learning for robotics”, “An unbiased look at datasets for visuo-motor pre-training” (R3’s refs [1-6]).
- **Contradictions with prior results**: R2 notes that the paper claims R3M outperforms other models (lines 288-293), but multiple other studies find CLIP or ImageNet backbones more effective. No explanation is provided for this discrepancy.
- **Missing implementation details**: The paper does not specify the number of demonstrations used for training, the number of tasks evaluated, or the breakdown by difficulty (easy/medium/hard/very hard). R2 and R5 note that experimental settings are unclear (e.g., number of random runs). In Section 5.1, R3 points out that with 5 demonstrations per task for 10 tasks, the fine-tuning dataset size should be 50, not 500 as reported.
- **Lack of analysis on why different representations perform differently**: R5 emphasizes that the paper offers no in-depth analysis linking policy performance to properties of the visual encoder (datasets, objectives, architectures, quantitative measurements). This is a key missing component.
- **Purpose of multiple views unclear**: R5 questions how findings transfer to settings with fewer or more views, and whether DINOv2’s cross-view alignment explains its best performance. The paper does not investigate this.
- **Value of augmentation techniques unclear**: R5 notes that DINOv2 is almost always the best, and there is no investigation into how to further improve it or why augmentations affect different models differently.
- **Few-shot adaptation method’s connection to pre-trained visual representations is unclear**: R5 points out that the self-distillation applies to the policy network’s latent features (head), not the visual encoder, so it is unclear how it addresses visual domain shift. The performance gain over simple fine-tuning is insufficient according to R1 and R3; R3 also recommends comparing with adapter-based methods like LoRA. R5 suggests comparing with ReVLA’s approach of resetting visual encoder weights after fine-tuning.
- **No vision-language model inclusion**: R1 notes the paper does not evaluate vision-language models (e.g., Qwen) or test unseen language tasks, which are important for generalist policies.
- **Title is overclaiming**: R4 states the title “Building Generalist Robot Policy” is not supported by the limited experiments; a more humble title is needed (e.g., Pi0 is cited as an example of a generalist policy).
- **Performance of self-distillation is not significant**: R1 and R3 both note that compared to fine-tuning, the gain from self-distillation is marginal, and the method is not clearly beneficial.

**Note**: One reviewer (R1) gave a score of 5 and was more positive about the paper’s contributions and the self-distillation idea. However, the other four reviewers (R2, R3, R4, R5) all scored 3 and expressed serious concerns about simulation-only evaluation, lack of novelty, missing comparisons, insufficient analysis, and overclaiming.
