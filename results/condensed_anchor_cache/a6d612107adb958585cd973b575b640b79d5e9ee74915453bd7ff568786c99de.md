- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes EReLELA, an algorithm that augments reinforcement learning with emergent language (EL) abstractions learned via referential games. The EL is used to derive intrinsic rewards to aid exploration in hard-exploration tasks. The authors also propose the Compactness Ambiguity Metric (CAM) to analyze abstraction quality. Experiments compare EL-based exploration to natural language (NL) baselines in procedurally-generated environments. Reviewers agree the research question is creative and the method is interesting, but raise significant concerns about the clarity of CAM and the experimental presentation, the lack of clear empirical gains, missing comparisons to state-of-the-art intrinsic reward methods, insufficiently hard environments, and missing baselines. One reviewer is markedly more negative, disputing empirical gains outright, while two are more concerned with presentation and clarity.

### Strengths
- The EReLELA algorithm is presented clearly and well motivated by the success of language-based abstraction methods for RL exploration and the potential of emergent communication (Reviewer 1).
- The paper provides a practical application of emergent languages, demonstrating their applicability—an important contribution to the emergent communication field (Reviewer 1).
- The research question—whether emergent language abstractions can be used to help RL agents in hard exploration problems—is creative (Reviewer 3).
- The method is interesting; the proposed CAM metric is a decent attempt at measuring abstraction quality (Reviewer 2).
- The problem is well contextualized in Sections 1 and 2 (Reviewer 2).
- The analysis of Zipf’s Law of Abbreviation in the learned emergent language is insightful (Reviewer 2).
- The ablation studies seem thorough (Reviewer 2, though clarity issues prevent full evaluation).

### Weaknesses
- **CAM definition and justification are unclear.** All three reviewers find the description of the Compactness Ambiguity Metric insufficient. Reviewer 1: “CAM is not clearly defined and/or justified… The description of the metric lacks detail regarding the specific mathematical operations involved in computing the histogram. It is unclear how the time intervals are precisely mapped to histogram bins, and the role of the hyperparameters $(\lambda_i)$ in this mapping is not sufficiently explained. The justification for using a histogram of compactness counts as a measure of abstraction quality is also weak.” Reviewer 2: “I struggle to understand the formulation of the proposed CAM in sec 3.2. I think neither eq 4 nor the relative ambiguity of a language are CAM, but I cannot find exactly how CAM is computed.” Reviewer 3 does not explicitly comment on CAM but its absence from empirical validation underscores the problem.
- **Experimental presentation is cluttered and difficult to interpret.** Reviewers 1 and 2 both note that the figures are noisy, hard to read, and lack clear legends and descriptions of settings. Reviewer 1: “The graphics themselves are quite noisy and refer to settings that are not described in detail (e.g., ‘Agnostic STGS-LazImpa-10-1 ELA+AccThresh=90+Distr=256+UnifDSS’). … I cannot determine what is learned about the proposed algorithm.” Reviewer 2: “Figure readability is sadly discounted by low resolution, small font size, and the lack of in-figure legends. … Undefined H3.1 and H3.2 in lines 337-338. $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ in Figure 2 captions seem out of the blue. Are they defined anywhere in the main text? Why do they imply ‘shared’ and ‘agnostic’?” Reviewer 3 also complains figures are too small and captions insufficient.
- **Lack of clear empirical gains for emergent language over natural language baselines.** Reviewer 3 strongly disputes the claimed performance: “Looking at Figure 2, it seems to me that the performance of the natural language abstraction agent (gray) and the emergent language abstraction agent (green) are within noise levels of each other. Thus, I find it difficult to conclude that emergent language abstractions are important, in particular since both agents use count-based exploration terms as well. These should be ablated.” Reviewers 1 and 2 are less dismissive but their inability to interpret the experiments weakens the evidence. This disagreement (Reviewer 3 more negative) is important to preserve.
- **Missing comparisons to state-of-the-art intrinsic reward methods based on language abstractions.** Reviewer 3 specifically demands comparisons to: Mu et al., “Improving Intrinsic Exploration with Language Abstractions” (NeurIPS 2022); Klissarov et al., “Motif: Intrinsic Motivation from Artificial Intelligence Feedback” (2023); Zhang et al., “OMNI: Open-endedness via Models of human Notions of Interestingness” (ICLR 2024). These are necessary to validate the claim that EL abstractions are preferable.
- **Need for harder exploration environments and additional benchmarks.** Reviewer 3 argues: “the authors need to evaluate on harder exploration problems, such as MiniGrid’s KeyCorridor-S3-R3 and MultiRoom-N10-S10, or MiniHack (Samvelyan et al., NeurIPS 2021). Moreover, I would like to see experiments beyond gridworlds, e.g., on Vizdoom (c.f. Henaff et al., NeurIPS 2022).” Current experiments are insufficient.
- **Missing baselines: tabula-rasa RL and RND.** Reviewer 3: “I believe it would be important to add a tabula-rasa RL agent, as well as only RND baseline to Figure 2.” This is needed to disentangle contributions of different exploration components.
- **Figure 3 experiments may not have finished.** Reviewer 3: “p8 Figure 3 looks to me like the experiments did not finish in time.” This undermines the reported results.
- **Presentation and typographical errors.** Multiple reviewers note typos, unclear phrasing, and inconsistent notation. Examples: Abstract “be done ne(?) with Reinforcement Learning”; Introduction typos; “it dynamics” → “its dynamics”; “NLs oracle” → “NL oracle”; superscript -1 on line 271 unexplained. Also, the “natural language” baseline is programmatically generated, so it should be called a “synthetic language” (Reviewer 1).
- **Applicability of the listener in intrinsic reward generation is not discussed.** Reviewer 3: “From Figure 1 it looks like the intrinsic reward is only generated from the speaker. Why shouldn’t one also derive the intrinsic reward from the listener?” This is a missing exploration of design choices.
- **Hypotheses H3.1 and H3.2 are not clearly defined.** Reviewer 1: “While I appreciate explicitly naming the hypotheses, they are not stated with enough clarity and precision to be testable.” Reviewer 2 also notes they are undefined in lines 337-338.
