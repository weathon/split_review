## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates how learning new text alters existing LLM knowledge, defining a “priming effect” where unrelated knowledge is contaminated. The key finding is that the magnitude of priming can be predicted from the pre-learning token probability of keywords; this correlation is robust across models (PaLM-2-xs/s, Gemma-2b, Llama-2-7b), model sizes, training stages (pre-training, fine-tuning), and tasks (instruction tuning, continual pre-training). The authors introduce the “Outlandish” dataset (1320 samples with diverse textual characteristics) and propose two mitigation strategies: a “stepping-stone” text augmentation technique and an “ignore-k” gradient pruning strategy (median priming reduction 50–75% and 50–95% depending on architecture). The paper also compares weight learning and in-context learning, finding a much weaker correlation in the latter. All four reviewers find the topic interesting and the results valuable, but raise concerns about mechanistic explanation, experimental scope, framing, and clarity. One reviewer (R2, score 6, confidence 3) is notably more critical of framing and the natural consequence of the metric definition; another reviewer (R4, score 8) reports no major weaknesses.

### Strengths
1. **Predictable priming effect** – The paper demonstrates that priming magnitude can be predicted from pre-learning keyword probabilities, with causal evidence from intervention experiments. This predictability has practical implications for controlling knowledge updates. (R1, R3, R4)
2. **Robustness across models and settings** – The correlation holds across multiple model families (PaLM-2-xs/s, Gemma-2b, Llama-2-7b), model sizes, training phases (pre-training, fine-tuning), and tasks (instruction fine-tuning, continual pre-training), enhancing generalizability. (R1, R3)
3. **Novel adaptation of “priming” from psychology** – The concept is creatively applied to LLMs, offering a fresh perspective on knowledge alterations. (R3)
4. **New dataset (“Outlandish”)** – A dataset of 1320 samples with varied textual characteristics and themes, supporting the study. (R1, R3, R4)
5. **Two practical mitigation strategies** – The “ignore-k” gradient pruning and “stepping-stone” text augmentation both reduce priming effectively (50–75% and 50–95% median reduction depending on architecture), enhancing the specificity of gradient-based learning. (R1, R3, R4)
6. **Comparison of weight learning vs. context learning** – The correlation is significantly weaker in context learning, highlighting differences between implicit and explicit optimizers. (R1)

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient mechanistic explanation** – The paper does not analyze how internal representations change when new text is introduced, nor explores architectural features making models susceptible to priming. The gradient updates’ impact on internal states is not examined. (R1) The coupling between priming and memorization in PaLM models but not in others is observed without explanation of why or under what conditions this occurs. (R3)
2. **Experimental scope limited to single-fact learning** – Experiments focus on learning one fact at a time, which may not reflect real-world multi-fact learning, interference between facts, or ordering effects. Multi-fact scenarios or comparisons with knowledge editing methods (e.g., Meng et al. 2022a,b; Ovadia et al. 2023b) are missing. (R1) Additionally, only 12 tokens were used in some experiments raising sample-size concerns. (R2, R3 question 1)
3. **Priming metric does not distinguish desirable from undesirable priming** – The metric captures both generalization and hallucination, whereas model editing literature uses more nuanced metrics like specificity, locality, and portability. Without distinguishing correct vs. incorrect associations, practical utility for factual accuracy is limited. (R3) The metric also ignores synonyms or hypernyms, only tracking keyword probabilities. (R1)
4. **Framing issues** – The title and language (“pollution”) imply all new data is harmful, but new data is necessary for acquiring knowledge; the paper itself notes new data can be beneficial or harmful, but the framing is inconsistent. (R2)
5. **Modulation strategies lack motivation** – If a practitioner knows a datapoint is undesirable, they could simply not train on it; the benefit of training with modulation strategies is not clearly justified. (R2)
6. **Some findings appear to be natural consequences of the metric** – The result that priming is more severe for unsurprising tokens may be a direct artifact of the definition of S_prime, since P_before appears in the denominator. (R2, also R3 question 3)
7. **Clarity and presentation** – The paper is poorly written; exact experimental setup requires reading the appendix. Figures are underexplained and too small. Specific issues:
   - Figure 2b: axes and significance of blue dots not defined.
   - Figure 7a: caption unclear; “matched” vs. “unmatched” undefined.
   - Figure 8: line plot inappropriate; a strip plot (like Fig 9) would be better.
   - Figures 10 and 11: too small, need explanation of hypothesis tested and meaning of t-test; Figure 11 missing p-values. (R3)
   - Typos: “2” should be “two” (L327); “TRIE-MERGE” should be “TIES-MERGE” (L911); “Interpretability” (optional). (R3)
8. **Experimental details missing or unclear** – 
   - Discrepancy: 10 categories vs. 11 (L213 vs. L247). (R3 question 1)
   - How is learning determined to be finished? (R3 question 2)
   - Model selection criteria not stated. (R3 question 3)
   - PaLM-S and PaLM-XS parameter counts not specified. (R3 question 4)
   - Why not group experiments by keywords (would be 110 groups instead of 1320)? (R3 question 5)
   - “3 presentations of an Outlandish sample” – clarification needed: out of how many total examples? (R3 question 6)
   - “priming post-learning” is redundant. (R3 question 7)
   - Dataset categories (real facts vs. succinct real facts) not clearly differentiated; examples per category missing. (R3 question 8)
   - Augmentation examples not given in Appendix A6. (R3 question 9)
   - K varied from 1 to 50: is this realistic relative to batch size? Is the frequency excessive? (R3 question 10)
   - Replacement of one minibatch sample for 20–40 consecutive minibatches: clarification needed. (R3 question 11)
9. **Limited comparison with related work** – No discussion or comparison with LLM knowledge editing or continual learning methods beyond a brief mention. (R1)
10. **Impact on real-world accuracy not quantified** – The paper does not demonstrate that changes in priming score translate to erroneous predictions; statistics on negative impact are lacking. (R1, also R2 question 2)
11. **Only three model families tested** – The question of trying more models was raised. (R4)
