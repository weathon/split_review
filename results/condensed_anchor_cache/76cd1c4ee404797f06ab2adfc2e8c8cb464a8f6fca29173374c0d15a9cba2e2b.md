## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper focuses on inference-time robustness for graph classification, proposing R-Pool, a pre-pooling operation that uses Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) to filter out nodes heavily affected by adversarial attacks. The method is model-agnostic, requires no retraining, and is supported by theoretical analysis linking pooling operations to robustness. Reviews acknowledge the clear writing and the underexplored problem, but raise major concerns about experimental sufficiency, baseline comparisons, novelty, methodology clarity, and attack/defense scope.

### Strengths
- The paper is well organized, with a clear flow and logical progression from challenges to solution (R1, R2, R4). The introduction effectively motivates the problem (R1).
- The theoretical analysis is sound and easy to follow, providing a formal grounding for the approach (R1, R2). Preliminary definitions of adversarial robustness are clearly explained (R3).
- The method does not require retraining, model architecture changes, or adaptation of the underlying GNN; it can be used with any pooling operation and model (R1, R4). This plug-and-play nature is a practical advantage.
- The literature review is comprehensive, making it easy to grasp background and related work (R3).
- Ablation studies and hyperparameter sensitivity experiments are conducted (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient and weak baseline comparisons.**  
   - The experiments compare against no (or very few) existing defense methods. Many established defenses that can be adapted to graph classification—such as Soft Median [1], MedianGNN [2], GCN-Jaccard [3], and others [4]—are not included as baselines (R1, R3, R4).  
   - In many cases shown in Table 1, R-Pool performs worse than the baselines that are included, making it difficult to justify effectiveness (R4).  
   - The paper should also evaluate R-Pool as a plug-and-play module on top of other defense methods (R1).

2. **Limited datasets, model architectures, and scalability.**  
   - Experiments use only three datasets, all with two classes (R3, R4). Results on datasets with more classes are needed (R4).  
   - Only GCN is tested; diverse architectures (e.g., GAT, GraphSAGE, pooling variants) and large-scale graphs are required to demonstrate generalizability (R3).

3. **Limited novelty and technical contribution.**  
   - The core idea—identifying and eliminating nodes affected by attacks—is similar to prior works like Soft Median, MedianGNN, and GCN-Jaccard (R3).  
   - The GEM score used for filtering closely resembles existing OOD detection methods (R4).  
   - The method’s simplicity does not represent a significant technical advance (R3, R4).  
   - *Contrast:* Two reviewers (R1, R2) view the theoretical support positively, but they do not refute the novelty criticisms.

4. **Justification of discarding high-degree nodes is insufficient.**  
   - The paper argues that attacked nodes tend to be high-degree, yet prior work suggests gradient-based attacks (e.g., PGD) more often target low-degree nodes (R2, citing Wu et al. 2019, Gosch et al. 2023, Li et al. 2023).  
   - No experiments are provided to demonstrate that high-degree nodes are indeed more significantly affected by the attacks used (R2).  
   - Degree-aware GNNs (e.g., Wu et al. 2019) have already studied this relationship, and the choice needs stronger justification (R2).

5. **Attack methods are relatively simple and outdated.**  
   - Only standard PGD-like attacks are considered; more advanced threats like node injection attacks (e.g., from the Graph Robustness Benchmark, NeurIPS 2021) are missing (R1).  
   - The integration of the attack’s unnoticeability constraint with the PGD budget is not detailed, e.g., how the new distance metric ensures unnoticeability is unclear (R2).

6. **Methodology description is unclear.**  
   - The writing of the method section is poor; for example, it is ambiguous how the “score” (line 343) is used—whether it simply filters nodes or also contributes to the pooling operation (R3).  
   - More precise explanation of the filtering mechanism and the role of the GMM is needed (R3).

7. **Missing ablation and analysis of pooling operations.**  
   - The paper should provide results comparing adversarial robustness with different standard readout operations (max-pooling, sum-pooling, average-pooling) to isolate R-Pool’s effect (R4).

8. **Practical and theoretical implications are not justified.**  
   - The paper does not discuss concrete real-world scenarios for graph classification robustness (unlike node classification where fraud detection is a clear example) (R2).  
   - The motivation for studying robustness in graph classification beyond extending existing ideas is not well established (R2).
