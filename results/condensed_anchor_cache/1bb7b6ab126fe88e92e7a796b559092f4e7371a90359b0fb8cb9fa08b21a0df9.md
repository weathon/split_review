## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ReConcile, a multi-agent multi-round framework that leverages diverse LLM agents (GPT, Bard, Claude) to improve reasoning. In each round, agents produce answers, explanations, and confidence scores, then discuss via a prompt that includes grouped answers, confidence, and demonstrations of answer-rectifying human explanations. A confidence-weighted voting mechanism yields a team answer. Experiments on several benchmarks (StrategyQA, ECQA, GSM8K, AQuA, MATH, etc.) show improvements over single-agent and multi-agent baselines, with up to 11.4% gain, and even surpassing GPT-4 on three datasets. Ablations highlight the importance of model diversity and convincing demonstrations.

### Strengths
- The problem of leveraging multiple agents’ diversity to improve reasoning is timely.
- The method is well motivated and clearly explained despite the framework’s complexity.
- The baselines and benchmarks are well chosen; the experimental methodology appears sound.
- Detailed ablation studies (Table 5) quantify the contribution of each component; the lift from multi-model diversity is largest and particularly interesting.
- The gains on StrategyQA are impressive.
- The idea of extending multi-agent debate to roundtable discussions with confidence and convincing is interesting.
- The paper provides useful insights for building atop the proposed work (e.g., effect of diverse models, confidence estimation).
- It is noted that when a stronger model (GPT-4) is included, it tends to dominate, converging to its solo accuracy.

### Weaknesses
- The method has many moving pieces (selection of convincing samples where the agent’s initial answer is wrong, team answer heuristics, confidence rescaling) that introduce potential bias relative to simpler baselines. The use of training-set examples where the agent initially errs may create a strong bias.
- It remains unclear what complementary benefits multi-agent discussion provides beyond simpler ensemble or self-consistency methods. In Table 3, weighted vote saturates after 3 rounds while majority vote continues improving, questioning the need for the complex mechanism.
- The confidence rescaling heuristics are not backed by experiments; their impact across tasks is unclear.
- The evaluation is limited to relatively simple reasoning tasks (StrategyQA, ECQA, GSM8K, AQuA). GSM8K can be solved with ~85% by Claude-2 alone and ~97% by GPT-4 Code Interpreter; the reasoning complexity may not justify a complex multi-round strategy. The improvement on the harder MATH dataset is marginal (8% reported in abstract but the gain over baselines is small).
- It is unclear whether the performance gain stems from the multi-model ensemble rather than the roundtable discussion mechanism. An explicit ensemble baseline (e.g., majority vote over models) is missing.
- Many hyperparameters (number of discussion rounds, voting weights) are fixed arbitrarily (e.g., 3 rounds). It is unclear if they are robust across tasks, if a dynamic stopping criterion would be better, and what guidelines exist for setting voting weights.
- The possibility that weak models could negatively impact performance (both final accuracy and convergence speed) is not explored. When GPT-4 is combined with weaker models (Bard/Claude2), performance drops significantly compared to GPT-4 alone.
- GPT-4 is not included in most baseline algorithms (e.g., Multi-Agent Debate), so it is unknown whether GPT-4 would benefit similarly from those simpler methods.
- Token counts are not discussed; the improvement may be partially due to increased computation and cost, not the method itself.
- The margin over baselines is larger on StrategyQA/ECQA than on GSM8K/AQuA, but no explanation is provided.
- There is an inconsistency: Table 2 reports weighted vote 79.0% on StrategyQA while Table 3 reports 78.7%.
- The grouping mechanism for “distinct categories” is unclear.
- It is unclear how 3 rounds of Debate with GPT-4 differ from ReConcile with GPT-4.
- Individual ablations of the convincing demonstrations for each model and more qualitative examples are needed.
- The paper should compare against recent reasoning improvement methods such as WizardMath and Step-Aware Verifier.
- The reported ChatGPT performance in Table 2 appears lower than in previous work (e.g., Making Large Language Models Better Reasoners with Step-Aware Verifier).
- One reviewer asks: How often do the agents settle on a single answer? (The paper does not address the consensus dynamics.)
