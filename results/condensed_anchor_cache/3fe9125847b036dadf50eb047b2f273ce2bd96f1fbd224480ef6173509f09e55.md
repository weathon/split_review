## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a time-adaptive differentially private federated learning (DP-FL) framework. Instead of spending privacy budgets uniformly across rounds, clients save budget in early rounds (when coarse-grained features are learned) and spend more in later rounds (when fine-grained features are important). The method achieves non-uniform spending by using lower client subsampling rates in the saving phase and normal rates in the spending phase. A theoretical analysis bounds the expected clipping bias, and experiments on MNIST and FMNIST show improved privacy-utility trade-offs under personalized privacy budgets.

### Strengths
- The idea of adaptively spending privacy budgets across time is novel, intuitive, and well motivated (R1, R2, R4). R4 calls it “fascinating and quite new.”
- The problem of improving performance in DP-FL is important (R2).
- Theoretical analysis is provided, including a bound on the expected clipping bias (R1, R2, R4). R2 notes the analysis gives insights; R4 describes it as “extensive.”
- Privacy analysis of the algorithm is given (R1).
- Related works are mentioned and discussed clearly (R3).
- Experimental results show a moderate amount of privacy budget saved throughout training, supporting the mechanism (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation**: Only two simple datasets (MNIST, FMNIST) and few baselines are considered (R1, R3, R4). R3 notes more complex datasets (e.g., CIFAR10/100) are needed. R2 requests additional experiments: a non-private baseline, ablation on the set of sampling rates and switching time, and testing in non-personalized FL and non-FL (DP-SGD) settings.
- **Missing comparison to adaptive clipping methods**: R3 and R4 note that existing algorithms (e.g., adaptive clipping threshold) are directly related and crucial to compare against. R3 specifically counters the paper’s claim that comparison is unfair, pointing to methods that reduce privacy leakage during tuning.
- **Unclear hyperparameter selection and design choices**: The motivation for design choices in Section 3 is not clearly explained (R2). The configuration of lower sampling rates and the switching round \(T_n\) is not justified (R2, R4). R3 asks how \(\{q_n^t\}\) and \(\{M_n^t\}\) are determined in experiments.
- **Potential flaw in privacy analysis (Algorithm 1)**: R3 argues the analysis is performed before training, but a client can only compute privacy spent after each round it actually participates in. The sampling rate \(q_n^t\) implicitly determines participation count, yet the algorithm pre-determines it. R3 questions what happens if a client participates in all rounds (at rate 1) and how the pre-training accounting can be valid. This casts doubt on the correctness of the privacy guarantees.
- **Assumptions and applicability concerns**: R3 notes big assumptions that limit applicability. The threat model assumes honest-but-curious clients and an honest server; R3 asks whether the analysis extends to an honest-but-curious server and mentions a recent ICML 2024 noise-aware aggregation paper.
- **Writing clarity**: R3 finds the draft hard to understand and requiring multiple reads. R1 notes that clearer definitions of notations and the DP-to-RDP mapping would help.
- **Missing experimental details**: R1 asks for \(\delta\) used in Table 1 and Figure 2, justification for high \(\epsilon\) values (10, 20, 30), and the number of communication rounds. R4 questions whether data sensitivity is considered when computing the DP noise scale and whether model heterogeneity would be a challenge.
- **Minor issues**: Exchange order of lines 14/15 in Algorithm 1 and missing input in Algorithm 2 (R3).
