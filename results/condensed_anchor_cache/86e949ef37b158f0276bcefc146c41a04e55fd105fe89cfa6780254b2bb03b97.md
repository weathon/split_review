## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a unified mirror descent (UMD) algorithm that convexly combines four base update rules (KL, EU, ME, ML) and optimizes the combination weights via zero-order methods (RS, GLD). It is evaluated on 24 tabular benchmark environments covering single-agent, cooperative, competitive, and mixed settings. Reviewer scores range from 3 (weak reject) to 5 (strong accept); confidence levels 3–4. The core idea is simple and well-motivated, but reviewers note substantial weaknesses in formalism, lack of theoretical analysis, questionable practical advantage beyond naïve algorithm selection, incorrect definitions, missing background attribution, and insufficient justification of the solution concept and weight-optimisation procedure.

### Strengths
- The algorithm is simple and built on solid existing methods; it does not add much computational complexity beyond the sum of the four base algorithms.
- The paper is well motivated by the observation that no single algorithm performs best across all types of decision-making problems.
- Experiments on tabular environments are extensive (24 benchmark environments) and include various analyses; UMD performs better than or on par with the best base policy in over 87% (21/24) games.
- The approach demonstrates an advantage of the proposed RS/GLD zero-order optimisation over vanilla RS/GLD in updating the combination weights.
- Originality: there are no known prior meta algorithms for combining mirror-descent-like update rules in decision-making settings.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty and theoretical analysis:** The method is essentially a convex combination of four existing algorithms with little theoretical justification. No analysis is given of why the method works, what dominates the optimal α, or whether the optimal solution lies inside the convex hull of the four base policy updates.  
- **Unclear solution concept and formalism:**  
  * The paper incorrectly defines a Dec-POMDP as covering non-cooperative settings; a partially observable stochastic game (POSG) is the correct formalism.  
  * The solution concept is not clearly defined. The soft-optimality formulation uses undefined entropy $\mathcal{H}(\pi_i)$ – it is unclear whether this denotes the entropy of a mixed action or the expected entropy of a policy.  
  * The claim that the solution is a Nash equilibrium is incorrect; the entropy regularisation makes it an agent quantal response equilibrium (AQRE) or a related concept.  
  * The evaluation metric “NE-Gap” is not well motivated for general-sum games; in zero-sum games it has a clear interpretation, but for general-sum it is not obvious that NE-Gap is meaningful.  
  * In Figure 4, it is unclear what “gap” means if the underlying solution is an AQRE (no AQRE gap is standard).  
  * The paper does not clearly tell whether all base update rules are solving for the same solution concept (KL and EU with moving magnets aim for Nash; ME and ML appear to solve for a φ-regularised equilibrium – but φ is not specified).  
- **Lack of clarity and missing definitions:**  
  * Definitions of $v_k$ and $\phi$ in the ME/ML update rules (page 5) are missing.  
  * The role of the magnet policy $\rho$ in Eq. (4), the definition of $\hat{\eta}$, and whether the same magnet update is used for both KL and EU are not explained.  
  * The RS algorithm description does not define L.  
  * It is unclear whether $v_k(a)$ denotes a single-step reward or cumulative value.  
  * The objective $\mathcal{L}(\alpha)$ depends on the current policy; the optimization method is applied to a constantly changing objective without justification of why the hyperparameter optimisation works.  
- **Insufficient justification and questionable advantages:**  
  * UMD looks more like an algorithm selection strategy than a true framework: a naïve “run all four and pick the best” would perform comparably without the extra overhead.  
  * UMD outperforms all baselines in only a few settings; the normalized improvement over the best base (KL) is small and sometimes negative (e.g., Leduc).  
  * Updating α requires computing expected rewards or NE gaps, which demands full knowledge of game payoffs and other agents’ policies, unlike the base policies that use only observed rewards.  
  * The random sampling mechanism for α is not justified.  
  * It is not clear whether zeroth-order optimisation is necessary, or whether a simpler bandit strategy (e.g., EXP3) would work with less computation.  
- **Missing experiments and comparisons:**  
  * All experiments are tabular; it would be critical to test UMD with neural-network policies in more complex environments, especially to see if the convex hull assumption holds under non-convex optimal solution sets.  
  * Tiny Hanabi results are limited to only some games.  
  * No experiment is provided to show whether UMD can solve a problem that none of the four base policies can solve individually.  
  * Hyperparameter selection and the amount of entropy regularisation are not reported, making comparison claims unjustified.  
  * It is unclear whether the experiments assume full feedback (access to all action values) or bandit feedback.  
- **Attribution and naming issues:**  
  * The material in Section 4.1 up through Eq. (8) is directly from (Sokota et al., 2023) and should be presented as background, not as a contribution.  
  * The “practical version” of the Euclidean update (after Eq. (8)) is not a true (magnetic) mirror descent because it uses an ad hoc projection instead of the Bregman-divergence-dictated projection.  
  * The name “Unified Mirror Descent (UMD)” already exists for a different algorithm (Juditsky et al., 2023); an alternative name should be chosen to avoid confusion.  
- **Missing historical references:** Important early works on mirror descent (Nemirovsky & Yudin, 1983; Beck & Teboulle, 2003) and stochastic games (Shapley, 1953; Mertens & Neyman, 1981) are not cited, despite the paper’s effort to cite recent work.  
- **Presentation issues:** Figure 1 is placed too early (page 2) but first mentioned on page 7; Section 4.1 is unclear (e.g., superscripts missing in Eqs. (5)–(10)); there are formatting errors.  
- **Minority negative contrast:** Two reviewers (scores 3) are particularly critical, pointing out the lack of advantage over naïve algorithm selection and fundamental formalism errors; their concerns should be weighed seriously despite the higher ratings from other reviewers.
