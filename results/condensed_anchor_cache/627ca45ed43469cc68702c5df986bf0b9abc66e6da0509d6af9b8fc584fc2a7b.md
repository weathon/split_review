## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies in-context learning (ICL) models from a meta-learning perspective, theoretically proving that ICL with transformers can express and replicate the hypothesis spaces of gradient-based, metric-based, and amortization-based meta-learners. It experimentally demonstrates that ICL learns data-dependent optimal algorithms on simple, homogeneous tasks but exhibits implicit (distribution-sensitive) learning on mixed tasks, highlighting limited generalizability. The paper proposes meta-level meta-learning (M²-ICL) for domain adaptability and meta-level curriculum learning for accelerated convergence, but these are only evaluated on simplistic synthetic data. Reviewers generally find the theoretical analysis solid and the insights valuable (especially the clarification that "ICL is not algorithm selection"), but express concerns about writing clarity, experimental validation on real-world tasks, definition of "optimality", and some incorrect assumptions.

### Strengths
- The theoretical foundation for understanding ICL models as learning algorithms is thorough and well-motivated, proving expressiveness and comparing ICL to traditional meta-learning algorithms. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- The exploration from a learning-to-learn perspective yields significant insights, notably the clarification that "ICL is not algorithm selection", which challenges previous interpretations and adds depth to the discussion. (Reviewer 2)
- The observation that different meta-learning methods generalize best on their respective task sets is interesting, and ICL trained on a mixture can outperform individual meta-learners. (Reviewer 4)
- Clarity in Sections 3 and 4 is commendable, making complex findings accessible. (Reviewer 2)
- The paper is well-motivated, well-written, and easy to follow (Reviewer 3) — note: Reviewer 4 strongly disagrees on clarity, so this is a point of disagreement.
- The proposed assumptions are proved and confirmed with solid theoretical analysis and experiments (Reviewer 3), though Reviewer 4 questions the experimental design.
- Highlighting the critical issue of generalizability in ICL models and offering potential solutions (meta-level meta-learning and curriculum learning) is valuable. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Writing and clarity**: The paper is poorly written with numerous typos (e.g., "tunie" at line 482) and lacks clarity, making it difficult to follow; a complete revision is necessary. (Reviewer 4) Key concepts such as "expressiveness", "learnability", and "generalizability" should be clearly defined early, and Figure 1 needs a clearer explanation. (Reviewer 2) The extensive theoretical background may limit accessibility for practitioners. (Reviewer 1) The related work section needs significant improvement; it should situate the work within the broader literature rather than simply listing studies, and references for MatchNet, ProtoNet, and CNP are missing at line 244. (Reviewer 4)
- **Incorrect or unsubstantiated assumptions**: Line 82–85 incorrectly claims that transformers are inherently permutation-invariant. (Reviewer 4) Line 453 mistakenly implies that all in-context learners are pre-trained; studies such as [1] and [2] demonstrate otherwise. (Reviewer 4)
- **Experimental validation is weak**: The proposed methods (meta-level meta-learning and meta-level curriculum learning) are only evaluated on simplistic synthetic data, lacking empirical evaluation on complex, real-world tasks where curriculum ordering could be less clear-cut. (Reviewers 1, 2) The experimental design does not reference standard literature or pipelines for task creation; authors should adopt state-of-the-art methodologies or justify deviations. (Reviewer 4) Task generation details are only in the appendix and should be included in the main text. (Reviewer 4) The results in Figure 7 (meta-meta-learner adaptation) seem trivial, and the meta-meta-learner does not outperform in a meaningful way, so the contribution of that section is unclear. (Reviewer 4) The curriculum learning experiments are insufficient to demonstrate effectiveness; a more robust evaluation is needed. (Reviewer 4)
- **Vague definition of "optimality"**: The paper mentions data-dependent optimality without clarifying criteria or metrics for measuring optimality across distributions, making it challenging to objectively evaluate claims. (Reviewers 1, 4) The notion of "optimal" algorithm is vague. (Reviewer 4) Questions: How would you define or quantify optimality in real-world settings with diverse task distributions? (Reviewer 1)
- **Missing explanations and insufficient analyses**: Why does M²-ICL without adaptation sometimes work worse than ICL? (Reviewer 3) For meta-level curriculum learning, how is task complexity defined for classification tasks? (Reviewer 3) What is the definition of \( p_f \) for task generation? (Reviewer 3) The paper tests ICL on three task types but does not investigate whether increasing dataset size or diversity systematically improves generalization across unseen distributions. (Reviewer 1) Does ICL prioritize simpler or more complex tasks? (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited scope**: The paper only focuses on ICL with transformers; ICL with other deep architectures are worth exploring. (Reviewer 3)
- **Main text and appendix feel disconnected**: Mathematical proofs are left in the appendix, which brings little contribution there. (Reviewer 4)
- **Figure 1 is unclear**: What guarantees the orthogonality among the three methods (gradient-based, metric-based, amortization-based)? (Reviewer 4)
