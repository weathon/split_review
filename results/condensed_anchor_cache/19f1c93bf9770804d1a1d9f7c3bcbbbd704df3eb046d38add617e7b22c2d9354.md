## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes MiLoDo, a data-driven decentralized optimizer trained to exploit problem features. It derives necessary conditions for consensus and optimality that any update rule must satisfy, and uses them to constrain the learned update parameterization, reducing the search space. Experiments on LASSO, logistic regression, MLP/MNIST, and ResNet/CIFAR show that MiLoDo-trained algorithms outperform handcrafted methods and exhibit strong generalization (e.g., trained for 100 iterations, run robustly for 100,000 at inference, and generalize to real data, higher dimensions, and different loss functions). However, reviewers raise concerns about novelty (techniques appear standard), fairness of comparisons (baselines may not be optimally tuned), limited scale of experiments, lack of evaluation on data heterogeneity, and practical assumptions (fixed network, synchronized updates, O(n²) parameter complexity).

### Strengths

- Distributed optimization is an important problem.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- The contributions are well-situated in the recent literatures on learned optimizers and decentralized optimization.
- The motivation for the parameterization introduced in equations (12)–(14) as well as the implementation in (22)–(24) is sound and interesting.
- The MiLoDo framework provides insights on the necessary mathematical conditions for ensuring consensus and optimality in decentralized training, which could be of separate interest.
- This work bridges the L2O literature with decentralized optimization techniques; the problem formulation is intuitive and the reduction in parameter search space works well in practice.
- The analytical results presented in the main paper align with existing decentralized optimization literature.
- Thorough experiments on small problems (LASSO, logistic regression, MLP/MNIST, ResNet/CIFAR) illustrate the promise of this approach.
- Impressive generalization: MiLoDo trained for 100 iterations runs robustly for 100,000 iterations at inference, and generalizes to real data, higher dimensions, and different loss functions.
- The inclusion of runtimes and transparency about the computational overhead is appreciated.

### Weaknesses

1. **Novelty and Contribution**: Two reviewers (scores 3) question novelty. Techniques such as variable duplication to create equality constraints are standard (e.g., ADMM). The "mathematical-inspired" label is seen as unclear, and the learned update rule is essentially tuning step sizes. The paper should better articulate what is genuinely new compared to existing work. One reviewer states: "If existing algorithms were given the best parameters, then their performance cannot be beaten by the proposed algorithm, which uses essentially the same algorithmic structure." Another notes it seems easier to learn the learning rate than to conduct the learning proposed here. Two other reviewers (scores 6) find the approach interesting but acknowledge the need for clearer differentiation.

2. **Motivation and Practical Relevance**: The motivation for using decentralized optimizers is unclear; there is no widely-adopted implementation today. The assumption of a fixed network with fully synchronized updates is difficult to satisfy in many applications of decentralized optimization. The framework does not address network structure beyond assuming strong connectivity.

3. **Scale of Experiments**: Experimental results focus on small problems (ResNet/CIFAR is moderate but solvable centrally with modest compute). The paper would be stronger with evidence at scales where distributed training is essential. Additionally, MiLoDo requires learning parameters for all connections among agents, leading to O(n²) complexity with network size, undesirable for large-scale applications.

4. **Data Heterogeneity**: The paper does not address data/function heterogeneity across agents, a key aspect of decentralized optimization. In experiments (MNIST, CIFAR-10), data is split evenly across agents, making the system essentially reduce to SVRG where consensus and optimality are trivial. The paper needs experiments on heterogeneous data and discussion of MiLoDo's generalization across degrees of heterogeneity. One reviewer indicated their acceptance decision depends on how this is addressed.

5. **Fairness of Comparisons**: The comparison with existing decentralized algorithms may be unfair. Existing algorithms are sensitive to hyperparameters like learning rate; it is unclear how these were selected. If optimally tuned, the proposed algorithm using the same algorithmic structure should not beat them. The paper should compare with step-size tuning methods and explain baseline hyperparameter selection.

6. **Experimental Details and Clarity**:
   - Whether the same 5-stage MiLoDo training procedure is used for all workloads (including MLP and ResNet), and how sensitive results are to this procedure.
   - Meta-training for MNIST and CIFAR uses (subsets of) the same dataset as evaluation; a clear separation between optimizees and evaluation workloads would be more convincing.
   - No intuition is given for the loss spike/instability (in F(x)) in Fig. 7 around iteration 500.
   - Timing experiments lack system details (e.g., hardware, software) needed to interpret the times.
   - Notation is inconsistent: in Sections 2 and 3 the learnable update rule is denoted **g**, while in Section 4 **g** is used for subgradients of the regularization term and the learned rule is **m**.
   - No references are cited to support the "weak generalization" challenge (lines 82–93).

7. **Memoryless Derivation vs. LSTM Implementation**: Section 4.1 assumes memoryless parameters (p_i^k, p_{i,j,1}^k, p_{i,j,2}^k), but Section 4.2 implements these with LSTMs (RNNs with memory across iterations). It is known that memory/momentum is important for efficient decentralized optimization (e.g., EXTRA, DIGing, SlowMo). The paper should reconcile this: is memory helpful but not necessary according to theory? Do the theoretical claims apply to the LSTM implementation?

8. **Agent-Specific Parameters**: Each agent learns individual parameters. A more elegant solution would train one set of parameters applicable to all agents. Though this may not satisfy the mathematical conditions, some discussion would be appreciated.

9. **Generalization to Higher Dimensions**: It is unclear how generalization to higher problem dimension works. Since the update rule is implemented as an LSTM, its weight shapes would depend on the problem dimension. The paper should explain.
