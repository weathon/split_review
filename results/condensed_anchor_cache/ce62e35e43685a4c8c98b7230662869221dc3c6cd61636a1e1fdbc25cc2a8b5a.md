## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies the generalization benefit of large learning rates in SGD training for a binary classification task using a one-hidden-layer CNN (with ReLU² activation and fixed second layer). The authors propose a feature-noise data generation model with weak features (small ℓ2 norm, present in every data point), strong features (large ℓ2 norm, present only in a fraction of data points), and noise. Theoretically and experimentally, they argue that oscillating SGD (large learning rate) learns both strong and weak features, while small‑learning‑rate SGD learns only strong features, thus generalizing poorly to test points containing only weak features. The key insight is that oscillation – caused by the large learning rate – enables learning of the weak signal.

### Strengths

* The topic is important: understanding how large learning rates benefit generalization is a crucial and challenging area.
* The paper is well written, easy to follow, and the intuition is clearly explained through a toy example with a single training data point (Section 3).
* The theoretical analysis is solid, and the proof that oscillatory dynamics lead to good generalization is a valuable contribution given the difficulty of analyzing such behavior.
* The data generation model (strong vs. weak patches) is interesting and can serve as a starting point for future work.
* The paper correctly identifies the role of signal‑to‑noise ratio in the data and gradient, echoing insights from related works (e.g., arxiv:2107.11774, arxiv:2303.13093).
* The novel message – that large learning rates help “select” which subset of data points are learned – is relevant and insightful.

### Weaknesses

* **Artificial problem setting.** Multiple reviewers question how natural or realistic the feature‑noise data model is. The network architecture (separate weight per patch) and training setup are tailored to the model, and it is unclear if the results generalize to realistic datasets like CIFAR‑10. Reviewer 3 in particular finds the setting too artificial, limiting relevance and significance.
* **Questionable choices in architecture.**  
  - The activation is ReLU² (“I have never seen this”). It is not justified whether the quadratic effect or the ReLU part is essential; the result may not hold for plain ReLU or plain quadratic activation.  
  - The second layer is not trained, which is very different from training multiple layers simultaneously (see arxiv:2205.11787).  
  - The model is referred to as a “CNN,” but most readers would likely not agree with that label.
* **Oscillation is assumed, not proven.** The oscillating condition \(|y_{i_t} f(\mathbf{x}_{i_t};\mathbf{W}^{(t)}) - 1| \geq \delta\) (Assumption 4) is stated as an assumption. While the authors show it is consistent with the dynamics, it is not derived from first principles. Removing this hypothesis – e.g., by adding label noise as in *Label noise (stochastic) gradient descent implicitly solves the Lasso for quadratic parametrisation* (Pillaud-Vivien et al., COLT 2022) – would strengthen the work. A discussion of necessary and sufficient conditions for the oscillation is missing.
* **Lack of generality.** The authors claim that results can be further generalized, but it is not clear how. The architecture seems hand‑crafted to the data model.
* **Unusual initialization.** Initial weights are set to \(d^{-1/2}\) with large \(d\). The necessity of this choice is not explained, and it is unclear how it affects the analysis.
* **Missing comparisons and ablations.**  
  - How does the analysis relate to simpler settings like diagonal linear networks, where oscillations lead to sparse features?  
  - No comparison with the regime of edge‑of‑stability is provided, despite briefly mentioning it.  
  - The paper does not test whether results extend to other activation functions or to end‑to‑end training.
