## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FairDropout, a group-label-free method to reduce reliance on spurious correlations. It builds on the observation that ERM memorizes minority examples (localized to specific neurons) and generalizes on majority groups. FairDropout redirects memorization to designated neurons that are dropped out at inference. Experiments on five datasets (Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, MetaShift, CXR) evaluate worst-group accuracy.

### Strengths
- The paper focuses on a practical setting without group annotations, which is promising for robustness.
- The memorization analysis in Section 3.2 (CelebA, Figure 2) provides illuminating empirical evidence that ERM overfits noise in minority training examples.
- Evaluation is comprehensive, categorizing methods by assumptions/extra data.
- One reviewer (score 5) found the idea novel in the spurious-features field, nicely incorporating memorization and opening new research directions; they praised the problem description (Section 3.1) and benchmarking as well-described with great empirical results.
- The method does not introduce additional computational overhead during inference (though training cost is questioned; see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and unclear distinction from example-tied dropout (Maini et al., 2023).** Reviewers note that FairDropout appears to be a direct application or rebranding of example-tied dropout, sharing the same hyperparameters \(p_{\text{gen}}\) and \(p_{\text{mem}}\). The paper provides no clear description or intuition of example-tied dropout, nor a delineation of how FairDropout differs beyond possible application to projection heads. One reviewer (score 5) also expressed uncertainty about whether this is new or just a rebranding.
- **Missing key baseline comparisons and non-standard reporting.** Several recent group-label-free methods are omitted: Simple and Fast Group Robustness (ICML 2023), Bias Amplification Enhances Minority Group Performance (TMLR 2024), Correct-n-Contrast (ICML 2022), LaBonte et al. (NIPS 2023), Li et al. (TMLR 2024). Reported worst-group accuracies are lower than some of these baselines. The reported performance of existing methods may not be standard, making assessment difficult.
- **Mixed empirical results.** Performance on Waterbirds is the worst among provided baselines; the explanation (lines 425–429) is deemed insufficient. Results on MetaShift and CXR are only incremental; improvements are strong on CelebA and MultiNLI.
- **Lack of theoretical and analytical depth.** The key observation (generalization vs. memorization) lacks theoretical analysis. It may not hold in under- or over-parameterization regimes (e.g., Section 5.3 of “An investigation of why overparameterization exacerbates spurious correlations”). Experiments with different backbones (ResNet-101, ViT) are needed, or theoretical justification.
- **Method description and identification of neurons are unclear.** Section 3.2 does not clearly explain how “certain neurons” are identified for memorization vs. generalization. Equation (2) is hard to understand (what does the gradient subscript \(j\) mean?). A reviewer asks: without group annotations, how is it possible to determine whether a neuron represents a memorizing or generalizing feature?
- **Lack of ablation studies.** No ablation on hyperparameters \(p_{\text{gen}}\) and \(p_{\text{mem}}\), dropout-layer placement, or sensitivity analysis. The approach is suspected to be very sensitive to hyperparameters (like JTT).
- **Potential computational prohibitive in training.** Determining memorization neurons for each training example could be prohibitive for large datasets and many internal neurons; training time or complexity analysis is missing.
- **Poor writing and presentation.** Numerous typos (e.g., Line 189: Fig. 2 → Fig. 1; Line 145: \(p_{tr}\)), grammar errors, format issues (different font sizes in Fig. 1). The introduction lacks clear group definitions (improved later in Section 3.1). Figures 1 and 2 are crucial but lack clarity: for Fig. 1 there is a mismatch between labels and text; Fig. 2b is unclear how obtained and what it represents (e.g., difference between minority group and worst-group, how probability is computed). The description of Maini et al. (lines 197–210) is unintuitive. Caption of Figure 5 is unclear. One reviewer noted that the paper is almost two pages under the limit and has room for improvement.
- **No anonymous code link** (line 323 states “Code is available at this ANONYMOUS LINK” but link is missing).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms (included as Weaknesses):**
  - Why is Waterbirds result the worst among baselines, given it is considered the easiest dataset? (No proper explanation provided.)
  - Isn’t equation (1) an oversimplification assuming performance on all groups except worst-group is satisfactory?
  - Why does the analysis in Section 3.2 focus on training worst-group accuracy (Fig. 2b, L215) rather than test accuracy?
  - How is Figure 5 (colored blocks next to images) interpreted?
- **Analysis limited to CelebA.** The memorization analysis in Section 3.2 is only on CelebA, which is a narrow face dataset; conclusions may not generalize to harder datasets like Waterbirds or ImageNet.
- **No guarantee that generalization neurons do not memorize or memorization neurons do not learn generalizable features.** Stronger evidence is needed, e.g., replicating the Section 3.2 analysis to show zeroing out generalization/memorization neurons flips predictions for majority/minority examples.
