## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes RIGBD, a framework that uses random edge dropping to identify poisoned nodes via prediction variance (theoretically justified) and a robust training strategy to mitigate trigger effects. Reviewers find the idea novel and well-evaluated, with strong theoretical grounding and clear presentation. However, they raise several concerns: computational overhead (especially on large graphs like ogbn-products), reliance on a homogeneity assumption (unaddressed for heterophilic graphs), hyperparameter sensitivity (drop rate β, iterations K), limited evaluation against non-subgraph backdoor attacks (e.g., spectral domain, SBA, nettack) and existing graph backdoor defenses, robustness of the node selection heuristic (outliers), and lack of adaptive attack discussion. There is moderate disagreement on scalability (Reviewer 4 praises efficiency, Reviewer 3 warns of prohibitive cost).

### Strengths
- **Novel and well-evaluated idea**: The use of random edge dropping to detect poisoned nodes via prediction variance is innovative, theoretically backed, and empirically validated (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). The paper also provides a thorough theoretical analysis explaining why this works (Reviewer 2).
- **Specifically designed for graph data**: The method leverages the behavior of malicious edges linking trigger subgraphs to poisoned nodes, a core insight for graph backdoor defense (Reviewer 2). It handles large-scale graphs by avoiding per-edge processing (Reviewer 4).
- **Robust training component**: The paper goes beyond detection to include a robust training strategy that reduces trigger impact, even if some poisoned nodes are missed, and the observation that removing triggers alone does not immunize the model is intriguing (Reviewer 1, 2).
- **Clear presentation and comprehensive experiments**: The paper is well-written, easy to follow (Reviewer 1, 3), and conducts extensive experiments on real-world datasets (Reviewer 3).
- **Stability for clean nodes**: The method ensures clean nodes maintain prediction stability, preventing false positives (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Efficiency and scalability concerns**: The method requires training the GNN encoder twice and performing K forward passes for variance calculation, which incurs large overhead. For very large graphs (e.g., ogbn-products), repeated forward passes and full retraining may be computationally prohibitive. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of how cost scales with nodes, edges, and hidden dimensions (Reviewer 3, minority lower score 6). In contrast, Reviewer 4 claims it handles large-scale graphs efficiently—this disagreement is explicit.
- **Reliance on homogeneity assumption**: In heterophilic graphs, normal nodes connected to dissimilar nodes may also exhibit high prediction variance under edge dropping. The paper does not evaluate on heterophilic datasets or provide theoretical justification for why poisoned nodes’ variance should be significantly higher in such settings (Reviewer 3).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The effectiveness of the random edge drop rate (β) and number of iterations (K) varies across datasets and requires careful tuning. There is no theoretical guarantee for selecting optimal values, which may reduce generalizability (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing evaluation against other graph backdoor types**: The method is only tested against subgraph-based attacks. It is unclear if it generalizes to (a) non-subgraph attacks (e.g., spectral domain backdoors [1], nettack targeted attacks) (Reviewer 2, 3), (b) all-to-all attacks where samples have different target labels (Reviewer 2), or (c) SBA (Reviewer 4). No results are provided for these cases.
- **Robustness of node selection heuristic**: The method ranks nodes by prediction variance and selects the top-ranked until a label change occurs. This approach is susceptible to outliers, e.g., nodes with few neighbors and high class-diversity, which may have large variance and halt selection or lead to incorrect target class identification. No adaptation for this special case is given (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient comparison with existing graph backdoor defenses**: The chosen baselines (GNNGuard, ABL) are not designed for graph backdoor attacks (adversarial attacks and traditional DNNs, respectively). The paper should compare with dedicated graph backdoor defense methods and discuss why it outperforms on DPGBA specifically (Reviewer 1).
- **Need for discussion of dirty-label attacks**: The paper should elaborate on how the method addresses the unique challenges of dirty-label backdoor attacks (subtle manipulation of features/structure) compared to clean-label attacks (Reviewer 1).
- **Similarity to influence function**: The idea of using prediction change under edge deletion is conceptually similar to influence functions, which measure edge/node impact without retraining. The paper should discuss and compare with influence function–based defenses (Reviewer 1).
- **No evaluation of adaptive attacks**: Potential ways to design adaptive attacks that bypass the defense are not discussed (Reviewer 4).
- **Potential insufficient supervision**: When the number of attacked nodes is large, the robust training (Eq. 6) minimizes prediction confidence on those nodes and uses only remaining labeled nodes for cross-entropy loss. This may lead to limited supervisory signal (Reviewer 3, question).
