## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Constant Rate Schedule (CRS) for diffusion models that ensures a constant rate of change in the probability distribution of diffused data. The schedule is automatically determined before training by measuring the rate of change of a distance (FID) in the forward process, then using it to set noise levels. Experiments on LSUN (bedroom/church/cat/horse), ImageNet 256×256, and FFHQ with latent-space and pixel-space models show improvements over fixed schedules.

### Strengths
1. **Originality and simplicity** (R1, R3, R4): The idea of automatically deriving a noise schedule by enforcing constant distributional change is simple and original. R1: “The idea is simple and the results look good, at least the proposed methods provide better results compared to the baselines.” R3: “To the best of my knowledge the idea is original … Optimizing the schedule in diffusion models is often overlooked … this work might lead to further research in that direction.”
2. **Principled adaptive method** (R2): The authors “make a novel contribution by explicitly deriving a scheduling procedure by leveraging constant-rate distributional change.” The approach adapts to dataset and model architecture, reducing reliance on handcrafted functional forms.
3. **Extensive validation** (R2): The CRS scheme “is thoroughly validated with extensive experimentation … across different datasets, samplers, and scheduling settings, highlighting its efficacy and broad applicability.” Results show improvement over baselines.
4. **Potential broader impact** (R2, R3): R2: “The generality of CRS suggests it could have broader applications beyond image generation.” R3: “I think such an approach might interest the community.”

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of clarity and reproducibility in methodology presentation** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
   - Section 4 lacks a clear, step-by-step procedural outline; a pseudocode with inputs/outputs would greatly aid comprehension (R2). Appendix B does not provide satisfactory details (R2, R3).
   - The computation of the distance and its derivative (eq. (14), using FID) is central but insufficiently explained. It is unclear how to compute the derivative of FID, which is defined on a point cloud (R1). R3 asks: “What is the overhead of computing InceptionV3 features at many noise levels?” and “What is the significance of computing FID scores between noisy images given InceptionV3 was trained on natural images?”
   - R4: “The authors fail to present a clear and comprehensive pipeline for their algorithm, leaving readers with an incomplete understanding of its implementation details.”
2. **Hyperparameter ξ** (R1, R2, R3):
   - The hyperparameter ξ is introduced in eq. (16) and later changed (e.g., ξ=1.2 in experiments vs. ξ=1.0 suggested by informal argument in Section 4.2). R1 asks how to determine ξ. R2 requests additional context for choosing ξ, especially given the variation, and a better justification for introducing it at all. R3: “This defeats the purpose of the paper as one of the goals is to derive adaptive schedules and reduce the number of hyperparameters but a new hyperparameter is introduced and not discussed. Changing this hyperparameter from the value of 1 also changes the motivation of the work since we no longer have a constant rate schedule.”
3. **Experimental concerns** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
   - **Limited relevance of reported NFE**: Most results are for NFE ≥ 50, which is of limited relevance for improving sampling speed given advanced solvers (UniPC, DPM-Solver-v3) achieving high quality in 10 steps (R4). For ImageNet-256 at 30 NFE, the obtained FID is considerably higher than DPM-Solver-v3 with 10 NFE (R4, citing Tables 19,20).
   - **Missing baseline and dataset**: No results on CIFAR10, where EDM has strong results (R3). R3 suggests evaluating CRS in that setting compared to the EDM baseline.
   - **Fairness of comparison**: In Table 10, the weighting function used for training EDM with CRS appears different from the original EDM paper; for fairness, EDM should be trained with the same schedule (R3).
   - **Overly strong results without explanation**: R1 notes that “the scores in all tables show quite, maybe too strong results” and requests a mathematical explanation why constant rate schedule improves performance so impressively. R2 asks for conjecture why CRS showed limited improvement for training pixel-space models (Section 5.2, Lines 430–431).
   - R4: “The algorithmic design … is overly simplistic, relying solely on the FID as the distance metric for computing $\tilde{D}(\alpha,\alpha')$ without exploring other potentially more suitable options.”
4. **Clarity and presentation issues** (R1, R2):
   - Wording “score-based matching” is not common (R1).
   - Acronyms (CRS, AYS) should be defined at first usage; vague notation (e.g., α thresholds 0.6 and 0.97) needs more context (R2).
   - Contributions summary (Lines 90–103) could be revised for clarity; Items 1 and 2 overlap (R2).
   - Discussion and Conclusion sections could be consolidated (R2).
   - R1: “If we need to compute FID whenever we compute the loss function during training … it sounds a little time consuming.” (Question about computational cost.)
5. **Mathematical derivation issues** (R4):
   - Appendix C merely replicates content from ScoreSDE and EDM without additional explanation or modification. Dependence on t is removed in Eqs. (8) and (9), and Eq. (10) is a null equation (R4).
   - R3 notes a typo in Eq. (17): should be $-\xi$ not $\xi$.
6. **Overclaimed novelty** (R3):
   - The claim that the identified schedule is “very different” from conventional ones should be tamed; similar schedules (e.g., α_t=1−t, σ_t=t in Stochastic Interpolant / Flow Matching) have been investigated (R3).
7. **Minor issues** (R2, R3):
   - Repetitive entries in bibliography (R3, line 588).
   - Clarify that CRS depends on the choice of probability metric/divergence earlier in the text (R2).

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 1 gave a rating of 5 (positive) despite the weaknesses, while Reviewers 2, 3, 4 gave 3 (negative). Reviewer 4 is most critical, especially on experimental relevance (NFE, advanced solvers) and mathematical derivations, stating the submission “clearly falls short of the standards expected for ICLR.”
