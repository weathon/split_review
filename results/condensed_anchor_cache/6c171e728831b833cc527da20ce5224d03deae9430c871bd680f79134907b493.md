## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper analyzes Transformer in-context learning (ICL) as functional gradient descent (FGD) for latent functions \(f(x)\), extending prior RKHS-based results to non-RKHS settings and categorical outputs via softmax and multinomial logistic regression. It establishes a connection between the Nadaraya-Watson kernel average and softmax attention, shows a single attention layer can exactly implement one FGD step, and gives approximate multi-layer extensions. Experiments on synthetic data and ImageNet classification test the effectiveness of single-layer transformers.

### Strengths
- **Generalization of prior ICL-as-FGD results:** The paper extends the functional gradient descent perspective (Cheng et al., von Oswald et al., Ahn et al.) from RKHS/linear regression settings to non-RKHS latent functions and categorical outputs via softmax probabilities. It establishes a connection between attention and Nadaraya-Watson kernel-weighted averaging, offering a unified framework that encompasses multiple prior works.
- **Clear mathematical derivation:** The theoretical connections between functional gradients, kernel averages, and attention mechanisms are presented with clear derivations, making the link explicit for the categorical case.
- **Empirical verification of constructed models:** Under certain initializations, experiments show that trained transformers behave consistently with the constructed functional gradient descent implementation (R2). Single-layer transformers achieve strong ICL performance on synthetic data and real-world ImageNet classification, demonstrating the practical relevance of the constructions.
- **Addresses an important topic:** The paper tackles a core question in theoretical understanding of transformers and ICL. Two reviewers (R1, R5) rated the paper 5, emphasizing the rigorous generalization, extensive experiments, and the essential nature of the topic; they found the presentation clear enough and the contributions meaningful. The analytic framework is noted as promising and potentially impactful if better formulated (R7).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution:** The theoretical results largely refine or extend known connections between attention and gradient descent (von Oswald et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024; Ahn et al., 2023). Lemma 1 is a standard RKHS result (found in classical texts like Schölkopf & Smola, 2002) and Proposition 1 concerns only the Nadaraya-Watson average, not attention—thus it does not show that one attention layer implements functional gradient descent, contrary to claimed contributions. The generalization to categorical data via multinomial logistic regression is considered straightforward and does not provide new insights into ICL dynamics beyond existing functional-space analyses (Cheng et al., ICML 2024). (R1, R2, R3, R4, R6, R7)
- **Lack of formalization and missing main theorem:** The central claim that transformers implement functional gradient descent is not stated as a theorem or proposition. Sections 3 and 4 lack formal results; the only two main results (Lemma 1 and Proposition 1) are seen as trivial or unrelated to attention. The writing is overly dense with premature notation, making the paper difficult to follow; it is unclear whether the system setup in Sections 2–4 remains consistent across sections. (R2, R5, R7)
- **Insufficient theoretical depth:**  
  * No analysis of the approximation error is given for the multi-layer constructions (Section 4), so it is unclear whether multi-layer FGD is a valid model for deeper transformers.  
  * The assumption that the first-order term dominates (residual \(\delta\) negligible) is not examined for highly nonlinear settings or deep architectures (R1).  
  * No optimization or loss landscape analysis is provided to show that the proposed parameter constructions can arise in trained transformers from scratch, though such analyses exist for related settings (Zhang et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024).  
  * The additive linear approximation for expectation updates (Section 4) lacks convergence or accuracy guarantees when data distributions are nonlinear (R1).   
  Proposition 1 does not specify the space of \(f\) or whether the derivative is well-defined (R4). (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Experimental validation is unconvincing:**  
  * There is no direct verification that trained transformers (trained from scratch) converge to the constructed FGD parameters. Missing comparisons include weight examination, loss landscape analysis, and explicit coincidence experiments like Figure 2 of Ahn et al. (2023) (R2).  
  * The observation that trained models stay near GD initialization only indicates GD is a stationary point, not that training converges to it (R3).  
  * The claim that single-layer transformers suffice is not justified: two-layer models fail to outperform one-layer, contradicting the expectation that more GD steps improve performance (R6). Harder tasks and deeper models were not tried; GD cannot converge in one step for all tasks (R6).  
  * Real-data experiments on ImageNet use VGG classifier features as input, making it unclear whether the strong results are due to VGG rather than ICL; testing with less learned features (e.g., middle layers) is missing (R4).  
  * The model architecture and trainable parameters are not clearly specified; it appears the attention layer may have no trainable parameters (R4). (R2, R3, R4, R6)
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** The paper is poorly organized—Sections 3 and 4 lack clear structure and the main conclusions are not highlighted. Notations are introduced prematurely in the abstract and introduction without sufficient explanation. Too much text compares to prior works in the body rather than in a concise Related Works section. Conclusions from experiments are unclear. Typos are present (lines 53, 117, 271). (R1, R2, R5, R7)
- **Overlap between reviewer questions and weaknesses:** Several reviewer questions highlight missing analyses or criticisms already listed, including:  
  * Q1 (R1, R2): What fundamentally new theoretical/empirical insights beyond prior works (von Oswald, Cheng, Ahn, Collins et al.)?  
  * Q2 (R1): Step size in Lemma 1 should be \(\alpha\) instead of \(\alpha/N\)?  
  * Q4 (R1): Does the first-order dominance assumption hold in highly nonlinear or deep settings?  
  * Q5 (R1): Numerical stability of softmax normalization?  
  * Q6 (R1): Effect of linear approximation on convergence and accuracy?  
  * Q7 (R1): Performance when context and query distributions significantly differ?  
  * R2: Request for loss landscape analysis and weight examination.  
  * R3: Request for analysis of multi-layer approximation error and convergence to GD from random initialization.  
  * R4: Clarify trainable parameters and space of \(f\) in Proposition 1; test single-layer with less learned features.  
  * R6: Why doesn't 2-layer outperform 1-layer; try more layers and harder tasks.  
  * R7: Proposition 1 not about attention; writing overly dense; conclusions unclear.
- **Minority positive reviewers disagree on novelty and presentation:** Two reviewers (R1, R5) rated the paper 5, considering the generalization rigorous, experiments strong, and presentation clear enough. However, the majority of reviewers (5 out of 7) rated 3, citing the issues above as substantial weaknesses.
