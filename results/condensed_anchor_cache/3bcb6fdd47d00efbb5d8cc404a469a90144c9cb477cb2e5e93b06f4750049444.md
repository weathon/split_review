## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the first mini-batch algorithm for maximizing a non-negative monotone decomposable submodular function under cardinality and p-system constraints. It builds on the sparsifier approach of Kenneth & Krauthgamer (ICALP 2024) and proposes a variant that samples a new sparsifier (mini-batch) in each greedy iteration using weighted sampling. The authors also compare uniform sampling and observe experimentally that uniform outperforms weighted despite having no worst-case guarantee. To explain this, they define two smoothing models (Model 1 and Model 2) and show via smoothed analysis that uniform sampling can be theoretically competitive under mild assumptions. Experiments compare mini-batch methods (uniform and weighted) against full lazy greedy and the sparsifier approach on cardinality-constrained tasks. The algorithm is simple and builds on existing work.

### Strengths
- Exploring submodular optimization beyond a black-box view is an interesting research direction.
- The paper is well-structured and easy to understand; definitions, explanations, and related work are clear.
- The discussion of uniform vs. weighted sampling and the smoothing model helps bridge the gap between worst-case theory and empirical performance, providing insights into why an algorithm without a worst-case guarantee can still do well.
- The use of smoothed analysis to study realistic inputs is a strong methodological point.
- Table 1 clearly summarizes the results, including a comparison with Kenneth & Krauthgamer.
- The paper draws connections to the “lazier-than-lazy greedy” algorithm (Mirzasoleiman et al., AAAI 2015) and explains how mini-batch sampling can be combined with that idea to reduce query complexity by a factor of Θ(k).
- The experimental suite for cardinality constraints is comprehensive.

### Weaknesses
- Technical contribution is limited. The algorithm is simple and the analysis is straightforward. The bounded-curvature results (not depending on Models 1/2) are not novel and involve only minor changes to the argument of Kenneth & Krauthgamer (R1,R2,R3). One reviewer (R1) was particularly critical, stating these results “don’t use ideas that novel compared to related work” and that the main difference is re-computing a sparsifier each iteration.
- Several results depend on Models 1 or 2 (see Table 1), but it is unclear whether these smoothing models are realistic for typical applications (R1 question: “Do you expect Models 1 and 2 would hold widely?”).
- Improvements over Kenneth & Krauthgamer mainly incorporate the curvature of the function into the bound on the number of function queries, making the bounds instance-dependent rather than truly general (R1).
- No theoretical guarantee is given for uniform sampling. A reviewer asks: if all f_i are bounded by a value R, why can’t concentration inequalities be used to obtain a guarantee for uniform sampling? (R1)
- Missing experiments on p-system constraints (only cardinality-constraint experiments are provided) (R3).
- Figure 1 is hard to read because 12 lines are indistinguishable; it is not possible to see which algorithm with β=10^{-2} performs best (R2).
- The practical motivation is weak: the lunch menu optimization example does not convince from a practitioner’s perspective, and the claim that N is often extremely large (N ≫ 1000) is not supported by clear examples. A reviewer asks: “Are there realistic examples where N ≫ 1000? It’s not clear to me how often we really encounter N distinct personalized submodular functions.” (R3)
- The cardinality constraint experiments use k ≤ 20, which is quite small; increasing k would show how fast the algorithms converge relative to lazy greedy (R3).
- It is unclear whether ICLR is the appropriate venue for this work; submodular maximization in its raw form seems one hop away from deep learning (R3).
- The quantity A_e when first introduced on page 3 is unclear: initially it appears to be a vector of all marginal values, but in Model 1 it is described as a random variable (R3).
- For the Uber pickups experiment, why use Lloyd’s algorithm to find centers instead of a data-independent grid? (R3)
- Section ordering could be improved: one reviewer suggests putting Section 4 (the smoothing models) before Section 3 for better continuity (R2).
