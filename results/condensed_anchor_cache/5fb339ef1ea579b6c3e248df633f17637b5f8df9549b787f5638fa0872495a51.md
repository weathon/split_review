## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents ManiBox, a bounding-box-guided manipulation method using a simulation-based teacher-student framework. The teacher policy (trained with privileged information via PPO) generates large-scale trajectory data, and the student policy (trained via imitation learning on bounding-box observations from multiple cameras) achieves zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. A scaling-law study shows that success rate as a function of data volume follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics, saturating with increasing data. The method is evaluated in simulation and real-world grasping tasks with varied objects and backgrounds.

### Strengths
- Well-written, easy to read, with sufficient experimental details and a supporting supplementary video.
- Addresses a relevant challenge in robotic manipulation; results and methodology are compelling and inspire future work.
- Improvement over baselines in the given grasping task.
- Insightful study on data scaling laws for robotic grasping (Michaelis-Menten relationship between success rate and data volume).
- Effective sim-to-real transfer, aided by a random masking strategy.
- Good background generalization across diverse backgrounds.
- General idea of leveraging vision foundation models for real-robot deployment is interesting and relevant.
- Real-world deployment verifies that inputs from open-vocabulary detection (YOLO) can transfer learned behaviors.
- Visual presentation (particularly Figure 2) effectively clarifies the proposed model.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing pseudo-algorithm**: A pseudo-algorithm that describes teacher training → trajectory selection → student distillation would improve readability (R1).
- **No failure case discussion**: The paper and video do not highlight or analyze failure cases (R1).
- **Reward design not mathematically described**: The reward function is mentioned but not given a precise mathematical formulation (R1).
- **Lemma 2 is a well-known result**: The claim of Lemma 2 (bounding boxes uniquely determine spatial position from multiple views) is a standard result in 3D computer vision (Hartley & Zisserman, 2003) (R2).
- **Limited comparison to baselines**: The baseline scores are simply 0, yet many object-grasping methods (learning- and heuristic-based) can grasp objects at different 3D locations. Why does vision-based ACT get a score of 0? Prior work (Zhao et al., 2023) shows strong ACT results with limited real data. The paper attributes this to spatial scaling but does not provide a direct analysis (R2, R4).
- **Limited object diversity**: Experiments use mostly spherical/cylindrical simple objects; only a few out-of-distribution objects are tested. The paper does not include deformable objects, articulated parts, or varying surface textures. The teacher policy trains on a single object (with scale randomization) in uncluttered tabletop scenarios, which is a very simple configuration (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer notes that this task has been addressed without interactive RL (citation: https://inria.hal.science/inria-00325794/document) (R4).
- **Unclear bounding box usage**: It is unclear how 2D bounding box detections are translated into 3D manipulation actions. Is the bounding box center used as a direct target, or is further processing needed to infer 3D pose? Are the bounding boxes 2D projections or full 3D representations? (R3)
- **Arbitrary fixed cubic space**: The use of fixed \(b \times b \times b\) cubes as operational spaces is not well-justified. Could oriented bounding boxes or shape-adaptive volumes work better? (R3)
- **Complex camera setup**: The system uses three cameras; it is unclear why stereo (two cameras) is insufficient, especially if relying on 2D bounding boxes. The necessity and role of the third camera (arm-mounted vs. environment) are not justified (R3). One reviewer suggests that inferring object state from a single RGB-D camera or from wrist-camera history would make deployment easier (R4).
- **Fragmented descriptions**: Simulation, real-world, and policy setups are described separately, making comparison difficult. A unified table or diagram would improve clarity (R3).
- **Lack of vertical variations**: Experiments seem limited to flat surfaces; it is unclear if the system can handle objects at varying heights or floating bounding boxes in mid-air (R3).
- **Formula 4 confusion**: Symbols and their meanings in Equation 4 are not clearly explained (R3).
- **Low novelty**: The method uses a specific student-teacher formulation with bounding boxes in the student's input space; the finding that expert data requirements scale with workspace size is unsurprising (R4).
- **Bounding boxes as object representation**: 2D bounding boxes provide only a rough convex-hull approximation, which is likely insufficient for objects with complex geometries or varying mass distributions. Grasping at the center may not be optimal or feasible (e.g., a banana). Most test objects are spherical/cylindrical; the teacher policy may not have learned diverse grips (e.g., top grasps) (R1, R4).
- **Abstract claim not substantiated**: The claim that “most models’ spatial generalization challenges stem primarily from high data requirements for spatial understanding” is not substantiated, as dataset size is the only variable investigated. Reformulating the problem (joint-space → end-effector, object-relative) could reduce unnecessary variability (R4).
- **Object generalization evaluation missing details**: The number of trials and object positions are not reported. Since the teacher policy was not trained on those objects, generalization is not inherently tested. Details are needed (R4).
- **Background generalization claims not verified**: The model's generalization ability is attributed to historical information, multi-camera data, and random masking, but none of these claims are verified via ablation (R4).
- **Dataset scaling analysis limited**: Only data volume is varied; conclusions about scaling are not controlled for other factors (R4). The data in Figure 3 shows significant deviation in the 10cm × 10cm × 10cm workspace; it is unclear if the data points come from a single seed (R4).
- **Teacher policy details missing**: The teacher policy does not have access to object size even though the student policy infers it from bounding boxes. Why not? (R4) The teacher’s state space is said to be high-dimensional, but it is unclear how exploration space was reduced (Appendix B.1 missing details) (R4). Hardware resources for teacher policy training are not specified (R3).
- **Student-teacher necessity questioned**: If there is a bijective mapping between privileged object representation and bounding-box detections, the teacher policy could be deployed directly from visual detections. Why is the student-teacher framework needed? (R4)
- **Collision avoidance unclear**: The model controls the robot via joint commands; it is not explained how self-collisions and table collisions are avoided (R4).
- **Trajectory quality criteria**: How are good trajectories distinguished from bad ones in the reward function? (R3) Are random points placed at the object’s center or across its surface? (R3)
- **Why not DAgger?**: The teacher policy could be queried for expert actions online; why is a static dataset of expert trajectories used instead of DAgger? (R4)
- **Simulation dynamics consistency**: Line 265 states “ensure its dynamics are consistent with the real world.” How was this ensured? (R2)
- **LSTM history length**: The history length of input observations for the LSTM student policy is not provided (R1).
- **Need for clearer visuals**: Simulation screenshots should include bounding box annotations to help readers understand the process (R3).
