## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the tension between group robustness methods (which use loss-based heuristics to identify minority groups without group annotations) and poisoning defenses. The authors show that group robustness methods inadvertantly amplify poison samples (e.g., ASR from 0% to 97%), that poisoning defenses remove minority samples (e.g., WGA drops from 55% to 41%), that this tension persists across centralized and federated learning settings, and that naively combining both families of methods fails to resolve the trade-off. A theoretical impossibility result is provided under simplifying assumptions. Experiments are conducted on Waterbirds and a 10% random subset of CelebA.

### Strengths
- The problem of inherent tension between group robustness and poisoning resilience is interesting and novel; exposing this incompatibility is an important contribution.
- Empirical results are well‑presented and clearly support the main claims.
- The paper is well written, easy to follow, and the takeaways from each section are explicit and clear.
- Figure 1 effectively conveys the key ideas.
- The experiments use a variety of group robustness and poisoning techniques and metrics (if not many datasets) and include an extension to federated learning.
- The theoretical result in a simple yet well‑motivated setting complements the empirical findings.
- Showing that a naive combination of both techniques fails demonstrates the depth of the challenge and opens a new direction for research.
- One reviewer rated the contribution as excellent (score 8); another rated it as poor (score 5), indicating strong disagreement about the significance and scope of the work.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset evaluation.** Only Waterbirds and a 10% random subset of CelebA are used. Both are small, vision‑only, and have binary spurious features. Waterbirds is known to be an easy benchmark (ERM can achieve similar worst‑group accuracy to Group DRO), contains incorrect labels and images with both classes, and its validation/test distributions are group‑balanced, which can skew model selection. One reviewer (R4) suggested Spawrious for vision or MultiNLI for language as more rigorous alternatives. R2 also expressed concern about the 10% CelebA subset. A table of group proportions for each dataset is missing.
- **Scope overclaimed.** The paper only considers group robustness methods that use a loss‑based heuristic to pseudo‑label minority groups (e.g., JTT, GEORGE). At the time of writing, only about three such methods exist. The writing does not properly reflect this limited scope; statements such as “group robustness methods fail to distinguish minority groups from poisons” are misleading. One reviewer (R3) rated the contribution as poor for this reason. Additionally, the related‑work section for group robustness is out of date and omits many post‑2021 methods that use loss‑based or other heuristics (R4).
- **No evidence the phenomenon persists for attacks with high success rate.** If a backdoor attack has very high ASR, poisoned examples should have low validation loss and would not be flagged as minority examples by methods like JTT. In most reported setups (Table 2), ASR is low (e.g., 20%), even when amplified. R1 similarly notes that the low ASR may be due to undesirable attack configuration; an attacker might choose a different approach with higher ASR that does not exhibit the same tension. The impossibility result only shows existence of hard‑to‑learn poisons but does not reason whether those poisoned points are useful for achieving attacker objectives.
- **Impossibility claim lacks principled understanding.** The analysis does not clearly define the threat model (attacker capability and objective). A more rigorous result linking an attacker’s maximization of its objective to the points that become amplified minority samples is expected. The current empirical and theoretical evidence does not fully establish inherent impossibility.
- **Missing comparisons and experimental details.** (a) The “standard” ASR baseline in Table 2 should be compared against the case where no group robustness method is applied, to show how much amplification actually occurs (R3). (b) R2 asks why ASR values in Table 3 (Waterbirds, SA setup) are negative and calls for elaboration on whether experiments were conducted for the other two types of poisoning defenses mentioned in Section 2.1. (c) The definitions in Section 2.2 are unclear: the groups differ from those in Sagawa et al. (2020) – this should be explicitly stated. Worst‑group accuracy should be clarified as computed over 4 or 8 groups. The definition of “minority group” (fewer than n/|G_Y| points) seems arbitrary and inconsistent with the literature (R4). (d) In Section 3.3, it should be clarified that I(x) ∈ [0,1] and that I(x)_y as “confidence” is misleading; “least confident” would use a measure like 2|I(x)_y – 1/2| (R4).
- **Writing and presentation issues.** (a) Title is too broad (“Poisoning Defenses”) given the specific focus on poisons that are hard to learn (R2). (b) Figure 1: the lighter‑colored circles/triangles (amplified points) need explicit labeling; the two shades of red are indistinguishable (R4). (c) Phrase “this boost is almost as high as it could have been” (Section 3.2) is confusing (R4). (d) “conciliate” should be “reconcile” (Section 5) (R4). (e) Citation to Wu et al. at bottom of page 7 should be in parentheses (R4). (f) BibTeX has extra braces and capitalization errors (R4). (g) Appendix A.2: “DLDB” should likely read “DLBD” (R4).
- **Reviewer questions that are criticisms or request missing experiments/ablations.** (a) R1: What is the intuition behind the poisoning configuration used (page 4)? Could gradient shaping (Hong et al., 2020) mitigate the tension? (b) R2: Explain negative ASR in Table 3. (c) R2: Provide results for other types of poisoning defenses from Section 2.1. (d) R4: Should Spawrious or MultiNLI be included to strengthen generalizability. (e) R4: Supply a table of group/class proportions for each dataset, including the 10% CelebA sample. (f) R4: Clarify whether worst‑group accuracy uses 4 or 8 groups and why groups differ from prior work. (g) R4: Improve definition of minority group. (h) R4: Correct I(x)_y definition of confidence.
