## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MLAQ, combining Q-learning with an LLM-based imagination space to enable zero-shot optimal decision-making. The framework includes a UCB variant for exploration, a mixed-examination mechanism to verify imagined transitions, and achieves over 90% optimal success on BlocksWorld and RoCo‑benchmark. Reviewers are split: two (scores 8,8) praise the novelty and strong empirical results; one (score 5) acknowledges the idea but raises cost and reward‑handling concerns; two (scores 3,3) find the writing unclear, the method overly complex, and the evaluation limited.

### Strengths
- **Novel integration of Q-learning with LLM-based world models** – multiple reviewers highlight the idea of generating imaginary transitions via an LLM to perform a full RL optimization without direct environmental interaction.
- **Strong empirical performance** – achieves >90% optimal success in challenging benchmarks (BlocksWorld, RoCo‑benchmark), especially on long‑horizon tasks. Results convincingly outperform existing LLM agents.
- **Useful technical components** – the UCB variant for balancing exploration/exploitation and the mixed‑examination mechanism (combining environmental feedback with self‑examination) are considered technically sound and meaningful contributions.
- **Modular and adaptable framework** – seen as potentially extendable to other domains and robotic platforms.
- **Ablations confirm importance of components** (BlocksWorld).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and missing details** – multiple reviewers (scores 3,3,5) found the paper hard to follow or missing crucial information:
  - How is the Q‑function represented? Is it tabular (as in preliminaries) or something else? How is a policy improvement step performed using the Q‑function?
  - What is the concrete action space? What is the breadth of actions the LLM can take?
  - The term “environmental tools” is used extensively but never clearly defined.
  - Terms like “imagination space” are not defined before use.
  - The abstract refers to a website (mlaq.site) that is empty.
  - Qualitative examples of agent performance are missing.
  - Which LLM is used in experiments? How large is it?
  - The method involves many components and would be impossible to implement from the description alone.
- **Limited evaluation scope** – only tested on well‑structured tasks (BlocksWorld, RoCo). Reviewers request evaluation on more complex or unstructured environments (e.g., function calling, tau‑bench, noisy sensors, dynamic obstacles). One reviewer notes the tasks are “toy game domains” and calls for discussion of broader applicability.
- **Cost and computational overhead** – Reviewer 2 raises a major concern: interacting with an LLM for step‑by‑step simulation is resource‑intensive (e.g., 40k tokens for 8‑step BlocksWorld in ablations). No analysis of trade‑offs between performance gains and computational/financial cost is provided. The reliance on multiple LLM calls (generation, verification, planning) could offset savings from avoiding real‑environment interaction.
- **Handling of sparse/delayed rewards** – the paper claims the world model is fully LLM‑based, but Algorithm 1 suggests step‑wise rewards come from the environment. How does the method propagate binary rewards (only at the final step, as in Figure 3) back through imaginary trajectories? Is the LLM world model also used to produce reward, or only transitions? The mechanism for dealing with delayed rewards is not addressed.
- **LLM world model limitations** – the LLM is used as a proxy for the environment, but its understanding of actions and outcomes may not align with real‑world physics or dynamics. The paper does not discuss potential biases, inaccuracies, or hallucinations introduced by the world model, nor how robust MLAQ is to such errors. The self‑examination mechanism depends heavily on LLM correction capabilities, which may fail in new or noisy settings.
- **Theoretical analysis incomplete** – the convergence properties of Q‑learning within the LLM‑based imagination space are not rigorously proven. While a sub‑linear regret bound is mentioned, further proofs (conditions for convergence to an optimal policy) are requested.
- **Missing comparisons and related works** – several reviewers point out omissions: no comparison to Decision Transformers (which also combine LLM with RL); no discussion of Sub‑goal Distillation or other zero‑shot approaches. One reviewer notes that the criteria for results are insufficient and a proper comparison with other methods is needed.
- **Conceptual concerns** – Reviewer 5 questions whether the framework is truly an “LLM agent,” since the policy is derived from Q‑learning, not directly from the LLM. The same reviewer argues that while claimed zero‑shot, the method includes substantial engineering (correcting imaginary transitions, refining the world model) that undermines the zero‑shot claim. The paper does not justify why it is appropriate to call the framework “zero‑shot”.
- **Paper organization and self‑containedness** – the paper assumes detailed knowledge of specific prior works (e.g., Mandi et al. for RoCo). It is not well‑organized; terms and definitions are introduced later or not at all.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity** – not discussed; reviewers ask how sensitive MLAQ’s performance is to the UCB coefficients and confidence bounds.
- **Training data for Q-planner** – unclear whether training is done on synthetic step‑wise rewards or ground‑truth trajectories from environment interaction.
- **Definition of “ground‑truth available actions δ(s)”** – unclear and not explained (node selection process).

*Note: Reviewers who gave high scores (8,8) were enthusiastic about the novelty and results, while a lower‑scoring reviewer (5) focused on cost and reward handling, and two lower‑scoring reviewers (3,3) primarily criticized clarity, missing details, evaluation scope, and comparisons.*
