## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies “indiscriminate miscalibration” in LLMs where both correct and incorrect predictions get similar confidence, proposes new metrics (DKL) and a label-free comparative inference method (LF-ICL) using unlabeled comparison samples in prompts. It claims improved classification (F1, accuracy) and calibration on five classification datasets with Llama models. Scores: 3, 6, 3 (two reviewers critical, one positive). Reviewer 2 (score 6, confidence 5) found the idea novel and experiments extensive; reviewers 1 and 3 (score 3, confidence 4) raised significant concerns about evaluation, baselines, theory, and writing.

### Strengths
- Identification of indiscriminate miscalibration is an interesting and important problem; it contrasts with prior findings that typically show monotonic confidence-accuracy trends (Figure 1b vs typical pattern).
- The proposed label-free comparative inference method is novel and does not require labeled examples.
- The method improves both classification metrics (F1 score, accuracy) and calibration metrics across experiments.
- Experiments were conducted on five different LLMs of varying sizes (Llama family).
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (noted by one reviewer).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Only five standard classification datasets (fewer than the 12–13 used in related work like Batch Calibration, Zhou et al. 2023; Generative Calibration, Jiang et al. 2023). Maximum 500 test samples feels limited. Only Llama-family models tested; generalizability to other architectures is unclear.
- **Missing key baselines and related works**:
  - Papers on calibration for LLMs/ICL are not cited or compared: Abbas et al. (2024) – linear probe calibration; Han et al. (2023) – prototypical calibration; Jiang et al. (2023) – generative calibration; Shen et al. (2024) – Thermometer. The authors’ claim (lines 358-359) that such methods “do not help with improving model classification performance” is contested, as these papers address both ECE and accuracy.
  - No comparisons with these post-hoc calibration methods; the claim that LF-ICL can combine with post-hoc calibration lacks experimental validation.
- **Unfair comparisons**:
  - LF-ICL uses multiple forward passes (e.g., 10 for 0-shot-agg) vs. single pass for standard ICL, without controlling computational cost. No ensemble baseline (e.g., 0-shot-ensemble ICL) is provided.
  - Adding two unlabeled comparison samples effectively increases shot count (0-shot becomes 2-shot, 3-shot becomes 5-shot), giving additional context. This confounds the comparison with standard ICL at the same shot count.
  - No ablation controlling for number of extra samples or ensemble size.
- **Lack of theoretical analysis and mechanistic insight**:
  - The bias averaging assumption in Equation 3 is not justified (theoretical or empirical evidence requested).
  - No exploration of why comparative inference improves calibration: e.g., attention patterns, internal representations, probing or representational similarity analysis.
  - No analysis of consistency across different model architectures and sizes.
- **Missing ablations and experiments**:
  - No ablation on comparison sample selection strategy (random vs. other methods) or sensitivity analysis.
  - No results for 3-shot-agg and 10-shot-agg in Table 1.
  - No standard deviations reported across different choices of test demonstrations (as done in Contextual Calibration, Zhao et al. 2021; Linear Probe Calibration, Abbas et al. 2024; Prototypical Calibration, Han et al. 2023).
  - No experiment on combining post-hoc calibration with few-shot prompting as claimed.
  - In post-hoc calibration experiments, ECE improvement is not robust; why are results not shown using the newly proposed DKL metric?
  - No comparison with a “with-labels” version of the method (adding labels for the two extra samples) to measure the effect of labels.
- **Clarity and explanation gaps**:
  - Difference between Figures 1(a) and 1(b) is not explained; no apples-to-apples comparison showing what causes the differing observations.
  - Method’s applicability to open-ended generation and language modeling tasks is unaddressed.
- **Computational cost**: Multiple forward passes increase inference cost significantly; post-hoc calibration requires validation data, which may not be available for open-ended tasks. No detailed timing analysis provided.
- **Reproducibility**: Code was not provided, raising reproducibility concerns.
- **Writing quality**: Numerous grammatical and typographical errors (specific examples: lines 027-228 redundancy; 094-097 grammatical issues; 135-136 “accuracies are”/“accuracy is”, “regardless whether”; 164 “a LLM” → “an LLM”; 195-197 “dataset” → “datasets”, missing space; 234-236 “incapable for describe”; 268-269 subject-verb agreement; 270-272 “without trained” → “without being trained”, “asking LM” → “asking the LLM”; 289-291 wording; 296 “is because of” → “is that, due to”; 298 confusing phrasing; 302 “bias to the samples” → “bias toward samples”; 303-304 wording; 341 “across 5 dataset.” → “across 5 datasets.”; 346-347 subject-verb agreement; 348 “Though” → “Although”; 367-368 “referencs” → “references”; 485 “restricts” instead of “restricted”). One reviewer (R2) found the paper clearly written, highlighting a direct disagreement on presentation quality.
