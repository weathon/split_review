## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the sample complexity of contrastive learning, where the goal is to learn a distance function (e.g., \(\ell_p\), cosine similarity, tree metric) from labeled tuples \((x,y^+,z^-)\) indicating that \(\rho(x,y^+) < \rho(x,z^-)\). The authors derive (near-)optimal bounds on the number of tuples needed for PAC-style generalization, showing \(\tilde\Theta(\min(nd,n^2))\) tuples for \(d\)-dimensional embeddings of \(n\) points under \(\ell_p\) distances. The core proof technique bounds the VC/Natarajan dimension by expressing the decision boundary as the sign pattern of a low-degree polynomial and applying Warren’s bound on connected components. Results cover realizable and agnostic settings, multiple negatives (\(k>1\)), quadruplet learning, and separable cases. Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 and MNIST/Fashion-MNIST with ResNet18 verify that the theoretical error rates predict the growth observed in practice.

### Strengths
- The proof is elegant and non-trivial: it transforms the contrastive learning classification rule into a polynomial sign pattern and bounds the number of possible satisfiable sign changes via Warren’s algebraic-geometric result, then applies Natarajan dimension bounds from Ben David et al.
- The bounds are tight or nearly tight for a wide range of distances: arbitrary distances, general \(\ell_p\) (even and odd \(p\)), and tree metrics.
- Both realizable and agnostic settings are treated, with sample complexity dependence on \(\epsilon\) matching standard PAC-learning results.
- The proof technique extends straightforwardly to \(k>1\) negatives and quadruplet samples, showing generality.
- The theoretical results are supported by experiments on real image datasets (CIFAR-10/100, MNIST/Fashion-MNIST) using ResNet18, demonstrating that the VC/Natarajan dimension bounds can predict error rate growth in practice, contradicting the folklore that such bounds are too loose for deep learning.
- The paper discusses possible directions for future work.

### Weaknesses
- **Non‑constructive and limited practical guidance** (Reviewer 1): The counting arguments and pigeonhole principle yield existence proofs but do not provide constructive algorithms or insight into how to choose the embedding dimension \(d\) in practice. The constants in the bounds are unlikely to be tight, and the theory does not explain which representations (e.g., what network architectures) are expressive enough to achieve the sample bounds.
- **Insufficient comparison with existing literature** (Reviewer 3, minority critic): The paper does not clearly delineate how its distribution‑independent bounds compare to prior results derived under specific distributional assumptions or with different loss functions, either in metric learning or contrastive learning. Concrete examples of improvement are missing.
- **Main body is too brief** (Reviewer 3): The core theorems and their implications are presented in a rushed manner, forcing the reader to rely heavily on the appendix for full understanding. More detailed explanations and examples should be included directly in the main text.
- **Structural/organizational issues** (Reviewers 2 and 3):  
  - Theorem 3.1 (arbitrary distances) appears misplaced in the middle of the \(\ell_p\) section; it would flow better if presented before the \(\ell_p\) discussion.  
  - Section 3 covers both \(k=1\) and \(k>1\) with the \(\ell_p\) focus, but Section 4 returns to general settings; a clearer separation (e.g., Section 3 for \(k=1\), Section 4 for \(k>1\)) would improve readability.  
  - The paragraph “Reducing dependence on \(n\)” feels disconnected from the main flow and could be integrated into the discussion after the main results.  
- **Table 1 clarity** (Reviewer 2): The row for “\((1+\alpha)\)-separable \(\ell_2\)” should be grouped with the \(\ell_p\) category. The “Same” labels for quadruplet learning and \(k\) negatives are ambiguous—distinct annotations are needed to show which distance functions they apply to.
- **Missing explanations and open questions** (Reviewers 3 and 1):  
  - The reason why upper bounds differ between odd and even \(p\) is not explained.  
  - It is unclear how the sample complexity behaves when the point set \(V\) is infinite.  
  - The paper does not address how the choice of different metrics (cosine similarity, tree metrics) changes the bound on \(d\) in practice.  
- **Typos and minor errors** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4):  
  - Theorem 1.3 contains a repeated phrase “for contrastive learning”.  
  - In the “Outline of the techniques” (p. 4), \(P\) is said to be a polynomial of degree \(2d\); it should be \(2\) (or the text is misleading).  
  - In the same paragraph, “\((x_1^-,x_2^-)\)” should read “\((x_3^-,x_4^-)\)”.  
  - Theorem 3.3, first line, may need the condition \(d < n\) instead of \(d < n\)?  
  - Definition 2.1 uses the symbol \(S_3\) before it is defined.  
  - Duplicated references: “Kulis Kulis” and “Warren Warren”.  
  - Page 8, second line: “\(<\)” should be “\(\leq\)”.  

*Note:* One reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 6) was notably more critical than the others (scores 8, 8, 8), particularly regarding the lack of comparison with existing bounds, the sparse main-body presentation, and the structural disconnects. The other reviewers rated the paper’s soundness and contribution highly, though the organizational weaknesses were also flagged by Reviewer 2.
