## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper theoretically and empirically analyzes the GSAM algorithm (a variant of SAM) under increasing batch sizes or decaying learning rates (cosine annealing, linear). It proves convergence (ε-accuracy) for both settings and conducts experiments on CIFAR-100 with ResNet and ViT, claiming that these strategies lead to flatter minima and better generalization than constant batch size/learning rate.

### Strengths
- **Topic and perspective (R1, R2)**: Well-chosen and interesting topic; the investigation of increasing batch size is a good perspective.
- **Theoretical analysis (R2, R3)**: The analysis appears sound. The upper and lower bounds for the difference between GSAM and GD are reasonable, and the convergence results (existence of a step yielding ε-accuracy under these schedules) are interesting.
- **Experiments (R2)**: Extensive experiments showing that increasing batch size avoids sharp minima in practice and leads to better generalization – this message is insightful.
- **Framework (R4)**: The work progressively establishes and justifies its framework, and the results are promising (one reviewer).

**Minority contrast**: One reviewer (score 5) gave higher marks, explicitly noting the bounds and convergence results as strengths, while also acknowledging significant weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental contribution and significance (R2, R4)**: The contribution is marginal/incremental. Convergence of SAM and its variants (including GSAM) has been established under different settings; this paper mainly fine-tunes GSAM with increasing batch size/decaying LR but lacks significant new theoretical or practical insights.
- **Theoretical limitations and gap with practice**:
    - The convergence analysis relies on unrealistic assumptions (R3): asymptotic full batch, making the results expected for GD and creating a large gap between theory and practical settings.
    - The theory requires many approximations (R1): GSAM approximates SAM, which approximates the exact min-max loss; theorems often reference other papers and are hard to verify.
    - The convergence theorems (2.3, 2.4) only guarantee expected gradient convergence, not individual training paths (R1). No bounds on higher moments are provided.
    - The analysis does not explain why increasing batch size/decaying LR avoids sharp minima (R2), limiting its theoretical insights.
    - The i.i.d. assumption A3 (losses are i.i.d.) is questionable in practice, as losses are dependent due to shared weight updates (R1, Q2).
- **Experimental validation unconvincing or incomplete**:
    - Results may reflect early‑stopping regularization (R1): the batch size progression {8,16,32,64,128} shows faster convergence than constant 128 (Fig.1 at epoch 40); similarly, small‑batch/large‑LR models outperform earlier. Claims of flatter minima are not clearly separated from faster convergence.
    - The conclusion (l537‑539) claims convergence to flatter minima, but only lower test loss after early stopping is shown – flatness is not directly measured (R1).
    - **Contradictory flatness result (R2)**: Table 2 shows that “SAM+C” and “GSAM+C” (with constant settings) exhibit no better flatness than vanilla SAM/GSAM, contradicting the paper’s premise.
    - SAM with fixed batch size/constant LR does not outperform SGD in the same setting (Fig.1, Fig.2), contrary to typical SAM behavior – needs explanation (R2, Q1).
    - Limited to CIFAR‑100, which is small and may not generalize; ViT results are weak and do not strongly support the theory (R3).
    - No synthetic experiments are provided to directly validate the theorems under controlled conditions (R2).
    - GSAM is not consistently better than SAM in accuracy or flatness; the choice of GSAM seems arbitrary and may favor the analysis (R3). Decreased flatness for ResNet is not interpreted (R3, Q1).
    - More datasets and ablation on weight initialization are needed (R4 Q16, Q17); the current evaluation is insufficient to prove effectiveness.
- **Clarity, writing, and presentation**:
    - Figures are poorly readable; labels and text are not publication‑ready (R1).
    - The writing is verbose, especially the introduction, making the motivation and issues unclear (R4). Parts of the paper (P1/015‑, P1/030‑033, P4/187‑196, P4/213‑217) are difficult to follow or lack coherence; Eq. (8) is trivial (R4).
    - Key terms (“perturbed empirical loss”, “maximum empirical loss”) are not sufficiently defined (R4).
    - Is Theorem 2.3 also Theorem B.1? The relationship is not explained (R4).
    - Numerous specific reviewer questions reveal missing details and insufficient explanation: e.g., the equivalence of (3) to (2) in [R1] (R4 Q5); the role of ξ in Assumption 2.1 (R4 Q7); whether ε in L323 is the same as in (2) (R4 Q15); the significance and triviality of bounds (L794‑795, L864‑878) (R4 Q12, Q13, Q14); Section 2.2 lacks GSAM-specific features (R4 Q10); whether Algorithm 1 contributes anything new (R4 Q11).
- **Minor**: The claim (l45‑47) that both increasing batch size and decaying LR produce flatter minima may be an overstatement; Smith et al. (2018) argued for benefits of increasing batch size over LR annealing, but not that both produce flatter minima (R1 Q1).
