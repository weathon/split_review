## Merged Review

### Summary
SWAMP (Sparse Weight Averaging with Multiple Particles) modifies Iterative Magnitude Pruning (IMP) by training multiple sparse models (particles) with Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) and different batch orders from the same matching ticket, then weight-averaging their masks. The method is motivated by linear mode connectivity and the model soups concept: particles lie in the same loss basin, so averaging can find a flatter solution with better generalization. The paper presents experiments on CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet (VGG-19, WRN, ResNet), and RoBERTa fine-tuning, plus a more efficient variant (SWAMP+). All five reviewers agree the paper is well-written and the experiments are extensive, but they note modest accuracy gains, limited novelty (essentially model soups applied to sparse training), high computational cost, and several missing justifications or analyses.

### Strengths
- Well-written, well-organized, and easy to follow; methodology is clearly described especially in Section 3.2 and Algorithm 1.
- Motivation is rooted in solid theoretical frameworks (lottery ticket hypothesis, linear mode connectivity, model soups). The loss landscape analysis (Figure 2) and Hessian trace analysis provide clear empirical support.
- Extensive experimentation across multiple tasks (vision and language), multiple datasets (CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet, GLUE via RoBERTa), and multiple architectures (VGG-19, WRN, ResNet). Comprehensive ablation studies (e.g., Table 5) demonstrate the importance of SWA and the weighted averaging mechanism.
- Additional studies (mask analysis in Table 4, SWAMP+ efficient implementation) are provided.
- The method is shown to be applicable to other sparse training methods (RiGL) in the appendix, suggesting broader applicability than IMP alone.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and contribution**: The method is a straightforward application of the model soups paper to sparse training/IMP. Several reviewers consider the contribution trivial and lacking novelty (Reviewers 1, 2, 4; Contribution scores all 2). One reviewer (Reviewer 2) acknowledges sufficient novelty for the sparse domain but agrees it is not the most novel direction.
- **Marginal gains**: Performance improvements over IMP are modest, especially on ResNet (Table 3) and ImageNet (Table 3 results are “relatively quite weak and not obviously significant” – Reviewer 2). The gains are also less pronounced for lower sparsity levels. This reduces the practical significance of the method.
- **Computational cost**: SWAMP with IMP is much more expensive than most compared sparse training methods at training time (IMP with weight rewinding is already expensive). The SWAMP+ variant reduces cost by using a single particle for early iterations, but this claim is only justified when networks are already very sparse; cost at lower sparsity is a concern (Reviewer 5). Neither SWAMP+ nor full SWAMP were tested on ImageNet with SWAMP+ (Reviewer 5). A comprehensive discussion of space/time overhead, especially for large models like Transformers, is missing (Reviewer 1).
- **Choice of architectures**: Reliance on wide networks (WRN, VGG-19) without explanation; VGG is not parameter-efficient and its use is questioned (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). The paper does not justify why these models were chosen.
- **Applicability to Transformers**: RoBERTa in Table 8 shows a noticeable performance drop even below 50% sparsity. It is unclear whether this limitation comes from IMP (the base method) or from general challenges of pruning RoBERTa. Validity for GPT-like architectures and comparison with alternative pruning strategies for Transformers is not addressed (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing ablation: SWA vs SGD**: The paper does not justify using SWA instead of SGD. Missing comparisons: (1) how does IMP perform with SWA vs SGD? (2) how does SWAMP perform with SGD vs SWA? (Reviewer 3).
- **Imprecise statements**: Example: “As illustrated in Figure 1, our algorithm achieves superior performance, which is on par with that of an ensemble consisting of two sparse networks.” In Figure 1, IMP-3 actually outperforms SWAMP, making the claim unclear (Reviewer 3).
- **Underperformance at low sparsity**: In Figure 3, for sparsity of 20%, the weighted average (WA) underperforms individual particles. Does this mean SWAMP harms performance at lower sparsity? The paper does not provide adequate justification for this phenomenon (Reviewer 4).
- **Why weight averaging instead of best particle?**: Since individual IMP runs can outperform SWAMP (Figure 1), why not simply take the best of multiple pruned weights? (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing details and questions**: 
  - Why do interpolated weights yield lower error than IMP weights (Figure 4)? A detailed hypothesis is requested (Reviewer 5).
  - Appendix B states the SWA learning rate is constant 0.05. Does this mean the minimum learning rate is 0.05 for SWAMP? And what LR is used for IMP? (Reviewer 5).
  - Do IMP and SWAMP use the same epoch \(T_0\) for weight rewinding? (Reviewer 5).
  - If SWAMP is a general method applicable to any sparse training method (as shown with RiGL in the appendix), why is the main body focused exclusively on IMP? (Reviewer 2).
