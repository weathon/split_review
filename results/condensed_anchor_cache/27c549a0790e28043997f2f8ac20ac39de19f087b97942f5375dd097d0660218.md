## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Directional Rank Reduction (DRR) for backdoor defense: it orthogonal-transforms the feature space, prunes the dimension that maximizes the third central moment of the feature distribution (interpreted as the “toxic direction”), and then inverts the transformation.  Reviewers find the idea novel and the connection between neuron pruning and rank reduction insightful.  However, they raise substantial concerns about theoretical assumptions (e.g., equality used in the utility function, strong distributional assumptions for clean data), insufficient experimental scope (single dataset, limited attacks, no adaptive attacks), lack of clarity in referencing and presentation, and the unrealistic requirement of having access to backdoored images.  One reviewer (score 6) is notably more positive about technical soundness and presentation, while the other three (score 5) express more critical reservations, especially about theoretical rigor and experimental completeness.

### Strengths
1.  **Novel idea and new insight.**  The concept of maximizing the third central moment and the connection between pruning and rank reduction are exciting and bring a fresh perspective to backdoor defense.  (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
2.  **Theoretical analysis is provided and is meaningful for future work.**  The paper gives a formal objective for defense and derives the optimal transformation.  (Reviewer 1)
3.  **Visualization of the separation constant C gives good justification for the theoretical assumptions.**  (Reviewer 2)
4.  **The proposed DRR method performs well on both ACC and ASR compared to baselines.**  On average, ASR is reduced by 4.5× and ACC increased by 1.45%.  (Reviewers 2, 4)
5.  **The paper is well-written and easy to follow** (Reviewers 1, 3); one reviewer (3) specifically states it is “technically sound,” though others disagree on that point.

### Weaknesses
1.  **Theoretical inaccuracies and unclear choices.**
    *   The utility function uses \( \|R - \gamma_r(R)\| \) and \( \|R\| - \|\gamma_r(R)\| \) without establishing strict equivalence; the same issue appears in the definition of \(E(R)\).  (Reviewer 1)
    *   The derivation on Page 4 assumes that all clean examples are centered around their mean with equal distance (a circular distribution).  Real-world data often deviates (e.g., elliptical), making the obtained direction \(v\) suboptimal.  (Reviewer 4)
    *   The justification for choosing the third central moment over first or second moments is not provided.  Why does the third moment work best for separating poisoned and benign features?  (Reviewer 1)
    *   The proof lacks rigor: one step “uses the consequence of the proof in the middle of the proof.”  (Reviewer 2)
    *   The reference to “Proposition 1” is not clearly cited; it is impossible to trace the origin of the claim.  (Reviewer 2)
2.  **Insufficient experimental evaluation and missing baselines/attacks.**
    *   Only one dataset (CIFAR-10) and one poisoning rate are tested.  (Reviewer 1 suggests using backdoorbench for standard settings.)
    *   The attacks tested (BadNet, Blended, CLA, WaNet, IAB) are not enough.  Missing important attacks: SIG [1], Smooth (low-frequency) [2], Adapt-blend and Adapt-patch [3] (especially relevant because DRR assumes latent separability).  Also missing recent input-specific backdoor attacks (2022).  (Reviewers 2, 3)
    *   No evaluation of adaptive attacks that try to evade the defense.  (Reviewer 2)
    *   The defense is evaluated assuming access to backdoored images, which is often unrealistic in practice.  The paper does not investigate whether the method works without the attacker’s backdoored data.  (Reviewer 3)
3.  **Concerns about the defense’s impact on clean performance and behavior.**
    *   The first equation on Page 3 suggests reducing the norm of the residual matrix to align clean and backdoored features.  This moves clean features toward backdoor features – does that hurt clean accuracy?  (Reviewer 4)
    *   In Table 2, row 3, DRR achieves higher ACC but not lower ASR compared to other methods.  Why does it not always lower ASR?  (Reviewer 4)
    *   Modifying the weight matrix can cause a performance drop; the paper should explain how the projection maintains clean performance.  (Reviewer 2)
4.  **Practicality and scalability issues.**
    *   The approach requires optimizing a vector in every layer, which could be computationally expensive.  Memory and complexity scaling with larger models/datasets need analysis; can the optimization be made more efficient?  (Reviewers 2, 4)
    *   How many extension directions \(v_i\) were actually used?  (Reviewer 2)
    *   How is the direction vector \(v\) initialized?  Do different initializations change results?  (Reviewer 4)
    *   In Figure 2, the separation constant \(C\) is insignificant for some layers – could those layers be skipped to reduce computation?  (Reviewer 4)
5.  **Presentation and clarity issues.**
    *   The paper lacks a clear algorithmic description of the method, hindering reproducibility.  An algorithm should be provided.  (Reviewer 2)
    *   Equations are not numbered, making cross-referencing difficult.  (Reviewer 4)
    *   Minor typo: missing \(\hat{x}\) in the definition of \(E(R(l))\).  (Reviewer 1)
    *   Notation for references is unclear (e.g., Proposition 1 not cited).  (Reviewer 2)
