## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies in-context learning in a minimalist setup: a linearly parameterized single-layer linear attention model pretrained on linear regression with Gaussian data and a Gaussian prior. The theoretical contributions include: (a) identifying the optimal solution and its excess risk as functions of pretraining sequence lengths and the data covariance; (b) characterizing the SGD training dynamics and deriving a task complexity bound that depends only on the number of tasks and not on the input dimension; (c) proving that with matched evaluation sequence length the model achieves nearly Bayes optimal risk (optimally tuned ridge regression), while mismatched lengths cause sub‑optimality.

### Strengths
- Original analysis of the single‑layer linear attention model using a one‑step gradient descent parameterization and derivation of the optimal step size (Reviewer 1).
- Introduction of novel operator polynomials to handle the order‑8 tensors that appear in the SGD risk analysis; this technique may be of independent interest (Reviewer 2).
- Statistical convergence bounds that explicitly depend on training sequence lengths and the data covariance matrix (Reviewer 2).
- A clear theoretical framework that isolates the effect of mismatched training‑evaluation context lengths, explaining when performance drops (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Task complexity bound of \(O(1/T)\), which is independent of the input dimension \(d\) (Reviewer 3).
- Well‑organized presentation with well‑delineated assumptions, making the paper a useful theoretical checkpoint for the community (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Timely contribution to understanding the statistical foundations of in‑context learning (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Assumptions that deviate from standard practice:** The pretraining algorithm in equation (6) – the equivalence of function classes does not guarantee identical behavior under gradient descent with different parameterizations; the paper does not address this discrepancy (Reviewer 1). The restrictions on the \(Q,K,V\) matrix structure and the use of purely linear attention are significant; if analogous bounds hold for other parameterizations, the uniqueness of attention would be unclear (Reviewer 4). The assumption that the initialization \(\Gamma_0\) commutes with \(H\) is strong and lacks clear motivation (Reviewer 1). The claim that \(H\) can be assumed diagonal “without loss of generality” is not sufficiently explained (Reviewer 1).

- **Clarity issues:** The paper is heavily notation‑driven and would benefit from more intuitive explanations of the key results (Reviewer 1). The term “number of contexts” is confusing – it appears to mean “number of sequences/datasets” (Reviewer 1). Equation (9) implicitly involves the dimension \(d\) through matrix operations, but this dependence is not made explicit (Reviewer 3).

- **Insufficient justification of theoretical choices:** The step‑size schedule in Theorem 4.1 (piecewise constant decay) is not explained in the problem context (Reviewer 1). The upper bound on the initial learning rate \(\gamma_0\) is not motivated (Reviewer 3). The inner product between matrices used in Theorem 4.1 is not defined (Reviewer 4).

- **Lack of empirical validation in the main text:** No numerical results appear in the main paper, making it difficult to judge the practical relevance of the theoretical findings (Reviewer 1). Specific experiments that would strengthen the paper: (i) training on tasks of varying dimensions to illustrate the dimension‑independent task complexity claim (Reviewer 3); (ii) demonstrating that a pretrained model performs poorly when test‑time context length differs from training (Reviewer 3); (iii) tracking training‑time convergence for different eigenvalue behaviors of the data covariance (Reviewer 2); (iv) testing on misspecified data (not drawn from the assumed Gaussian distribution) to see if the risk still decays (Reviewer 4). Several reviewers recommend moving the appendix experiments into the main body (Reviewers 1, 4).

- **Counterintuitive dimension independence claim:** The result that task complexity is independent of \(d\) is surprising; one would typically expect \(O(d^2/T)\) because the task distribution involves at least \(d^2\) parameters. The paper’s own experiments appear to show that \(T\) is exponentially larger than \(d^2\), which seems to contradict the claim that \(T\) can be much smaller than \(d^2\) (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing analysis for realistic training setups:** The theoretical framework assumes training sequences all have the same fixed length. How does the excess risk change when sequences of varying lengths are used? (Reviewer 2). The paper uses a single matrix \(\Gamma\) to absorb query, key, and value matrices; it does not discuss whether the statistical bounds would hold if \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) were trained separately (Reviewer 2).

- **Minority critical voice:** One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 5) was significantly more critical than the others, particularly regarding the clarity, the justification of the pretraining algorithm, and the choice of step‑size. The other three reviewers (scores 8, 8, 6) were overall positive but shared many of the same requests for additional experiments and clarifications.
