## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an alternative to KL-constrained RLHF by targeting a per-prompt reward distribution derived from human demonstrations. The method minimizes the squared difference between the reward of generated responses and a target reward (the reward of a human demonstration for the same prompt). Experiments on three tasks (calibrating sequence-level log-likelihood, mitigating over-specialization/reward hacking on IMDB, and multi-objective calibration on TL;DR summarization) are presented, and the method is compared against baselines with varying KL strengths.

### Strengths
- Novel approach: addressing reward over-optimization by altering the reward function rather than adding policy regularization (e.g., using the difference between a generated response’s reward and a per-prompt target reward from human demonstrations) is a creative idea.
- Broad experimental coverage: the paper investigates three distinct RLHF challenges – covariate shift (log-likelihood error accumulation), reward over-optimization (reward hacking on IMDB), and multi-objective specification (length vs. preference on TL;DR) – on multiple datasets (IMDB, TL;DR, controlled sentiment generation).
- Thorough baseline comparisons: for each use case, the authors include multiple baselines with strong and weak KL regularization, providing a detailed empirical picture.
- One reviewer rated the paper 6 and praised the clear presentation of objectives and the thorough experimentation; that reviewer found the setting well-motivated for the three use cases.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues:** The paper contains grammatical typos, undefined variables (e.g., what is \(t(x)\) in the definition of \(D = (x, t(x))\)?), and poorly explained concepts. For example, the construction of a prompt-continuation dataset by grouping reviews by their first 10 tokens is described as a heuristic that may not generalize. One reviewer rated presentation as “poor,” while another found it clear; however, multiple reviewers struggled to understand key technical details.
- **Imprecise use of “reward over-optimization”:** The phenomenon in Gao et al. (2023) refers to ground truth reward first increasing then decreasing during policy fine-tuning. The paper’s Figure 2 shows only distribution mismatch, which does not necessarily demonstrate such a change in ground truth reward. The term is used loosely, conflating different issues.
- **Reliance on human demonstrations and a ground-truth reward model undermines the RLHF motivation:**  
  - If high-quality human demonstration data are available, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is a simpler alternative that does not suffer from reward over-optimization. The paper does not compare its method against SFT, and from the reported results SFT appears on par.  
  - RLHF is typically used precisely because human demonstrations are hard to obtain, while preference labels over pairs of generated text are cheaper. Here, the method requires both human demonstrations and a ground-truth reward model, shifting the problem to pure RL with a given reward – a different setting that limits the scope of applications.
- **Potential loss of RLHF generalization capability:** By minimizing the L2 distance between the reward of generated responses and a target reward fixed at the human-demonstration level, the policy may not extrapolate beyond human demonstrations or improve upon them, which is a key benefit of RLHF.
- **Misleading name:** “Target reward distribution” suggests a distribution, but the method uses a dataset of prompt–target-reward pairs, not a distribution over rewards.
- **Missing important baselines:**  
  - For sequence-level log-likelihood calibration, the optimal baseline is PPO-ptx (InstructGPT objective with a pretraining loss), which is not included.  
  - For over-specialization, the standard approach is reward model training with KL regularization; this should be directly compared.  
  - For multi-objective calibration, the common method is a linear combination of objectives with a fixed \(\alpha\); this baseline is missing.  
  The lack of these baselines makes it difficult to assess the true contribution of the proposed method.
- **Uncertain source of benefit:** It is unclear whether the reported improvements come from the changed reward function or from collecting additional samples per prompt (the difference between a human generation and a model generation provides a fixed target). This setting does not generalize to preference datasets where only comparison data are available.
- **Redundant and overlapping use cases:** Sequence-level log-likelihood calibration and over-specialization capture the same underlying issue (model divergence indicates over-specialization). The multi-objective use case, with a fixed \(\alpha\), reduces to a single objective similar to the previous cases.
- **Missing experimental details:**  
  - The sampling scheme used for the proposed method and the baseline RL algorithm in the calibration experiments is not specified.  
  - In Figure 4, the metric arrows conflict with the bolded best scores (e.g., \(R^{\text{pref}}\) arrow points up, best score 5.64, but the value 4.15 is bolded).  
- **Sensitivity not explored:** The choice of distance metric (L1 vs. L2) for defining the reward is not tested.
- **Evaluation quality concerns:**  
  - Win rate evaluation is conducted using a Llama2-13B chat model without justification for why a more powerful API model was not used, and no guarantee of the quality of automated ratings is provided.  
  - The diversity metric (type/token ratio) only captures token-level uniqueness, covering only a small aspect of reward over-optimization consequences.
