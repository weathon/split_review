## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Data Prediction Denoising Model (DPDM), a student-teacher fine-tuning method that improves sampling efficiency of diffusion models (DMs) by training stronger denoisers. The authors claim that the standard score-matching objective produces weak denoisers, limiting sample quality when the number of function evaluations (NFE) is small. DPDM trains a sequence of denoisers by minimizing probability divergences between denoiser-recovered and ground truth data distributions, initialized from a teacher DM. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet 64×64 show DPDM outperforms baseline methods with few NFEs.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and practical relevance**: The proposed DPDM is novel, introducing a new generative framework that directly addresses the practical performance drop of DMs under few-step generation. It provides a strong technical contribution by designing a sequence of stronger denoisers.
- **Clear exposition and motivation**: The paper is well-written and clearly structures the motivation, beginning with a well-supported empirical observation of DM degradation at few steps. The early sections are didactic.
- **Theoretical support**: Some theoretical justifications are given for the training gradient of the proposed smoothed KL divergence, and the importance of minimizing distribution divergence is emphasized, adding depth.
- **Convincing experimental results**: DPDMs outperform many recent baselines on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet 64×64 in terms of FID with few NFEs, convincingly demonstrating their effectiveness for few-step generation.
- **Extensive comparisons**: The paper conducts extensive comparisons with existing multi-step generative models, covering numerous recent methods.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Rating 8) was notably more positive than the other three (Ratings 5,5,6), but all acknowledged the contributions while raising concerns.

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty overlap with Diff-Instruct**: DPDM shares many similarities with Diff-Instruct (smoothed KL divergence, student-teacher fine-tuning, gradient updates). Diff-Instruct also evaluated sampling quality with small NFEs. This makes the actual novelty of DPDM unclear.
2. **Incomplete theoretical analysis for multiple denoisers**: While justification is given for a single denoiser, the extension to multiple denoisers (Eq. 3.4) lacks rigorous derivation. The weighting function w(t) is introduced without clear justification, and its impact on convergence/stability is not analyzed.
3. **High computational overhead vs. training-free methods**: DPDM requires training an auxiliary DM, a retrained DM, and a multi-step denoiser, incurring extra memory and training time. The paper provides no detailed breakdown of these costs, making it difficult to assess practical benefits over training-free methods that require no additional overhead.
4. **Limited scope of experiments**: Results are restricted to low-resolution datasets (CIFAR-10, ImageNet 64×64). No results are shown for higher-resolution images (e.g., 256×256, 512×512) or conditional generation tasks (text-to-image, image-to-image), limiting generalizability.
5. **Missing inference efficiency metrics**: The paper focuses on inference efficiency but reports only NFEs. Critical metrics such as inference memory, seconds per iteration, and overall inference time until convergence are not provided, preventing a full comparison.
6. **Key idea and training objective unclear**: The training loss for the separate denoiser appears mathematically equivalent to the original DM L2 loss on predicted noise, raising the question of whether this is simply fine-tuning. The paper does not sufficiently clarify how the proposed objective differs from standard DM training and why it leads to improved few-step performance.
7. **Contradiction with DDPM theory in sampling**: The sampling method treats the clean image prediction as the mean of the next-step distribution, which contradicts DDPM theory (which predicts the mean of the denoised distribution, not the clean image itself). This deviation is not properly justified.
8. **Mathematical foundation not clearly established**: The paper claims a solid mathematical foundation, but the connection between the stated theory and the practical algorithm is not clearly laid out, leaving the justification for the method’s effectiveness unclear.
9. **Specific performance regression at very few steps**: In Table 4, DPDM performs worse than Diff-Instruct for NFE < 4, indicating a limitation at the smallest step counts that is not explained.
10. **Missing convergence analysis for multiple denoisers**: Convergence behavior of the multiple student denoisers during training is not addressed.
11. **Typographical issues**: Duplicate references, duplicate paragraphs (e.g., “Training Efficiency and GPU-memory Cost” appears both in main text and appendix), and the full form of NFE is missing upon first introduction (Page 2).
