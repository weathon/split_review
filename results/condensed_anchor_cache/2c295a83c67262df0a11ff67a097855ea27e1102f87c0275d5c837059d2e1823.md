## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper conducts a meta-analysis of over 100 papers and new experiments across 14 models and 20 datasets to determine where chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting helps. It finds that CoT provides strong gains primarily on math, symbolic, and logical reasoning tasks, with minimal benefit on other task types. On MMLU, gains are tied to the presence of an equals sign. The paper further separates planning and execution, showing that CoT’s benefits come from improved symbolic execution but that symbolic solvers can outperform CoT. The work is praised for its comprehensive analysis and clever experimental design, but several reviewers note that the core findings are already known, the categorization of tasks is unclear, model variation is under-explored, and the recommendation to use symbolic solvers lacks nuance about their brittleness.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive meta-analysis and experiments**: The paper provides an extensive survey of CoT results from 110 papers, grouping tasks into 14 categories, and supplements this with direct comparisons on 20 datasets across 14 models. This dual approach is unique and thorough. (R1, R2)
- **Clever experimental details**: Identifying the equals sign as a key determinant of CoT gains on MMLU is an insightful and well-controlled analysis. The design ensures answers appear earlier for direct answering vs. CoT, providing a clean comparison. (R2)
- **Separation of planning and execution**: The decomposition using PAL and comparison against symbolic solvers is a novel and useful framework. The discussion connecting to early CoT work (Nye et al., 2021) adds valuable context. (R2)
- **Well-written and detailed**: The paper is clearly written and presents its empirical evidence exhaustively. (R1)
- **Qualitative and quantitative depth**: The paper offers both performance metrics and insights into why CoT works on certain tasks, enhancing understanding. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty** (minority view: R1, R3): Two reviewers note that the core finding—CoT mainly helps on math and symbolic reasoning—is already well-known among researchers. The paper’s contributions appear limited despite the comprehensive investigation. (R3 adds that definition of symbolic vs. non-symbolic reasoning leads to confusion.)
- **Missing justification for task categorization**: The paper distinguishes 264 datasets but does not explain how the 14 categories were defined. A detailed rationale would help practitioners apply the recommendations. (R1, echoed by R3)
- **Insufficient model-specific analysis**: The paper makes broad claims about CoT’s effectiveness without always specifying which model(s) generated the results, assuming generalization. Examples: Figure 3 does not list the 5 representative models; line 320 analysis of few-shot CoT vs. direct is unclear; Figure 4’s color coding is not explained. Given that Fig. 6 shows variation across models, a model-wise breakdown would strengthen conclusions. (R1)
- **No discussion of model size in meta-analysis** (R2): The meta-analysis aggregates results without distinguishing model sizes. Work like Kojima et al. (2022) shows size is crucial; 7B models may not show CoT gains, potentially biasing the meta-analysis. The direct comparisons partly address this, but a size-aware analysis in the meta part would be stronger.
- **Overly strong recommendation for symbolic solvers** (R2): Section 5 concludes that LLMs should be paired with symbolic solvers when possible, but the difficulty of reliably generating program inputs is not discussed. Engines are brittle; minor errors cause failure. For example, on FOLIO, CoT and plan+CoT execution outperform plan+tool solver, likely due to high unparseability. The (LLM, LLM) pair may be more robust in practice, adding nuance that the paper omits.
- **Unclear writing on specific points** (R2):
  - The statement at line 323 (“Free response capabilities may be hindered…”) is unclear—what hinderance is being referred to?
  - Framing a common belief as “informally, we believe many readers hold the following view” is less precise than a direct citation.
- **Missing analysis on why CoT sometimes hurts performance** (R3): The paper notes that CoT can decrease performance in some tasks but does not analyze the key reasons or speculate whether this issue will persist in future LLMs.
