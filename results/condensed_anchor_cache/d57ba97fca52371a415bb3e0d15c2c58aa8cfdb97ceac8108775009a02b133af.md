## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper develops a quantum sparse online learning algorithm for logistic regression, SVM, and least squares, building on the truncated gradient descent method of Langford et al. (2009). Under efficient quantum access assumptions, it achieves the same regret bound \(O(1/\sqrt{T})\) while improving the time complexity from \(O(Td)\) to \(\widetilde{O}(T^{5/2}\sqrt{d})\) – a quadratic speedup in the dimension \(d\). However, the regime of improvement requires \(d > \widetilde{\Omega}(T^5)\) (or \(T < \widetilde{O}(d^{1/5})\)), which is rarely satisfied in practical settings. The paper provides theoretical analysis but lacks any empirical validation, and the presentation suffers from insufficient quantum computing preliminaries and unclear novelty.

### Strengths
- The paper is easy to read and clear even for readers not knowledgeable about quantum computing (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 5 also commends the clear presentation of differences with prior work).
- It provides a thorough theoretical analysis with guaranteed regret bounds (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4).
- The time complexity improvement (quadratic speedup in \(d\)) is clearly stated and compared with the classical baseline, particularly beneficial in high‑dimensional regimes (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4, Reviewer 5).
- The algorithm is applied to three well‑studied problems (logistic regression, SVM, least squares) that are popular in machine learning, giving the work broad potential impact (Reviewer 5).
- The use of quantum amplitude estimation/amplification techniques (inner product estimation, norm estimation, state preparation) is explained, and the work is considered foundational with potential to inspire further studies (Reviewer 2).
- The improvement is argued to be vital in the big‑data era where high‑dimensional learning is increasingly common (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of empirical evaluation** – no experiments or simulations are provided, which makes the proposed improvements less persuasive (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 notes the paper does not discuss how feasible the algorithm is to implement.
- **Practical regime of speedup** – the condition \(d > O(T^5)\) (or \(T < O(d^{1/5})\)) is rarely satisfied in typical deep‑learning settings where \(d\) and \(T\) are of the same order. The authors should give concrete, cited examples of high‑dimensional learning problems where this condition actually occurs (Reviewer 4, Reviewer 5). Reviewer 1 questions the intuitive dependence between \(T\) and \(d\) and whether large \(T\) would accumulate errors.
- **Presentation and accessibility** – the paper allocates too much space to standard models (regression, SVM) while providing insufficient quantum‑computing preliminaries. Notations such as \(O_u|j\rangle|\bar{0}\rangle = |j\rangle|u_j\rangle\) (Section 3) and definitions in Section 4 are not accessible to readers without a quantum background (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 asks for an introductory overview of quantum algorithms and an updated related‑work section covering recent advances in both truncated gradient descent and quantum algorithms.
- **Unclear theoretical novelty** – the paper relies on established quantum techniques (inner‑product estimation, norm estimation, state preparation) but does not clearly articulate the authors’ own theoretical contributions. A high‑level proof sketch is missing (Reviewer 5). Reviewer 3 questions whether the speedup stems from the quantum‑access assumptions rather than from algorithmic novelty.
- **Notation, typos, and missing definitions** – there is a discrepancy between the notation for transposition and iteration (both \(T\)) (Reviewer 2). A typo appears in Assumption 1, and Assumption 1(ii) states \(A,B \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}\) even though \(A\) or \(B\) can be zero in the examples below (Reviewer 3). The inputs and outputs of Algorithm 1 are not explicitly defined (Reviewer 2). The paper misuses \(\citep\) and \(\citet\) (Reviewer 5).
- **Unclear or missing motivation** – the research motivation needs to be strengthened and the baseline (Langford et al. 2009) is considered somewhat outdated (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 3 asks: what does “exponential improvement in the dependence on \(\|u^*\|_2\)” refer to on page 4? Also, why does the gravity parameter \(\alpha\) depend on the learning rate \(\eta\)?
- **Open questions that parallel criticisms** – Reviewer 2 asks whether the method can be extended to nonlinear models (if not, please specify limitations). Reviewer 3 asks whether the speedup arises entirely from assumptions of coherent \(\widetilde{O}(1)\)‑time access to features and weights. Reviewer 5 asks whether Assumption 2 (introduced in this paper) is also used in Langford et al. (2009); if not, is the comparison unfair?
