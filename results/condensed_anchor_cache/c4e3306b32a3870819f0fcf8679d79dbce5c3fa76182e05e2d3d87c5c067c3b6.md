## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Gaussian Concept Subspace (GCS) as a robust alternative to single concept vectors for probing and steering concepts in LLMs. GCS models concept representations as a multivariate Gaussian (diagonal covariance) learned from multiple linear probes trained on different probing datasets (10,000 GPT-4o‑generated samples per concept). Concept vectors are sampled within a 1σ range. Experiments measure faithfulness (cosine similarity among sampled vs. observed vectors) and plausibility (hierarchical clustering that matches human‑defined topic hierarchies). Intervention experiments on sentiment steering show that GCS‑sampled vectors (1000 per concept, averaged) can outperform mean‑difference or single‑probe vectors in steering strength while maintaining coherence, though results vary with steering strength parameters. The approach is simple, robust, and shows promise for general use, but has limitations in reliance on large LLM‑generated datasets, computational cost of sampling, and evaluation via GPT‑4o rather than human judgment.

### Strengths

- Good motivation and clear introduction; the paper is well‑written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3).
- Meaningful endeavor to model variance across probe vectors, addressing the known unreliability of single concept vectors (R1; R3 calls it a creative extension solving a major robustness issue).
- Most experiments are well‑designed and results convincing; plausibility plots are clear and informative (R1). Extensive validation across multiple LLMs with different sizes and architectures (R2).
- Key result: In sentiment intervention, 1σ samples from GCS collectively outperform the mean difference or single‑probe vector (R1). R3 notes GCS is a probable Pareto improvement over single‑vector methods without significant extra cost.
- GCS reveals hierarchical concept structures that align with human understanding, providing insights into LLM concept representations (R2, R1).
- The approach is intuitive, simple, and straightforward for enhancing robustness of concept vectors (R4). R3 suggests it could become the new standard for concept vector extraction/steering.
- R3 considers the significance good, anticipating facilitation of future work in representation engineering.

### Weaknesses

- **Dataset and computational requirements**: The approach requires large high‑quality LLM‑generated datasets (10,000 GPT‑4o samples per concept) to estimate variance – a significant limitation for some use cases (R1). Steering by applying and averaging results from 1000 sampled vectors is computationally expensive and needs discussion (R1).
- **Modeling assumption**: The Gaussian with diagonal covariance is a simplification. R1 asks whether a full covariance matrix was tried (e.g., on a smaller model). R2 points out that using Gaussian distributions in representation learning is not entirely new and the paper should clearly distinguish GCS from other probabilistic models. *Disagreement on novelty: R1 and R3 consider it a creative and significant extension, while R2 rates contribution lower (2) and calls for clearer distinction from prior probabilistic models.*
- **Evaluation methodology concerns**:
    - Intervention evaluations are entirely model‑based (GPT‑4o) and may not be accurate; a small human sanity check (e.g., comparing coherence scores) would strengthen soundness (R1, R3). Similarly, including human‑generated texts (e.g., IMDB) alongside GPT‑generated texts for training would improve validity (R3).
    - Table 1: GCS is not consistently superior to single concept vectors; results depend on the strength parameter. Statistical tests across parameters are lacking, and the highlighting criteria are unclear and potentially misleading (R4). R1 adds that the averaging of 1000 steering vectors should be clarified and error bars added.
    - Faithfulness metric (similarity among sampled vs. observed vectors) is confusing: sampled vectors are more similar, but this is expected due to 1σ truncation. The paper does not clarify what ideal behavior is – the metric may just illustrate variance magnitudes per layer (R1).
    - Plausibility and faithfulness labels are not well‑justified: R3 and R1 ask for more explanation of why reproducing hierarchical concepts and similarity measures correspond to these terms.
- **Lack of broader validation**: Experiments focus on limited tasks/datasets (movie reviews, sentiment). R2 suggests exploring wider tasks/domains. R3 also suggests testing on other text types (e.g., tweets) and checking how concept vectors change. R4 misses demonstration that the Gaussian effectively models multidimensional subspaces (e.g., testing interpolation between “love” and “comedy” concepts).
- **Specific experimental details needing clarification** (R1):
    - How exactly are vectors sampled “within 1σ”? (Reject sampling? Algorithm 1 wording ambiguous.)
    - What is the similarity function? If cosine, results may be skewed by mean vector distance from origin.
    - Equation 8: what is **C**? The notation **wᵢ ∈ C** is confusing.
    - Subsampling 1,000 from 10,000: with or without replacement? This critical detail is buried in implementation.
    - Figure 5 (PCA): which vectors define PC space? Why not project the Gaussian mean instead of the mean of 1000 samples?
    - Appendix H: how is the range of **hˡ** found? Only best‑performing examples are shown; random samples should be shown (R1).
    - Appendix I: are samples random or hand‑picked?
    - “Repetitive or chaotic” phrasing in rating prompt (App. H.4) should be justified vs. “coherent” or “fluent”.
- **Open questions** (R4, echoed elsewhere):
    - Why not simply use the mean vector of GCS rather than sampled vectors? The mean would be more robust and avoid noise.
    - Provide an intuitive explanation for why GCS improves intervention performance compared to a single concept vector.
- **Minor issues**:
    - Related work on hierarchical concepts is light; R1 suggests references.
    - R1 advises moving subsampling details earlier in the paper.
    - Typo: “generalized” should be “generalizable” (R1).
    - Ambiguity in text describing 1σ–5σ sampling (p. 6) needs rewording (R1).
