## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a rank-one model editing method to correct unreliable behavior (neural trojans, spurious correlations) by aligning predictions on corrupted inputs with those on clean samples. It uses attribution-based layer localization (Integrated Gradients) to identify the primary layer responsible for misbehavior and integrates a dynamic editing process (iterative editing with thresholds) to adjust model behavior. Experiments on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, and ISIC show the method reduces attack success rate (ASR) to near zero and removes spurious feature bias without sacrificing overall accuracy, often with as few as a single clean-corrupted sample pair. Reviewers generally agree results are strong and the direction is promising, but express significant concerns about clarity, novelty over prior editing work (ROME, Santurkar et al.), limited scope of evaluated spurious correlations (fixed patches only), and theoretical justification for why rank-one updates target unreliable behavior. One reviewer (score 3) is substantially more negative, finding the presentation confusing and the method insufficiently distinguished from existing approaches.

### Strengths

- The problem is clearly defined, relevant, and the paper is well-written (Reviewer 1). The method shows compelling results against all baselines, achieving the editing objective effectively (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- Attack success rate (ASR) for backdoor attacks is dropped to nearly zero without compromising overall accuracy; bias toward spurious features is removed without accuracy loss (Reviewer 3).
- The approach addresses issues of performance degradation and data volume constraints by retaining both original and corrupted samples (Reviewer 2).
- Algorithm 1 cleverly uses attribution-based dynamic editing with appropriate thresholds for δ and ε (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments consider critical issues (backdoor attacks, spurious correlations) and include a realistic ISIC dataset, showcasing effectiveness in a more practical setting (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The result is potentially important for mechanistic interpretability, demonstrating editing techniques applied to a second modality (image classifiers) and alleviating existing concerns (Reviewer 3).
- The research direction is promising; the suite of editing tools has not been fully exploited for domain adaptation (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is hard to read; Section 4 is not well integrated (Reviewer 3). The presentation is confusing and feels promotional rather than a rigorous scientific contribution (Reviewer 2). Paragraph 207–215 seems important but the methodological change to sidestep challenges is unclear and lacks empirical evidence that it is responsible for improved performance (Reviewer 3).
- **Definition of "unreliable behavior"**: The term is used to describe predictions influenced by backdoors or spurious correlations, but a precise definition is needed, especially for why a rank-one update would target these specific issues (Reviewer 2). The connection between the rank-one update and mitigation of these issues is not well-established; theoretical justification is lacking (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Novelty over ROME**: The core key-value optimization (Eq. 1) is not novel; the primary contribution should be Eq. (2) (Reviewer 2). It is unclear how the method differs from ROME beyond (i) applying it to image classifiers, (ii) iterative/dynamic editing, and (iii) including corrupted samples in training (Reviewer 3). The dynamic editing (iterative application) appears to only marginally improve over static editing and repeated editing has been explored before (e.g., [1]); the paper does not discuss how dynamic layer selection compares to existing repeated-editing approaches (Reviewer 4).
- **Scope of spurious correlations**: The evaluated spurious patterns are fixed, consistent patches (identical to the Trojan triggers), which is unrealistic for real-world spurious correlations (e.g., backgrounds, lighting, pose) (Reviewers 3, 4). Established benchmarks like Waterbirds are not studied; even the ISIC experiment uses relatively consistent patches, limiting applicability to more diverse spurious features (Reviewer 3).
- **Practical applicability and assumptions**: The method relies on identifying unreliable behaviors (requires a clear signal of misbehavior) and requires paired corrupted and cleansed samples. Obtaining a perfectly cleansed sample is challenging in practice, especially for complex data modalities or sophisticated attacks (Reviewer 1). When the original training dataset or clean samples are unavailable, editing is unclear (Reviewer 2). The method may be limited to simpler architectures; attribution may struggle with complex computational graphs involving many skip connections, group norm, or layer norm (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical and technical concerns**: Eq. (2) requires gradient disagreement for estimating function disagreement and a closed-form representation of f, which is not available for deep networks; how this is handled in practice is not addressed (Reviewer 2). It is unclear how a feature mapping function is defined for each layer; relying solely on the loss in the final iteration may be insufficient (Reviewer 2). Updating x̂ using x as a teacher lacks justification of trustworthiness and step sizes; could cause instability if low-confidence (Reviewer 2).
- **Challenges motivating the method are poorly described and not empirically demonstrated**: Challenge 1 (k* not included in the statistics matrix C) is stated as fundamental to domain adaptation but never explained why it is specific to that setting; Challenge 2 (lack of data in domain adaptation) could be described more simply. No empirical evidence is given that without addressing these challenges, rank-one editing fails or performance degrades (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient evaluation against stronger attacks**: The paper does not quantify the severity of the poisoning attacks considered or compare them to other attack types; it does not address whether adversarial attacks could circumvent the correction mechanism (Reviewer 1).
- **Minor presentation issues**: Figure 3 appears to have errors (yellow arrow pointing wrong way, key v* vs. k*) (Reviewer 3). The paper contains contradictory statements (L43 vs. the text about two challenges) (Reviewer 3). A suggestion: Table 4 should highlight accuracy on samples without the spurious correlation to better show the cost of relying on spurious features (Reviewer 3).
