## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ElasticTok, a method for adaptive image/video tokenization that encodes visual data into variable-length tokens. During training, a random number of tokens is dropped from the end of each frame's encoding. During inference, a search (binary or exhaustive) finds the maximum number of tokens to drop while meeting a reconstruction loss threshold. For long videos, a block processing mechanism (stack of 4 frames per block) is used with independent masking per block. Experiments analyze reconstruction quality and VLM task performance at different token counts, demonstrating up to 2-5x fewer tokens for long videos. Code is shared.

### Strengths
- The problem of adaptive tokenization for images and videos is interesting and crucial for long-context tasks. The application to auto-encoding of images and videos is novel, and related work on adaptive representations and compression is well covered (R1).
- The method provides an effective way to compress the number of tokens adaptively. The length of used tokens aligns with human intuition: more complex images use more tokens (R2).
- Empirical results demonstrate effective representation of long videos with up to 2-5x fewer tokens (R2).
- Exploiting temporal coherence to learn a more compact video representation is well motivated. Figure 4 shows the dropout strategy can reduce tokens as intended (R3).
- The idea of adaptive token lengths is valid, especially for generating/analyzing longer sequences. The paper is well written and easy to follow; code is shared; experiments are reasonable and evaluate clear aspects (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Writing and clarity issues (R1, with disagreement from R4 who found the paper well written).** Specific problems: (i) A background section on Ring attention should be the first paragraph of the method since Ring attention is a major component; details of the autoregressive video model are missing. (ii) Algorithm 1 and 2 define variable "x" (output of PatchifyRearrange) shape B×L×D, but L is not defined; from the next line it seems "x" should be B×(L/B)×D; all variables must be clearly defined. (iii) Captions in Tables 1 and 2 do not state which dataset (image vs. video) is used. These issues need to be fixed.
- **Naive left-aligned masking strategy (R1, R4).** The method always drops the leftmost tokens, assuming the most important information is at the beginning. This is unlikely true for complex visual data where crucial details may be distributed throughout the sequence. The fixed section is always masked regardless of content complexity. The method lacks a mechanism to identify and prioritize the most informative tokens. Visualization of what the leftmost tokens capture, and details about position encodings, would help address concerns about efficacy.
- **Computational cost of adaptive selection at inference (R1, R3, R4).** The current adaptive selection strategies (binary search, exhaustive search to match a threshold) are computationally expensive. The encoder must be run repeatedly for each sampled mask at test time, which is inefficient and does not scale well for video. The paper does not sufficiently analyze or compare this overhead against fixed-length encoding with a similar computational budget. For the neural regression predictor (Table 4), the evaluation is limited: it only reports the error rate in predicting the optimal token count, but not reconstruction accuracy, total inference time/FLOPs, or the impact of regressor errors on downstream tasks. The computational overhead of the regressor itself is not quantified against the savings from reduced token processing. A direct comparison of total cost (search + reduced decoding) is missing.
- **Missing comparisons with other token compression methods (R1, R2).** The paper should benchmark against state-of-the-art learned compression techniques (e.g., vector quantization, latent space compression), not just fixed-length tokenizers. Additionally, a baseline using a single embedding (Matryoshka-style masking directly on the encoded output) should be compared, as it would be more efficient than re-running the encoder for each mask.
- **Loss of reconstruction details (R2).** Demos show noticeable quality loss (e.g., the loading icon on a cellphone completely disappeared/distorted), suggesting high-frequency information is discarded. A rigorous analysis of the types of details lost and under what conditions is needed. The paper should report PSNR/SSIM vs. compression ratio, and investigate how quality degradation correlates with the number of tokens dropped (provide curve/table). The impact on downstream task performance should also be assessed.
- **Inefficient training masking strategy (R1).** Independent per-block mask sampling during video training leads to a combinatorially large space, with many useless combinations (e.g., strong masking on the first frame but no masking on the next when there is no motion). A next-frame masking strategy with better inductive biases would be more efficient for training larger models.
- **Missing details on neural regression predictor (R3).** It is unclear how the regressor is fine-tuned: does it see the entire video context, and does the regression loss update the encoder weights? The paper should also include a heuristic baseline (e.g., using motion as a proxy for token count). A citation to "Adaptive Computation Time for Recurrent Neural Networks" (Graves, 2016) is recommended.
- **Video dataset details (R1).** More information about the video dataset is needed, and whether it will be released to support reproduction.
- **Results only from continuous model (R1).** Ablations or results using the quantized FSQ model are missing.
- **Unclear experimental specifics (R1, R4).** For Figure 4, it is unclear whether exhaustive search or another method was used to determine the ideal token length for each input. For Table 3, the baseline is trained with 50% of tokens and the method with varying numbers; how is inference done in both cases – do they both use 50% of tokens?
- **Limited demonstration of practical benefits (R2).** The paper does not convincingly show applications that clearly benefit from elastic token compression over other compression methods.
