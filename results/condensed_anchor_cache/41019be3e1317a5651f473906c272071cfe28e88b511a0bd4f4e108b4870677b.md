## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Omission, a weighted Bernoulli sampling method for dynamic data pruning that avoids repeated samples while achieving minimal variance of the gradient estimate. Practical techniques (exponential moving average, score smoothing, normalization) are added for robust implementation. The method is evaluated on image classification (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100) and LLM instruction finetuning, with an ablation study. Two reviewers rated the paper 6 (confidence 2 and 4), one rated 5 (confidence 4). While the theoretical motivation is appreciated, one reviewer finds the central idea not novel and the notation/justification unclear.

### Strengths
- The proposition of a dynamic data pruning method that yields an unbiased gradient estimate of minimal variance while avoiding repeated samples is a valuable theoretical contribution.
- Practical techniques (score smoothing, score normalization, estimation from historical statistics) are employed for efficient and robust implementation.
- Extensive empirical study on image classification tasks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-100) and LLM fine‑tuning; Omission performs competitively against related algorithms. An ablation study investigates the importance of the practical techniques.
- Overall, the paper is well written and easy to follow the main idea.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior work:** One reviewer (R3) finds the paper lacks novelty and originality. The optimal sampling distribution has been derived more rigorously in earlier work (Ting & Brochu, 2018; Wang & Zou, 2021) and subsequent developments. This contrasts with other reviewers who considered the theoretical contribution valuable.
- **Practicality of computing ||g_i||:** Theorem 1 and 2 require ||g_i|| for all i; the proposed solution (using historical statistics) is not convincingly justified. Even if addressed, the approach may remain unrealistic.
- **Clarity of notation and exposition:**
  - Notations are poorly defined; relationships among n, B, b are unclear, as is how batch variance relates to empirical loss.
  - Section 3.2.1 (Exponential Moving Average of Gradient Norm Squared) is not clearly written; its value and motivation are hard to understand.
  - Section 3.2 focuses only on logit loss without proper context, confusing non‑experts.
- **Incorrect claim:** The statement “in practice we can keep sampling until a fixed batch size is reached” is scientifically incorrect – it changes the selection distribution and invalidates all theoretical results.
- **Limited trials and statistical significance:** Empirical results are reported over only four trials; performance gains of Omission are not always significantly large relative to error bars. It is unclear whether gains would remain stable with more trials.
- **Missing experimental details:**
  - Table 4 does not provide full training (no‑pruning) performance, making it hard to judge relative benefit.
  - The pruning ratio used in the experiments of Table 4 is not stated.
  - The term “Prune Ratio” is not defined.
- **Presentation issues:**
  - It is unclear what the numbers in the tables mean (likely test accuracy, but not explicitly stated).
  - The figures are not distinguishable in grayscale.
  - The order of tables (Table 1 and Table 5 discussed before others) is confusing; why not move Table 5 to be Table 2?
  - The meaning of “drop_last=True” (a software argument?) is not explained.
- **Reviewer disagreement:** While two reviewers rated the paper 6 and found it well‑written and theoretically sound, the third reviewer (R3) gave a rating of 5, citing lack of novelty, serious notation problems, and the incorrect batch‑size claim.
