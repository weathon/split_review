## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a framework for evaluating sparse autoencoders (SAEs) by comparing them against supervised feature dictionaries on a specific task (indirect object identification, IOI, in GPT-2 Small). Supervised dictionaries are constructed using known attributes (IO, S, Pos) and evaluated on approximation, control, and interpretability. The same metrics are then applied to unsupervised SAEs (task-specific and full-distribution SAEs, including Gated SAE and Top-K SAE). The authors find that SAEs capture interpretable features for IOI but are less successful at controlling the model than supervised features, and observe feature occlusion and over-splitting. All reviewers note the study is thorough but limited to a single task and model.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- The problem of evaluating interpretability methods without ground truth is highly relevant and interesting (Reviewer 3).
- The experiments are well-defined, intuitive, and easy to understand (Reviewer 3). Metrics of sufficiency, necessity, and control are used (Reviewer 1).
- The study is thorough on the IOI case, with extensive results in the appendix (Reviewer 4).
- The proposed necessity, sufficiency, and control methods are potentially generalizable to other settings (Reviewer 4).
- The paper addresses small nuances in SAE evaluation, such as the discussion in Section 4.2 and the “Preventing…” paragraph in 4.3 (Reviewer 4).
- Results are interesting and useful: task-specific SAEs outperform full-distribution SAEs, highlighting the importance of data quality and that human priors may be beneficial for SAE training (Reviewer 3).
- Mathematics are well explained and formulas aid understanding (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

- **Single-task and single-model limitation.** The evaluation focuses only on the IOI task with GPT-2 Small. It is unclear whether the constructed dictionaries, methodology, and findings generalize to other tasks, semantic domains, or model architectures (Reviewers 1,3,4). Reviewer 4 notes this is a stepping stone but presenter also states the case study is restrictive. Reviewer 3 calls this a major concern limiting practical utility; Reviewer 1 says empirical validation is scarce.
- **Manual effort and scalability.** The supervised dictionaries rely on predefined attributes (IO, S, Pos) that were identified by prior work. The manual effort to construct such ground-truth baselines for new tasks is significant, and the paper does not discuss how to choose attributes without another interpretability method. This limits scalability to new models and tasks (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation and readability issues.**
    - Crucial formulas and methods are in the appendix rather than the main text—e.g., the necessity formula (Appendix 7.4, line 878) (Reviewer 4). The probing accuracy section seems out of place and lacks results (Reviewer 4).
    - Interpretability sections (Section 3.2, Figure 5, Section 4.4) are described as exploratory and qualitative; Reviewer 4 suggests moving them to the appendix for coherence.
    - Some parts are hard to read, e.g., the title under Figure 1 and the interpretability paragraph in Section 3.2 (Reviewer 1). There are broken references in the appendix (line 883, 935, 1849) (Reviewer 4). Typos: missing parenthesis at line 434, “objective 4” should be “Equation 4” (Reviewer 4).
    - Reviewer 2 notes the English language should be improved.
- **Lack of novelty and weak motivation.** Reviewer 2 states the main idea is not very novel and the motivation should be enhanced. The paper reads more as an exploration of whether SAEs can recover the IOI task than a proposal of a general evaluation framework (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient experiments and analysis.** Reviewer 2 requests more results and analysis. Reviewer 1 notes lack of investigation into hyperparameter sensitivity and training data size. The discussion and limitations section (Section 6) is too short (Reviewer 4).
- **Specific methodological concerns.**
    - **Sufficiency metric (Figure 3, Left).** The ratio of average logit difference may hide per-example changes. Showing a distribution or absolute changes would be more informative (Reviewer 4).
    - **Necessity metric.** The main text shows only the difference between the average-plus-error and the average, not the direct difference between the reconstruction and the average. The crucial necessity formula is relegated to the appendix (Reviewer 4).
    - **Interpretability section (4.4).** The section lacks concrete interpretations and visualizations of SAE latents. Appendix 7.13-7.14 give possible interpretations but not actual labels; it is difficult to assess practical interpretability gains (Reviewer 3).
    - **Control / probing accuracy.** The probing accuracy section seems out of place because no results are shown. A linear probe may itself disentangle attributes, making the evaluation questionable (Reviewer 4).
    - **Equation 2 bias term.** The bias is defined as \(E[a(p)]\); clarifying whether this is by design and its implications would help (Reviewer 4).
    - **F1 score definition (Section 4).** The method for determining which binary attribute “A” corresponds to each latent “f” is not clearly explained. Examples of F and A would be useful (Reviewer 4).
    - **Search for counterfactual latents (Section 4.3).** The process of optimizing to find the SAE latent that performs the task seems roundabout and assumes knowledge of the relevant features. Actual labeling of SAE features before usage would be more realistic (Reviewer 3).
- **Related work placement.** The related work paragraph is large and uses much terminology; moving it earlier would help readers position the framework (Reviewer 1).
