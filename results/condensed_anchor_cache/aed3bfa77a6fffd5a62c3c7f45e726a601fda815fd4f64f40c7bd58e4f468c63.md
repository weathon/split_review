## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces RouteLLM, a framework for training routers that dynamically select between a stronger and weaker LLM during inference to optimize cost-performance tradeoffs. The routers are trained using human preference data from Chatbot Arena and data augmentation. Experiments on MMLU, MT Bench, and GSM8K demonstrate over 2x cost savings with minimal quality loss, and the routers generalize to unseen LLM pairs and domains without retraining. One reviewer (score 8) found the approach novel and comprehensive; two others (scores 6, 5) raised concerns about novelty, baselines, and analysis depth.

### Strengths
- **Novel direct use of human preference data**: The framework trains routers using human preference data (from Chatbot Arena) rather than reward models, which is a fresh approach to the routing problem.
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Experiments cover multiple architectures (e.g., BERT-style, causal LLMs, matrix factorization), strong/weak model pairs (e.g., GPT-4 vs Mixtral-8x7B, Llama-3-8B vs Llama-3-70B), and common benchmarks (MMLU, MT Bench, GSM8K, other test sets).
- **Clear real-world metrics**: Metrics like “average performance gap recovered” and “call-performance threshold” reflect practical cost-quality tradeoffs. The introduced APGR (Eq. 7) quantifies performance relative to cost.
- **Significant cost savings**: RouteLLM achieves over 2x cost savings on MMLU, MT Bench, and GSM8K with minimal impact on response quality. On MT Bench, it achieves performance comparable to GPT-4 with a cost saving of ~3.7x.
- **Generalization and adaptability**: Routers generalize to unseen data, unseen LLM pairs, and new domains without retraining, as demonstrated across multiple test-time model substitutions. Diverse win prediction methods (e.g., SW ranking, matrix factorization, BERT, causal LLM) are implemented and compared.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and weak baselines (raised by Reviewer 2, score 6)**: Training routers to harness different LLM strengths is widely studied [1–4]; generalization to OOD data [5] and using LLMs to generate training data for routing [6] are not compared. Only a random router baseline is used, ignoring more advanced prior methods [1–6]. The SW ranking similarity to prior work [7] is noted.
- **Incomplete overhead analysis (Reviewer 2)**: SW ranking is expensive ($37 per 1M requests, 2.9 requests/sec) and slow, hampering practical use. The cost of embedding extraction (required for SW ranking and matrix factorization) is not included. Model size ratios are inconsistent: BERT-base (110M) vs causal LLM (8B) is ~1%, but BERT-base’s cost overhead is ~60% of the causal LLM and its throughput only 60% higher (Table 7); more estimation details are needed.
- **Narrow routing scope (Reviewer 1, score 8)**: The binary routing focuses on “strong vs. weak” models, but does not explore other binary differences such as specialization in coding vs. writing, or different training data distributions. This limits real-world applicability and raises the question of how well the router would separate models close in ability (e.g., two 7B models) or models with different specializations rather than a strict strength ordering (Reviewer 1’s questions).
- **Missing analysis of routing decisions and parameter guidance (Reviewer 3, score 5)**: There is no detailed breakdown of which query types (e.g., math, reasoning, creative) are routed to strong vs. weak models under different α cost thresholds (Sec. 5.1). This makes it unclear how to set α for specific use cases. Guidance on selecting the best win prediction method across scenarios is also lacking.
- **Robustness for ambiguous queries not explored (Reviewer 3)**: The router’s decision-making for queries where strong and weak models may perform similarly is not analyzed, leaving a gap in understanding its reliability.
- **Dependence on expensive data augmentation (Reviewer 3)**: Performance still heavily relies on data augmentation using high-cost LLM-judged labels, raising questions about scalability and practicality.
- **Unexplained architecture performance differences (Reviewer 1)**: The paper does not discuss why the causal LLM architecture benefits so much from data augmentation while other architectures do not, nor the underlying mechanisms. This limits understanding of how to apply the approach to new scenarios.
