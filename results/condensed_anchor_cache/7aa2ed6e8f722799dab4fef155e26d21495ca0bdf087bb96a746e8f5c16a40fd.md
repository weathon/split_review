## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) as an alternative to MLPs, inspired by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem. KANs replace fixed node activations with learnable univariate functions on edges, parametrized as B‑splines. The representation is generalized from two layers to deeper stacks. The paper provides an approximation theorem, training techniques for interpretability (pruning, symbolification, grid extension), and experiments on small‑scale science tasks: knot theory, Anderson localization, symbolic regression, and a 2D Poisson PDE. Reviewers generally praise the originality, clear writing, and theoretical motivation, but raise major concerns about experimental comparisons (weak MLP baselines, missing state‑of‑the‑art comparisons in symbolic regression and PDE solving), theoretical assumptions (smoothness of inner functions, unknown constant dependence), lack of larger‑scale validation, insufficient reproducibility details (no provided code), and overclaimed statements. Lower‑scoring reviewers (6) find the experiments insufficient and the claims overstated, while higher‑scoring reviewers (8) are more positive but share many of the same criticisms.

### Strengths

- The paper presents an original and well‑motivated neural architecture inspired by the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem, creatively combining KST with modern deep learning and generalizing the original two‑layer representation to multiple layers. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- The architecture is clearly explained; the paper is well‑structured, well‑written, and easy to follow, with helpful figures illustrating differences from MLPs. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- Comprehensive coverage: theoretical foundation (approximation theorem), detailed architectural design, implementation details and tricks, training for interpretability (pruning, symbolification, grid extension). (R2, R3, R4)
- The approach renews attention to the KAT for practical machine learning and addresses why earlier attempts failed. (R1, R2, R4)
- Clearly states the “comfort zone” and limitations of KANs, and points to future directions. (R2)
- Interesting conceptual contributions, such as the view of external vs. internal degrees of freedom. (R2)
- Extensive numerical evaluations across diverse domains (toy datasets, special functions, Feynman datasets, knot theory, Anderson localization, PDEs) demonstrate potential in small‑scale scientific discovery. (R1, R3)
- The emphasis on interpretability and the ability to extract symbolic formulas is particularly relevant for scientific applications where understanding learned mechanisms is crucial. (R5)
- Theoretical and empirical evidence of favorable scaling laws compared to MLPs is promising. (R3)
- The work has already generated considerable interest in the community, opening new research directions. (R3)

### Weaknesses

- **Experimental comparisons are inadequate and potentially unfair.** (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
  - The paper primarily compares KANs against basic MLPs with small widths (e.g., 5 or 20), full‑batch L‑BFGS optimization, and Glorot initialization — which are not standard or best practices for MLPs. KANs may have many more parameters/flops due to fine spline grids (up to 1000). (R3, R4)
  - Comparisons to state‑of‑the‑art methods are missing: no symbolic regression baselines like PySR (Cranmer, 2023); no advanced PINN frameworks like PirateNets for PDE solving; no comparisons with other interpretable models (e.g., Neural Additive Models). (R1, R3, R4, R5)
  - Claims of superiority are overstated given the weak baselines and lack of fair, comprehensive evaluation. (R3, R4, R5)
  - Lower‑scoring reviewers (R3, R4) consider the experimental evaluation insufficient; higher‑scoring reviewers (R1, R2, R5) also note these weaknesses but are more optimistic.

- **Theoretical concerns about smoothness and the approximation theorem.** (R1, R2, R4, R5)
  - Theorem 2.1 assumes inner functions are \((k+1)\)-times continuously differentiable, but the Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem only guarantees continuity; these functions can be highly non‑smooth. The argument that deeper/wider representations may avoid this lacks theoretical or empirical support. (R1, R4)
  - The bound in Theorem 2.1 depends on an unknown constant \(C\) that implicitly depends on dimension; the paper provides no analysis of this dependence, and the claim of “beating the curse of dimensionality” with finite grid size lacks strong evidence. (R1, R5)
  - The proof of Theorem 2.1 is unclear: the residual error bound in line 894 appears to ignore the accumulation of approximation errors through layers (the bound for exact vs. approximate inputs is the same). The authors should clarify how the residual bound is obtained and whether constants are identical in the theorem and proof. (R1)
  - The theorem assumes the target function’s structure (number of layers \(L\)) is known, which is unrealistic; its applicability when depth is unknown is not discussed. (R2)

- **Interpretability claims need better evidence and baselines.** (R1, R3, R5)
  - The symbolic regression protocol (Appendix E.2, Figure 10) is heuristic and sensitive to hyperparameters; no automatic metric for symbolic recovery accuracy is reported. (R1, R3)
  - The paper does not compare KAN‑based symbolic discovery against state‑of‑the‑art symbolic regression methods (e.g., PySR). The arguments in the appendix against those methods are not convincing. (R3)
  - The mechanism for automatically converting learned splines into symbolic functions is not adequately explained. (R1)
  - The sparsity loss (Appendix E) lacks an ablation study. Without it, a layer might learn multiple identical functions (e.g., three \(x^2/3\) instead of one \(x^2\)), hindering interpretability. (R2)

- **Hyperparameter selection and reproducibility details are missing.** (R2, R3, R4, R5)
  - No systematic methodology for selecting the number of layers or nodes per layer; it is unclear whether prior knowledge is needed or whether pruning/grid search is used. A step‑by‑step description for experiments is missing. (R2)
  - Important training details are omitted: data normalization (inputs appear to require [0,1] for KANs; are MLPs normalized identically?), batch size, learning rate, total number of training points, domain of functions, architectures for continual learning. (R3, R4)
  - No code was provided with the submission. (R3, R4)
  - The influence of hyperparameters like grid size \(G\) and spline order \(k\) is not explored; no guidance on automatic selection. (R5)

- **Computational cost is understated and hidden in the appendix.** (R5)
  - KAN training is slower than MLPs by roughly a factor of 10 and cannot leverage batch computation as efficiently. These practical limitations are only mentioned in the appendix and should be discussed prominently in the main text, along with analysis of memory usage and FLOPs. (R5)
  - The computational complexity of grid extension and grid refinement is not analyzed. The update of spline grids (p. 16) is a critical step but is not adequately explained. (R3, R4)

- **Larger‑scale validation is absent.** (R4, R5)
  - All experiments are small‑scale (e.g., simple closed‑form functions, Poisson equation). More challenging benchmarks are needed, such as regression on PDE solutions (PDEBench, PDEArena), image regression, or even MNIST, to assess scalability. (R4, R5)

- **Continual learning example is too trivial.** (R3, R4, R5)
  - The toy example in Appendix J is clearly constructed to favor KANs’ local B‑splines; it does not demonstrate practical advantages. More realistic continual learning settings should be tested, or the claim removed. (R5) A fair comparison would use MLPs with compactly supported activations (e.g., RBF). (R3)

- **Page limit and appendix organization.** (R5)
  - Important sections (Related Works, Discussion) are moved to the appendix to stay within 9 pages, which is not a fair use of the appendix. (R5)
  - The appendix is very long (>25 pages); the LAN section (Appendix P) feels like a separate paper and may not be necessary. (R3, R5)

- **Minor issues.** (R1, R4, R5)
  - Typographical errors: “Interpretabe” → “interpretable” (line 16), “KAT” → “KAN” (line 205), “pareton” → “pareto” (line 473), “altnertives” (abstract), “staricase-like”, “maximu”. (R1, R5)
  - Some language is overly dramatic (e.g., “sentenced to death”). (R5)
  - The analogy of computations on “edges” vs. “nodes” is not compelling; KANs and MLPs are more similar than the paper suggests (e.g., B‑splines can be seen as additional input features like Fourier features). (R4, R5)
  - Figure 2 lists six examples but only shows three. (R4)
  - Figure 7 plots the squared error rather than the error itself. (R4)
