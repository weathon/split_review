## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CAIAC, a counterfactual data augmentation method for offline RL that generates synthetic transitions by swapping causally "action-unaffected" state dimensions between independent trajectories. It uses the Causal Action Influence (CAI) metric to identify these dimensions. Each reviewer finds the idea interesting and the paper well-written, but all raise concerns about limited novelty relative to CoDA (Pitis et al., 2020) and incremental contribution. Reviewers 1 and 2 give scores of 5, Review 3 gives a score of 3 and is more critical of the underlying assumptions and missing baselines.

### Strengths

- The idea of using local causal independence for counterfactual data augmentation is neat and interesting, as it can break spurious correlations in offline data.
- The paper is well-motivated and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3). The formulation of local causal models is clear.
- CAIAC can generate feasible synthetic transitions from a fixed dataset without requiring new environment interactions.
- The proposed approach is independent and can be used with any learning algorithm.
- Experiments on offline self-supervised skill learning and offline RL (Franka Kitchen, FetchPush) show effectiveness to some extent (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**: The method draws heavily on Seitzer et al. (2021) for CAI influence measurement and on CoDA (Pitis et al., 2020) for counterfactual generation via component swapping. If CAIAC is essentially CoDA with a different metric for identifying independent dimensions, the algorithmic contribution is incremental (R1, R2, R3). A figure clearly illustrating the difference between CAIAC, CoDA, and CODA-ACTION would help (R2). The paper does not evaluate on a didactic toy task (e.g., SpriteWorld from CoDA) to isolate the benefit (R2).
- **Empirical results are weak**: In Table 1, CAIAC outperforms baselines with obvious significance in only 3/6 Franka Kitchen tasks (Kettle, Microwave, Bottom-burner) and struggles in the remaining 3 (Slide Cabinet, Light Switch, Hinge Cabinet) (R2). The failure-case analysis for the last three tasks is missing; the method should generally work given the spurious correlation design, so explanations are needed (R3). In the FetchPush goal-conditioned task (Section 5.2), CAIAC appears the worst among all methods, and the gap is not due to randomness – this may relate to the assumption that swapped states are irrelevant to the goal (R3).
- **Assumptions may limit applicability**:
  - _Goal relevance assumption_: Swapping states deemed action-independent assumes they are irrelevant to the goal. This fails in long-horizon tasks where such states (e.g., object position not directly affected by current action) are critical preconditions for later subtasks (R3).
  - _Fixed state factorization_: The method requires a predefined factorization of the state space into independent entities. This is often unavailable in real-world scenarios with raw sensor data, limiting applicability (R3).
- **Missing empirical validation of claims**: The paper claims that CoDA and CODA-ACTION are unable to recover the correct causal graph and create dynamically infeasible data, but provides no empirical evidence. One could validate this by simulating augmented transitions to check feasibility (R2).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**: Only CoDA and CoDA-ACTION are compared. Additional baselines from causal RL (e.g., [1, 2, 4, 7, 9] cited by R3) and related work (MoCoDA [1], GuDA [2] suggested by R2) are needed for fair comparison (R2, R3).
- **Incomplete analysis of scalability and data regimes**: The paper only partially addresses performance in high-dimensional, low-data regime environments. A more thorough exploration of computational demands and scalability is needed (R1). Performance in abundant data regimes is also unclear (R1).
- **Parameter sensitivity**: The threshold θ is set differently per task (θ=0.1 for FetchPush, θ=0.3 for Franka Kitchen), but no guidance on how to select θ is provided (R3).
- **Unsupported statement**: The claim that "the transformer model is able to discover the causal graph and creates realistic counterfactuals" (Section 5.2) is not supported by evidence (R3).
- **Missing related literature**: The paper omits important references on causal reinforcement learning [1–11] (R3).
- **Unclear figure**: Figure 1 does not clearly illustrate what is being swapped and what augmented data is generated; the caption should explicitly state this (R2). Conversely, Figure 6 is helpful and should be moved to the main paper (R2).
