## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Collab, a mixture-of-agents decoding strategy for inference-time LLM alignment without retraining. It dynamically selects, at each token, one policy from a pool of off-the-shelf aligned LLMs based on a long-term utility metric derived from an implicit Q-function. Theoretical analysis establishes optimal performance with respect to a target reward. Empirical evaluations on two datasets (Berkeley Nectar and HH-RLHF) across seven setups show that Collab outperforms single-agent decoding baselines, achieving up to 1.56× improvement in average reward and 71.89% GPT-4 win-tie rate. Reviewers’ scores are 8, 6, 6, 5 (accept), with substantial disagreement on clarity, experimental rigor, and practical relevance.

### Strengths
- **Parameter efficiency**: No need to update billions of parameters as in RLHF; alignment is achieved at inference time (R1, R2).
- **Use of specialized models**: Can leverage existing domain-expert LLMs without requiring their reward models or policy parameters (R1). The problem of ensembling LLMs at inference is timely and important (R3).
- **Dynamic merging without retraining**: Unlike prior mixture-of-experts methods that require tuning or explicit expert demonstrations, Collab dynamically selects models per token (R1).
- **Theoretical justification**: Sound theoretical analysis (R1, R3 nice to have). Novel routing via Q-function (R4).
- **Simple, intuitive method** (R3).
- **Empirical performance**: Strong results that outperform baselines (R4). Diverse evaluation across seven setups (R1—but only two datasets noted by R1).
- **Practical applicability**: Useful when reward models or policy parameters are unavailable (e.g., industry settings) (R1).

One reviewer (R1, score 8) was highly positive about the theoretical soundness, writing, and practical relevance. Another reviewer (R4, score 5) found the core idea novel and results strong, but was far more critical of the presentation and experimental clarity.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing experimental details**: Training of the Q-function is not described, yet it is a crucial component. Experiment descriptions (models, rewards, setup) are only in the appendix, and the choice of experiments is not justified (R4). How were agents initialized? Were they explicitly trained for alignment? (R2).
- **Algorithm inconsistency**: The method is presented as an extension of Controlled Decoding (CD), but Equation 5 does not include the reference policy probability, unlike CD’s Equation 3. Also, Algorithm 1 mentions top-p while Line 428 mentions top-K (R4).
- **Insufficient alignment baselines**: Missing comparisons with standard alignment algorithms such as DPO and PPO (R2). The cost of Q-function training vs. DPO/PPO/RLHF is unclear (R2).
- **Unclear why multiple agents are needed**: Is the implicit Q-function sufficient to guide a single policy directly, or is the multi-agent collaboration essential? (R2). Why can Collab correctly answer a question when neither Agent-1 nor Agent-2 can? Does it acquire abilities none of the agents have? (R3).
- **Choice of reference policy**: The objective’s KL term depends on a reference policy whose selection is not justified. The sub-optimality bound crucially relies on the reference policy being close to both the optimal policy and the target reward (R1).
- **Lack of computational analysis**: The method likely requires O(N²) LLM queries per generated sequence (inference of all policies for each token). No wall-time or computational cost comparison with baselines like Best-of-N sampling or single-agent decoding (R1, R3). Reviewer 1 wanted compute analysis; Reviewer 3 explicitly flagged O(N²) concern.
- **Evaluation on limited, generic tasks**: The experiments are on only two datasets (Berkeley Nectar, HH-RLHF) and do not test on complex objective tasks (reasoning, coding, math) that could better demonstrate domain-expertise benefits and avoid reward model / GPT-4 bias (R1, R2, R3). No per-subtask analysis (e.g., harmlessness vs. helpfulness) to show what the method improves (R3). One reviewer (R4) argued that metrics like win rate, diversity, and coherence are not clearly linked to the reward-maximization motivation; evidence that the target reward correlates with win rate is missing.
- **No human evaluation**: GPT-4 evaluation does not automatically imply alignment with human preferences; human studies are needed to support alignment claims (R1).
- **Unclear SoTA reference**: The paper calls its baseline “SoTA decoding strategy” without naming it (Transfer Q*? Controlled Decoding?) (R3).
- **Missing connection to hierarchical RL**: The method is closely related to hierarchical RL (learning a policy to choose among sub-policies), but this connection is not discussed (R4).
- **Minor presentation issues**: Line 97-99 appears repeated (R1). The paper is well-written per R1, but R4 found it hard to read and lacking in clarity.

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* R1 (score 8) considered the paper well-written, the evaluations diverse, and the theoretical justification sound. R4 (score 5) found the paper hard to read, the experimental details missing, and the metrics misaligned with the motivation. R2 and R3 (both 6) were generally positive but called for stronger baselines, more diverse tasks, and clarity on compute cost.
