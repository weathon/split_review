## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Causal Modelling Agent (CMA), a framework that synergizes Large Language Models (LLMs) for metadata-driven reasoning with Deep Structural Causal Models (DSCMs) for data-driven modeling in causal discovery. CMA iteratively updates a causal graph by querying an LLM for edge modifications and fitting a DSCM constrained to the current graph. Evaluations on benchmark datasets (including Kıcıman et al. (2023) benchmarks) and a real-world case study on Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) biomarkers show that CMA can outperform previous data-driven and metadata-driven approaches. New insights into causal relationships among AD biomarkers are obtained.

### Strengths
- The combination of LLM and SCM into a unified framework is novel and interesting.
- The model architecture (iterative LLM queries + deep structural causal model fitting) is convincing.
- Encoding intermediate results in a JSON format and feeding them to the LLM is a clever design choice.
- The paper is well-structured and clearly written.
- Experimental results on both synthetic benchmarks and real-world tasks (AD, Arctic sea ice, Sangiovese wine) are encouraging and show promise.
- New, previously unknown causal relationships among Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers have been derived.

### Weaknesses
- **Technical contribution and novelty concerns (minority reviewer, score 3):** One reviewer finds it hard to assess the technical contribution, especially compared to similar concurrent works (Long et al., 2023; Ban et al., 2023). The other reviewers (scores 8,8,6) consider the approach original and valuable.
- **LLM behavior and sensitivity:**
  - It is unclear how the LLM uses memory of previous iterations. Does the LLM always update edges to maximize data fitting? If fitting becomes worse after an update, does the LLM “regret” its decision? How does the learning trajectory compare to standard local-search-based causal discovery?
  - The stochastic nature of the LLM is ignored; the same question may yield different answers.
  - The sensitivity/robustness of CMA to perturbations of input distributions, especially in the presence of weak causal links, is not analyzed. The generalization performance under distribution shift is unclear.
- **Data usage and evaluation:**
  - The data is used only to fit the graph constrained by the LLM output; other information (e.g., conditional independence tests) is not exploited.
  - Due to class imbalance (no edges far outnumber edges), accuracy can be misleading. Additional metrics such as TPR and FDR should be reported.
  - In Section 4.1, average data likelihood is reported without reference values to judge whether values are good.
  - The threshold for DAG-GNN in Table 1 is not explained (how selected).
- **Discrepancies in reported numbers:**
  - The NHD for GPT-4 in Table 2 is 0.35, but Kıcıman et al. (2023) report 0.22 in their Table 7. This discrepancy needs clarification.
- **Missing results and presentation issues:**
  - Table 2 lacks results for TCDF, NOTEARS (Temporal), and NOTEARS (Temporal) on the Alzheimer’s disease and Sangiovese datasets, with no explanation.
  - Table 1: align results to the right for quick magnitude comparison.
  - Table 2: add up/down arrows to indicate whether higher/lower values are better.
- **Definitions and related work:**
  - The term “metadata” is used ambiguously between domain knowledge in the LLM and the JSON-formatted memory passed between iterations; a formal definition is needed.
  - Related work could be strengthened by citing surveys (Li & Zhu, 2022 on deep causal models; Varghese et al., 2013 and Márquez & Yassa, 2019 on AD neuroimaging; Huang et al., 2021 and Kretschmer et al., 2016 on benchmarking) and by discussing the critique in Zečević et al. (2023) regarding LLMs’ causal limitations.
- **Need for further evidence:** For the new causal relationships discovered among AD biomarkers, further support from experimental or observational data would strengthen the contribution.
