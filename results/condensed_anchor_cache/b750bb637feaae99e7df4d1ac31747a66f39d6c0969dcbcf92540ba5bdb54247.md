## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper uses multimodally aligned embeddings (from ImageBind) across visual, auditory, and text domains to build encoding models that predict fMRI responses from subjects watching silent videos. The study generates multi‑event captions, converts them to speech, and then extracts joint text/audio/video features. It compares encoding model performances across single‑modality and multimodal representations, concluding that multimodal alignment improves brain prediction and that the visual system primarily extracts semantic scene descriptions. Scores are 3, 8, and 1 across reviewers; most critical reviews (R1, R3) are negative, while one (R2) is very positive.

### Strengths
- The idea of leveraging multimodal alignment to probe multimodal representation in the human brain is relatively novel and interesting (R1).
- The paper is written clearly (R1).
- The authors provide concise interpretability of model performance by relating output embeddings to specific brain regions, giving good intuition of cognitive responses to stimuli (R2).
- Reviewers acknowledge the importance of studying similarities and differences between modalities (R3), though R3 also notes this is not actually studied in the current paper.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing visualization and methodological clarity**  
  Before reporting correlations (Fig. 2), it would be useful to show actual activations (e.g., PCA or t‑SNE) of high‑dimensional network features and fMRI signals to qualitatively assess their structure and relationship (R1, R3).  
  Basic reporting norms are violated: axes in Fig. 2 are unlabeled, error bars are absent, y‑axis units/scale are missing, and it is unclear how correlation values are computed (across voxels, time points, or trials) (R1, R3).  
  The number of features and amount of training used for each modality are not spelled out; the order in Fig. 2 is ambiguous (does it reflect feature count, dataset size, or model success?) (R1, R3). This makes it hard to interpret whether higher correlations stem from model capacity rather than alignment with brain data (R1).

- **Causality/confound due to correlated features**  
  The features across modalities are highly correlated, so fitting analyses cannot deduce which modality the brain actually represents. Example: if a ball appears in image, text, and audio, language areas may be fit by visual features even though they do not represent visual information, and vice versa. Controlled experiments with rigorously uncorrelated (or orthogonalized) feature dimensions are needed to disentangle contributions (R1, R3).

- **Lack of baselines, ablation, and comparisons**  
  There are no comparisons with different baseline models, different neural network architectures (e.g., other multimodal models besides ImageBind), or ablation studies. This makes it impossible to assess whether the observed correlations are specific to the proposed approach or would replicate with other models (R1, R3). **Disagreement**: one reviewer (R2) states there is sufficient ablation study, but the other two reviewers (R1, R3) note the complete absence of ablation studies and baselines.

- **Questions about experimental design**  
  Are the videos shown with sound? If not, why use only silent videos? (R3)  
  What is the purpose of converting caption to speech? In the best case, speech is redundant with the text caption; in the worst case, it adds noise (R3).  
  It would be useful to conduct experiments that dissociate visual from audio and language information (e.g., only visual, only language), and ideally orthogonalize modalities (e.g., show a ball while presenting the word “chair”) to isolate contributions (R3).

- **Missing metrics and evaluations**  
  Consider using additional metrics like MSE to quantify prediction error beyond correlation (R2).  
  A canonical correlation analysis (CCA) between the joint embeddings and fMRI signals could provide complementary insight (R2).  
  Validate findings across different models for the text, video, and audio encoders to test generalization (R2).

- **Minor issue**  
  In Figure 1, the colors of the arrows or the order of the modalities appear flipped (R1).
