## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a causal framework for measuring and optimizing long-term fairness in reinforcement learning, formalized as the difference in average expected qualification gain between groups. It decomposes this disparity into direct (DPE), delayed (IPE), and spurious (SPE) effects, and connects DPE to benefit fairness. The policy is optimized via PPO with an added fairness penalty. Reviewers find the problem important and the decomposition interesting, but raise concerns about theoretical novelty, lack of guarantees, insufficient empirical evaluation, and incomplete positioning relative to prior work.

### Strengths
- Studying long-term fairness via qualification gain disparity is well-motivated and socially important (R2, R4).
- The causal decomposition of long-term fairness into direct, delayed, and spurious impacts, and the connection to benefit fairness, are interesting (R1, R3, R4). One reviewer specifically highlights the idea of using \(\pi_{PS}\) as clever (R4).
- The fairness penalty decomposition is useful in practice (R3).
- Experiments demonstrate that the proposed fairness mechanisms achieve approximate benefit fairness and work as intended (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty of the causal decomposition is questioned**: One reviewer finds it “not surprising” and notes it closely mirrors static causal fairness decompositions, only replacing indirect effect with delayed impact (R1). Another reviewer similarly finds the novelty unclear compared to prior causal fairness work in RL (Hu & Zhang 2022, Hu et al. 2023, Yu et al. 2022) and says the differences are minor (e.g., using qualification gain instead of qualification rate, and not considering RL) (R4). Some reviewers find the perspective novel (R2, R3), but the incremental contribution is a recurring concern.
- **Insufficient theoretical guarantees**: The method has no provable convergence guarantees and does not quantitatively characterize how penalty coefficients \(\beta^{KL}\) and \(\beta^{\Lambda}\) affect fairness or model performance (e.g., precision/recall per round) (R1, R3). The claim of a “general framework” based on MDP may not hold in real-world settings violating Markov assumptions (R1).
- **Empirical evaluation is limited and raises several issues**:
  - The advantage over baselines is marginal: in Figure 2, the difference between worst and best is only about 0.2% (R2). 
  - In Figure 3, the qualification gain disparity does not reach zero and grows with horizon; authors should explain this and show settings where disparity can approach zero (R2).
  - In Figure 5, IPE and SPE components are rarely influenced by the fairness mechanisms; it is unclear why and how the benefit fairness regularization helps (R4).
  - Figure 4 lacks error bars, making it harder to assess variability (R4).
  - The evaluation does not check whether model performance (e.g., per-round utility) improves or whether fairness-aware optimization yields Pareto-superior outcomes for all groups; only utility gaps are reported (R3).
- **Incomplete connection to related work**: The paper does not sufficiently compare with strategic classification, performative prediction, or repeated distributionally robust optimization (Hashimoto et al. 2018, Perdomo et al. 2020) (R3). It also omits two existing fairness notions related to qualification gain: equal improvement (Guldogan et al. 2022) and bounded effort (Heidari et al. 2019) (R4). A reviewer also asks how the proposed metric relates to equalizing rewards across groups (Wen et al. 2021) (R2).
- **Missing explanation of connections to conventional fairness metrics**: It is unclear why equalized odds (both TPR and FPR) and loss disparities have no counterparts in the causal formulation; the authors should explain this (R3).
- **Simplicity of fairness optimization**: The penalty-based regularization is a straightforward stacking of terms; its novelty is unclear (R4). A reviewer also asks why a penalty is used instead of re-weighting (R3).
- **Minor issues**: Proposition 1 may contain a typo—the inner sum already marginalizes feature \(x'\) (R1). It is also asked whether the delayed impact can be expressed in terms of benefit fairness similarly to DPE (R1).
