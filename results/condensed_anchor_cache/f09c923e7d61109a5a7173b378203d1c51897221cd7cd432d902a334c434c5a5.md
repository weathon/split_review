## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a decentralized multi-agent reinforcement learning method called Anticipation Sharing (AS). In AS, each agent maintains and shares “anticipated” action distributions of its neighbors, then updates its own policy using a surrogate objective that averages individual anticipated advantages subject to a KL constraint. The goal is to align individual and collective objectives without sharing rewards, values, or model parameters. The method is validated on three mixed-motive tasks (Exchange, Cooperative Navigation, Cooperative Predation) and compared against baselines including value sharing, policy parameter sharing, and independent learning.

### Strengths

- **Problem importance**: The mixed-motive setting, where agents have private rewards but must cooperate to maximize social welfare, is understudied and important (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Novelty of the method**: Anticipation Sharing provides a fresh perspective on decentralized cooperation by having agents model and share anticipated teammate actions (Reviewer 3).
- **Theoretical and empirical validation**: The paper includes both theoretical analysis (theorems with proofs) and experiments in simulated environments (Reviewers 3, 4). Mathematical notation is mostly well-defined and proofs are written rigorously (Reviewer 4; Reviewer 1 also notes mathematical definitions are present).
- **Empirical results**: The proposed method converges more stably and to higher returns than baselines across tasks; results are robust to number of neighbors and neighbor distance hyperparameters (Reviewers 1, 4). The experimental figure is clear with appropriate text size, well-differentiated colors, and consistent legends (Reviewer 4).
- **Practical relevance**: The discussion covers decentralized learning challenges and privacy concerns, highlighting the method’s real-world significance (Reviewer 3).
- **One reviewer** (Reviewer 1) notes that the clipping method makes intuitive sense and, given the popularity of PPO, should provide performance gains.

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and theoretical justification**: 
  - The mathematical underpinnings are difficult to follow; the proofs of Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Theorem 3 do not appear convincing and the inequalities are not fully justified (Reviewer 2).
  - In contrast, another reviewer (Reviewer 4) found the proofs rigorous and well-defined, indicating disagreement on theoretical rigor.
  - The introduction of the surrogate optimization objective in Section 4.3 is not justified; it is unclear why this complex function is a good choice and what advantage it offers (Reviewer 2).
  - The derivation of the constraints in Section 4.4 from the optimization problem in Section 4.2 is not straightforward (Reviewer 2).
  - The motivation and intuition for the method are not clearly communicated: why should we expect AS to outperform existing methods? Sharing action distributions conveys similar information to sharing rewards/values, so why is it a better assumption? (Reviewer 4)
  - The quantity defined in Definition 2 (expectation over individual anticipated advantages) is unclear; since each agent has a different reward function, averaging advantages may not be meaningful. The authors fail to provide intuition for this quantity and why bounding its difference is useful (Reviewer 4).
  - The terminology “anticipated policies” is confusing; “teammate modelling” would be more descriptive (Reviewer 4).
  - Algorithm 1, lines 13-14 appear to share policies rather than action distributions; a clarifying note is needed (Reviewer 4).
  - There is a minor typo in the proof of Theorem 1 after equation (20): “E_s'” should be “E_{s'}” (Reviewer 1).
  - The sentence “Theoretically, we established that the difference between agents’ actual action distributions and the anticipations from others bounds the difference between individual and collective objectives” is not clearly connected to the actual theoretical contributions presented (Reviewer 2).
  - The paper occasionally lapses into redundancy, with repeated ideas or arguments that could be streamlined (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing experiments and analysis**:
  - The simulated environments are overly simplistic; no tests are conducted in more complex scenarios (larger number of agents, more intricate state spaces, more complex reward structures) to demonstrate scalability and robustness (Reviewer 3).
  - No experiments are performed in challenging scenarios with actual social dilemmas (e.g., prisoner’s dilemma, tragedy of the commons) to test whether the method avoids local Nash equilibria (Reviewers 1, 3).
  - The method is only evaluated in fully observable settings; it is unclear whether the theory and algorithm would hold in partially observable environments (Reviewer 1).
  - It is impractical to assume each agent has an accurate anticipated policy for all other agents. Missing experiment and discussion on how inaccuracies in teammate modelling affect performance (Reviewer 4).
  - The experimental analysis is speculative and makes unsubstantiated causal claims. For example, the paper states that value-sharing baselines fail because “policy updates of agents lack a coordination mechanism,” but no evidence is provided. This is a hypothesis, not a conclusion (Reviewer 4).
  - The results are not well explained: why do some baseline methods perform so poorly? Why is AS very similar to value sharing in the Predation task? What is the trade-off a practitioner should consider between AS and simpler methods like value sharing? (Reviewers 2, 4)
  - The method does not address credit assignment issues when agents have conflicting goals or imbalanced contributions to the global reward (Reviewer 3).
  - Non-stationarity introduced by agents continuously updating their anticipations (a moving target problem) is not discussed or analyzed (Reviewer 3).

- **Related work**:
  - Missing references to opponent modeling and teammate modeling literature, including the Albrecht et al. survey (arXiv:1709.08071) which covers both CTDE and ad-hoc teamwork settings (Reviewers 1, 4).

- **Overclaims**:
  - The abstract describes the method as a “paradigm shift,” which is an overbearing claim not supported by the results (Reviewer 4).
