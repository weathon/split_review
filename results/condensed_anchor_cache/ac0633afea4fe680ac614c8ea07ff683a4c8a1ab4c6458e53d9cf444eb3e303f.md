## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ProxCoRL, a curriculum learning strategy for multi-task reinforcement learning that selects tasks based on the Zone of Proximal Development, balancing task difficulty and similarity to a target distribution. The method is derived from a simplified theoretical analysis and evaluated on sparse goal-reaching and bipedal walker stump-track tasks.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, well-motivated, and presents the technical details clearly (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The balanced task selection strategy ensures tasks are neither too difficult nor too simple for the agent (Reviewer 1).
- The approach is supported by a mathematical analysis in a simplified learning setting (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The method addresses curriculum learning toward arbitrary target distributions, a departure from prior work that considers only uniform distributions (Reviewer 4).
- Appropriate baselines are selected, and the overall context with related work is good (Reviewer 2).
- Reviewer 2 finds the empirical results convincing (though see weaknesses for disagreement).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and relation to prior work.** The core idea (proximal update via KL divergence) is largely unchanged from ProCuRL; the extension to a target distribution simply adds a weighting term and does not represent a significant conceptual leap (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 rates the contribution as excellent, creating a disagreement on novelty.
- **Weak theoretical foundation.** The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) is based on a highly simplified setting (single state, two actions, specific reward structure, contextual bandit) that diverges from general RL with continuous states/actions, temporal dependencies, delayed rewards, and exploration-exploitation trade-offs. The direct transfer of the derived curriculum strategy to general contextual MDPs is not thoroughly justified, and the impact of these differences is not discussed (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Reviewer 3 further questions whether Theorem 1 can be extended by relaxing its assumptions and notes that the derivation from Equation 1 to Equation 2 (replacing V* with V_max) lacks mathematical justification.
- **Hyperparameter concern.** The claim of eliminating domain-specific hyperparameter tuning is contradicted by the need to determine V_max (maximum possible value), whose estimation may be non-trivial and require domain knowledge (Reviewer 1).
- **Task similarity metric.** The metric for task similarity (L2 distance of context parameters) may not correlate with intuitive similarity in many environments. Reviewer 4 provides an example: a table-top manipulation with a drawer and cube where the L2 distance between two desired states is small but the strategies differ substantially in complexity (one requires opening/closing the drawer). This raises concerns about the metric’s generality.
- **Inconclusive empirical advantage.** The proposed method does not clearly outperform baselines overall (Reviewer 3). In the PointMass-s:2G environment (non-uniform target distribution), ProxCoRL performs similarly to the uniform-sampling variant ProxCoRL-un (Figure 2(b)), which undermines the claimed contribution of the task-correlation term (Reviewers 1, 4). Reviewer 2 considers the results convincing, creating a disagreement on empirical strength.
- **Limited evaluation.** Experiments are conducted only on relatively simple tasks with state-based policies. Evaluation on vision-based RL would make the contribution more impressive (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing analysis of exploration-exploitation.** The theoretical analysis does not consider the exploration-exploitation trade-off, which is a critical aspect of reinforcement learning (Reviewer 1).
- **Minor presentation issues.** The paper has several citation style errors (e.g., “Hallack et al., 2015” should use \citep instead of \citet) in Sections 2, 3.1, and 4. Figure 2 plot lines are too thick, reducing readability (Reviewer 2).
