## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes selective poisoning strategies to enhance clean-label backdoor attacks under a constrained threat model where the attacker provides only data for the target class (e.g., face recognition) and has no knowledge of the victim model or other classes. Two sample selection methods are introduced: (1) using a pretrained model to identify “hard” samples via latent clustering, and (2) using an out-of-distribution dataset to train a surrogate model when a pretrained model is unavailable. The methods are applied to existing attacks (BadNets, SIG, Narcissus) on CIFAR-10 and GTSRB. The paper claims higher attack success rates with lower poisoning rates compared to random selection and demonstrates resilience against defenses like STRIP and Neural Cleanse.

### Strengths
- **Threat model relevance.** The attacker only needs access to target class data, reflecting real‑world constraints (privacy, geography). Prior demonstrations that existing attacks under this model are ineffective constitute an important finding.
- **Novel sampling strategies.** The two data‑selection strategies (pretrained model or OOD‑based surrogate) can be combined with multiple existing clean‑label attacks (BadNets, SIG, Narcissus) to boost their performance under the studied threat model. Effectiveness holds even when assumptions about the pretrained model or OOD dataset are relaxed.
- **Thorough analysis.** Evaluation across several attacks, datasets, and defenses shows convincing transferability—the surrogate model is well differentiated from the victim model.
- **Practical applicability.** The method improves attack success rates with fewer poisoned samples, offering efficient resource use in realistic decentralized data collection.
- **Clarity and motivation.** The paper is easy to follow, with clear motivation supported by empirical evidence.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns.** Reviewers 1, 3, and 4 note the method is primarily a data‑selection strategy. The concept of using a pretrained model to identify “hard samples” for poisoning has been explored in prior work (e.g., [6,7,9] in R4’s references), and the paper does not clearly articulate distinctions. The claimed “new threat model” was previously examined in [10,11] (dirty‑label vs. clean‑label discussions).
- **Insufficient baselines.** Only random sampling is compared. The paper should compare against [Gao et al., 2023] (Not All Samples Are Born Equal…), which can be adapted to the threat model by using a pretrained model.
- **Limited dataset and domain scope.** Evaluations are restricted to CIFAR-10 and GTSRB; performance on other dataset types and applications remains unknown.
- **Outdated and missing defenses.** Defenses tested (STRIP, Neural Cleanse) are older. More recent and adaptive defenses (e.g., sample‑specific anomaly detection, [13,14] in R4’s references) should be evaluated. Hyperparameter settings for each defense are not described.
- **Training length issues.** CIFAR‑10/GTSRB models are trained for 300 epochs, far longer than standard (≈100 epochs) [Turner et al., 2019; He et al., 2016], making attack success likely due to overfitting. Early stopping (validation loss plateau) should be employed to simulate a competent defender.
- **OOD strategy unclear.** The OOD‑based approach is only vaguely described: how the OOD dataset is obtained and what requirements (e.g., domain similarity) are needed to maintain attack effectiveness are not specified.
- **Narcissus discrepancy.** Results for Narcissus differ from those reported in the original paper; the authors should explain why.
- **Overclaims.** Statement (Line 156‑159) that accessing only samples from a single non‑target class is a more difficult setting is potentially overclaimed. The claim that the threat model is “the most constrained” ignores other reasonable scenarios (e.g., an opportunistic attacker who cannot choose which samples to manipulate).
- **Missing experiments.** 
  - Include results using all training samples as a reference.
  - Narcissus results should be in the main text, not appendix.
  - Test on untargeted clean‑label attacks (e.g., UBW‑C from [12] in R4).
  - Evaluate against more advanced backdoor defenses.
- **Dependency on pretrained models.** The pretrained‑model strategy assumes availability of a relevant pretrained model, which may not always be practical.
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms.**
  - Why use the OOD strategy if it works worse than the pretrained model?
  - Why use latent clustering instead of the loss from a pretrained zero‑shot classifier like CLIP? Why VICReg instead of a more general feature extractor (e.g., CLIP)?
  - Where are the training settings adapted from?
  - How does the attack perform if the victim distributes target class data to multiple sources?
  - What potential mitigations exist against these attacks in the studied threat model?
- **Reviewer disagreement.** Reviewer 2 (score 8) is significantly more positive regarding novelty and analysis; the other reviewers (scores 5,5,6) raise substantial concerns about novelty, baseline strength, evaluation breadth, and overclaims.
