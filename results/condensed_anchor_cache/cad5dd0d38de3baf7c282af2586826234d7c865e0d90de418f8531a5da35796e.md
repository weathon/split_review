## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes two inference-time methods for controlling large language model behavior without human annotations: SELFCONTROL, which uses gradients of the model's self-evaluation of a prompt suffix to steer latent representations; and SELFCONTROL-Prefix (PrefixController), a trained module that encapsulates learned representations for efficient multi-behavior control. Experiments on detoxification, truthfulness, emotional tone, and privacy show improvements over prior work, but reviewers raise concerns about the method's mathematical soundness, evaluation thoroughness, and practical efficiency.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R1). The idea of using suffix gradients to guide learned representations is novel and interesting (R1).
- The work addresses a vital area—cheap, reliable, scalable LLM alignment—and succinctly motivates the need for transparent, human-free methods (R2).
- There is a nice progression from the instance-level SELFCONTROL to the more general, lower-cost PrefixController, and both are evaluated across diverse tasks (detoxification, truthfulness, emotional tone, privacy) (R2, R3).
- Results are compelling, showing state-of-the-art performance (e.g., 8.3% improvement in detoxification, 48.2% in privacy protection) (reviewers generally acknowledge; R3 notes flexibility).
- Design choices in Section 3 are clear (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Mathematical foundation and objective pitfalls**  
   The objective function resembles DPO but may allow the model to push down both logP+ and logP_ (pushing down logP_ more) while achieving the same margin, leading to undesirable directions. R1 provides a concrete example: given tokens "Yes", "No", "Nope" with equal probabilities (margin 0), a valid solution increases "Yes" to 1/2 and drops "No" to 0, but an equally valid solution drops "Nope" to 0 while keeping "Yes" and "No"—the second harms generation quality. The authors do not address how the method prevents this without additional constraints.

2. **Lack of statistical significance and reporting**  
   Tables (e.g., Table 2, 3, 4) do not report standard deviations/errors, making it unclear whether results are statistically significant (R3). This weakens the conclusions, especially for tasks where improvements are small.

3. **Limited improvement over simpler baselines**  
   Comparing Tables 2 and 3, SELFCONTROL's improvement over basic system prompting is marginal, despite requiring much longer search and runtime (R1). This questions whether better prompting techniques (e.g., Tree of Thoughts, RISE) could be more efficient and effective (R1).

4. **Efficiency and scalability concerns**  
   - SELFCONTROL increases token count per query substantially (original prompt + model output + suffix) (R2).  
   - The algorithm requires K responses per iteration (value of K and number of iterations not specified; running time in Table 4 shows SELFCONTROL takes nearly 10× longer than uncontrolled) (R2).  
   - PrefixController's training cost (e.g., number of queries needed to train one adapter) is not reported, making the claimed efficiency trade-off unverifiable (R3).  
   - It is unclear whether each control requires a separate adapter or if conditional/prompt-independent versions exist; the amortized cost may be prohibitive (R3).

5. **Single-task control limitation**  
   While the paper claims suffixes can be combined, it appears SELFCONTROL only steers one behavior at a time (e.g., happy or non-toxic, not both) (R2). Composable control is attributed to PrefixController, but there is minimal evaluation beyond Figure 4—essential experiments showing simultaneous high scores for privacy and detoxification are missing (R2).

6. **Ablation casts doubt on gradient utility**  
   Table 7 shows that replacing suffix gradients with random vectors can *improve* performance for some models (R2). The paper acknowledges this but does not provide deep analysis of why or what it implies about the method's core mechanism (R2, R3).

7. **Evaluation design issues**  
   - Privacy protection is tested only on avoiding email addresses—a narrow task achievable with a regex; subtle privacy breaches are not tested (R2).  
   - Emotional tone evaluation relies solely on a GPT-3.5-turbo score without guaranteeing desirable content; Tables 20–28 show many outputs achieving the target emotion but deviating from intended content, which metrics mask (R2).  
   - No evaluation on reasoning tasks (e.g., GSM8K, MATH) with a suffix like "Your response was correct. Yes or No?"—such tests would boost generalizability claims (R1).  
   - Some emotional control results severely underperform, but no explanation is given (R3).

8. **Unsupported claims and missing justification**  
   - Abstract lacks a clear problem statement, making it unclear what specific issue is being addressed (R3).  
   - The claim "gradients are naturally engineering model representations" is unsupported by logic or citation (R3).  
   - The statement that common biases "generally do not apply to our method" is disagreeable—even with forced yes/no answers, token statistics are subject to training biases (R3).  
   - It is not clearly justified why prompt engineering cannot be used for tasks like sentiment control (R3).

9. **Presentation and clarity issues**  
   - Many typos (e.g., "optimize the its parameters", missing comma in "greatly outperforms") (R3).  
   - Too much content makes it hard to focus on key contributions; Sections 4.5 and 4.6 feel squeezed in (R2).  
   - Key information is missing or hard to find: for example, number of SELFCONTROL iterations not stated, and a worked example of iterative progression would be helpful (R2).  
   - Equation (1) likely has an input mismatch: the left-hand side should presumably be H_input (R1).  
   - Appendices come across as a data dump from many investigations rather than curated supporting material (R2).  
   - Figures 10 and 11 appear identical (R2); the link to HH-dialogue examples in Appendix F2 is broken (R2).  
   - Mathematical reasoning examples (appendix) seem strong but are relegated; e.g., they could replace or supplement privacy results (R2).

10. **Minor reviewer disagreements**  
    While R1 found the work novel and well-written (score 6, confidence 4), R2 and R3 gave lower scores (5,5) and were more critical of the method's practical value and evaluation rigor. R2 particularly questioned whether the method's core insight (suffix gradients) is truly essential given the random-vector ablation. R3 questioned statistical significance and missing justifications. The contrast is preserved: R1's stronger enthusiasm for the writing and idea is noted, but the majority sees significant weaknesses.
