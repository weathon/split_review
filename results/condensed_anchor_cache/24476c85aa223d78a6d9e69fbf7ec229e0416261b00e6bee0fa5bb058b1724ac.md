## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes QQ-STR, a framework for 2D bounding-box-based invisible object tracking under occlusion and containment, inspired by object permanence. It comprises three modules: a Visual Module (VM) with off-the-shelf object detector and human pose estimator; a Spatial Relation Reasoner (SRR) that generates candidate spatial-relationship graphs between objects across frames; and a Spatial-temporal Relation Analyst (SRA) that uses a conditioned diffusion model to predict trajectories and selects the best one. Evaluation is on a synthetic dataset (LA-CATER), an older real dataset (Liang et al. 2018), and a newly collected real-world RGB-D dataset (iVOT – 49 videos, 12 scenes, 31k frames, 171 annotated trajectories). Comparisons are made against OPNet, PA, RAM, and AAPA. The method outperforms baselines on mIoU.

### Strengths

- The core idea of using a graph structure for occlusion/containment reasoning in multi-object tracking is technically novel. The separation into explicit qualitative reasoning (SRR) and quantitative location prediction (SRA) provides a new perspective on object permanence.
- Experiments are conducted on multiple datasets (synthetic + two real-world, including a contributed dataset iVOT). The real-world dataset is a valuable contribution for future research, being larger and more complex than existing ones.
- Adapting a diffusion model for trajectory generation is interesting and useful to the community.
- Ablation studies (Table 4) provide insights into the effectiveness of the SRR and SRA components.

### Weaknesses

- **Many important details are missing or unclear:**
  - Hand positions are extracted (Sec. 3.3) but their usage is never explained in later sections.
  - The paper does not clarify whether occlusion and containment are distinguished when generating spatial-relationship graphs, nor why object trajectories are used without identity information – object characteristics should affect occlusion/containment.
  - The method enumerates all possible spatial relations in the first frame (candidate PG1), which may cause scalability problems when there are many objects.
  - The process of generating candidate graphs and selecting candidates is not clearly justified; the correction module’s “shiver error” assumes fixed object size, but in real-world scenarios objects change in scale and aspect ratio across adjacent frames.
  - The definition and use of variables (e.g., hand positions H_i, inconsistent use of t/T/k and superscript/subscript) contain typos and are confusing; e.g., “H_i = {(pl_i^t, pr_i^t), (pl_i^t, pr_i^t), …}” should be “H_i = {(pl_i^1, pr_i^1), (pl_i^2, pr_i^2), …, (pl_i^t, pr_i^t)}”.

- **The framework is over-complicated and lacks end-to-end coherence:**
  - The system integrates many separate models (object detector, human pose estimator, correction module, diffusion-based trajectory predictor) trained separately. The paper does not articulate how outputs of one module feed into the next, nor how error signals propagate. This raises concerns about learning optimal representations and interactions.
  - There are many hyperparameters and correction stages; their effects are not analyzed, and no experiments demonstrate robustness to different hyperparameter settings.
  - Compared with SOTA methods, results in Table 2 are not clearly superior enough to justify this complex design; the method’s performance gain over simpler baselines is not evident.

- **Insufficient qualitative and ablation evidence:**
  - No qualitative results are shown in the main paper (no image visualizing tracking results). The supplementary contains only two short videos without baseline comparison. A visual comparison (e.g., multiple frames of QQ-STR vs. the closest baseline RAM) is needed to demonstrate improvements and failure cases.
  - No justification for choosing diffusion models over established trackers like SORT, DeepSORT, or recent MOT trackers. An ablation comparing these choices is necessary to justify the diffusion model.
  - The method mimics Kalman/Particle filters (predict-update); a comparison to such classical methods is essential to gauge the gap and to determine whether deep networks are even necessary (e.g., does Kalman filter already solve the synthetic dataset?).

- **Incomplete evaluation on standard benchmarks:**
  - The authors argue CATER is not used because it only has classification/relation labels (Sec. 2), but the proposed method could still be evaluated on CATER’s Snitch-localization task (as in Tokmakov et al.’s Table 1). This dataset is more widely used and would provide a more complete comparison.
  - The claim of handling object permanence should be tested on real-world multi-object tracking datasets like KITTI (as in the RAM paper Table 2). Even partial gains (e.g., reducing ID switches) would support the claim.
  - Evaluation on Occluded Video Instance Segmentation (OVIS) dataset is also recommended.
  - The proposed iVOT dataset is poorly described: no details on number/length of sequences, diversity of scenes and object types, or annotation process. Moreover, Table 3 lacks experimental results of SOTA methods on iVOT (only the proposed method is shown).

- **Presentation issues:**
  - Figures 1 and 2 treat the modules (VM, SRR, SRA) as black boxes, conveying little about the method. They are not tightly connected to the text. More descriptive figures showing inner workings would greatly improve readability.
  - The paper is hard to read due to complicated variable definitions, typos, and unclear connection between figures and text.
  - Tables and figures should be placed at the top of pages for professional appearance.
