## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Speech Robust Bench (SRB), a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating ASR robustness to diverse corruptions. It combines multiple existing datasets and perturbation types: clean (Librispeech, TEDLIUM), far-field (AMI), multi-talker (CHiME-6), accented English (Mozilla CommonVoice), text-to-speech (XTTS), adversarial attacks (utterance-specific and utterance-agnostic), environmental noise (white noise, environmental noise, echo/RIR), and digital augmentations (SoX effects). Metrics include WER for clean speech, WER degradation (WERD) for adversarial attacks, and difficulty-normalized WER/WERD (NWER/D) for other corruptions, using a neural network to estimate speech quality weights. The benchmark is evaluated on state-of-the-art models: Whisper, wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, Canary, and variants. Key findings: Canary performs best on clean speech; Whisper is robust to digital augmentations and echo/RIR; wav2vec 2.0 is robust to adversarial attacks; positive correlation between robustness and model size. Additionally, analyses on Spanish (multi-lingual models more robust on English than Spanish) and gender (data-dependent disparities) are conducted. The pipeline is open-sourced.

### Strengths

1. The benchmark is comprehensive, covering a wide range of adversarial and non-adversarial perturbations, and evaluates several state-of-the-art ASR models (Whisper, wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT, Canary) with extensive experiments. [R1,R2,R3,R4]
2. The dataset description is detailed and the pipeline is open-sourced, contributing a reusable resource to the speech community. [R4]
3. The paper provides clear take-away summaries in Section 4, aiding interpretation. [R1]
4. The analysis includes correlations between model robustness and parameter count, as well as subgroup analyses by accent (English/Spanish) and gender. [R1,R2]
5. The paper addresses the critical question of whether robustness improvements come from additional data variants or simply more data (Section 4.4). [R4]
6. The work is placed fairly within existing literature and uses a familiar section layout. [R1]
7. The significance to the community is high, as it facilitates evaluation beyond standard Librispeech test sets. [R1]

### Weaknesses

1. The novelty is not clearly articulated. The main contribution appears to be assembling more noise sources and perturbations into one benchmark, but the paper does not sufficiently differentiate from existing robustness metrics or frameworks. A comparison table across the four modules (clean data, perturbation bank, transcription extraction, metric computation) with prior work would clarify the unique contributions. [R2,R3,R4]
2. The benchmark protocol lacks sufficient detail for independent reproduction. Specific issues:
   - The term "perturbations" is used for accented speech, TTS, and interpersonal speech, which are not perturbations; "domain" is more appropriate. The taxonomy (Figure 2) should be revised accordingly. [R1]
   - For each domain, the exact dataset, version, and split are not specified (e.g., which Librispeech split, CommonVoice version, whether accented labels are used). A dedicated subsubsection per domain in Section 3.2 with full specifications is needed. [R1]
   - Adversarial perturbation generation details are missing from the main text. [R1]
   - Text-to-speech generation: the source text is not described; ideally, generated audio files should be shared for reproducibility. [R1]
   - Section 3.1 omits Spanish datasets and noise domain datasets (WHAM!, MUSAN). [R1]
   - The speech perception weights for NWER/D computation must be provided for reproduction. [R1]
   - It is unclear whether Spanish data is part of the benchmark or an extra analysis. [R1]
3. The evaluation relies on synthetic noise added to clean speech, which may not capture the complexity of real-world noisy conditions (non-stationary noise, reverberation, etc.). The paper should address how the benchmark relates to or can be extended to naturally occurring noisy speech. [R3]
4. The paper lacks discussion of strategies for improving robustness (e.g., data augmentation, noise-robust training, model architecture). Such suggestions would transform the paper from an evaluation study into a guide for building robust ASR systems. [R3]
5. The experimental sections need reorganization. The narrative flow is unclear; experiments should be grouped by research questions with explicit statements of purpose. [R3]
6. The paper does not compare with or include hybrid audio processing pipelines, which are increasingly popular (e.g., Kaldi-era practices). [R4]
7. The multi-lingual scope is limited to English and Spanish, and the models are mostly mono-lingual. The claim of "multi-lingual scenario" should either be removed or supported with additional experiments using other languages and multi-lingual models (e.g., Google T5). [R4]
8. Presentation and formatting issues hinder readability:
   - Figures 4, 5, and 7 are too small to read. [R1,R4]
   - Some tables and figures exceed page margins (e.g., Table 4, Table 6). [R1]
   - Table 1 and Table 2 use different formats; booktabs and no vertical lines are recommended. [R1]
   - Appendix A is empty. [R1]
   - Citation of Canary (Elena Rastogueva) is incorrectly formatted, missing data, and exceeds margin. [R1]
   - Typos: line 41 ("is comphe.." → "are comprehensive"), line 107 ("them" → "those"), line 161 ("others" inappropriate), line 346 ("than" → "compared to"), and misuse of "accuracy" for ASR predictions. [R1]
9. Some analysis details are confusing: DeepSpeech and speech-t5 appear in Section 4.1 but not in Table 1; Section 4.4 introduces additional models that should be introduced earlier. The usage of WER vs WERD is unclear for domains like accent and TTS, where it is not obvious what constitutes X and X_p audio sequences for WERD calculation. [R1]
10. The paper does not correspond the listed methods to earlier works, highlighting differences and novel variants covered. [R4]
