## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a method to train goal-directed dialogue agents by applying offline reinforcement learning (RL) to synthetic dialogues generated by an LLM-based “imagination engine.” The approach aims to overcome LLMs’ difficulty with multi-turn, goal-oriented interactions by first simulating human-human conversations from task descriptions, then optimizing a policy on those data. Experiments on teaching and preference elicitation tasks are reported, with evaluations including user studies. One reviewer (score 5) was notably more positive than the others (scores 3, 3, 3).

### Strengths
1. The imagination engine is a novel approach for creating a diverse, goal-directed dialogue dataset without requiring extensive human-collected data. It creatively leverages LLMs to simulate human-like conversations.
2. The paper is well written and easy to follow, with clear discussion of two key hypotheses (effectiveness of synthetic data + RL vs. direct prompting; and offline RL vs. behavior cloning) and detailed presentation of the methodology (MDP, RL, imagination engine).
3. Combining synthetic data with RL trains agents to ask clarifying questions and make goal-directed decisions over multiple turns. Multi-step optimization yields better quality data.
4. Comprehensive experiments against multiple baseline methods are provided, including detailed examples comparing response quality.
5. Empirical results, including user studies (12 users), suggest that RL-based agents achieve more natural dialogue flow and effective information gathering compared to direct LLM prompting, and improve outcomes in teaching and preference elicitation tasks.
6. The method efficiently applies offline RL to synthetic dialogues, enabling scalable agent training without real-time interactions.

### Weaknesses
1. Several claims are vague or unsupported (e.g., line 76: “In effect, the LLM can imagine what a human could do, but not to what an optimal agent should do”; lines 250-253: “Since inferring the human’s persona is an important skill we want downstream learning agent to acquire”). More intuition and validation are needed.
2. The quality of the synthetic data from the imagination engine is not sufficiently discussed. It is unclear how closely the LLM followed instructions when generating dialogues (e.g., using a sampled reward score as part of the input). Multiple reviewers note that synthetic data may not reflect actual user behavior: simulated users may appear overly cooperative, lack typical randomness, or diverge significantly in highly personalized/complex tasks. This leads to “over-idealized” agent behavior that may struggle with real-world unpredictability.
3. The idea of using LLM-simulated conversations plus offline RL is not new. The paper should compare with prior work (e.g., https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.262/). Also, the term “goal-directed dialogue” might be better connected to established terms like “target-driven conversation” or “proactive dialogue” in NLP.
4. The evaluation is primarily in synthetic settings and limited to 12 human annotators, which is a small sample size and may introduce bias. The number of turns may not fully indicate effectiveness; satisfaction could be assessed by other dialogue system metrics. No consistency check among raters is reported. Objective evaluation metrics (e.g., BLEU, ROUGE on a held-out 10% test set) are recommended as supplements.
5. Using GPT-2 as the base model is difficult to justify; more powerful open-source models like LLaMA should be considered. The assumption that “models trained with RL outperform those using prompts” is tested only with GPT-3.5; the exact prompt used is not provided, and experiments with more advanced models (e.g., GPT-4o) are needed to strongly support the assumption.
6. Training with offline RL on a synthetic dataset suffers from distribution shift when real-world dialogues differ from training data. Optimistic estimation techniques only partially mitigate this. The absence of real-time feedback limits the agent’s adaptability in dynamic, complex scenarios.
7. Some research on dialogue uncertainty from an information-gathering perspective (e.g., “Uncertainty of Thoughts,” https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03271; “MEDIQ,” https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00922) should be compared with, as more advanced prompting methods may be more cost-effective than RL data collection and training.
8. The paper does not clarify the size of the synthesized dataset, whether it was entirely used for RL training, the size of the test set, or the reward assignment for non-terminal states (line 277: r = r_i only if s’ = τᵢ is the full dialog – what is r when it is not the end?). It is also unclear why the criteria from the Critique Step are not introduced already in the Imagination Step.
