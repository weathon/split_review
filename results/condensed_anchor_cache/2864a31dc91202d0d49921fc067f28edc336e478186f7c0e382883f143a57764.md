## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a method where multiple language model instances individually propose and jointly debate their responses and reasoning processes over multiple rounds to arrive at a common final answer – a form of voting applied to LLMs. The method is evaluated on six datasets covering mathematical and strategic reasoning, plus a new benchmark for factual correctness (biographies of famous computer scientists). Results show improved accuracy over baselines including self-consistency. Ablations explore the effects of number of agents, rounds, and integration with chain-of-thought.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: The proposed multi-agent debate method is novel, and the paper appears to be the first to study multi-agent ensemble + reflection for LLMs (Reviewers 1,2,3,4; Reviewer 2 explicitly notes novelty, Reviewer 4 notes the new benchmark).
- **Extensive experiments**: The method is tested on multiple reasoning and factual accuracy tasks (6 datasets) with careful ablation of parameters (#agents, #rounds) and analysis of how chain-of-thought improves performance (Reviewers 1,2).
- **Insightful ablations**: Results suggest that different language models and prompts can complement each other, which may point to a new direction for inspecting knowledge and uncertainty in LLMs (Reviewer 3).
- **Clear presentation**: The write-up is very clear, and the appendix contains useful details (examples, performance scores, dataset descriptions) (Reviewer 1).
- **New benchmark**: A new benchmark for evaluating factual accuracy of computer scientist biographies is introduced (Reviewer 4).

*Reviewer contrast*: Reviewer 1 (score 8, fairly confident) is more positive, while Reviewers 3 (score 5, absolutely certain) and 4 (score 5, confident) are more critical; Reviewer 2 (score 6, confident) is moderate.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of deep analysis**: The paper does not adequately explain *why* multi-agent debating improves reasoning. The method essentially ensembles multiple answers plus reflection, but the analysis does not touch on the underlying reason; on uncertain answers (e.g., Figure 9 reasoning is not relevant) and even on reasoning tasks (Figure 2 Round 2) models mostly copy or echo the correct answer rather than engaging in in-depth debate or reasoning (Reviewers 2,3).
- **Missing comparisons**: 
  - No comparison to simple majority voting (e.g., Minerva-style) with a large number of samples (e.g., 50 or 100). The baseline in the paper uses only about 6 answers (3 agents × 2 rounds); a comprehensive comparison is needed (Reviewers 1,2).
  - No table systematically comparing the proposed method to other voting/refinement schemes (Reviewer 1 requested this).
- **Limited model variety**: The main experiments use only GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 as the base model. PaLM is used in only one additional experiment, which is insufficient (Reviewer 2).
- **Overstated novelty**: The primary experiment essentially uses different random seeds to generate varied responses from ChatGPT and then refines them; the additional experiment with different models (ChatGPT, Bard) is very limited (Reviewer 4).
- **Resource inefficiency**: The method is resource-intensive, requiring multiple agents and rounds, making it unsuitable for lengthy questions or answers; it can exceed context limits and reduce performance (Reviewer 4).
- **Presentation issues**: The paper is drawn out despite the straightforward idea, dedicating too much space to prompt examples (Reviewer 4).
- **Unclear details**: 
  - What do “short” and “long” represent in Figure 3? (Reviewer 3)
  - Why does “summarization” improve performance? Summarization inevitably causes information loss, which seems counter-intuitive; experiments on other tasks are requested (Reviewer 3).
  - It is unclear when agents become more “agreeable” – is it when there are more similar answers (regardless of correctness) or when the answer is actually correct? The relationship between agreeableness and instruction tuning/RLHF should be verified using pre-aligned checkpoints (Reviewer 3).
