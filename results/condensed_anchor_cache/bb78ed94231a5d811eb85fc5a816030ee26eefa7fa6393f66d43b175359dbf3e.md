## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a data selection algorithm that first performs a (k,z)-clustering of embedded data points and then uses the cluster structure to define sensitivity-based sampling probabilities. The weighted subset is claimed to approximate the full-data loss under a H"older continuous loss assumption, with a multiplicative (1±ε) factor and an additive term proportional to the clustering cost. Theoretical guarantees are given for linear regression, and experiments are conducted on gas sensors and CIFAR for neural networks. While the high-level idea (combining clustering with coreset sampling) is of interest, reviewers raise serious concerns about novelty, missing comparisons, restrictive assumptions, errors in the theoretical derivation, and poor presentation.

### Strengths
- The problem of data selection is important and will interest the community (R1, R4).
- Using (k,z)-clustering as a preprocessing step is an interesting and natural idea; for small z it offers robustness to outliers and yields diverse samples (R2, R3).  
- The proof technique is clear, elegant, and easy to follow (R1, R3, R4).  
- The proposed coreset is query efficient: it requires only O(k + 1/ε²) model inferences, and the procedure is simple (R4).  
- Empirical results on neural network tasks show that the method is competitive with or outperforms the compared baseline (R1: "experiments for the neural network seem good"; R4: "performs better on several datasets").  
- The connection of H"older continuity to coreset construction via (k,z)-clustering is innovative (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and incremental contribution.**  
   - The approach is essentially a straightforward combination of the coreset idea from Sener & Savarese (2018) and standard sensitivity-sampling results from the coreset literature, with only minor modifications (R1).  
   - The theoretical analysis is not novel: the proofs are direct applications of Bernstein’s inequality and follow well-known templates (R1).  
   - The paper uses a crude approximation to sensitivity (not the standard definition), which is similar to “lightweight coresets” and leads to an additive error that can be large (R1).  

2. **Missing theoretical and empirical comparisons.**  
   - For linear regression, the theoretical guarantees are weaker than those from leverage-score sampling, and it is unclear how the method improves upon existing sensitivity-sampling works (e.g., Chen & Price, Chen & Derezinski) in terms of sample complexity or runtime (R1, R2).  
   - Only a single baseline (Sener & Savarese 2018) is compared empirically; many more recent coreset methods cited in the related work are not evaluated (R4).  
   - No quantitative comparison of coreset size, error bounds, or query complexity with prior coreset constructions is provided; a summary table is missing (R4).  

3. **Restrictive and poorly justified assumptions.**  
   - Assumption 10 for linear regression (|b_i – b_j| ≤ ‖a_i – a_j‖₂) imposes a global Lipschitz-like condition on the regression target that is unlikely to hold in many practical scenarios and limits the kind of noise allowed (R2, R3).  
   - The H"older continuity assumption is placed on the original sample space rather than on the embedding space that the algorithm actually uses; this discrepancy leads to errors in the proof and the algorithm (R4 points 3a, 3b).  
   - No empirical evidence is provided to support the H"older continuity assumption on real datasets; it is unclear whether it holds for neural networks (R4, R3 question).  
   - The authors do not explain how the H"older continuity property is verified or ensured in the experiments, nor do they present experiments that leverage the distinction between H"older and Lipschitz continuity (R2, R3).  

4. **Errors in theoretical reasoning.**  
   - The application of Bernstein’s inequality in Appendix B.2 appears flawed: the expectation of Xᵢ is Σ ℓ(e) w(e) p(e) = Σ ℓ(e)/s, yet the proof bounds |Σ ℓ(e) – Σ w(e) ℓ(e)| without accounting for the scaling factor (R4). This part needs careful correction.  

5. **Presentation, readability, and consistency.**  
   - The paper is not reader-friendly: notation is imprecise and inconsistent (e.g., 𝒜 is not defined properly and is overloaded; C is not defined correctly; norms are used interchangeably ‖·‖ vs. ‖·‖₂; Δ is introduced in Section 3.1 without definition) (R2, R1).  
   - Algorithms 1 and 2 are never referred to in the main text; Algorithm 2 mentions x₀ in step 2 but computes it in step 3 (R1, R2).  
   - There are writing errors: e.g., what is X in Theorem 1? (R1); “there exists a real-valued constant” should be “constants” (R3).  
   - Several definitions and terms require significant time to parse; the space could be better used for clear variable definitions rather than restating Bernstein’s inequality (R2).  

6. **Insufficient experimental detail.**  
   - Regression experiments do not report runtime, making it impossible to verify the claimed speed advantage over leverage-score sampling (R1, R3).  
   - Neural-network experiments lack key details: the number of epochs used for uniform sampling of size k′; the runtime of uniform sampling in Figure 2(a) for comparison; in Figure 4, the statement “independently run each data point 100 times” is ambiguous (does it mean 100 independent experiment runs with different sampled sets?) and runtime is not provided for the margin baseline, which is the best but also the most expensive (R3).  
   - The extra computational cost of computing embeddings is not factored into the total time; this cost may be comparable to model inference in other methods (R4).  

7. **Conceptual clarity and scope.**  
   - The paper’s title highlights “sensitivity sampling” but the method does not use the standard definition of sensitivity from the coreset literature (R1).  
   - The experiments do not showcase the claimed advantage of handling H"older continuous losses; they rely on standard losses (squared error, cross-entropy) that are already Lipschitz (R2).
