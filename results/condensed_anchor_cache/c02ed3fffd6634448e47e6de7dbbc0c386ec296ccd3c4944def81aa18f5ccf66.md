## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes encoding medical ontologies (SNOMED CT) using Holographic Reduced Representations (HRR) to create structured concept vectors for transformer models (BERT). The HRR embeddings are generated by composing atomic vectors based on ontology structure, with PyTorch autograd support. The method is evaluated on the MIMIC-IV dataset for clinical diagnostic code prediction, with pre-training on masked language modeling and fine-tuning on mortality and disease prediction tasks. The authors report improved performance over standard embeddings, especially for rare codes. One reviewer was strongly positive (score 6), while two were more critical (scores 3, 3).

### Strengths
- Novel integration of HRR with transformers to encode ontological structure, offering a new approach for incorporating domain knowledge (all reviewers). The positive reviewer (R1) especially praised this as a creative and impactful advancement.
- The method is compatible with PyTorch autograd, facilitating adoption (R2).
- Results show improvement over baselines, and t-SNE visualizations demonstrate that codes with similar frequencies are grouped together (R2).
- Paper is well-structured with clear exposition and relatable examples (R1; other reviewers found presentation issues – see Weaknesses).
- Approach holds potential for personalized medicine and enriching PLMs with domain knowledge (R1, R3).
- R1 found the research quality moderate but detailed in experimental procedures.

### Weaknesses
- Performance improvements in fine-tuning are not large (R2, R3). R3 noted comparable performances across BERT adaptations (Table 1, Fig. 1). R2 questioned under what circumstances the method would be needed given the small gains.
- Lack of efficiency comparison: the paper claims efficiency but provides no runtime or resource comparisons (R2).
- Evaluation limited to a single dataset (MIMIC-IV) from one medical center; multi-center evaluation (e.g., eICU) is needed (R2).
- Methodological novelty is not clearly articulated: insufficient detail on how HRR vectors interact with the attention mechanism and feed-forward networks (e.g., concatenation, addition, or other interaction) (R3). No discussion of computational challenges such as increased memory usage or need for specialized operations (R3).
- Missing ablation studies and hyperparameter sensitivity analysis (e.g., HRR dimensionality, learning rate) (R3).
- Reproducibility concerns: missing details on dataset preprocessing, architectural designs, baselines implementation, library versions, and hardware (R3). R1 also noted lack of dataset details (MIMIC-IV citation, preprocessing steps in Section 2.1).
- Missing comparisons with other disease diagnosis benchmarks (R3).
- Missing separate performance metrics for frequent vs. infrequent codes in Table 1, despite this being a stated goal (R1).
- Presentation issues: abstract too long, details break the flow, grammar mistakes and typos (R2). R1 additionally requested clearer explanations for abbreviations (SNOMED CT) and citations for MIMIC-IV.
- Unclear rationale for the ICD mapping method (Section 2.2.1) – why not map all ICD-10 codes directly to ICD-9 and then to SNOMED CT? (R1)
- Statistical test choice not justified: one-sided Dunnett’s test used without explanation, especially given overlapping confidence intervals and close mean values in Table 1 (R1).
- No discussion of limitations (R1, R3).
- Specific questions from R3 indicate further issues: Fig. 1 shows HRRBase underperforming relative to others, suggesting potential weakness of the base method; Table 1 shows comparable performance, so what distinguishes HRR from unstructured?; Fig. 2 lacks visualization for the Unstructured baseline, making it hard to compare embedding spaces.
- The positive reviewer (R1) raised these weaknesses as missing details and justifications, while the two critical reviewers (R2, R3) found them more fundamental (e.g., insufficient performance gains, limited novelty, reproducibility issues).
