## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Coarsened Graph Learning (CGL) and its semi-supervised variant SCGL, which learn a coarsened graph directly from node attributes and optionally labels, without relying on an initial graph structure. The coarsened graph’s feature matrix, adjacency matrix, and node-to-supernode assignments are jointly optimized via a multi-block non-convex problem solved with Block Successive Upper-bound Minimization (BSUM). The authors claim reduced computational complexity, adversarial robustness, and improved downstream node classification performance. Evaluations compare CGL and SCGL against GCond, SCAL, and FGC on several homophilic and heterophilic datasets, measuring classification accuracy and runtime.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles the highly relevant problem of graph coarsening for large graphs, with a focus on scalability.
- Eliminating dependence on the initial graph structure lowers computational complexity and provides inherent robustness against adversarial attacks that target graph topology.
- The optimization approach directly learns a coarsened graph from features, effectively combining graph structure learning with coarsening.
- The method relies only on node features and labels, achieving strong classification performance even when no initial graph is available; one reviewer (R1, score 6) found this particularly impressive and praised the innovation of bridging the sparsity of PY with the homophily of coarsening.
- The writing is clear and accessible (noted by R2) and the paper proposes two useful settings (CGL and SCGL; highlighted by R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Motivation and practical relevance** – Several reviewers questioned why learning from structureless graphs is essential or where it applies in practice (R2, R4). The paper does not convincingly argue why ignoring the original graph structure is beneficial, especially when that structure contains useful relational information even if noisy. A minority reviewer (R1) considered the approach innovative, but others (R2, R4) felt the motivation requires stronger theoretical and practical grounding. In particular, the claim that a coarsened graph improves node classification lacks theoretical support (R4).

2. **Experimental reproducibility and baseline comparisons** – Baseline results are not fully reproduced. GCond’s performance in the paper differs from typical values on Cora, Citeseer, and Flickr; this may stem from not using the standard 80/10/10 data split (R1, R3). The experimental setup—data splits, hyperparameters, and how they differ from prior work—is insufficiently detailed (R1, R3). Only a small set of baselines (GCond, SCAL, FGC) is included; many other graph coarsening methods exist (R2). The comparison with FGC is not in-depth enough (R1). Additionally, the framework is only evaluated on node classification; graph-level tasks (e.g., graph classification) are ignored (R4).

3. **Methodological clarity and design choices** – The use of hard assignments for nodes to supernodes seems to assume an inherent clustering; soft assignments are not considered and the rationale is missing (R3). The motivation for each optimization step is unclear; for example, the adaptation of spectral constraints is introduced without sufficient detail or justification (R2). Notations such as `Wh.Data` and `L.Data` are not clearly explained (R4). The paper would benefit from showing concrete examples of coarsened graphs to demonstrate effectiveness (R4). The role of label information may be harmful when labels are not correlated with the downstream task (R3).

4. **Scalability and hyperparameter tuning** – BSUM can be computationally expensive and converge slowly for large-scale problems with many variables or large block sizes (R2). The method depends heavily on extensive hyperparameter tuning; the authors do not provide guidance on how to select hyperparameters (R1’s question). The label masking process to prevent leakage of validation/test labels during training is not explicitly defined; a clear mathematical formulation of how label information propagates to the coarsened graph while ensuring no leakage is needed (R1).

5. **Presentation and evaluation gaps** – Adversarial robustness is emphasized in the introduction, but the corresponding results and discussion are mostly deferred to the appendix; including some in the main text would improve consistency (R3’s question). An interesting observation from Table 10—that perturbed data sometimes yields higher performance than unperturbed data—is left without comment (R3’s question). The paper’s introduction is scattered and difficult to follow (R3). The evaluation is mainly on homophilous datasets; testing on large heterophilous graphs such as Penn94 would provide a more comprehensive assessment of generality (R1).
