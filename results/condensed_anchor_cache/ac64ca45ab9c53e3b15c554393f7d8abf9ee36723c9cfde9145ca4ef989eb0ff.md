## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces HELMET, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating long-context language models (LCLMs) across seven diverse, application-centric categories. It addresses issues in existing benchmarks—low coverage, insufficient lengths, unreliable metrics, and incompatibility with base models—by supporting controllable lengths up to 128k tokens, using model-based evaluation (GPT-4o), and enabling few-shot prompting for base models. Evaluating 51 LCLMs, the authors find that synthetic tasks like NIAH poorly predict downstream performance, task categories exhibit low correlation, and open-source models lag behind closed-source ones on complex reasoning tasks, with the gap widening at longer contexts. The paper recommends RAG tasks for fast model development and advocates holistic evaluation.

### Strengths

1. **Comprehensive and diverse task design**: The benchmark covers seven real-world categories (including retrieval-augmented generation, passage re-ranking, long-document QA, citation-based generation, ICL, etc.), enhancing representativeness of LCLMs in real applications. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)

2. **Support for ultra-long inputs**: Accommodates controllable input lengths up to 128k tokens, suitable for evaluating frontier models. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 1 notes limitations with RoPE scaling—see Weaknesses)

3. **Reliable model-based evaluation**: HELMET’s metrics using GPT-4o reflect human judgment better than n-gram matching, offering more reliable model ranking. (Reviewers 2, 4; Reviewer 3 questions validation—see Weaknesses)

4. **Compatibility with base models**: Few-shot prompting allows evaluation of models without instruction fine-tuning, broadening LCLM applicability. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3 asks which tasks are compatible—see Weaknesses)

5. **Extensive model evaluation**: 51 LCLMs tested across multiple dimensions, tasks, lengths, and model types (open/closed-source), providing valuable insights into architecture and size effects. (Reviewers 3, 4)

6. **Insightful performance analysis**: Findings include limitations of synthetic tasks as predictors, distinct trends across categories, and performance gaps between open- and closed-source models, guiding future research. (Reviewers 1, 3)

7. **Correlation analysis**: Investigating performance correlation among task types provides a new perspective to understand LLMs’ long-context ability. (Reviewer 1)

8. **Improved prompting and evaluation strategy**: Effectively stabilizes evaluation results. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses

1. **Subjective interpretation of “expected” rankings**: The authors dismiss benchmarks like RULER and ∞BENCH as unreliable because larger models sometimes underperform smaller ones (e.g., Llama-8B outperforming Gemini Pro, Llama-70B), attributing this to benchmark noise rather than investigating possible model issues (e.g., overfitting, architectural limitations). This reasoning may be premature and biased; more analysis considering both possibilities is needed. (Reviewer 3, strong concern; partially echoed by Reviewer 1 who notes the “expected” ranking is a bit subjective)

2. **Limited validation of model-based evaluation metrics**: Paper claims model-based metrics using GPT-4o are more reliable but provides only minimal details on human-model agreement. No human evaluation results, agreement rates, or analysis of discrepancies are reported. (Reviewer 3, moderate confidence; Reviewer 2 also notes unreliable metrics as a problem but accepts the solution—this weakness highlights the gap)

3. **High complexity and resource consumption**: The benchmark involves seven task categories, each with its own data preprocessing, input formatting, and evaluation scripts, creating a high barrier to entry and logistical overhead. Running the full suite is time-intensive; it would be beneficial to identify a few key subtasks that maintain consistency with full testing to save time. (Reviewer 2, moderate confidence)

4. **Low correlation among tasks complicates overall assessment**: Low correlation between different task categories makes it challenging to assess a model’s overall long-context handling ability if it excels only in certain tasks. Lack of a unified metric hinders determination of which models are truly superior. (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 1 also notes lack of deep analysis on why json-kv correlates higher with re-rank than with RAG or LongQA)

5. **RoPE scaling settings unsuitable for 128k/64k testing**: With ABF, the scaling factor should be at least 2× the target extension ratio. For Llama3 with 8k context, a scaling factor of at least 32 is needed for 128k, which the paper may not have used. (Reviewer 1, high confidence, technical weakness)

6. **Missing details and figures**: Figure 2 is missing from the paper. Also, the ‘depth’ parameter in Figure 11 is not defined clearly—specify whether top-to-bottom corresponds to beginning to end of context. (Reviewer 1, moderate confidence; these are missing experiment details)

7. **Limited coverage of domain-specific tasks**: While application-oriented, HELMET may not capture LCLM capabilities in highly specific domains like legal or medical texts, limiting applicability in niche areas. (Reviewer 4, moderate confidence)

8. **Heavy reliance on closed models for comparison**: Using GPT-4 as judge and baseline raises questions about reproducibility in an entirely open-source setting, limiting accessibility for some researchers. (Reviewer 4, moderate confidence)

9. **Lack of testing with Gemma series**: Gemma models have a unique attention head dimension of 256 (vs. typical 128), which may impact long-context performance; including them would strengthen the study. (Reviewer 1, moderate confidence—asked as a question but constitutes a missing experiment)

10. **Unclear compatibility of base models with non-RAG tasks**: Reviewer 3 asks which HELMET tasks other than RAG are compatible with base models without instruction-following. The paper only mentions few-shot prompting for base models but does not clarify task-specific applicability. (Reviewer 3, question that overlaps with a missing detail)

11. **Need for more direct comparison with existing benchmarks**: Paper critiques existing benchmarks but offers limited evidence of HELMET’s improvements beyond Figure 1. More direct model ranking comparisons on HELMET vs. existing benchmarks would strengthen arguments. (Reviewer 3, moderate confidence)

12. **Question about open-source models trained on synthetic data**: Could open-source models trained on synthetic datasets achieve comparable results with additional tuning on HELMET’s diverse tasks? This points to a missing ablation or analysis of synthetic data influence. (Reviewer 4, question reflecting a missing experiment)
