## Merged Review

### Summary
The Roll-AE autoencoder enforces spatiotemporal shift- and permutation-invariance in representations of micro-electrode array spike train data using a set-theoretic loss with linear assignment. On synthetic and electrophysiological (iPSC-derived neuronal culture) datasets, the learned embeddings outperform those from a standard autoencoder and a data-augmented autoencoder when used to predict traditional neural metrics. The model also shows potential for characterizing phenotypic clusters across siRNA treatments.

### Strengths
- The core idea of enforcing invariance to temporal shifts and spatial electrode permutations in a set-theoretic manner is novel and addresses an important, specific problem in MEA data analysis.
- Empirically, Roll-AE outperforms the two baseline autoencoders on the benchmark classification tasks (synthetic data) and when predicting neural metrics.
- The stochastic variant of the loss (stochastic shift-invariance) is introduced to reduce the otherwise prohibitive cost of the full set-theoretic loss (R1).
- On biological data, the clustering of genes in Fig. 6 (based on Roll-AE embeddings) appears meaningful and potentially useful for developing therapies.
- The study includes experiments on both synthetic and real electrophysiological data, providing evidence for the model’s ability to capture biologically relevant phenotypes without predefined metrics.
- One reviewer (score 5) found the paper well written and easy to understand.

### Weaknesses
**Evaluation and presentation of results**
- Multiple reviewers (R1, R4) note that all quantitative evaluation is done *indirectly* via a decoder trained on latent embeddings to predict neural metrics; no comparison of reconstruction loss or example reconstructions for the different architectures is provided.
- No error bars or statistical tests are reported for results (Fig. 5; also mentioned by R1, R4). Means are plotted without measures of variability, making it impossible to assess the significance or reliability of performance differences.
- In Fig. 4a the y‑axis label appears missing/wrong; Fig. 4b lacks clear labels and a colorbar; Fig. 5 and Fig. 6 use very small font sizes; light-colored bars are hard to read against a white background (R4).
- Figures are not always referenced in order in the text (R4). The introduction is considered too long, making it harder to identify the paper’s core contributions.

**Missing baselines and comparisons**
- No comparisons to existing invariant representation learning methods for autoencoding or neurophysiological data are provided. Reviewer 3 specifically mentions lack of discussion/citation of [1] “Learning time-invariant representations for individual neurons from population dynamics”, [2] “Capturing cross-session neural population variability through self-supervised identification of consistent neuron ensembles”, and [3] “Deep site-invariant neural decoding from local field potentials”.
- The paper does not compare to point process models that the authors themselves mention (line 76) as addressing invariance issues in MEA data (R4).
- Self-attention–based models (e.g., Set Transformer) are known to have permutation-invariance properties and should be compared (R4).
- The standard autoencoder and data‑augmented autoencoder baselines are not used in the Treatment Clustering and Neural Metrics Credentialing sections (R1).

**Methodological clarity and novelty concerns**
- The unique methodological contribution is not very clear; the approach appears similar to adapting existing 2D/3D orientation-invariance methods to spike‑train shift invariance (R3).
- The claim that Roll-AE is a “foundation model” is questioned by multiple reviewers (R1, R3, R4). There is no evidence that the model can be trained once and applied to new datasets without re‑training from scratch; the term is typically reserved for large models trained on many datasets.
- The paper lacks a detailed discussion of limitations, e.g., potential information loss from permutation aggregation, sensitivity to specific patterns, or scenarios where Roll-AE might not excel (R2).
- The impact of hyperparameters (e.g., shift‑sampling rate, binning size) on performance and computation is not thoroughly examined (R2, R4). Using MLPs for the encoder/decoder is costly in parameters; CNNs with temporal convolutions could be a more efficient alternative (R1).

**Lack of interpretability and comparison to traditional neural metrics**
- It is unclear how the autoencoder embeddings are better than simply using traditional neural metrics directly. The analysis in Fig. 6 uses PCA, which the paper itself criticizes (lines 37‑40) as a shortcoming of conventional MEA analysis (R4). The same relationships might be found by applying PCA to the neural metrics themselves.
- The model is designed to overcome the issue of manually picked hyperparameters in neural metrics, yet the autoencoder itself depends on hyperparameters (e.g., number of units) and no experiments explore their effect (R4). Additionally, the simpler baseline autoencoders sometimes outperform Roll-AE on various genes in Fig. 5, but this is not discussed (R4).
- No visualizations of the latent representations (e.g., via UMAP) for different temporal shifts of the same input are provided (R3). Interpretability of specific embedding dimensions is not addressed (R2).

**Computational and practical concerns**
- Processing all cyclic and electrode permutations is computationally heavy; the stochastic variant alleviates this, but the efficiency–performance trade‑off is not explored in depth (R2). A quantitative comparison of memory and computation time (like Fig. 8) for the baseline architectures and the overhead of Eq. (1) is requested (R1, R3).
- There is no discussion of how Roll-AE handles noise or missing data in real‑world MEA recordings (R2).
- Generalization to other spatiotemporal invariant data (e.g., ECG, sensor data) is not demonstrated or discussed (R2). Similarly, the authors note that deep learning MEA analysis has focused on *in vivo* data, but they do not explain why *in vivo* methods cannot be applied to *in vitro* recordings (R4).
- The paper does not state whether the electrophysiological dataset or source code is publicly available (R4).
