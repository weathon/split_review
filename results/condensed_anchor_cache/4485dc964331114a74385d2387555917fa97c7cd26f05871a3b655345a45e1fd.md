## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes AntifakePrompt, which reformulates deepfake detection as a visual question answering (VQA) task by soft-prompt tuning InstructBLIP. A learnable pseudo-word is inserted into the prompt to guide the model to answer “Yes” or “No” for real/fake images. Experiments on datasets from 3 held-in and 20 held-out generative models (covering text-to-image generation, image editing, and adversarial attacks) show an average accuracy improvement from 71.06% to about 92% over unseen domains. One reviewer (score 5) found the method novel and highly effective, while the other two (scores 3) had substantial concerns about novelty, experimental fairness, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- Well-written, with clear technical details that facilitate reproduction.
- Formulation of deepfake detection as a VQA task is a novel and commendable attempt in the field.
- Soft-prompt tuning alters no original parameters, providing a viable and efficient finetuning technique.
- Achieves superior detection performance and strong generalizability across a broad range of generative models.
- Validation is extensive, covering many datasets and generative sources.
- Addresses a timely topic (deepfake detection from diffusion models) and offers a formal framework for using VLMs as detectors.

### Weaknesses
- Novelty is limited: the prompt-tuning technique is straightforward and not highly original; the method lacks deep analysis of why it is effective specifically for deepfake detection (e.g., which visual features VLMs leverage) and could be applied to other visual tasks with simply a different text prompt.
- Experimental validation is insufficient to demonstrate the superiority of the method itself (vs. the model backbone):
  - Comparisons with Wang2020 and DE-FAKE are unfair because they use different training data and backbones; the contribution of the pretrained encoder is unclear. Results for those baselines retrained on the same data and backbone are needed.
  - The LoRA-finetuned baseline performs better than the proposed method on most non-attack tasks (93.04% vs. 91.09%) but worse on attack tasks; no insight is given for this phenomenon, which undermines the claim of solid superiority.
  - Missing comparisons with SOTA baselines for general diffusion-model detection and cross-forgery generalization (e.g., methods cited as [1]–[6] in the reviews).
  - The test set is biased toward diffusion models; performance on well-known deepfake benchmarks (DFC, DFDC, FF++) is not reported.
  - The dataset includes a category labeled “Deeperforensics” that should be correctly labeled “Deepfakes” or “Face Swap.”
  - Validation on the De-fake dataset is also missing.
- Metric choice: using simple average accuracy across datasets with varying sizes and importance may be misleading; weighted average accuracy or AUC should be reported.
- The method does not leverage unique capabilities of VLMs, such as providing visual explanations or reasoning for detection decisions, which could add significant value.
- Questions that overlap with criticisms (all should be addressed):
  - Provide Wang2020 and DE-FAKE results under the same training data and backbone.
  - Discuss the source of performance gain (pretrained encoder vs. prompt tuning vs. training algorithm).
  - Explain why LoRA outperforms the proposed method on non-attack tasks and whether other finetuning algorithms could yield better models.
  - Explore whether more specific questions (e.g., “Is this photo real or generated by deep learning?”) improve detection.
  - Evaluate on additional datasets (DFC, DFDC, FF++, De-fake) and use better metrics.
  - Compare with SOTA cross-dataset deepfake detectors.
