## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper provides a theoretical analysis of the role of multi-level image features in the generalization and quality perception ability of CNN-based blind image quality assessment (BIQA) models. Three theorems are proposed: (1) a generalization bound under distribution invariance showing that increasing feature level reduces generalization, highlighting the value of low-level features; (2) a tighter bound under distribution shift, demonstrating the impact of domain differences; (3) a Betti number analysis showing that high-level features increase representation power at the cost of generalizability. These findings reveal a fundamental tension between robust generalization and strong representation. The paper also proposes a loss function (Eq. 15) with consistency and parameter regularizers based on the theoretical insights, and validates the findings with experiments on several IQA datasets.

### Strengths

1. **First theoretical generalization boundary in the IQA domain.** This work provides foundational theoretical guidance for improving BIQA model generalization, which is rare in the field (R4.1, R3.1, R1.1, R2.1).  
2. **Both key scenarios investigated.** The analysis covers both distribution invariance and distribution shift between training and test sets (R4.2, R2.2).  
3. **Conflict between generalization and representation uncovered.** The tension between robust generalization and representation power is explicitly derived (R2.3, R4 summary).  
4. **Actionable insights from theory.** The theoretical results are translated into a new loss function (Eq. 15) with two regularizers, offering practical suggestions for training CNN-based BIQA models (R1.2).  
5. **Experimental validation.** Extensive experiments are conducted to empirically validate the theoretical findings, and the results are consistent with previous research observations (R4.3, R3.2, R1.3 [but with noted limitations]).  

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty and contribution.** The theoretical derivations primarily analyze existing consensus in the field using established frameworks (Rademacher complexity, Betti numbers) without presenting new insights, methods, or theoretical analysis approaches. The core differences/adaptations from general deep network theory are not well explained, reducing guidance for future work (R3.1, R2.1, R2.2).  
2. **Theoretical issues and missing rigor.**  
   – Theorem 1’s conclusion that increasing depth *L* reduces generalization is not rigorous, because deeper networks can also lower empirical error, potentially benefiting the overall bound. A trade-off discussion is needed (R4.2).  
   – Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 appear largely redundant in their core conclusions (R3.3).  
   – The derivation of Theorem 2 has gaps: (a) how Eq. 45 is obtained, (b) substituting Eqs. 44-45 into Lemma 2 does not clearly yield Eq. 46, (c) the definition of *Z* is missing ϵᵢ (R3.4).  
   – Theorems 1 and 3 require incompatible activation functions: ReLU is not Pfaffian (fails for Theorem 3), tanh is not non-negative (fails for Theorem 1) (R3.5).  
   – Corollary 1’s meaning is not explained in the paper (R3.6).  
3. **Focus on model depth, not multi-level feature fusion.** The analysis concentrates on depth, but cannot effectively explain the role of multi-level feature fusion within the same network (R3.2).  
4. **Missing validation for Theorem 3.** The experimental section validates only Theorems 1 and 2; Theorem 3 (Betti number analysis) lacks empirical support (R3.7).  
5. **Loss function and regularizers poorly detailed.** The Consistency Regularizer and Parameter Regularizer in Eq. (15) are introduced with insufficient explanation, and the loss function itself is not thoroughly described (R3.8).  
6. **Limited scope of models and datasets evaluated.**  
   – Only a few CNN-based BIQA models are tested; results for the transformer-based MUSIQ show negligible gains, suggesting the method may not generalize to non-CNN architectures (R1.2, R2.4).  
   – The largest IQA database (FLIVE) is omitted without justification, despite the paper’s claim that generalization is limited by small-scale training datasets (R1 Q2).  
   – Cross-database experiments are limited: Tables 1-2 lack a baseline with μ,ν=0 to isolate the loss function’s benefit; Table 4 uses only one pair (KADID→CID) without justifying its representativeness (R1.1).  
   – The paper does not test on hand-crafted models (e.g., BMPRI) or additional DNN methods (e.g., RichIQA, StairIQA) (R2.4, R2 Q4).  
7. **No analysis of loss function impact.** Theorem 2 indicates the loss function strongly affects generalization, but the paper does not analyze or compare with advanced IQA loss functions (e.g., NIMA, Norm-in-Norm) (R4.3, R4 Q).  
8. **Generality to other tasks and distortions not discussed.** Whether the findings apply to CNN-based general regression tasks or to different distortion types (UGC vs. synthetic) is unexplored (R4.4, R1 Q3).  
9. **Missing RMSE metric.** Only SRCC and PLCC are reported; RMSE would provide insight into absolute prediction error (R1.3).  
10. **Readability and presentation issues.** The paper’s organization and clarity need improvement; several symbols (e.g., b, M, δ, Lϕ in Eqs. 7-8) are undefined, and there are writing errors (e.g., Fig. 1(b) y-axis label) (R1.4, R4.1, R3.9).
