## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies transfer of high-level RL skills across environments with different state and action spaces. It compares three approaches: (1) separate policies per environment, (2) pretraining on one environment then finetuning on another with state-space padding to bridge mismatches, and (3) an environment-agnostic shared latent policy (SEAL) trained on data from multiple environments. Experiments on Meta-World (MW) and Franka Kitchen (FK) show that SEAL achieves some skill transfer and that adjusting the data mixture between environments can improve sample efficiency (up to 84% fewer gradient update steps). The paper emphasizes simplicity and minimal architectural modifications to existing algorithms (SAC).

### Strengths
- The problem of transferring policies across environments with different state and action spaces is important and timely, a step toward generalist agents. [R1, R3, R4]
- The SEAL architecture (state → latent → task-specific action decoders) is intuitive and the overall approach is easy to follow. [R1, R2, R3]
- The method uses minimal modifications on top of standard RL algorithms (SAC) and the authors plan to release code. [R2]
- The paper is straightforward and does not overclaim; it studies a challenging transfer scenario (state/action space differences) beyond reward changes or in-task transfer. [R2, R4]
- The finding that the data mixture ratio between environments influences skill transfer is an interesting and potentially significant result. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and relation to prior work**: The shared latent space approach is common in prior multi-task RL works; the paper instantiates it in one specific way (e.g., multi-headed SAC) without algorithmic novelty. [R2, R3] Reviewer 4 disagrees, stating the architecture is novel to their knowledge. [R4] The paper lacks discussion of related methods such as Hausman et al., D'Eramo et al., and meta-RL. [R3, R4] The term “skills” is used but does not align with standard formulation (options). [R3]
- **Extremely limited experimental evaluation**: Only 2 random seeds are reported (insufficient; at least 5 with IQM recommended per Agarwal et al. 2021). [R4] Experiments are run on only 4 tasks across 2 environments; no diversity in task complexity or similarity is tested. [R2, R3] The number of network updates is used as a sample efficiency metric, which is questionable. [R3] There is no direct comparison between the three methods (separate training, pretrain+finetune, SEAL) in a single plot; individual plots suggest SEAL underperforms separate training. [R1]
- **Pretrain+finetune baseline is poorly motivated**: Padding the state space does not account for semantic alignment of dimensions (e.g., end-effector position may map to object position), making the baseline questionable. [R1, R2]
- **Insufficient analysis of the learned latent (SEAL) space**: No qualitative or quantitative evaluation of whether the latent captures meaningful structure (e.g., distances, dynamics) vs. arbitrary embedding. [R2] The paper does not discuss failure modes (e.g., overfitting to source tasks, inability to generalize). [R2] The explanations for why data mixture matters remain intuitive and lack empirical grounding. [R4] No study of different representation objectives (reward-free vs. task-specific). [R2]
- **Missing architectural and training details**: Hyperparameters (latent dimension, activation functions, regularization), architectural specifics (whether state embeddings and action heads share parameters), and design choices for evaluation (100 episodes, 50000 gradient steps) are not substantiated. [R2, R4] The training procedure for SEAL is not formalized as an algorithm. [R4] Table 1 contains unexplained N/A columns that require cross-referencing with Appendix (Tables 4–6). [R4]
- **Clarity and formatting issues**: Section 2 lacks mathematical definitions for trajectories and task sampling probability p(t). [R4] The paper does not clarify whether the policy outputs a probability distribution or a bounded action space. [R4] Section 3.3 should use distinct symbols for state embeddings and action heads if they are not shared. [R4]
- **Unaddressed reviewer questions that overlap with missing experiments/analysis**:
  - How does padding enable data sharing when state dimensions have different semantic meanings? [R1]
  - How does SEAL perform when environments are more different (e.g., legged robot vs. tabletop manipulator)? [R1]
  - Can qualitative results show how the latent representation captures environment structure? [R2]
  - Would the approach work on pixel-based tasks (e.g., CNN+SAC with shared embedding)? [R2]
  - Does gradient surgery (Yu et al. 2020) relate to the observed negative interference in MTMHSAC? [R4]
  - How does diversity of skills in the source environment impact transfer? What causes overfitting of value functions in MW? [R4]
  - Do different neural architectures enable scaling laws for more complex environments? [R4]
  - Is the alignment of task IDs (Appendix D) related to relational structure (Mohan et al. 2023), and is this common practice? [R4]
