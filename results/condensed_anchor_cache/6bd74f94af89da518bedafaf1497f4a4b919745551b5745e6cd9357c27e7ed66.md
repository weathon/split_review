## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides theoretical and empirical evidence that Structured State Space Models (SSMs) and Transformers are fundamentally limited in function composition and multi-step reasoning. It proves that one-layer SSMs cannot efficiently compose functions without impractically large state sizes, that Chain-of-Thought prompting requires a number of steps that grows exponentially with composition depth, and that multi-layer SSMs are constrained to log-space computation (class L), preventing them from solving NL-complete problems unless L=NL. Empirical evaluations on function composition, multi-digit multiplication, dynamic programming, and Einstein’s puzzle corroborate these limitations, showing significant performance degradation even with advanced prompting.

### Strengths
- Novel theoretical insight on SSMs’ limited ability to perform function composition, echoing similar prior work on Transformers (R1).
- Experiments validate the theory across multiple composition tasks (function, spatial, temporal, relational) (R1, R2).
- Originality: precisely defines the problem and presents new theorems (R2).
- Clarity and rigor: theoretical and empirical sections are clearly structured, concepts are well defined (R2).
- Significance: lower bound on Chain-of-Thought steps required for iterated function composition is an interesting result highlighting practical challenges (R2).
- Important contribution extending rigorous complexity-theoretic analysis from Transformers to SSMs, proving sharp resource requirements for function composition (R3).
- Well written, well referenced, settles important questions about limitations of current sequence-modeling architectures (R4).
- This kind of theoretical work is very much needed (R4).

### Weaknesses
*(Reviewer opinions were split: two reviewers (scores 8) considered the paper a strong contribution with no major weaknesses, while two reviewers (scores 6) raised concerns. One reviewer (score 6) expressed serious doubts about the correctness and formalization of the theoretical results; those concerns are listed first.)*

**Substantial theoretical concerns (R1):**  
- The proof of Theorem 1 appears to rely on the specific prompt ordering g → f → x. It is unclear whether the proof works for other orderings, especially the intuitively easier order x → g → f.  
- The definition of Chain-of-Thought in Theorem 2 is not clearly distinguished from standard autoregressive decoding; the paper should clarify.  
- Theorem 4 has multiple issues:  
  - The theorem uses the class L (decision problems) but should use FL (function problems).  
  - The theorem assumes \(p,d = O(\mathrm{poly}(N))\) but the proof assumes \(d = O(1), p = O(\log N)\) (lines 359, 377, 379) and implicitly \(dp = O(\log N)\).  
  - No assumptions are stated about how \(A_t, B_t, C_t, D_t\) (functions of \(x_t\)) are computed; the space complexity of these computations is missing.  
  - The conclusion that SSMs are equivalent to log-space algorithms is an artifact of the assumption \(Lpd = O(\log N)\). If \(p,d = O(1)\) (realistic SSMs), the theorem would imply linear SSMs can only decide regular languages (an unexpected and unaddressed consequence). If \(p,d = \mathrm{poly}(N)\), the limitation disappears. This sensitivity to the scaling assumption indicates the model does not capture actual SSMs.  
  - The paper should formalize inputs and outputs as strings (as in FL) and consider infinite precision to avoid the floating-point limitation; the current proof does not extend to infinite precision.  
- The criticism that SSMs’ computational power is bounded by the space used to represent floating points equally applies to the cited works (Merrill & Sabharwal 2023, 2024) that Theorem 3 is based on. Changing the assumption on how precision \(p\) scales with \(N\) even slightly can invalidate the theorem (e.g., if \(p = \mathrm{poly}(n)\), SSMs could potentially solve P-complete problems).

**Missing discussion and future work (R2):**  
- The paper lacks a limitations and future work section for practitioners; it should discuss specific architectural modifications suggested by the results.  
- In practice, complicated reasoning tasks often use tree search (e.g., AlphaProof, GPT-f, HyperTree Search) and self-correction (Self-Correction, SCoRe) beyond naive chain-of-thought augmentation. The paper should discuss how these methods relate to the theoretical findings.

**Comparison to prior work (R3):**  
- More comparison to the results of Peng et al. would be helpful for readers.

**Accessibility and framing (R4):**  
- The main manuscript could provide more intuitive background on communication complexity and computational complexity concepts to make the methodology accessible to a general ML audience.  
- The paper should discuss the possible gap between formal theoretical results and practical settings, the (non-)robustness of the results to closing that gap, and what empirical studies are needed to bridge it.  

**Minor issues (R4, R3):**  
- Formatting error causing the heading of Section 7 to come too close to the previous paragraph.  
- A summary figure of empirical results would be useful in the main manuscript.  
- The “Implications for General Artificial Intelligence” paragraph seems better placed in the Conclusions or a separate “Implications” section rather than in “Related Work”.  
- Clarify the relationship between the numerical experiments and the main theorem with respect to the number of layers (R3).
