## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces *rehearsal* for policy optimization: the agent plans over a diverse set of learned dynamics models and acts adaptively in the true environment. The proposed algorithm, ReDM, generates a set of dynamics models by optimizing for diversity and eligibility (ability of random trajectories to achieve high reward), then trains a meta-policy that can adapt to any model in the set. When interaction data is available, it is used to regularize model generation. Experiments show ReDM can learn a valid policy with zero interaction data on InvertedPendulum, MountainCar, Acrobot, and outperforms offline RL baselines on Hopper and HalfCheetah with limited (200/5000 transitions) or mismatched random data. The method is motivated by a theoretical bound (Thm 3.3) decomposing the performance gap into model error, adaptation cost, and environment support error, which are linked to diversity and eligibility.

### Strengths
- The problem setting (learning a generalizable policy with minimal or no environment interaction) is novel, important, and well-motivated by the cognitive notion of rehearsal.
- The proposed method (ReDM) is clearly described, with comprehensive theoretical analysis (Thm 3.3, error bounds) that grounds the design of diversity and eligibility rewards.
- The approach of generating a diverse set of dynamics models from only the reward and termination functions, without interaction data, is original and distinct from traditional model-based RL (e.g., MAPLE uses ensemble learning).
- Experiments are insightful and include ablation studies (number of models, diversity vs. eligibility trade-off, zero-data setting) that help understand the method’s mechanism.
- The method is shown to work in both zero-data and limited-data offline settings, outperforming prior model-free and model-based baselines on Hopper and HalfCheetah.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope**: Evaluations are restricted to low-dimensional MuJoCo tasks (InvertedPendulum, MountainCar, Acrobot, Hopper, HalfCheetah). Missing comparisons on Walker2d, more complex/high-dimensional environments (e.g., Ant, Humanoid, image-based tasks), and full D4RL datasets. One reviewer notes the method’s scaling to high-dimensional state/action spaces and sparse-reward settings is unclear. A reviewer also asks whether ReDM works on MuJoCo with zero interaction data (only tested on simpler gym tasks).
- **Strong assumptions limit applicability**:  
  - Requires access to the reward function, termination function, and initial state distribution of the target environment. This is unrealistic in many real-world scenarios.  
  - Assumes dynamics can be easily parameterized as a neural network and optimized via RL, which may be unstable or inefficient for complex dynamics.  
  - The eligibility reward uses a random policy to sample N trajectories; this may be inefficient in continuous state/action spaces and may not explore effectively, making assessment of model quality inaccurate. The choice of N is not reported.  
  - The method struggles with sparse-reward environments because random trajectories may never achieve non-zero reward, making the eligibility term ineffective.
- **Connection between diversity/eligibility and the theoretical gap (Thm 3.3) is not made precise**: It is unclear why increasing diversity directly controls epsilon_e and epsilon_a. In fact, if all models are identical, epsilon_a=0, so diversity might increase epsilon_a rather than decrease it. The paper lacks a direct proof that the proposed optimization objectives (Sec 3.3) control the three error terms from Thm 3.3.
- **Missing discussion of closely related work**: MAPLE (very similar but different model generation process) is not mentioned in the related work. Also missing references to model-based generalization methods and unsupervised RL for diverse skill discovery.
- **Presentation issues**:  
  - No standard deviations reported for Table 1, and no error bars in Figure 7.  
  - Terms like \(D_{TV}\) are used in the main theorem without definition in the main text.  
  - Algorithm 1 is uninformative; better replaced by Algorithms 5/6 from the appendix.  
  - Figure 1 x-axis label misleading (“Tasks” vs. better description).  
  - Figure 3 caption is insufficient (“model loss” not explained).  
  - The axis “Number of models” in Figure 3 should read [0,10,20,30,40]?  
  - Figures 2 and 3 should be swapped.  
  - The paper writing is overly focused on theory; a figure explaining the rehearsal framework and its benefits would help readability.
- **Unclear evaluation details**:  
  - In Figure 6, it’s not clear what “relative performance” is (relative to what baseline?).  
  - In Figure 7, are results averaged over Hopper and HalfCheetah and over each gravity level?  
  - The explanation of the optimal policy gap is confusing (sentence: “This discrepancy highlights the candidate model set’s capability to derive a proficient policy in the model itself” – does “model” mean true dynamics?).  
  - The claim that biasedness in interaction data hinders policy optimization in offline RL is not always true (expert trajectories can help avoid dangerous states).
- **Setting ambiguity without interaction data**: In the zero-data setting, there is no information about the target environment’s dynamics. It is not justified how the generated models can approximate the real dynamics sufficiently well. There is a risk that the generated models are far from the target, and the method lacks grounding points. One reviewer questions whether ReDM could work in D4RL (Hopper, HalfCheetah) without any interaction data.
- **Computational complexity and training stability not analyzed**: The alternating training of dynamics models and meta-policy may be unstable or converge poorly; no discussion of parameter count, training time, or convergence properties.
- **Use of random policy for eligibility may be suboptimal**: A reviewer suggests a human-crafted planner could be more effective and practical, as it is as easily accessible as the reward function in many settings.
