## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Adversarial Adaptive Sampling (AAS), a min-max formulation that simultaneously optimizes a PINN solution and a deep generative model (normalizing flow with soft Lipschitz constraint via Sobolev regularization) which samples collocation points adaptively. The key idea is to minimize the residual while pushing the residual-induced distribution toward a uniform distribution by implicitly embedding the Wasserstein-1 distance into the loss, derived from the dual of an optimal transport problem. Theoretically, the paper shows that an optimal solution exists where the normalized squared residual converges to a uniform distribution in Wasserstein distance. The method is tested on two toy problems and one high-dimensional nonlinear PDE, compared to DAS (Tang et al., 2023) and RAR. Reviewer scores: 5, 6, 1, 8.

### Strengths
- The min-max formulation for adaptive sampling with an optimal transport connection is novel and promising (Reviewers 1,2,4).
- The paper provides good theoretical proofs of convergence for the adaptive learning distributions (Reviewers 1,3).
- The method shows clear numerical benefits on the considered examples, particularly on sparse data and the two-peak PDE, outperforming DAS (Reviewers 1,2).
- The motivation is clear, and the theory and algorithm are well described (Reviewers 3,4; though Reviewer 2 found the presentation hard to follow).
- The approach brings confidence to inference by linking variance reduction to a uniform residual profile (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines and benchmarks:** The experiments lack comparisons to more established adaptive sampling techniques, especially for high-dimensional problems; only one type of PDE is tested (linear/nonlinear), and tests on singularly perturbed or parametric equations are missing (Reviewers 1,4). RAR is not tested for PDEs in (12) and (13) (Reviewer 1 question).
- **Novelty and similarity to prior work:** The algorithm is alternating (maximization/minimization) rather than truly simultaneous; the claim of "first time to minimize the residual and seek the optimal training set simultaneously" is not fully justified compared to DAS-PINNs (Reviewer 1). The work is very similar to Tang et al. (2023) except using a GAN instead of normalizing flows for the pushforward map (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing training details and robustness metrics:** Training times and standard deviations across different seeds are not reported, making it difficult to assess practical efficiency and robustness; the adversarial (GAN-style) training and the use of the REINFORCE trick (high variance) may cause instability and hinder convergence (Reviewer 1). Implementation details (number of parameters for the PINN and density networks) are missing (Reviewer 2).
- **Motivation for generative model vs. simpler sampling:** It is unclear why a full generative model (normalizing flow) is necessary compared to simpler methods that sample proportional to the squared residual; the computational overhead is not justified (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear theoretical link to optimal transport:** The minimization step only provides an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance (page 6), not a guaranteed decrease; Theorem 1 asserts the existence of a path to uniformity, but it is not shown that gradient descent follows that path. Plots of residual evolution across all experiments are missing (Reviewer 1). Notation is unclear: Assumption 1 refers to \(r\) as an operator without specifying domain/codomain; Theorem 1 uses \(\nu\) while derivation uses \(\mu_r\) (same?); the quantity approximated by KRNet is not followed up after Section 3.2; Equation (9) introduces \(p^*\) without definition; the abbreviation KRNet is not cited in the last paragraph of page 3 (Reviewer 2). The variance reduction proof is not explicitly provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Point cloud quality and high-dimensional concerns:** The generated point clouds contain very short distances (not locally smooth), resembling poor FEM node distributions; this suggests wasted calculations and may lead to a curse of dimensionality. A possible remedy would be to use quasi-random point distributions or incorporate repulsive forces (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of discussion on limitations:** The paper does not discuss further development or limitations, especially regarding the challenge of controlling the Lipschitz norm constraint in the regularization term (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor presentation issues:** Use `\citep` throughout; Eq. (4) should have min-max on both sides; the term "proper constraint" is undefined; the last paragraph of Section 3 is convoluted; it is unclear why \(I^D\) is used instead of \(\Omega\) when introducing KRNet; text in the training-set figures is barely readable; typo: "resultd" (Reviewer 1). The paper is generally hard to read with unclear notation (Reviewer 2).
