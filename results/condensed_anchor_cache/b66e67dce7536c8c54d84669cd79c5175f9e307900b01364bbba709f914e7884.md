## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies scaling laws for equivariant architectures, specifically an E(3)-equivariant transformer (GATr) on a rigid-body dynamics benchmark. Experiments vary model size, training steps, and dataset size. Findings: (1) equivariance improves data efficiency, but data augmentation can close the gap given enough epochs; (2) compute scaling follows a power law, with equivariant models outperforming non-equivariant ones at each tested compute budget (nominal FLOPs); (3) optimal allocation of compute between model size and training duration differs between model types. The paper is timely, well-motivated, and addresses an important problem for geometric deep learning. However, the study is limited to a single synthetic task (3D n-body dynamics), a single equivariance group (E(3)), and a single architecture class (transformers), making broad claims about “equivariance at scale” overstated. There is significant disagreement: two reviewers (R1,R2; scores 5,5) find the work sound and useful; two (R3,R4; scores 3,3) consider the experimental scope too narrow and the conclusions misleading, especially regarding the use of nominal FLOPs versus wall-clock time and the relevance of the infinite-data, single-epoch setting.

### Strengths
- **Timely and well-motivated** (R1,R2,R3,R4). The question of whether equivariance matters at scale is actively debated; systematic empirical guidance is needed.
- **Clear writing and explicit assumptions** (R1,R2). The paper is well written and makes design choices clear (R1,R2). Limitations (Section 5) are highlighted, including throughput differences in Figure 6 (R1).
- **Exhaustive ablations** (R2,R4). Experiments cover scaling of model size, training tokens, and compute across a reasonable range (R2,R3). The study of optimal allocation of compute (model size vs. training duration) is valuable for practitioners (R4).
- **Technical soundness** (R1). The experimental setup is carefully designed; assumptions are explicit.
- **Specific findings** (R2,R4). Power-law scaling results, data efficiency comparisons, and the difference in optimal compute allocation between equivariant and non-equivariant models are concrete contributions (R4). The data efficiency result with augmentation closing the gap is noted (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope: only one task and one equivariance group** (R1,R2,R3,R4). The study uses only a synthetic 3D rigid-body dynamics problem with E(3)-equivariance. This is insufficient to support the broad title and abstract claims (R1,R2,R3,R4). Many important problems (e.g., protein folding, molecular generation, medical imaging, PDEs, image classification) are not examined (R1,R2,R3,R4). For rigid-body dynamics, state-of-the-art models (e.g., FIGNet) are not equivariant, and a canonical reference frame (gravity + center of mass) with O(2) augmentations is standard and likely more efficient (R4). Therefore, the paper effectively studies only “Does E(3) equivariance matter at scale for 3D n-body dynamics?” (R1).
- **Only one architecture class (transformers)** (R2,R3,R4). Other prevalent equivariant architectures (e.g., spherical-harmonics-based Equiformer, GNNs, convolutional networks) and non-transformer non-equivariant baselines are not considered (R2,R3,R4). For PDEs, Fourier Neural Operators and CNNs are more common than transformers (R3). Discrete groups (e.g., C_n, D_n) are not studied; community results differ for image rotations (R2,R4).
- **Use of nominal FLOPs instead of wall-clock time is misleading** (R1,R4). Conclusions about compute efficiency based on nominal FLOPs ignore poor GPU utilization of equivariant models (R1,R4). A version of Figure 1 using GPU-hours or inference/wall-clock time should be provided (R1,R4). The gap between models might shrink (R4). Inference speed comparisons are missing (R1).
- **Unrealistic infinite-data / single-epoch setting** (R4). The “infinite-data” regime uses a single epoch, making data augmentation irrelevant. This scenario is not typical in practice; real applications often have limited data or multiple epochs. The emphasis on this limit is misleading and should be discussed separately (R4). For finite data, non-equivariant models with augmentation can catch up given enough epochs (R1).
- **Overclaimed general conclusions** (R1,R4). The claim that “strong inductive biases… may be less beneficial for large datasets and large compute budgets” is too strong given the limited experimental scope (R1). The conclusion “equivariant models outperform non-equivariant ones at each tested compute budget” is based on nominal FLOPs and is misleading (R4).
- **Hyperparameter tuning not sufficiently specified or separate** (R2,R4). The same learning rate and batch size are used for both model classes, even though optimal values likely differ (R4). The procedure for obtaining the family of hyperparameters is unclear (R4). Batch size should be maximized per model’s memory usage to simulate real resource allocation (R4).
- **Missing discussion of related work on scaling equivariance** (R1). Frame averaging, canonicalization, and other techniques for scaling equivariant networks are not discussed (R1).
- **Clarity issues in scaling law section** (R3,R4). Notations in Section 3.3 are confusing: D is used for both training length (lines 256,295) and number of training tokens (line 268) (R3). Takeaway from Table 2 coefficients is unclear (R3). Iso-FLOP lines are not shown in Figure 3 (R4).
- **Hierarchical attention design is under-explained** (R1). It assumes knowledge of which tokens belong to which object (masking). This adds another problem-specific inductive bias, making comparisons with architecturally simpler non-equivariant models less general (R1). It is not shown that this modification is equally beneficial for both models (R2).
- **No study of data quality or noise** (R2). The effect of noisy labels on the equivariance vs. non-equivariance choice is not addressed (R2).
- **Optimization details missing** (R2). The susceptibility to learning rate scheduling, optimizer choice, and early stopping criteria is not analyzed (R2). The early stopping criterion is unspecified (R2).
- **Augmentation details not fully described** (R4). The distribution of augmentations (especially translations) is not given; test data augmentation status is unclear (R4).
- **No comparison to state-of-the-art rigid-body dynamics models** (R4). The baselines do not match community-reported performance; an equivariant version of a top model (e.g., FIGNet) would strengthen the case (R4).
