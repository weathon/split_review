## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a deep reinforcement learning approach for the stochastic k-server problem on graphs, using a graph convolutional network (GCN) backbone with a decomposition of the action-value function into global and local terms. The aim is a scalable, transferable generalist policy applicable across graph topologies and request distributions without retraining. Experiments compare to baselines including Work Function Algorithm (WFA) and greedy methods, reporting total travel costs on various graphs.

### Strengths
- The stochastic k-server problem is well-motivated and important, with practical applications such as ambulance dispatch. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Training a generalist policy that can solve any k-server problem without retraining is an interesting and valuable objective. (R1, R2, R4)
- The proposed RL method is scalable, achieving high accuracy on graphs up to ~1000 nodes and outperforming several baselines in travel cost. (R2; minority reviewer more positive: R2 emphasizes scalability and strong empirical performance)
- The comparison of a learning-based approach to non-learning methods provides useful insights. (R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and related work**: GCN+RL for combinatorial optimization is not new; the idea of using graph embeddings (e.g., Struct2vec, GAT) and training over a distribution of graphs to obtain a generalist policy is already well-established (surveys [1,2]; Khalil et al. 2017; Kool et al. 2018). The paper fails to discuss this line of work and differentiate its contributions. (R3, R4; R1 also notes missing prior work Jiang et al. 2018)
- **Action-value decomposition unclear**: The purpose of the decomposition into \(Q^{\text{global}}(s)\) and \(Q^{\text{local}}(s^{\text{local}}_{x_a}, a)\) is not formally defined. Equations (3)-(5) lack clarity; the validity of the approximation in Eq. (5) and how much the estimated \(Q(s,a)\) differs from the true value under a given policy is not addressed. (R1)
- **Missing baselines**: Key baselines are absent: Harmonic, MLP DQN, and Balance are missing from Table 3 (unknown arrival probabilities); the algorithm from Khadiev & Yagafarov (2020) for trees is omitted; only Vanilla DQN is compared, not enhanced versions (e.g., PER, Rainbow) or actor-critic methods (SAC, PPO). (R1, R4)
- **Incomplete experimental analysis**:
  - Performance is reported only as total travel cost; no optimality gaps or competitive ratios are provided, making it hard to assess absolute quality. (R2)
  - The paper does not analyze the policy to explain why the RL approach beats well-known baselines (especially WFA). (R2)
  - Vanilla DQN shows comparable performance to the proposed method on smaller graphs and trees (Table 1). (R1)
  - The transfer analysis (Figure 3) is limited; only one n value is shown; scaling beyond n=100 is not examined. (R1)
  - The "generalist GCN-RL" performance is very close to that of plain GCN-RL, raising questions about the benefit of training on a distribution. (R3)
  - Instance difficulty is not discussed; results for offline k-server (when arrival information is fully specified) are missing, which would help interpret the seeming superiority. (R1, R2)
  - Contradictory statements about which methods require "burn-in" for estimating arrival probabilities; it is unclear which methods assume known arrival distributions. (R1)
  - Training computational effort is not compared among the three learning baselines, only post-training efficiency is considered. (R1)
- **Missing ablations**: The only ablation removes the global Q-value component. There is no investigation of: different RL methods, different decomposition approaches, the effect of training distribution over problem instances, or the individual contributions of the GCN backbone vs. the decomposition. (R1 claimed it is impossible to ablate the two components, which reduces general applicability of the decomposition method.) (R1, R4)
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: No theoretical results are provided for the decomposition approach, the generalization capabilities, or the optimality of the learned policy. (R1, R4; R1 notes that ignoring theory makes the problem seem easy compared to other DRL applications)
- **Writing and presentation issues**: Problem objective and assumptions (online vs. offline) are not formally stated in Section 2.1; notation is inconsistent (e.g., \(\pi^*\) presented as a function of a single state then of four inputs); terminology (e.g., "low computational effort") is vague; the phrase "perform as an optimal policy" needs suboptimality error; the reference to the main DQN paper is missing. (R1)
- **Limited interpretability**: The reasons for the generalist policy's superiority are not analyzed; no policy analysis or visualization is provided. (R2)
