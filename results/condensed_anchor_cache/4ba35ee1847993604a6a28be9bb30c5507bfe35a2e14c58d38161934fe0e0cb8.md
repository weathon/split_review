## Merged Review

### Summary
All reviewers rate the paper 5 (borderline) and recommend reject. They agree the task of updating factual knowledge in text-to-image models is interesting and ReFACT’s approach (editing a single text-encoder layer in closed form) is simple, efficient, and well-explained. However, significant concerns are raised about limited evaluation scope, missing baselines, insufficient generalization/specificity testing, unclear comparison to prior work (textual inversion, [1]), lack of hyperparameter ablation, and real-world applicability. Reviewer 4 finds technical novelty very limited; Reviewer 3 is less confident (3). All request stronger experimental validation to demonstrate advantage over trivial alternatives.

### Strengths
- The task of updating outdated facts in text-to-image models without full retraining is practically important and interesting (all reviewers).
- ReFACT is simple and easy to implement: it updates only a tiny fraction of parameters in a single text-encoder layer using a closed-form solution without extra training data (R1, R3, R4).
- The method achieves efficient, precise factual updates while preserving image quality, with strong generalization to related concepts as shown in experiments (R1).
- The paper is well-organized, clear, and results are reproducible (R1, R3).
- Empirical results demonstrate successful replacement of old concepts with new ones (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation scope**: The evaluation dataset is relatively small; more varied prompts (e.g., synonyms, paraphrases, “current President of the USA”) are needed to test robustness (R1, R3). R2 specifically asks to test unseen prompts like “prince of wales is drinking coffee” that were not seen during finetuning.
- **Missing simple baseline**: A trivial baseline—replacing the target phrase with the intended new meaning directly in the prompt (e.g., “President of the USA” → “Joe Biden”)—is not included. This baseline would likely succeed on many shown examples without any model editing and must be compared quantitatively (R3).
- **Generalization and specificity concerns**: It is unclear how ReFACT handles synonyms or other descriptions (e.g., “American president”, “head of the military”) and ambiguous prompts (e.g., “a photo of the president” without specifying country) (R3). The provided generalization evaluation is not comprehensive enough.
- **Overfitting risk**: The careful selection of positive/negative prompts may cause overfitting—the method performs well on the chosen prompts but may fail on other related sentences (R2). Example shown in Fig1/3 may not hold for unseen phrasing.
- **Evaluation methodology unclear**: It is not specified whether the text encoder is updated once per concept or once per image during the ROADS/TIME evaluation. Also, the paper should report FID/CLIP scores on standard text-to-image benchmarks (e.g., MS-COCO) to verify that the model’s general generation ability is preserved (R2).
- **Limited technical novelty**: The approach largely applies a prior method [1] to text-to-image models, offering incremental contribution (R4). Comparison with existing editing approaches like “textual inversion” is insufficiently detailed—differences should be clearly stated (R1).
- **Missing ablation on hyperparameters**: The number of negative samples \(N\) in the contrastive loss is large but not ablated; its impact should be studied (R4).
- **Real-world applicability issues**: Each mistaken concept requires human feedback and additional fine-tuning, which does not scale. The method cannot handle unseen visual concepts (R4).
- **Scaling to multiple edits**: It is unclear whether multiple edits targeting similar text-encoder regions (e.g., presidents of many countries) would interfere with each other (R3).
- **Context-dependent edits**: The method currently applies edits globally—there is no mechanism to make edits conditional on context (e.g., leaf color depending on season) (R3).
