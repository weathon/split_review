## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Defensive Prompt Patch (DPP), a prompt-based defense against jailbreak attacks on LLMs. DPP uses a hierarchical genetic algorithm (HGA) from AutoDAN with an added utility-preservation loss to iteratively refine a suffix (or prefix) prompt that is appended to every query. The final product is a human-interpretable string, e.g., “Please examine the past rules carefully…”. Experiments on Llama-2-7B-Chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 report low Attack Success Rate (ASR) with minimal utility degradation, outperforming several baseline defenses. Reviewers agree the method is novel and achieves strong defense performance, but express significant concerns about clarity, methodology (especially reliance on HGA and prototype selection), inadequate ablation and comparisons, limited model scope, and insufficient analysis of why DPP works better than simpler prompts.

### Strengths
- DPP achieves a significant reduction in jailbreak ASR while maintaining minimal utility degradation compared to existing defense strategies (R1, R3, R4).
- The defense framework leverages two log-likelihood based scores to simultaneously optimize for low ASR and high utility; this appears interesting and novel to a good extent (R4).
- DPP is effective across multiple LLM platforms (Llama-2-7B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2) and shows robustness against adaptive attacks (R3, R4).
- The final product of the method is a simple, human-interpretable string appended to each query, making deployment straightforward (R2).
- The paper conducts a comprehensive evaluation against various non-adaptive and adaptive attacks, providing convincing defense results (R4).  

**Disagreement note:** Reviewer 2 gives a lower overall rating (3) and criticizes clarity, methodology, and lack of ablation much more strongly than others; that reviewer also finds the presentation poor (score 1). Reviewer 1 and 3 express moderate methodological concerns but still rate 5.

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear motivation for human readability of the defense prompt.** Unlike attack methods where readability evades perplexity-based detection, defense prompts do not need to be human-readable. The paper emphasizes this property (Table 1) but does not justify it, and token-wise methods (e.g., RPO) are not considered (R1).  
2. **Unclear contribution and heavy borrowing from AutoDAN’s HGA.** The main algorithmic novelty is a straightforward utility-preservation loss; the HGA is taken directly from AutoDAN with no modifications. The choice of HGA for defense is not justified, and the unique challenges of this optimization setting (e.g., added loss making convergence harder) are not analyzed (R1). No insight is provided into why HGA is suitable (R3).  
3. **Initial prototype selection is ad hoc and not ablated.** The algorithm depends on the choice of initial defense prompts, but these are selected without systematic justification. Unlike AutoDAN (which uses existing human-designed jailbreak prompts), no discussion or ablation on initial prompts is given (R1, R4). It seems unlikely that only the prototype prompt is well-designed while iterations lack significant improvement; a fair prompt initialization across methods should be tested (R4).  
4. **Lack of ablation studies and comparisons to simpler alternative prompts.** The paper does not ablate each component of the DPP algorithm. Appendix H suggests that simply appending unrelated prompts may be competitive, yet this hypothesis is not tested (R2). For instance, GoalPrioritization and Self-Reminder produce prompts with similar meanings, but it is unclear why DPP performs better (R4).  
5. **Insufficient comparison with other state-of-the-art defense methods.** Both prompt-based and model-based defenses exist; the paper evaluates only a few baselines and lacks a broader comparison, especially with recent prompt-based approaches (R4).  
6. **Limited model evaluation.** Only two models are tested (Llama-2-7B-Chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2), both 7B and released March 2024 or earlier. Generalizability to other prominent LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) is not explored (R3). On Mistral, utility degradation does not outperform other defense methods (R2).  
7. **Writing clarity and presentation issues.** The paper is unclear throughout: Figure 1 is unexplained (ellipse around datapoints), Table 1 claims “Gradient-Based Search” but Algorithm 1 does not involve gradients, adaptive attacks description (line 364) does not match the actual evaluation in Appendix I, and the ASR computation differs across attacks without explanation (R2). The appendix has poor LaTeX formatting (Algorithm 4, typos like “segements”, “offsrpings”) and does not seem ready for submission (R2).  
8. **Time cost and overhead not reported.** HGA is known to be very time-consuming (R3). The computational overhead of DPP (optimization and inference) is not evaluated or compared to other baselines (R1, R3).  
9. **Lack of insight into why DPP improves defense.** Although perplexity is mentioned, it does not explain why defense performance is enhanced. More examples of intermediate prompts during iterations, along with their defense performances, are needed to understand the algorithm’s effectiveness (R4). The adaptive attack setup is unclear: for adaptive GCG, it is not specified whether the modifiable subset includes the DPP tokens (R2).  
10. **Reporting of results may be for selected best DPPs.** It is unclear whether reported ASRs are for manually picked DPPs or for randomly sampled ones (R2).
