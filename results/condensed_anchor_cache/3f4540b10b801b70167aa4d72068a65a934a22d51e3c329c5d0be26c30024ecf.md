## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Gradient Trajectory Pursuit (GTP) for data selection in LLMs. GTP performs joint selection of data points by matching gradient trajectories under an L0-norm regularized objective, moving beyond independent top‑k selection. The algorithm uses compressive sampling for efficiency and can be distributed. Experiments on ALFWorld and three instruction‑tuning evaluation sets show that GTP consistently outperforms top‑k selection and competitive baselines, e.g., achieving full performance with as little as 0.5% of the data.

### Strengths
1. **Timely and important problem** – Data selection for effective LLM training is a critical issue, and the paper addresses it in a well‑motivated way (all reviewers).
2. **Intuitive and reasonable idea** – Moving from independent top‑k selection to joint selection based on gradient trajectory matching is intuitive and captures the collaborative potential among data samples (R2, R3).
3. **Consistent empirical improvements** – GTP shows clear performance gains over existing gradient‑based baselines (especially LESS) on ALFWorld and instruction‑tuning tasks. For example, it can use as low as 0.5% data to reach full performance (R2, R3, R4). R4 notes that the gains are “fairly impressive” though somewhat modest against the strongest baseline.
4. **Clear and reproducible presentation** – The paper is written with enough detail that the described approach should be reproducible (R1, R4). R2, however, found the presentation substantially needing improvement (see Weaknesses).
5. **Non‑trivial contribution to the field** – The departure from independent selection and the use of compressive sampling are seen as a meaningful advance (R2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty compared to LESS** – The method is very similar to LESS (Xia et al., 2024); the main differences are replacing cosine similarity with L2 norm, adding sparsity regularization, and using ideas from Needell & Tropp (2009) to avoid greedy top‑k selection. Performance improvements over LESS are incremental (R1, R3, R4). R1 finds the contribution “very similar” and R4 calls it “not so large.”
2. **Unclear target stage of LLM training** – The paper does not specify whether GTP is designed for pre‑training, post‑training, or both. The heuristic of using early training parameters is ambiguous, and it is unclear how the method interacts with optimizer and learning‑rate schedule choices (R1).
3. **Insufficient parameter selection guidance** – It is unclear how to choose the set of model parameters for gradient matching, how large that set should be, and why the early‑training trajectory is an effective choice (R1).
4. **Limited experimental scope** – The main multi‑baseline comparison is conducted only on ALFWorld. On instruction‑tuning datasets, only LESS is compared. Missing comparisons include Random, G‑DIG, and other models from different families and sizes, especially Llama (which could not be used for reasons not fully explained). The strong performance of the random baseline in some setups is not explained (R1, R2, R3, R4). R2 and R4 note that experiments are less thorough than prior work (LESS).
5. **Writing and presentation issues** – Reviewer 2 found Section 3 very hard to follow: the derivation of Equation 1 from the main idea is unclear, algorithm blocks need more textual explanation, and symbols such as “supp” and “NNLS” are not defined. R1 and R4, however, found the paper clear; this contrast is noted (R2 vs. R1/R4).
6. **Missing computational analysis** – There is no complexity analysis, no empirical runtime results, and no GPU memory cost report. The use of PCA may also pose scalability challenges for large‑scale experiments (R3; also asked by R2 about Section 3.3).
7. **No qualitative analysis** – The paper would benefit from an analysis of the kinds of examples selected (e.g., the 5% subset) to provide insight into what the method captures (R2).
8. **Difficulty interpreting some results** – Some experimental results are not easy to make sense of (R4). For example, it is unclear whether all methods in Table 2 are subject to the same subset size constraint (R4).
9. **Unaddressed robustness** – It is not shown whether the selected subset remains effective under different optimizer configurations or learning‑rate schedules (R1).
