## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper applies Large Deviation Theory (LDT) to analyze the generalization error and implicit bias of SGD. The empirical loss is decomposed into the expected loss and a term involving the "abnormality" of the generalization gap. The gradient is similarly decomposed, and the authors argue that SGD inherently biases models toward less abnormal and more concentrated losses, explaining its regularizing effect. Experiments on deep CNNs (Inception V3, batch sizes 1–256, small n=50 dataset) are provided.

### Strengths
- The three-way decomposition of the empirical loss and of both full-batch and stochastic gradients (equations 3, 11, 12) is a compelling heuristic, with one term (the gradient of abnormality) being highly similar across GD and SGD. This is a non-obvious, original finding. [R1, R2]
- The paper offers a very novel perspective on an important question (implicit bias of SGD) by applying LDT, and the work is well-organized and well-written. [R2, R3]
- The analysis hints at a practical variant of SGD by introducing a “skip” mechanism based on the test set (lines 471–486). [R3]

### Weaknesses
- **Core assumption about data independence:** Theorems 1, 2, 4 and Proposition 5 consider fixed parameters θ and study the empirical loss under random draws of dataset D. However, in machine learning the training dataset is not sampled independently of θ—this is the core challenge of learning theory. Therefore it is unclear whether these results are relevant to actual training dynamics. [R1, R2]
- **The training loss may be an outlier:** In overparameterized neural networks trained to zero loss, the empirical loss on the specific training dataset lies at the far left of the distribution over dataset draws, while the rest of the distribution centers around the expected loss. LDT describes tail behavior but may not capture such an outlier; the paper does not address whether LDT gives meaningful bounds for \(\min_\theta \hat L(D,\theta)\) versus \(\hat L(D,\theta)\). [R1]
- **Informativeness of the decomposition:** The decomposition (3) splits the generalization gap into “abnormality” and “concentration”. Experimental Figure 2 shows that relative to GD, SGD improves *both* abnormality and concentration. This contradicts the paper’s argument (lines 323–365) that SGD’s success is due to abnormality alone. The Occam’s razor explanation is that the decomposition is not particularly enlightening. [R2]  
  - Additionally, the claim that “SGD prevents highly abnormal generalization errors” (line 402) is supported only by Figure 3 and lacks broader experimental justification. [R1]
- **Relationship between abnormality and generalization loss:** From equation (4) and (5), the abnormality of the generalization error is approximated by the square of the generalization loss. Hence high abnormality is essentially another way of saying high generalization loss; it is unclear what extra insight the LDT framework provides beyond analyzing the loss directly. [R1]
- **Ambiguity about the “large n” assumption:**  
  - The paper uses n=50,000 as a “universal cut-off” (line 210) but also experiments with n=50 (Figure 1, right) and batch sizes as small as 1, 32, 64 (line 309). It is unclear when n is large enough for LDT to apply. [R3]  
  - The empirical test of equation (9) with n=50 appears to work well even though 50 is not large, raising questions about the theory’s applicability. [R3]  
  - Since mini-batches are typically not large, the practical relevance of LDT for analyzing mini-batch SGD is uncertain. [R3]
- **Confusion in Section “GD” (lines 244–298):** This section is titled “GD” yet discusses different batch sizes and Figure 2 (which uses various batch sizes). The relationship between GD and mini-batch training is not clearly explained. [R3]
- **Uninformative test-set intervention (lines 471–486):** Showing that using test-set information to skip SGD steps improves generalization is obvious and does not support the theoretical analysis. Moreover, Figure 5 shows this intervention also improves concentration, not just abnormality, further undermining the decomposition’s utility. [R2]
- **Minor issues:**
  - Figure 1 (left): the horizontal axis and meaning of three instances of empirical loss for Inception V3 are not labeled; the three instances appear to have different expected losses, conflicting with the statement in line 064 that each model’s empirical loss has mean equal to \(L(\theta)\). [R3]
  - Theorem 1 includes an absolute value on the rate function, even though the rate function is already signed as positive; Figure 1 (center) shows negative values for the rate function; these inconsistencies need clarification. [R3]
- **Missing experimental breadth:** The experiments are limited to small datasets (e.g., 50 samples), which may not reflect typical deep learning scenarios where models learn specific representations. Broader experiments are needed to make the heuristic convincing. [R1]

*Note:* One reviewer (R3, score 6) is markedly more positive than the other two (R1, R2, both score 3). While the above weaknesses capture the main critical points, the positive minority view is preserved in the Strengths section.
