## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Rationality of Thought (RoT), a prompt engineering technique grounded in cognitive psychology that guides LLMs through steps (identification, decomposition, reflection, calculation, evaluation) to reduce cognitive biases. The authors construct a cognitive bias dataset of 464 questions across 29 bias types. RoT improves GPT-4 accuracy on this dataset by 18.7% and shows small improvements on arithmetic and common-sense reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, AQUA-RAT, ARC-c) in a zero-shot setting, outperforming few-shot CoT in some cases.

### Strengths
- The approach is novel, well-grounded in human psychology, and includes clear steps: identification, decomposition, reflection, calculation, and evaluation. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The paper is well-written, easy to read, and clear overall. (R1, R4)
- The constructed cognitive bias dataset (464 questions, 29 bias types) is valuable for assessing the rationality of LLMs. (R2, R3, R4)
- RoT is simple and effective: it significantly improves GPT-4 on the cognitive bias task (+18.7%) and outperforms CoT on the proposed dataset and some reasoning benchmarks. (R2, R3)
- The method works especially well for large models like GPT-4. (R1)
- The method is clean, well-motivated, and the results are promising. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Ablation studies are missing.** It is unclear which parts of the wordy RoT prompt contribute most to performance and whether all steps are necessary. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Data collection process is insufficiently detailed.** It is not specified whether all questions are taken from prior literature or if novel (rephrased) problems were created. The dataset’s limited scope (464 questions, 29 biases) raises concerns about comprehensiveness, overfitting, and potential cultural bias. The lack of selection criteria makes it difficult to assess representativeness. (R2, R4)
- **Prompt engineering process is not transparent.** The rationale behind the chosen steps, the iterative process used to arrive at the final prompt, and the search space are not described. (R2)
- **Limitations and costs are not discussed.** Computational cost (inference time, token usage), generalizability to other tasks and models, and the fact that RoT’s steps explicitly encourage “probability calculations, Bayesian methods, and other data analysis techniques” without evidence that models follow this instruction are not addressed. (R2)
- **RoT shows only small or mixed improvements on arithmetic/common-sense reasoning tasks.** Compared to CoT: GSM8K +0.4, SVAMP –0.6, AQUA-RAT +4.8, ARC-c –0.3 / +0.7. The improvement over CoT for GPT-4 is only ~3% absolute, and performance degrades on some tasks. No deep analysis is provided for AQUA-RAT’s gain. (R3)
- **Little insight into why RoT reduces cognitive biases**, despite its grounding in human cognition. (R3)
- **Missing comparisons** to other decomposition prompting methods (Least-to-most, Tree of Thoughts). (R3)
- **Lack of experiments on other model families.** Results on LLaMA were negative (noted post-rebuttal), and experiments on Claude are recommended. (R3)
- **Evaluation focuses on multiple-choice questions**; position bias is not considered. (R3)
- **Visual presentation could be improved.** Figure 1 does not show the exact RoT prompt (relegated to the SI); Table 1 adds little value; Figure 3 text/legend is too small. (R4)
- **Missing important references.** Prior work using cognitive psychology to study LLMs (Binz & Schulz 2023) and contrasting views on LLM reasoning (Ullman 2023, Mitchell & Krakauer 2023) should be cited. (R4)
- **Inconsistency in CoT usage:** zero-shot CoT is used in Section 3 (cognitive bias) and few-shot CoT in Section 4 (reasoning benchmarks); zero-shot CoT surprisingly decreased performance on the cognitive bias dataset. The authors do not explain this choice or result. (R4)
- **Equation issues:** the equation on page 3 is unnumbered, the variable p_rot does not appear, the subscript \in \mathcal{D} is misplaced, and the equation implies optimization that is not performed. (R4)
- **Minor formatting and writing issues:** inconsistent spacing, a conclusion followed by a related work section, and a name error on page 9 (“Research by Alaina …” cites a different author). (R1, R4)
- **Role of Appendix Table 4 is unclear** – it is not explained where that algorithm is used. (R1)
- **Reviewer disagreement:** one reviewer (R2, score 8, confidence 5) is notably more positive than the others (scores 6). The more positive view is that the method is effective and the dataset valuable; the negative concerns noted above reflect the majority assessment. (R1, R3, R4)
