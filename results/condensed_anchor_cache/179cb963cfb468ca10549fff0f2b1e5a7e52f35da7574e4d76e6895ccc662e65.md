## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Residual Factorized Fourier Neural Operator (Res‑F‑FNO), which adds extra residual connections to the Factorized FNO (F‑FNO) to recover small‑scale features lost by Fourier mode truncation, and a “skip‑corrector” that takes larger time steps to reduce accumulated error over rollouts. A training strategy is also introduced where a random time step within each trajectory is selected per sample each epoch. The method is evaluated on a custom dataset of 118 simulations (generated with OpenFOAM) of 3D turbulent flow around a cube. The authors report a >30% error reduction over F‑FNO and >50% reduction over 100 time steps when using the skip‑corrector.

### Strengths
- The paper studies a 3D turbulent flow benchmark (cube in a channel), which is challenging and significant for the CFD/ML community. 
- The custom dataset extends the available test problems for neural operators into three‑dimensional fluid dynamics.
- A comparison against the standard F‑FNO baseline is provided on a hold‑out test set.
- Including the phase variable (obstacle/air) as an input channel is a good practice (though a reference should have been given, e.g. Khoo et al. arXiv:1707.03351).
- The discussion of accumulated errors versus taking larger time steps (via the skip‑corrector) is intriguing.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty is marginal.** Additional residual connections around FNO layers have already been used (e.g. in the official FNO GitHub repo, Spherical FNO [Bonev et al. 2023], and the neuraloperator library). The skip‑corrector is conceptually similar to hierarchical time stepping (e.g. Pangu‑Weather [Bi et al. 2022]) and is misrepresented: it ignores the fine‑grained F‑FNO predictions and instead solves the simulation at a coarser time resolution, then the fine‑grained model merely upsamples that coarse output. This is not a “corrector” but an orthogonal augmentation; any errors from the coarse model are propagated by the fine model.
- **Notation and presentation issues.** The paper copies FNO’s architecture description but uses the letter $t$ for both layers and time, and $x$ for both spatial coordinates and domain coordinates. In Figure 1, the dimensions of the FFN weight matrices $W_1$ and $W_2$ are apparently wrong. The skip‑connection (page 4) says “add the output $\mathcal{P}(a(x))$” but Figure 1 says “$\mathcal{P}(x)$”; it is unclear what $\mathcal{P}$ maps and whether the model remains an operator learner.
- **Missing key details about the dataset and problem setup.** The turbulent flow is described as “simulated until it reaches a state of convergence” – does this mean statistical equilibrium? The equation used (RANS or DNS?), Reynolds number, initial conditions, boundary conditions (especially the outflow boundary – periodical BC is used by default in FNO but that would cause vortices to bounce back, inconsistent with Figure 3), forcing, and the inflow wind directions are never specified. The computational domain appears square in cross‑section, which is inadequate for resolving von Kármán vortices in the wake; classical CFD studies (e.g. Sohankar et al. 1998) use elongated domains.
- **Evaluation is insufficient.** Only normalized mean‑squared error is reported. Spectral metrics and example rollout visualizations are needed. The method is tested on a single dataset and compared only to F‑FNO (no comparison with plain FNO, tensorized FNO, or other operator architectures). The choice of evaluating at 500 and 2000 epochs appears arbitrary – neither convergence nor the cost‑benefit tradeoff is justified. Ablation studies (e.g. on the skip connections) are superficial and do not meet ICLR standards.
- **Skip‑corrector analysis is lacking.** The parameter $n$ (the time step of the skip‑corrector) is not studied; no rationale is given for why a particular $n$ is chosen. The corrector introduces discontinuities in the rollout (visible as kinks every few steps in Figure 5a). It is unclear how much of the prediction skill comes from the auxiliary solver versus the Res‑F‑FNO itself. Long‑term stability when rolled for many steps is not addressed.
- **Training strategy analysis is weak.** The proposed method of picking a random time step per sample each epoch is standard practice in many sequence‑to‑sequence models (e.g. DDPM). Its claimed benefit – reducing the number of training iterations – is not substantiated: the authors do not show that it reduces the total number of samples seen or the FLOPs to convergence. Moreover, training for 2000 epochs offers only marginal improvement over 100 epochs, raising the question of whether the extra cost is justified.
- **Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (Reviewer 4) found the contributions slightly more acceptable (score 3, contribution 2) but still critiques the lack of novelty and evaluation depth. The other three reviewers rated contribution as 1.
