## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper applies Fenichel’s persistence theorem to continuous attractors in RNNs, classifying them as bounded (BLA) or unbounded (UBLA). It argues that bounded attractors remain stable under parameter perturbations and avoid exploding gradients, while unbounded attractors can diverge and cause gradient explosion. The theoretical analysis is demonstrated with 2D ReLU RNNs on line‑ and ring‑attractor systems. Implications for neuroscience (homeostatic mechanisms) and machine learning (training stability) are discussed.

### Strengths
- Tackles a difficult problem and proposes an original application of Fenichel’s invariant manifold theorem to continuous attractors in RNNs (all reviewers).  
- The presentation is clear and the visualizations (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) effectively illustrate the key concepts (R1, R2, R3).  
- The 2D examples successfully demonstrate the theoretical distinction between BLA and UBLA (R1, R3).  

### Weaknesses
- **Generality is severely limited.** Experiments use only 2D ReLU RNNs with specific parameter values (Eqs. 9, 10) and two attractor types (line, ring). It is unclear whether the findings extend to higher dimensions, other activations, or more complex tasks (R1, R2, R3, R4).  
- **Experimental setup is narrow.** Robustness is studied only when the model is already at its optimum and only for small weight perturbations; learning from arbitrary initializations or far‑from‑optimum dynamics is not explored (R1: “only learning at small displacements from prespecified attractors”; R4: “may be artifact of initialization”).  
- **No concrete prescription for building robust RNNs.** The framework does not yield a novel architecture, training strategy, or initialization scheme beyond the specific BLA model (R2, R4). The advice to use bounded smooth stable manifolds and rely on gradient restorative forces is seen as incremental and essentially restates known dynamical‑systems principles; even the experiments note that “in practice, it is difficult to maintain any of the continuous attractors” (R4).  
- **Applicability of Fenichel’s theorem to ReLU RNNs is questionable.** ReLU RNNs are piecewise‑linear (piecewise‑smooth) systems, where switching manifolds can cause discontinuity‑induced bifurcations not covered by smooth manifold theory (R2). The paper does not address how Fenichel’s theorem can handle such boundaries.  
- **Stronger claims about exploding gradients are undercut by existing literature.** Chaotic or unstable dynamics always cause exploding gradients regardless of architecture (Mikhaeil et al., 2022) (R2). The paper does not rule out chaos in higher dimensions and does not incorporate recent results showing that specific bifurcations in ReLU RNNs are always associated with exploding gradients (arXiv:2310.17561) (R2).  
- **Lack of mathematical precision.** The paper does not prove that satisfying Fenichel’s theorem is sufficient for training convergence; it merely provides illustrations (R3). The relationship between the 2D results and general RNN training is left vague (R3).  
- **Unaddressed role of chaos.** The paper mentions bifurcations into chaos as an alternative cause of exploding gradients but does not analyze whether hidden‑state attractors can ever be chaotic or structurally stable (R3). Necessary and sufficient conditions for stable training remain unidentified (R3).  
- **Overstated claims in the introduction.** The introduction asserts that the results “nullify” the fine‑tuning problem in neuroscience and provide “general principles … for evaluating and designing new architectures and initialization strategies” – statements that are not supported by the limited experimental evidence and are contradicted by the paper’s own negative conclusion (R4).  
- **Presentation issues.** Fig. 3 is cluttered and difficult to parse (R1). Notation inconsistency ( \(\mathcal{M}_0\) vs \(M_0\) ) and grammatical errors appear (R3).  
- **Biological implications are not developed.** How the results relate to biological learning algorithms or homeostatic mechanisms is mentioned but not elaborated (R1).  

**Reviewer contrast:** Three reviewers (scores 5) found the idea interesting though preliminary; one reviewer (score 3, R4) judged the contribution as incremental, the claims as unsupported, and the practical guidance as absent.
