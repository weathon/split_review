## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes SERSAL, a synergy learning framework that iteratively refines an LLM for zero-shot tabular prediction via a small student model (FT-Transformer). In each loop, the LLM generates noisy labels for an unlabeled training set; the student model is trained on these labels using DivideMix (a semi-supervised method designed for noisy labels); the student's predictions then fine-tune the LLM. Experiments on 10 medical tabular datasets and 3 other binary classification datasets show performance improvements over various prompting baselines, with further gains from multiple loops. Two reviewers (scores 8,8) find the work well-written and the synergy learning mechanism interesting and sound. Two reviewers (scores 5,5) raise concerns about the zero-shot framing, the mismatch between motivation and actual prediction pipeline, insufficient baselines, and limited evaluation scope.

### Strengths

- The limited generalizability of LLMs to tabular data, especially heterogeneous numerical features, is a well-known problem and an active research area.
- The idea of using an LLM as a noisy labeler and combining it with semi-supervised learning (DivideMix) to iteratively improve both the LLM and a small model is interesting and novel.
- The synergy learning mechanism—where the small model first learns from low-confidence LLM annotations, then after quality control generates prompts to refine the LLM—is well-designed and sound.
- Extensive evaluations on 10 healthcare tabular datasets show stable performance improvements.
- Generalizability is also tested on 3 binary classification datasets from other domains and 1 simulated (Fake) dataset.
- Ablation experiments investigate the impact of design choices such as soft vs. hard labels, early stopping, and the correlation between LLM confidence and label accuracy.
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with clear objectives, methodology, and summaries in tables/figures.
- The direction of using small models to promote LLMs is promising, especially as LLMs continue to grow in size, making full retraining or fine-tuning infeasible.

### Weaknesses

- **Zero-shot terminology and framing**: Two reviewers (R1,R4) strongly argue that the setting is not zero-shot because the method uses unlabeled training instances from the target distribution. Zero-shot typically means no training instances (labeled or unlabeled) from the target dataset are provided. R1 states the work does not really enhance "zero-shot tabular prediction for LLMs" since final predictions come from the small model (not the LLM), unlabeled data is used for training, and the paper does not directly address LLM numerical insensitivity. R4 similarly questions whether "no label" equates to "zero-shot" and suggests the setting is more appropriately called unsupervised. R2 and R3 do not contest the zero-shot framing, but the minority view is critical.
- **Misalignment between motivation and actual method**: R1 notes that Algorithm 1 (line 13) uses the small model's parameters to produce test predictions, not the LLM. The title and framing suggest enhancing LLM zero-shot prediction, but the final predictor is the small model. The paper does not resolve LLM numerical insensitivity as claimed in the Introduction.
- **Baseline comparison issues**:
  - R1: The proposed approach fine-tunes the LLM, but baselines (vanilla zero-shot, zero-shot CoT, TabLLM, LIFT) are only tested in zero-shot prompting mode, not fine-tuning. TabLLM and LIFT were originally proposed for fine-tuning but are not used that way. This makes the comparison unfair.
  - R1: AUC results in Table 2 are highly sensitive to prompt format and feature serialization, which is well-known in the literature; the paper does not account for this to ensure fair comparison.
  - R4: Missing baseline: generative models pretrained on large tabular data (e.g., GTL) also demonstrate zero-shot capabilities; comparison is needed to assess novelty.
  - R4: In Table 2, TabLLM (zero-shot) outperforms GPT-3.5 zero-shot; why would this happen given GPT-3.5's larger knowledge base? Also, the meaning of "TabLLM + SERSAL (GPT-3.5)" and the role of each component is unclear.
- **Data contamination and generalizability**:
  - R1: All main datasets are public and likely in LLM pretraining corpora, limiting generalizability. Evaluations should include private datasets or public datasets released after the LLM's cutoff date. The Fake dataset ablation only tests an unreasonable domain; it does not address a reasonable domain with very low initial accuracy.
  - R1: Need to test whether the LLM's confidence remains well-calibrated on future unseen datasets; otherwise errors compound across iterations.
- **Clarity and detail of DivideMix adaptation**: R1 and R3 both request more explanation of how DivideMix is applied (early stopping, hyperparameter selection without a validation set, adapted loss function). R2 also asks for theoretical underpinnings (memorization effect, why a better small model can be learned from noisy LLM outputs).
- **Dependency on initial LLM performance**: R2 notes the method requires the LLM to have latent domain knowledge to work. The fault tolerance limit (lowest initial accuracy for SERSAL to be effective) is not discussed. The Fake dataset only tests an unreasonable domain; a more thorough sensitivity analysis with realistic low-accuracy scenarios is needed.
- **Computational complexity analysis**: R2 points out the lack of detailed analysis (time and memory complexity for LLM annotation, small model training, LLM fine-tuning; scalability with dataset size; impact of hyperparameters). Given the novelty of using small models for efficiency, this analysis is important to justify the approach.
- **Restricted task type**: R3 notes the experiments are limited to binary classification; multi-class classification and regression are not evaluated, which is a common benchmark in tabular learning (e.g., cited NeurIPS 2021 paper). The potential for more complicated tasks or other data types is not demonstrated or discussed.
- **Multi-loop experiments are limited**: R3 states the experiments with multiple SERSAL loops (Table 4) are light; more loops and more datasets should be tested to confirm trends.
- **Only ECD and LI datasets shown in Figure 2 and Table 4**: R1 asks whether similar trends hold for other datasets.
- **Sample-by-sample annotation cost**: R4 questions whether LLM annotation is done per sample, and if so, the time/API cost is high. Provide time cost analysis.
- **Safety concerns for medical data**: R4 asks how data security is ensured when processing medical data with LLMs, especially when fine-tuning the LLM (citing Nature 2023). The paper lacks discussion of medical domain knowledge specific to the tasks.
- **Reference formatting**: R2 notes misuse of LaTeX citation commands (\citep, \cite) in several places (Line 65, 76, 85, Fig. 1 caption).
