- Decision: Accept
- Scores: 5, 6, 8, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper benchmarks the empirical performance of 12 causal discovery algorithms under 8 types of model assumption violations, focusing on differentiable causal discovery methods. Experiments are conducted on synthetic data generated from Erdos-Renyi and scale-free graphs. The authors observe that differentiable methods exhibit robustness (measured by SHD and SID) under most misspecifications except scale variation, and provide theoretical explanations for this performance.

### Strengths
1. **Important, understudied problem**: The paper addresses the critical question of how causal discovery methods perform when their core assumptions are violated—a topic highly relevant for real-world application. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)
2. **Extensive and well-designed benchmark**: The selection of 8 misspecification scenarios and 12 algorithms is comprehensive, and the experimental setup is clearly explained. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4)
3. **Valuable empirical evidence for differentiable methods**: The benchmark provides strong evidence for the robustness of differentiable DAG learning algorithms, particularly in the latent confounder and measurement error settings. (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 1)
4. **Theoretical insights**: Section 4.1.2 offers interesting theoretical explanations for the empirical performance of differentiable methods under certain misspecifications (e.g., measurement error and unfaithfulness). (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Synthetic data limitations and lack of real-world validation**: No experiments are conducted on real-world datasets, which limits relevance for practitioners. Multiple reviewers note this as a major gap. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
2. **Unrepresentative graph structures**: The use of only Erdos-Renyi and scale-free graphs is criticized as unrepresentative of real causal structures; a wider range of generative models or semi-synthetic approaches should have been included. (Reviewer 1, explicit also raised in Reviewer 4’s minor point)
3. **Unaddressed combined misspecifications**: Only isolated violations are tested; real data often involves multiple simultaneous issues (e.g., confounding + measurement error + heterogeneity). How methods perform under combined violations is unknown. (Reviewer 2)
4. **Scale non-invariance issue is not sufficiently discussed or mitigated**: The paper finds poor performance of differentiable methods on scaled datasets. However, recent work (Deng et al., 2024) shows that these methods can be made scale-invariant with proper loss functions and global optimization. The paper should discuss this and potentially incorporate such fixes. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Fundamental concern about variance-induced shortcuts (non-standardized data)**: Benchmarking only on non-standardized data means differentiable methods can exploit variance accumulation (noise ratio “shortcuts”), as known from Reisach et al. (2021) and Ng et al. (2024). Thus the claimed “counter-intuitive robustness” may simply reflect that the noise ratio still favors the correct graph. Conclusions about robustness are unsupported without testing on standardized causal models. (Reviewer 4, main point 2)
6. **Linear Gaussian setting is non-identifiable and limiting**: The only linear model tested uses Gaussian noise with equal variances, which is known to be non-identifiable for most algorithms (except PC and GES). This weakens conclusions drawn from that setting. (Reviewer 4, main point 1)
7. **SHD values in Tables 2 and 3 are implausible or exceed theoretical bounds**: For example, with 50 nodes and an average of 2 edges per node (ER-2), the maximum SHD should be around 200 (if direction flips count as two), yet values above that appear (e.g., 388, 552). The authors must explain this discrepancy. (Reviewer 4, main point 3)
8. **Latent confounder results appear to reflect empty graphs only**: In Table 2, SHD values for the latent confounding columns match exactly 2× number of edges (e.g., 20 for 10 nodes, 40 for 20 nodes, 100 for 50 nodes) for almost all methods, suggesting that every method simply outputs an empty graph. This contradicts previous benchmark results (Montagna et al., 2023) where some methods showed some robustness. Clarification is needed. (Reviewer 4, main point 4)
9. **Claimed “optimal/competitive” performance on nonlinear data is unsupported**: In Section L343 the authors claim differentiable methods achieve optimal/competitive performance on nonlinear data, but the reported SHD values are very poor (e.g., 10 nodes: SHD ~10, 20 nodes: ~30, 50 nodes: ~70–85, when the number of true edges is small). The analysis appears incorrect. (Reviewer 4, main point 5)
10. **Statistical rigor is missing**: Only 10 iterations per setting are used, with no uncertainty bounds, confidence intervals, or standard errors. Significance is not computed. This weakens the reliability of the results. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4’s main point 6)
11. **Running times not compared**: Some algorithms require long training times, which may influence apparent performance. A comparison of computational cost is missing. (Reviewer 3)
12. **Theoretical analysis is incomplete**: The analysis in Section 4.2.1 is limited to measurement error and unfaithful datasets; extending it to all scenarios would be valuable, especially in light of Ng et al. (2024). (Reviewer 4, question 1)
13. **No recommendation for practice**: Despite the benchmarking goal, no single most reliable method is recommended for real-world use. (Reviewer 2)
14. **Limited discussion of implications**: The paper lacks a summary or discussion of broader implications for practitioners. (Reviewer 1)
15. **Overselling language**: Phrases like “ground breaking” and “transformative potential” are unsupported and not objective. (Reviewer 4, minor point 2)
16. **Strong, unsupported statements**: Claiming that causal sufficiency and absence of measurement error are “imperative” and “fundamental prerequisites” is factually incorrect, as methods exist to handle these violations. (Reviewer 4, minor point 3)
17. **Incorrect citation**: Reference to Lachapelle (2019) (GRAN-DAG) when discussing identifiability is wrong; that paper does not contain identifiability results. (Reviewer 4, minor point 4)
18. **Overlap with prior benchmark**: Several scenarios (6 out of 9) are already covered in Montagna et al. (2023), which limits novelty. (Reviewer 4, minor point 5)
19. **Tables 2 and 3 are difficult to read**: Many lines packed into small font; consider moving to appendix. (Reviewer 4, minor point 1)
20. **Vague phrase**: “true mechanisms remain unclear when applied to real data” (L64) is obscure and should be clarified. (Reviewer 4, question 2)
21. **Denser graphs not explored**: The tested graphs have low degree; it is unknown whether results hold for denser structures. (Reviewer 1, question 4)
22. **Many algorithms share same SHD/SID in latent confounder setting**, which warrants intuition. (Reviewer 1, question 3—this is a request for explanation, overlaps with point 8).

**Reviewer disagreement noted**: Reviewer 3 (score 8) is strongly positive about the evidence for differentiable robustness; Reviewer 4 (score 3) is strongly critical, arguing the conclusions are unsupported due to non-standardized data and flawed SHD reporting. Reviewer 1 (score 5) is cautiously positive but notes methodological concerns; Reviewer 2 (score 6) is moderately positive but highlights missing real-world validation and combined violations.
