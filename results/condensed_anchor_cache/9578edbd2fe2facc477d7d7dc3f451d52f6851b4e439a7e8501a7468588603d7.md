## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces AQUATIC-Diff, a method that applies additive vector quantization (AQ) and quantization-aware fine-tuning to compress diffusion models, achieving high compression ratios (e.g., W1.5A8). Reviewers agree the problem is important and the empirical results on LDM‑4 ImageNet are notable. However, there is significant disagreement on the novelty and practical value. Two reviewers (scores 5) are more positive, highlighting the first application of AQ to diffusion models and the FLOPs savings. Two reviewers (scores 3) are more critical, questioning the novelty, the efficiency overhead, the sufficiency of experiments (no text‑to‑image, no DiT), and the reliability of FID as a metric. The paper's presentation also drew criticism.

### Strengths
- First application of codebook‑based additive vector quantization to diffusion models, adapting techniques from LLM quantization.
- Achieves unprecedented compression levels, including the first successful W1.5A8 quantization.
- Provides a dynamic trade‑off between quantization‑time GPU hours and inference‑time savings, combining benefits of PTQ and QAT.
- FLOPs reduction (as opposed to just BOPs) is noteworthy, as it can directly reduce latency on off‑the‑shelf hardware.
- Code is included for reproducibility.
- Honest reporting of multiple evaluation aspects: algorithm runtime, FID, IS, and precision.
- The problem of compressing diffusion models is important.
- Good empirical results demonstrated in Table 2.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and lack of diffusion‑specific insights:** The method primarily applies existing vector quantization and QAT techniques from LLMs to diffusion models. The modifications (KAQ, LAQ) are considered straightforward design choices rather than deep insights. Reviewers note the paper does not provide observations about what makes diffusion model quantization special (e.g., weight distribution analysis). It is unclear whether existing LLM quantization methods (AWQ, GPTQ, QuaRot) could be directly applied without the proposed adaptations. The use of PeFT (originating from LLMs) on convolutional architectures is questioned; it is not clear why full model tuning is not possible or why PeFT is appropriate for CNN post‑training quantization.
- **Practical efficiency and overhead concerns:** Vector quantization is theoretically slower than uniform quantization on GPUs. KAQ uses an extremely small group size of 9, leading to large codebook memory and high dequantization cost (in contrast to typical LLM group sizes of at least 64). The paper lacks a detailed computational requirement analysis compared to existing methods (AWQ, GPTQ, QuaRot, TesseraQ). Table 5 claims latency and FLOPs results but provides no latency numbers; it is unclear if compression translates to inference benefits after accounting for additional scales and overhead. The baseline is 32/32; comparing to 16/16 or 8/8 would likely show increased FLOPs for the proposed method.
- **Experimental scope and generalizability:** Experiments are limited to LDM‑4 on ImageNet (class‑conditional). No evaluations on text‑to‑image models (e.g., Stable Diffusion) or large‑scale models are conducted. The method is designed for U‑Net architectures, while current trends favor Diffusion Transformers (DiT); generalizability to DiT is not shown. The scale of experiments is small compared to related work that tests on Stable Diffusion (e.g., TFMQ‑DM).
- **Evaluation limitations:** The primary metric (FID) is problematic: the authors acknowledge that FID can improve when visual results worsen, and results improve with higher quantization levels. Additional metrics like PSNR from the original model’s outputs and more generated images for visual inspection are needed. Visual results are insufficient, showing only the “golden fish” class. The paper would also benefit from metrics like ID (undefined in tables) and Precision (undefined).
- **Comparison with baselines:** The results in Table 2 are not far from EfficientDM while costs differ significantly. In Table 3, EfficientDM achieves similar or better metrics with 50% less wall time, despite requiring 5× more fine‑tuning steps. In Table 1, EfficientDM has better performance with less GPU time. Even though the authors state EfficientDM code was unavailable, the published results should be acknowledged. A uniform quantization baseline (e.g., 8/8) is expected to have near 32/32 image quality with much lower FLOPs; the paper does not compare against such baselines, making it unclear whether the proposed method is beneficial.
- **Overfitting and stability concerns:** Overfitting during QAT and its impact on stability/convergence are not addressed. The AdamW observation is seen as an engineering detail rather than a scientific contribution.
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** The paper is difficult to follow: overly long sentences, inconsistent citation formatting, missing references (Line 485), confusing figures with short captions, and notation inconsistencies (e.g., concat operators in Eq. 4 vs Eq. 5, notation change from $b_{i,m}$ to $b_{im}$). Section 3 is dense and unclear about what parts are novel; extension to appendix is suggested. Figure 7 shows $\mathbf{x_{t+1}}$ as output of the optimizer, which is confusing. Line 53 incorrectly states that SDXL has 6.6B parameters (it has 2.6B) and was no longer state‑of‑the‑art at submission.
