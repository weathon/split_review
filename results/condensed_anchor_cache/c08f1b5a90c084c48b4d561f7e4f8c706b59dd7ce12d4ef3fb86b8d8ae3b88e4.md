## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies vertical federated learning (VFL) with graph data where clients hold partial node features and distinct edge sets. It proposes a model-splitting method and a communication-efficient algorithm GLASU that uses lazy aggregation (aggregating only certain GNN layers) and stale updates (skipping some cross-client embedding communication) to reduce overhead. The authors provide a convergence analysis and experiments on real-world datasets showing accuracy close to centralized training with reduced communication time. The work targets an important problem, but reviewers raise significant concerns about the problem setting, privacy, novelty, and experimental design.

### Strengths

- The paper is generally clearly written and easy to understand (R1, R4). The two proposed techniques (lazy aggregation and stale updates) are simple to implement (R1).
- The studied problem (VFL × graph learning) is of practical value; communication overhead is a key challenge that this work directly addresses (R4).
- The framework is flexible and compatible with various GNN backbones (R2, R3). It accommodates different GNN structures while focusing on communication reduction (R2).
- The two techniques demonstrate effectiveness in reducing communication while maintaining model accuracy (R2, R3).
- The paper provides a theoretical convergence analysis that addresses biased stochastic gradients and correlated updates arising from graph data (R3).
- Extensive experiments are conducted on multiple datasets and GNN backbones, showing comparable performance to centralized training (R3).
- A special case study of generality into other algorithm design is included (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Privacy**: The method transmits node embeddings and gradients in plaintext. The authors only briefly mention that privacy mechanisms (e.g., DP, homomorphic encryption) could be applied, but provide no experiments or analysis of their impact on accuracy or efficiency (R1). Moreover, all clients hold the training labels, which creates a privacy risk: a department using private features from others would have to share its labels without any guarantee (R3). Missing experimental verification of privacy-utility trade-offs.
- **Narrow problem setting**: The paper assumes each client shares exactly the same set of nodes (fully aligned), which is unrealistic in practice—different departments typically have only partial overlap. It further assumes all clients have access to the same labels, whereas in reality some may lack labels or have distinct label spaces (R3, R4). The algorithm is designed only for the fully aligned case; handling mis‑aligned nodes and varying label availability is not discussed (R4). Additionally, the experiments use only 3 clients; including more clients would make the results more persuasive (R2).
- **Novelty and prior work**: The technical solutions are seen as straightforward and not entirely new. Stale updates for VFL (without GNN) have been proposed by Fu et al. 2022 and Liu et al. 2022; a method similar to lazy aggregation (avoiding some neighborhood aggregation) appears in Peng et al. 2022 (R4). The paper does not clearly discuss how it differs from these prior works. Also, the approach heavily depends on neighbor sampling (every Q iterations all clients use the same set of sampled neighbors), which may not adapt to GNNs with global receptive fields such as graph transformers (R1).
- **Algorithm discrepancy**: Section 3.2 states that all layers \(l_1,\dots,l_K\) are aggregated, but Algorithm 2 ends when \(k=2\) on the server, implying that \(S_m[l_1]\) is never aggregated. This inconsistency requires clarification (R1).
- **Straggler problem**: Lazy aggregation reduces synchronizations from \(O(L)\) to \(O(K)\), but does not address the straggler problem caused by unbalanced execution times among clients due to varying edge sets (R3).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The choice of \(K\) (lazy aggregation frequency) and \(Q\) (staleness update frequency) is not discussed; there is no guidance on how to select them or which layers to aggregate lazily (R3). Experimental results raise questions: in Table 2, \(K=2\) achieves better accuracy than \(K=4\) (R1), and in Table 3 (Amsterdam), increased staleness leads to performance improvements, which is counterintuitive and unexplained (R4). The GAT test accuracy curve exhibits significant fluctuations, suggesting sensitivity to the GNN backbone or aggregation method (R1).
- **Lack of communication overhead characterization**: The paper does not provide statistical profiling to quantify the communication overhead of centralized training (R3). There is no direct comparison of communication time between GLASU and baselines; how runtime is calculated is unclear (R3).
- **Experimental limitations**: The datasets used (Cora, Citeseer) are simple and small (R4). The random edge‑splitting process (each client uniformly samples 80% of edges) needs clarification—whether this ensures each client has nearly full graph information, and whether the method works under unbalanced (non‑i.i.d.) edge distributions (R1, R3). It is also unclear why simulating a centralized GNN (Sim.) suffers accuracy loss while GLASU‑1 can improve over it (R4). Unbalanced splitting cases that better reflect real‑world scenarios are not tested (R3).
- **Convergence rate comparison**: The analysis extends from FedBCD, but how GLASU’s convergence rate compares with FedBCD is not provided (R3).
- **Potential weakness in stale‑update design**: The “all but m” representation used in stale updates averages global information minus a client’s own information; when embeddings are identical, the model may struggle to discern which part was omitted (R2).
