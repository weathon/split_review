## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a self-supervised feature re-representation loss inspired by the Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential from thermodynamics. The loss applies repulsive and attractive forces between feature pairs to balance intra-class compactness and inter-class separation. Experiments on 2D image classification, 3D point cloud classification, and segmentation tasks using ViT and ResNet backbones show that adding the LJ loss improves performance over the baseline without it.

### Strengths
1. **Novel physics-inspired loss**: The use of the Lennard-Jones potential with repulsive and attractive forces is a motivated, novel idea for feature clustering in deep learning. The design aligns well with self-supervised pair interactions and draws a connection to contrastive learning. Discussion of the theory provides a new perspective on overfitting.

2. **Validation across multiple tasks and architectures**: The method is evaluated on diverse computer vision tasks (classification, segmentation) and on both 2D and 3D data, using both ViT and ResNet backbones. Performance gains with LJ loss over the baseline without LJ loss are consistent and large in most settings (Reviewer 3 notes consistent gains; Reviewer 1 and 4 also note validation across architectures/datasets, though Reviewer 1 calls datasets limited).

3. **Multiple reviewers acknowledge novelty**: Despite disagreements on motivation and experimental rigor, all reviewers agree the idea is interesting. Reviewer 3 strongly endorses the novelty; Reviewer 1 says "exploring theoretical results from physics for deep learning is an interesting direction"; Reviewer 4 calls it "a novel approach to feature clustering."

### Weaknesses
1. **Unconvincing motivation and inconsistent analysis**: The claim (L247) of zero net force at r=σ is inconsistent because the gradient is not zero there and the loss value can become negative by minimization. Analysis below Eqn.5 and Eqn.7 contradicts itself: for r<σ, Eqn.5 says repulsion dominates, but Eqn.7 says attraction dominates. The loss (Eqn.6) appears to merely push all pairs toward a pre-defined similarity σ, which does not significantly help representation learning (Reviewer 1).

2. **Missing essential comparisons**: The experiments only compare LJ loss against the same baseline without it. There is no comparison with contrastive losses (e.g., SimCLR, MoCo), other additive regularization losses, or state-of-the-art self-supervised methods. The paper claims benefits over contrastive learning but provides no numerical evidence (Reviewer 2, 3, 4). Additionally, no comparison with prior work proposing similar ideas (e.g., Song et al. 2024) is provided (Reviewer 2).

3. **No ablation on hyper-parameters**: The loss depends critically on λ (regularization weight), σ, and ε. No ablation study is given for any of these hyper-parameters, even though σ is explicitly noted as sensitive across tasks (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). Failure to analyze their effects undermines the method’s reliability.

4. **Overclaimed theory and redundant sections**: The “Theory” section (Sec. 3.1–3.3) contains no new concepts, definitions, or theorems. The relationship between global/local features (Sec. 3.2) and the LJ loss is never explained, and Sec. 3.2 appears redundant with no logical connection to Sec. 3.3. This section could be removed to save space (Reviewer 2, 3, 4).

5. **Weak and incomplete related work**: The related work omits recent architectures (transformer, KAN), misunderstands self-supervised learning (ignoring methods that do not need positive/negative pairs, such as BYOL, Barlow Twins, MAE), and fails to mention physics-guided deep learning frameworks like ReduNet (Reviewer 2). There is also no discussion of why the method is called “self-supervised” when the experiments use supervised downstream tasks (Reviewer 4).

6. **Concerns about transferability and overfitting**: Self-supervised methods often transfer well, but the proposed loss may reduce feature mobility by overfitting to the training task. No theoretical or empirical analysis of transferability is provided. Additionally, the paper’s focus on inter-class distance (Lines 288–293) is confusing because it then states classification should focus only on intra-class distance (Reviewer 2).

7. **Marginal or inconsistent improvement**: In Figure 5, the baseline with LJ loss performs worse than the original baseline, raising doubts about robustness. Improvements elsewhere are described as marginal, and no state-of-the-art comparison exists to demonstrate that the gains are meaningful (Reviewer 1, 4). (One reviewer, Reviewer 3, sees large consistent gains, but this contrasts with the Fig.5 evidence.)

8. **Writing quality and terminology issues**: The main text is verbose and tedious; Sec. 3.1 and 3.2 could be removed. The term “RE-representation” appears only in the title, abstract, and conclusion without definition. The meaning of “SE Loss” in Figure 1 is unclear. There are typographical errors (e.g., line 081, 125, 126) and the appendix logic is unclear (Reviewer 1, 3, 4).

9. **Limited and small-scale datasets**: Only small-scale datasets (CIFAR, TinyImageNet) are used for classification. Larger benchmarks such as ImageNet are needed to validate the method’s effectiveness on more realistic settings (Reviewer 1).
