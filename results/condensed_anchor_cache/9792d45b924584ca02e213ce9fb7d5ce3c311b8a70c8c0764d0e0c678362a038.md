## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers agree that the problem of partner sampling for training cooperative agents is important and that applying regret-based curriculum (RACCOON) is a novel idea with promising applications. However, opinions diverge on experimental design, evaluation clarity, and the significance of the results. Reviewer 3 (score 8) is most positive, praising the originality, clean algorithm, compelling analysis of sampling trends, and well-motivated ablations. Reviewers 1 and 2 (score 5) and Reviewer 4 (score 6) are more critical. Key points of disagreement/concern: whether the two-agent, fully observable Overcooked setting is sufficient or appropriate for MARL claims; whether the multi-task results show meaningful cooperation; the simplicity of the regret estimator; the lack of explicit partner diversity mechanisms; and insufficient explanation of experimental setup and baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a novel area in zero-shot coordination / ad-hoc teamplay: autocurricula design for partner and task selection during training. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4)
- The proposed RACCOON method is relatively straightforward and can be easily plugged into most existing ZSC/ad-hoc teamplay methods. (Reviewer 1)
- The paper addresses overfitting challenges inherent in MARL by focusing on inter-agent cooperation and zero-shot generalization, moving beyond environment-specific memorization. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4)
- The analysis of sample probability as a function of updates (both for partner skill level and task) is compelling and reveals limitations of naive population sampling. (Reviewer 3)
- The algorithm is cleanly written and easy to follow. (Reviewer 3)
- The tensions between relative vs absolute regret, and other methods for formalizing regret in the appendix, are well motivated and ablated. (Reviewer 3)
- The concept of applying regret to partner sampling is new and contributes beyond prior UED literature. (Reviewer 4)
- The paper compares RACCOON against mainstream sampling approaches and extends experiments with deeper analysis (varying skill levels, task difficulties, scalability). (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Multi-task experimental setup is unconvincing.** Training a single agent on all 5 Overcooked layouts simultaneously yields barely >2 dishes on the 3 harder layouts (Forced Coordination, Counter Circuit, etc.) across all baselines, suggesting agents did not learn meaningful cooperative policies. (Reviewer 1) This contrasts with single-task results (shown in appendix) where DR and Minimax perform better on those same grids—the paper should explain this discrepancy earlier and more clearly. (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4’s related question)
- **Experimental design limited to two agents.** The method’s effectiveness in a true multi-agent context is questionable without results involving three or more agents. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4) In a fully observable two-agent setting, the necessity of MARL over conventional RL is unclear, because state/observation complexity does not increase substantially. (Reviewer 2) The paper does not discuss how to scale to systems with more than two partners or handle heterogeneous partners (assuming interchangeability). (Reviewer 4)
- **Regret estimation is simplistic and potentially unstable.** Using “difference between maximum return ever achieved with π' (on that task) and current return” as regret depends on effective exploration, does not account for variance, and may use a poor proxy for true maximum return (especially early in training). More robust methods (moving average, uncertainty estimates) could improve stability and effectiveness. (Reviewer 4) Additionally, as the student’s policy improves, the regret equation (Section 3.3) could produce negative scores, which is not addressed. (Reviewer 1) The introduction of a regret variable that uses pre-trained policies feels somewhat misaligned with RL’s philosophy of learning through online interaction. (Reviewer 2)
- **Partner generation is too simple.** The method uses random initialization + adding past checkpoints without explicit diversity mechanisms (e.g., TrajeDi, LIPO, CoMeDi). (Reviewer 1)
- **Readability and clarity issues.** Figures are low quality, and the text is hard to follow in places. The experimental setup (e.g., Overcooked configurations, skill level definitions, baseline implementations) is not clearly explained. (Reviewer 4) Specifically, it is unclear at first that evaluations in Section 5 are multi-task: prior Overcooked work mostly uses single-task. The paper should emphasize in the “Tasks” section that agents are trained on all 5 grids plus partners, and add a note to Figure 2’s caption clarifying this. (Reviewer 3) Also, the measurements for Forced Coordination and Counter Circuit in Figure 3 appear to differ without explanation. (Reviewer 4) The choice of Counter Circuit for scalable sample efficiency experiments (Figure 6) is not justified. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing qualitative analysis of failure modes.** For harder grids in the multi-task setting, the paper should analyze whether agents are learning different norms vs. missing basic actions (e.g., passing items at the top vs. bottom). Including examples of state sequences comparing RACCOON and DR failure modes would strengthen the analysis. (Reviewer 3) The paper also does not explain why agents struggle multi-task but succeed single-task on the same grids, especially since RACCOON samples harder grids more. (Reviewer 3)
- **Insufficient justification for environment choice.** The paper should explicitly explain why Overcooked is suitable for studying generalization, overfitting, and zero-shot evaluation. (Reviewer 2)
