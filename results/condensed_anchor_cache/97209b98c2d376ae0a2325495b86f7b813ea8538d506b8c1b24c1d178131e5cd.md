## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes CPFL, a federated graph learning framework that applies the existing graph coarsening method FGC locally on each client before standard FedAvg-style parameter aggregation, aiming to provide privacy protection for graph data. The authors claim that coarsened graph representations and the resulting model parameters conceal sensitive information, and provide a theoretical analysis suggesting the coarsening process satisfies differential privacy. Experiments on molecular, protein, and social network datasets compare CPFL only against DP-SGD and show that CPFL maintains higher model utility while providing privacy.

### Strengths
- The idea of applying graph coarsening to privacy-preserving federated learning offers a novel perspective compared to traditional DP-based methods (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The approach is straightforward and simple to implement, which may facilitate practical adoption (Reviewer 4).
- Experimental results demonstrate that CPFL maintains model performance and, in some cases, outperforms DP-SGD across diverse datasets (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The authors provide a theoretical discussion attempting to explain why the coarsened graph feature is difficult for an adversary to invert (Reviewer 3 notes this positively, though see Weaknesses for caveats).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: CPFL is essentially a direct combination of FGC (an existing graph coarsening method) with standard FedAvg; no methodological innovations beyond this integration are introduced. The coarsening technique is only applied at the client side, and the FL aggregation follows FedAvg exactly (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).  
- **Weak and unrigorous privacy analysis**: The claim that graph coarsening satisfies differential privacy is not properly justified. The proof (lines 303–305) incorrectly conflates graph reconstruction with edge-DP: “This signifies that the reconstructed graph retains a similar number of nodes as the original graph, but contains more edges compared to the original graph. Thus we can say that the two dataset G(V, E) and Gr(Vr, Er) are differentially private” – this is not how edge-DP is defined, and no ε value is given. The sensitivity analysis (lines 308–360) is misapplied and the conclusions are confusing; reviewers remain uncertain whether higher sensitivity is good or bad for privacy (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The coarsened graph and original graph are not exact DP neighbours, so the theoretical guarantee is inaccurate (Reviewer 3).  
- **Unrealistic threat model**: The honest-but-curious server is assumed to have white-box access to the coarsened graph dataset (Equation 7). However, in the proposed framework the server only receives model parameters (Figure 2). A practical attacker would only see model parameters, not the coarsened graph. This mismatch undermines the claimed privacy protection (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation**:  
  - Only one baseline (DP-SGD) is compared. Missing comparisons with other privacy-preserving techniques such as secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption, sparsification, or graph condensation (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
  - No experimental evidence is provided that CPFL actually resists specific privacy attacks (e.g., reconstruction, membership inference) proposed in the paper (Reviewers 2, 3). Attack experiments are needed to demonstrate real privacy protection.  
  - No computational overhead (running time, RAM usage) of the graph coarsening step is reported (Reviewer 4).  
  - Parameter analysis is missing: e.g., in Table 2, does r=0.1 in CPFL correspond to ε=8 in DP-SGD? No clarification is given (Reviewer 2). The claim that higher coarsening ratios simultaneously improve both privacy and utility is self-contradictory and unexplained (Reviewer 4).  
  - Some results appear anomalous and are not explained: e.g., in Table 1, why do FedProx and GCFL+ show the same performance across CPFL and DP-SGD (both 0.62 and 0.73 respectively)? (Reviewer 2)  
- **Notation inconsistencies**: For example, on page 7, P/p, d/n, k/m are used interchangeably; coarsening ratio r is defined as m/p but later referred to as k/P (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- **Framework scope limited**: CPFL currently only supports graph-level tasks; extension to node/edge-level tasks is not discussed (Reviewer 4).  
- **Unclear necessity of coarsening in FL**: Since the server only receives model parameters (not the coarsened graphs), the privacy-protection role of graph coarsening is unclear. Any performance improvement from coarsening may simply come from graph denoising at each client, unrelated to FL privacy (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing theoretical bounds**: The paper does not quantify information loss from coarsening or its impact on model utility. A theoretical or empirical bound is needed (Reviewer 3).  
- **Repetitive presentation**: The paper contains lengthy, repetitive sentences that could be shortened to make room for more important content (Reviewer 4).
