## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes alternating iterative algorithms (combined with maximum block increasing and a block-search strategy) for locating the largest/smallest elements in a factorized tensor. Theoretical convergence results and experiments on synthetic and real-world data are provided. The four reviewers diverge strongly: one (score 5) sees strong quality and clarity; another (score 3) acknowledges novelty but questions theoretical depth; two (scores 1) judge the work as assembling known ideas with major technical flaws, insufficient comparisons, and trivial or incomplete proofs.

### Strengths
- Novel continuous optimization model that applies to CP, Tucker, and TT formats (R1).
- Significant improvements in both accuracy and efficiency over existing methods in numerical experiments (R1).
- Convergence results that could make the approach valuable for a well‑formulated problem (R2).
- Quality: algorithm shows remarkable advances in accuracy and efficiency; comparisons with existing methods underscore superior performance (R3).
- Clarity: precise, consistent mathematical notations throughout (R3).
- Paper is generally well structured and written (R4).
- In some cases good results are obtained when searching for the minimum of CP‑tensors (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Conceptual novelty and framing**  
  – The paper takes known algorithms and merely connects them for the special case of finding extremum of CP‑tensors; no fundamentally new method is introduced (R3, R4).  
  – A major oversight: the symmetric tensor eigenvalue formulation is used, but different factor matrices are optimized by alternating optimization; symmetry is mentioned in the problem definition (Section II) but never used afterward (R2).  

- **Theoretical analysis**  
  – Theorem 1 (equivalent optimization problem) lacks analysis of the number of minimizers, convexity/concavity of the objective, and the risk of local minima; these properties are critical for understanding algorithm performance (R1).  
  – Theorem 2 only proves that a subsequence converges to a stationary point; there is no guarantee that the entire sequence converges or that it reaches the global optimum, and no discussion of limit cycles or other non‑convergent behaviors (R1).  
  – Theorem 3 establishes linear convergence rate only under the strong assumption that the initial point is sufficiently close to the optimum; the paper does not discuss how to obtain such an initial point or the possibility of multiple optimal solutions (R1).  
  – The theorems are considered rather trivial, and one is not proved at all; there is no serious convergence analysis (R3, R4). (R3 notes that this is common for many similar methods.)  
  – The proof of Theorem 1 is unclear: “$x^{(n)}=1$” is not properly defined; if it means a constant vector, the maximization over it is meaningless. If it means $\|x^{(n)}\|_2=1$, then equation (A.1) coincides with problem (2.3) but does not prove the claimed stronger result—that continuous solutions coincide with the discrete binary unit‑vector solutions (R2, R3, R4).  

- **Insufficient and biased comparisons**  
  – Comparisons are given with only two methods (and only one for the minimum case), which is clearly insufficient (R3, R4).  
  – Table coloring is unfair: when both the proposed method and its competitor achieve the best result, only the presented method is colored (e.g., Table 2 minima); all best entries should be marked (R3, R4).  
  – Table 1 (random vectors) is not honest: the *star sampling* method can be adapted to find the minimum via the transformation $m\mathcal{E} - \mathcal{A}$ (increasing CP‑rank by only 1), so it would likely perform just as well; *star sampling* already wins for maximum finding, making the contribution for that case negligible (R3, R4).  
  – For the Rastrigin and Schwefel functions (used as model functions), the open‑source *optima_tt* code (Chertkov et al. 2022) applied directly to the TT‑tensors (exact TT‑rank 2, $n=4096$, $d=10$, $k=5$) obtains: Rastrigin min 0.0014192582…, max 403.5327475…; Schwefel min 0.000725485…, max 8379.65727…. These results are similar to those reported, suggesting the combination of CP‑compression and the proposed method is meaningless (R3, R4).  

- **Experimental reproducibility and fairness**  
  – No code is provided, so experiments cannot be repeated (R3, R4).  
  – The running times in Table 4 (real‑world data) are not relevant: for the COVID dataset, expanding to the full tensor and searching the maximum would be much faster; also, the compression time of these datasets into CP format is not accounted for (R3, R4).  
  – The link to the MNIST dataset is invalid (requires login and password) (R3, R4).  

- **Parameter‑free claim**  
  The paper initially states the algorithm is parameter‑free, but the final Algorithm includes an adjustable parameter $b$ (R3, R4).  

- **Missing experiments and scalability**  
  – Only two model functions (Rastrigin, Schwefel) are tested; experiments on other functions are absent (R4).  
  – No experiments scaling to significantly higher dimensions ($N=100,1000$) that would test the method’s ability to overcome the curse of dimensionality (R4).
