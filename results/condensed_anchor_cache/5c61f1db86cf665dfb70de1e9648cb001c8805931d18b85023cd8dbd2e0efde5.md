## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces DiffsFormer, which uses a Transformer-based diffusion model to generate augmented stock factor data, aiming to alleviate low signal-to-noise ratio and data homogeneity. The method involves training the diffusion model on a large source domain with many steps, then “editing” samples from a smaller target domain using fewer steps, with predictor-free guidance from Ho & Salimans (2022). Experiments on CSI300 and CSI800 datasets with eight baseline models report relative improvements of 7.3% and 22.1% in annualized return ratio. Reviewers acknowledge the interesting and relevant problem, well-designed experiments, and observed improvements in returns and Weighted IC across many architectures. However, concerns about novelty, writing clarity, experimental rigor, and missing comparisons are widespread.

### Strengths

- The concept of applying diffusion models to stock data augmentation is interesting, particularly for alleviating data homogeneity. (R1, R3)
- Clear and robust experimental validation on real-world financial datasets (CSI300, CSI800) with eight ML models, and assessments of generated data fidelity and diversity. (R1, R3, R4)
- Improvements in returns and Weighted-IC are observed across a wide range of architectures. (R1, R4)
- The paper is well-written and appropriately motivated. (R1, R4) [Note: minor disagreement – R2 and R3 found writing poor, see Weaknesses.]
- The problem tackled (stock market prediction) is financially lucrative and attractive. (R2)

### Weaknesses

**Novelty and Technical Contribution** (R2, R3)
- The framework lacks novelty: it mostly applies well-known ideas (diffusion models, SDEdit, classifier-free guidance) to a specific domain. (R2)
- The “editing” concept is essentially SDEdit (arXiv:2108.01073) but that work is not cited or discussed. (R2)
- The time‑efficiency improvement proposed in Section 3.2 is considered unnecessary and may be incorrect – training complexity does not depend on T_enhance, and reducing the diffusion horizon during training can lead to a poor model. (R2)
- Guidance is used as a black box; the specific choice of predictor‑free guidance (from Ho & Salimans, 2022) is not clearly explained. (R2, R3)
- There is no direct experimental support for the claimed advantage of training on the source domain with T=1000 steps then using T’<<T steps for augmentation. The impact of different T’ values is not shown, and T is not mentioned in the main text. (R3)

**Presentation and Clarity** (R1, R2, R3)
- Writing quality is below the bar for some reviewers: technical concepts (diffusion models, stock factors, SNR, data homogeneity) are poorly defined, vague, or misleading. (R2, R3)
- Figures are not referenced in order; Figures 1 and 3 are not referenced at all. Figure 1’s purpose, the model used, and its timeframe (Oct 2023–Aug 2024, not matching the test period) are unclear. (R3)
- Terms like “stock factors,” “return ratio,” and “editing” are used before being defined. (R2, R3)
- The phrase “average number of stocks experiencing significant price drops” is confusing; without total stock counts per sector it is unclear if the shown behavior indicates homogeneity. (R3)
- The claim “Since the target domain is a subset of the source domain” (L237) is not previously established. (R3)
- The dimensionality of data points (158‑dim factor vector per day, or an 8×158 matrix?) is not clearly stated. (R3)
- Source/target domains should be specified in the main text, not only in the appendix. (R3)
- Notation in L237 (&#120;&#94;(t) over only the last term) is confusing. (R3)
- The term “editing” is a metaphorical source of confusion; it is unclear whether it refers to the forward process (x_0^t→X_{T’}^t) or the reverse (x_{T’}^t→&#119909;^0) or both. (R3)
- “Predictor‑free guidance” is not clearly connected to the “classifier‑free guidance” of Ho & Salimans (2022). (R3)
- Several tables (3, 5) compare “Performance” without stating which metric is used. Table 3 mentions W‑Distance but the text says FID; are they identical? (R3)
- Figure 4b is referred to before Figure 4a. (R3)
- There is a strong disagreement on writing quality: R1 and R4 found the paper well‑written, while R2 and R3 found it poor. (all reviewers)

**Experimental Evaluation Gaps – Baselines and Metrics** (R1, R3, R4)
- Missing comparison with other time‑series augmentation approaches (e.g., Kollovieh et al. 2024, Seyfi et al. 2022, Wiese et al. 2020, Xia et al. 2024). (R4)
- Alternative generative baselines like GANs (Quant GANs, Market‑GAN) are not compared; a proper comparison should include computational cost, training stability, and data quality. (R1)
- Traditional forecasting metrics (RMSE, MAPE, MAE) are not reported; Weighted‑IC does not fully capture forecasting capabilities. (R4) [R1 also requests Sharpe, Sortino ratios.]
- The paper claims improvements in excess return, but many methods show lower IC and RankIC after augmentation (Tables 8,9); this discrepancy is puzzling since models are optimized for all stock predictions. Significance tests (t‑tests, ANOVA) are missing – many differences are small relative to standard deviations (e.g., Table 2: Ours vs Original 0.012±0.038 vs 0.022). (R3)
- The “top30drop30” strategy for excess returns incurs significant transaction costs in the A‑share market; the authors should justify it, include results with transaction costs, or use a top‑K approach. (R3)
- Excess Return (ER) values in Tables 1 and 2 are identical to Return Ratio (RR) in Tables 8 and 9 – this needs explanation. (R3)
- Only forecasting tasks are evaluated; classification (direction prediction) and DRL approaches are not tested. (R4)
- Different sets of baseline models are used for CSI300 and CSI800; the selection should be consistent or justified. (R3)

**Domain Shift, Fidelity–Diversity, and Robustness** (R1, R3)
- Domain shift between source (e.g., CSIS with higher volatility) and target (CSI300 lower volatility) is not analyzed. Spurious correlations may be learned; statistical properties of generated data should be compared to both domains to show the augmentation is beneficial. (R1)
- The trade‑off between fidelity and diversity is not quantified (e.g., using FID or similar). The impact of different guidance strengths on diversity is missing. (R1)
- The argument that distilling high‑volatility knowledge improves only top‑30 stocks, not all stocks in CSI300, suggests the method may not be generally applicable. (R3)
- Rebuttal results show performance highly dependent on the training/validation/test split; the best‑case split is used for reporting, raising robustness concerns. (R3)
- When source and target domains are identical (Table 4), improvements are marginal and likely not statistically significant, contradicting the claim in L495‑496. (R3)

**Interpretability and Conditioning** (R1, R4)
- Interpretability is lacking: feature importance (SHAP, LIME) and temporal dynamics should be explored, with discussion of limitations. (R1)
- No qualitative examples (time series plots) are provided to illustrate conditioning on industry/sector or other guidance signals. (R4)

**Minor** (R1, R3)
- Comparison with the latest SOTA in stock forecasting (attention mechanisms, graph neural networks) is missing. (R1)
- The specific values used for T and T’ (T=1000, T’ not given) are important and should be stated. (R3)
- CSIS in Table 4 is undefined. (R3)
- The paper may be a better fit for a domain‑specific venue rather than ICLR. (R2)
