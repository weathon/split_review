## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper evaluates how RLHF (PPO, DPO) on general-purpose preference data (Anthropic HH-RLHF) impacts five trustworthiness dimensions: toxicity, stereotypical bias, machine ethics, truthfulness, and privacy. Experiments on Pythia (1.4B–6.9B) and Llama2-7B show RLHF improves ethics by 16.5–18.5 percentage points on average but worsens bias (+150%), truthfulness (–25%), and privacy leakage (+12%), with minimal toxicity change. A novel influence-function-based data attribution method for RLHF is introduced. The study highlights that RLHF does not automatically guarantee trustworthiness and calls for more targeted alignment strategies.

### Strengths
- Systematic, multi-faceted evaluation across five trustworthiness dimensions (toxicity, bias, ethics, truthfulness, privacy) comparing SFT, PPO, and DPO, providing nuanced insights into alignment trade-offs.
- Important finding that privacy leakage increases specifically due to DPO/PPO algorithms (not SFT), and that RLHF worsens social bias and truthfulness while improving machine ethics.
- Novel application of influence-function-based data attribution to the RLHF setting, offering a method to traceback the impact of individual training data points on trustworthiness, which could inform future data selection for alignment.
- Highlights limitations of using general-purpose preference datasets (Anthropic HH) for achieving trustworthiness, emphasizing the need for more targeted alignment strategies.
- Well-structured and easy to follow (Reviewer 1); empirical rigor with multiple models and controlled benchmarks (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Contribution overlap with prior work**: The evaluation of RLHF impact on bias, toxicity, truthfulness, and ethics has been reported previously (Wang et al. 2024, GPT-4 tech report, Llama 2 tech report, Askell et al. 2021, Glaese et al. 2022). The paper should more clearly distinguish its methodology and findings from these works.
- **Model scale and recency**: Experiments limited to models up to Pythia-6.9B and Llama2-7B; results may not generalize to larger (e.g., 70B) or more recent models (e.g., OLMo, Llama 3) due to differences in pretraining data deduplication and diversity.
- **Single preference dataset**: Relies solely on Anthropic HH-RLHF; results may vary with other datasets (e.g., UltraFeedback). No exploration of targeted or combined datasets for each trustworthiness dimension.
- **Toxicity evaluation concerns**: Uses Perspective API, which prior work (EMNLP 2023, arXiv 2312.12651) has shown can introduce inaccuracies or biases. Only 5 generations at temperature=0.5 (vs. 25 and temperature=1 in original protocol); lower temperature may skew distributions. Motivation for parameter choices not justified; robustness across settings not tested.
- **Statistical and methodological rigor**:
  - Claim that "toxicity first increases notably after SFT" (lines 228–229) lacks statistical support; error bars overlap for two larger models (Figure 2 left), suggesting non-significant differences.
  - Claim that "both PPO and DPO significantly increase stereotypical bias scores" (Section 4.2) may conflate models' propensity to agree with any claim (sycophancy, Sharma et al. 2023) with actual bias, as evaluation does not control for difference between stereotyped and non-stereotyped groups.
  - Evidence for increased determinism from Table 5 (max log-likelihood difference of 0.78 between base and SFT) may be within noise; temperature=0.5 used for toxicity generations further narrows distributions. Temperature for Appendix F experiments needs clarification.
- **Influence function analysis lacks actionable insights**: The attribution analysis provides no concrete findings on the optimal composition of RLHF training data or how to fix misalignment. The hypothesis that the HH dataset is out-of-domain for evaluation benchmarks is unsupported; perplexity comparisons across datasets could substantiate this.
- **Missing discussion of mitigation strategies**: The paper does not explore why misalignments occur (e.g., reward function design, regularization) or propose solutions such as adversarial training, constrained optimization, or targeted data curation within RLHF.
- **Trade-offs between trustworthiness dimensions not addressed**: Real-world trade-offs (e.g., bias vs. truthfulness) are noted but not analyzed; insights on how improving one dimension might harm others are lacking.
- **Clarity and figures**:
  - Figures 5 and 11 are crowded, with small fonts and overlapping data points, reducing readability.
  - Dense methodology sections (3 and 5) lack intuitive explanations and concrete examples for influence-function attribution and RLHF process.
  - Need clarifications on: zero-shot output format adherence (especially for TruthfulQA and bias yes/no), definition of FPR in ethics evaluation, prompt sensitivity, and motivation for privacy evaluation task.
- **Minor: Missing citations and typos**: Should cite RLHF-HH dataset and other preference datasets (Cui et al., Ethayarajh et al.), sycophancy work (Sharma et al. 2023). Typos: line 398 "8 with 9" → "Equation 8 with Equation 9"; line 407 "in 9" → "in Equation 9".
