- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies off-policy biases in multi-step goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL). The authors categorize bias into “shooting” (TD errors along the trajectory) and “shifting” (advantage at goal states) and propose a new algorithm, Bias-Resilient MHER (BR-MHER), that combines quantile regression, a truncated multi-step target, and prior components from MHER(λ) to exploit beneficial bias while mitigating harmful bias. Empirical results on robotic control tasks are presented. All four reviewers rated the paper 3 (fair). There is general agreement that the bias decomposition is an interesting contribution, but the paper is criticized for poor writing, lack of clarity, missing motivation, deficient experimental validation (especially no ablation studies), and questionable reproducibility. Reviewer 2 and 4 are somewhat more positive about the structure and empirical gains, while Reviewer 1 and 3 are more critical about the evidence of superiority and presentation.

### Strengths
- The formal classification of bias into “shooting” and “shifting” types is regarded as interesting and novel by all reviewers. Reviewer 3 finds it “quite interesting” and Reviewer 2 notes it “can be an interesting novel analysis.”
- The problem of sparse rewards in GCRL is widely recognized as important (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 states it “can have a concrete impact for several applications.”
- Reviewer 4 praises the paper’s structure: “very clear structure and is well organized” and “the logic is clear.” (Note: this contrasts with other reviewers who found the writing poor; see Weaknesses.)
- Reviewer 3 finds the introduced methodology “quite intuitive and follows nicely from the analytical considerations.”
- Reviewer 2 reports “the empirical results show a fairly significant improvement on performance and bias reduction for the proposed method compared to other baselines.” Similarly, Reviewer 4 states the algorithm “consistently performs well on the six tested domains.” (However, Reviewer 1 and 3 contest this; see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
**Writing and presentation quality** (criticized by all reviewers)
- Many typos and undefined symbols, hurting readability. Examples: “Quatile” for “Quantile” (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4); missing Eq. (8) reference (Reviewer 4); “can truncate” should be “can be truncated” (Reviewer 4); “the Huber loss threshold (κ) is 10” stated twice (Reviewer 3); grammar errors and repetitions (Reviewer 3).
- Undefined notation: $\delta_{\bar \theta}$ before Eq. (3) (Reviewer 1); $\mathcal{B}$ in Eq. (1) undefined (Reviewer 4); “learning target for a TD error” unclear (Reviewer 1); $r_i^{\tau^g_\pi}$ not defined in Eq. (4)-(5) (Reviewer 4).
- Vague abstract and undefined terms: “resilience” not defined (Reviewer 2); “beneficial bias” not explained (Reviewer 2); “robotic benchmark” not specified (Reviewer 2).
- Convoluted analysis: Reviewer 3 states “the current way the analysis is presented makes it unnecessarily convoluted and hard to read” with “overuse of equations without proper context.”
- Imprecise statements: The argument in Section 5.1 about transitions with high Q-values is never made explicit (Reviewer 3); the claim about truncation (“the need for rapid reward propagation … is naturally reduced”) is questionable because the truncation always produces an upper bound, so it should accelerate propagation (Reviewer 3); Proposition 1 about advantage function is unclear (Reviewer 1); “Why the first term in eq. 3 called the off-policy advantage?” (Reviewer 1).
- Section 4.3 is unclear (Reviewer 1).
- Naming confusion: In the appendix, TMHER(λ), QR-MHER, and BR-MHER all use the λ target, but only TMHER(λ) indicates it, causing confusion (Reviewer 4). “Shoot” vs “shooting” inconsistency (Reviewer 4).

**Motivation and justification** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- Lack of motivating examples for why the bias decomposition is needed. Reviewer 2: “it is unclear to me what is motivating the proposed method, and why we need to decompose the bias, there seems to be no motivating examples on why this is a problem.” Reviewer 3 echoes this: “I believe there should be a focused discussion … on what exactly the proposed method does, and how it does differently compared to other baselines.”
- Claims that shifting bias is “unique to multi-step GCRL” are not justified. Reviewer 3 asks: “I do not see why the decomposition of Equation 6 would not hold in arbitrary RL problems.” Reviewer 4 similarly questions whether it is truly an off-policy bias or just an estimation error.
- Why 10-step learning is important or challenging is not explained; Reviewer 1 notes it is simply a hyperparameter.
- No comparison to other bias-reduction techniques in off-policy RL, e.g., clipped double Q in TD3 (Reviewer 2).

**Experimental evaluation concerns** (all reviewers)
- **Lack of ablation studies**: The proposed algorithm combines multiple components (quantile loss, huber loss, truncation, MHER(λ)), but there are no ablation studies to isolate which component contributes most to performance or bias reduction. Reviewer 3: “the paper is entirely lacking ablation studies/analytical experiments to understand which type of bias is most damaging and which component … is playing the most effect.” Reviewer 3 further asks: “is it the huber component of the loss/the quantile component/ or MHER(λ) that is most helping to fend off shooting bias?”
- **Limited environments and seeds**: Reviewer 3 notes “both the number of environments and training seeds is quite limited.”
- **Conflicting evidence on superiority**: Reviewer 1 states “the experimental result does not show the superiority of the proposed method, according to Figure 1. The proposed method BR-MHER only achieves better performance on 2 of 8 tasks.” In contrast, Reviewer 2 reports “fairly significant improvement” and Reviewer 4 says “consistently performs well on the six tested domains.” This disagreement is not resolved in the paper.
- **Redundant baseline comparison**: Reviewer 3 criticizes comparing with each baseline individually as unnecessarily redundant, and suggests summarizing by picking the best value of n for each baseline.
- **Missing details for reproducibility**: Reviewer 3: “no details are provided as to how the OpenAI environments are modified (as stated in the 3rd paragraph of Section 6.2), and there are no details/pseudocode descriptions to understand exactly how the TSB and ISB metrics to estimate bias are computed in practice.” Reviewer 4 also asks for clarification on environment modifications.

**Technical soundness issues** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- Equation errors:
  - In the unlabeled equation before Eq. (2), $\theta$ and $\bar{\theta}$ appear to be swapped (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4). This makes the derivation in A.8 invalid.
  - Eq. (1) confusingly defines $Q_\theta$ as equal to the n-step target value (Reviewer 1).
  - Eq. A12: $s_{t+1}$ should be $s_{t+i}$? (Reviewer 1).
  - Eq. (4) and (5): exponent of $\gamma$ in the final term is off; $r_i^{\tau^g_\pi}$ undefined; it is unclear whether Eq. (4) holds and how (Reviewer 4).
  - Reuse of $\delta_\theta$ in Eq. (3) conflicts with its earlier definition as the TD error (Reviewer 4).
- The “shooting bias” definition may couple with bias from the target network (Reviewer 4).
- The “shifting bias” appears to be “just an estimation error instead of a bias due to the off-policy issue of HER” (Reviewer 4).
- Proposition 1: “The advantage function should be positive for the optimal action” (Reviewer 1), questioning the correctness of the claim.
- Near Eq. (6): “why ‘for a successful … are zero’? I cannot know why” (Reviewer 1).

**Specific reviewer questions that constitute criticisms** (incorporated above where applicable; additional items):
- Reviewer 4 asks: “What doesn’t an episode or an n-step trajectory terminate upon reaching a goal? And why can’t we just set the values of goal-reaching states to zero?”
- Reviewer 4 asks: “How can we get Eq. (4)? Isn’t $Q_\theta$ the estimation of the action value function?”
- Reviewer 2 asks: “How exactly do you define resilience?”
- Reviewer 1 asks: “Why are 10-step learning scenarios important and challenging? I believe this is a hyperparameter for the algorithm.”
