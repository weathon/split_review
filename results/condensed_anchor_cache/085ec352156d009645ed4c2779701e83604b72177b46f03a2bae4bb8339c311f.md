## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Reprompting, an iterative Gibbs-sampling-based algorithm that automatically generates chain-of-thought (CoT) prompts for a given task without human intervention. Starting from zero-shot CoT, it alternates between sampling new recipes conditioned on other examples and selecting those that lead to correct answers. Experiments on 5 subtasks from BIG-Bench Hard show improvements over human-written CoT (+9.4 points on average) and baselines like Auto-CoT and Self-Consistency. The method also demonstrates transfer from GPT-3.5-turbo to text-davinci-003 and costs between $80 and $800.

### Strengths
- **Novel and well-motivated approach**: Several reviewers found the formulation of CoT prompt optimization as sampling from a joint distribution via Gibbs sampling to be novel, interesting, and theoretically grounded (R1, R4). R4 noted that in a field where methods are often heuristic, this theoretical framing is a solid contribution.
- **Practical and easy to implement**: The algorithm is simple, easy to grasp, and can be implemented without access to model parameters (e.g., through APIs). Reviewers appreciated its simplicity and potential for wide adoption (R2, R3).
- **Promising empirical results**: The method consistently outperforms human-written CoT, Auto-CoT, and CoT+Self-Consistency on the tested tasks (R1, R2, R4, R5). R1 also noted convincing evidence of algorithmic convergence.
- **Solves a real-world problem**: Human-crafted CoT prompts are costly; Reprompting reduces this burden, with costs ($80–$800) far below hiring and training annotators (R3).
- **Strong revision improvements**: R4 noted that the authors added comparisons to Self-Consistency and Auto-CoT, cost analysis, and transfer experiments, which significantly strengthened the paper.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baselines and comparisons**: Multiple reviewers identified a lack of comparison to other prompt optimization methods (e.g., APE, COSP), parameter-efficient fine-tuning (soft prompt tuning, LoRA), and additional reasoning baselines (ComplexCoT). R2 and R3 stressed that without comparing to other optimization schemes, beating human-written CoT is not surprising. R5 specifically noted that post-rebuttal results show Reprompting underperforms ComplexCoT on GSM8K (79.5 vs. 82.8) and Number Theory (28.5 vs. 33.4). R1 also asked for comparisons to [1][2] (automatic prompt engineering). The current set of baselines is considered insufficient.
- **Insufficient ablation and analysis of components**: The paper lacks ablation studies to isolate the contribution of key design choices:
  - *Rejection sampling approximation*: The algorithm approximates LLM likelihoods via random rejection (top of p.4). R3 and R1 called for justification of this approximation, quantitative evidence of how much performance is lost compared to exact sampling, and analysis of the impact when LLM likelihoods are highly non-uniform. R5 additionally noted the lack of an ablation on the rejection probability parameter.
  - *Conditioning on previous CoT*: Algorithm 1 line 14 does not condition on the current CoT when sampling a new one. R3 asked how much better the method would work if it incorporated prior guesses (citing Yang et al., 2023).
  - *Decoupling LLMs*: The motivation for using two different LLMs for initialization and sampling is to increase diversity, but R3 noted no evidence supporting this claim or guidance on when to decouple.
  - *Message m*: R5 questioned whether the specific message ("Let’s think step by step...") was applied to all baselines; if not, an ablation is needed to attribute improvements to the reprompting technique rather than the message itself.
- **Limited evaluation benchmarks**: The experiments are restricted to 5 out of 23 BIG-Bench Hard tasks. R4 raised the concern of cherry-picking tasks where CoT is known to be effective, and requested testing on tasks where CoT does not improve much to demonstrate robustness. R3 and R5 also noted the absence of popular benchmarks like GSM8K, Math, and MMLU, which are common in the reasoning literature. R4 expected additional tasks between review cycles.
- **Questionable assumptions and theoretical approximation**: The algorithm relies on assumptions (1) and (2) about in-context learning (conditional independence given demonstrations). R5 argued these assumptions are not standard in LLMs and are difficult to verify in practice, potentially limiting the method's applicability. R1 similarly found the connection between in-context learning findings and the specific approximation in Eq. 4 not clearly established, and requested a more rigorous error analysis. While R4 appreciated the theoretical grounding, other reviewers (R3, R5) felt the method has been "shoehorned" into the Gibbs sampling narrative with many approximations that break the original motivation.
- **Weak transfer claim**: R5 noted that the transfer experiment (GPT-3.5-turbo to text-davinci-003) uses only a single model pair, making the claim of "transferring knowledge from a stronger to a weaker model" insufficiently supported.
- **Few-shot experimental setup ambiguity**: R1 highlighted the need to clarify the number of examples used during CoT generation (e.g., 20) versus the number in the final prompt (K), and how performance varies with this number.
- **Contrasting views on contribution**: R4 rated the contribution highly (3) and recommended acceptance (original score 6), while R2 and R3 rated contribution at 2 (fair), and R5 remained concerned about assumptions (soundness 2). This disagreement reflects that the novelty and practical value are appreciated by some but the lack of thorough evaluation and theoretical rigor holds others back.
