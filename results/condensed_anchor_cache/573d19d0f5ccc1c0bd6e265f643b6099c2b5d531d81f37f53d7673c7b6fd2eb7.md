## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a pseudometric, the Tree Mover’s Distance (TMD), based on optimal transport between computation trees, for attributed graphs. It shows MPNNs are Lipschitz under this metric and can separate graphs with non-zero distance, leading to a universal approximation theorem and generalization bounds for MPNNs on attributed graph distributions. The framework unifies graph and graphon perspectives, extending previous theory limited to graphs without attributes or lacking compactness. Empirical results on MUTAG demonstrate correlation between TMD and MPNN outputs.

### Strengths
- **Novel metric and theory**: The TMD, based on iterated degree measures and optimal transport over computation trees, provides a novel pseudometric for attributed graphs. The theoretical analysis is solid and thorough, demonstrating that MPNNs are both Lipschitz continuous and can separate attributed graphs distant under this metric, establishing a bidirectional connection between the metric and MPNN output.
- **Comprehensive theoretical framework**: The work unifies generalization and expressivity theories for attributed graphs, providing a comprehensive overview of MPNNs under the lens of attributed graphons. It covers universal approximation, generalization bounds, and compactness, integrating and extending prior work (including graphon-based analysis) with fewer assumptions. One reviewer notes this is a notable achievement in GNN research.
- **Novelty of optimal transport application**: Introducing optimal transport to measure graph similarity via computation tree distributions is novel and elegant, offering a fresh perspective on the expressivity of GNNs.
- **Lipschitzness and separation**: The proof that MPNNs are Lipschitz and can separate distinct graphs under the metric is a strong contribution, enabling both continuity and discriminative power.
- **Unified graph and graphon perspective**: The framework applies to both discrete graphs and continuous graphons, enabling analysis of dense graphs and providing a link between discrete and continuous representations.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited empirical validation**: Experiments are restricted to one small real-world dataset (MUTAG). The results show that TMD does not significantly outperform existing methods like Chen et al. (2022)’s d^{(3)}_{WL}, yet this is not adequately discussed. The evaluation should be expanded to additional graph classification benchmarks, including comparisons on graph matching or clustering tasks. Also, the choice of GIN and GraphConv (among many MPNNs) is not motivated, and a discussion of how well empirical generalization aligns with the theoretical bound is missing.
- **Sparse graph limitation**: The metric and theory rely on graphon-based analysis, which assumes dense graphs. The authors note that “sparse graphs are always considered close to the empty graph under our metrics,” but provide no explanation for this behavior or discussion of how it limits applicability to sparse graph domains. Whether the theory can be extended to sparse graphs is unclear.
- **Computational cost**: The TMD has O(n⁵) complexity (though linear in depth L), making it impractical for large graphs. This high polynomial dependence on the number of nodes n is a major bottleneck for real-world use.
- **Limited applicability to more powerful GNNs**: The metric is designed for MPNNs corresponding to the 1-WL test; it cannot analyze more expressive architectures (e.g., k-GNNs, F-MPNNs) that incorporate higher-order structure or complex message passing.
- **Restrictive aggregation assumption**: The analysis assumes normalized sum aggregation, which is less common in practice than mean or max aggregation. The implications of this choice on the generality of the theoretical results are not discussed.
- **Presentation and structure issues**: (i) Notations are overly abundant and similar, making the paper hard to follow. (ii) Main results (Section 5) appear late (page 9) and seem disconnected from the earlier distance analysis; the universal approximation and generalization bound appear independent of the graph/graphon distance framework. (iii) Section 5’s insights are not particularly novel: the generalization error bound depends on covering numbers and Lipschitz constants, which is standard and unsurprising. (iv) Relevant references on generalization of MPNNs are missing (e.g., Oono & Suzuki 2020; Li et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2023). (v) The contribution list in the introduction should be revised to better summarize findings and their broader impact.
- **Insufficient discussion of related metrics**: The relationship to existing metrics, such as those in [1][2], the Weisfeiler-Lehman distance, and Chen et al. (2022)’s d^{(3)}_{WL}, is not clarified. The authors should explicitly compare their metric to these approaches, especially concerning the use of optimal transport and hierarchical measure representations.
- **Unjustified design choices**: The use of Unbalanced OT instead of standard OT is not justified. Also, in Figure 2, the alignment between TMD and MPNN output worsens when attributes are added, but no explanation is provided.
- **Missing analysis for node-level tasks**: The theoretical framework focuses on graph-level tasks; it is unclear whether the results extend to node classification problems.
- **Lack of connection between generalization bound and proposed distance**: It is not shown how Theorem 7 (generalization bound) relates to the distance metrics proposed earlier; the bound derivation appears independent of the prior analysis, weakening the claimed unity of the framework.
