## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces RAFT (Retrieval-Augmented Forecasting of Time-series), a method that augments time series forecasting with a retrieval module. The retrieval module uses Pearson correlation to find the `m` most similar lookback windows (keys) from the training set, retrieves their corresponding future horizons (values), and computes a weighted average using softmax of similarity scores. These retrieved patches are concatenated with the input and fed into a linear/MLP model. A multi-period extension downsamples the series at different periods and repeats the retrieval process. Experiments on eight benchmark datasets (energy, traffic, weather) show improved MSE/MAE over several baselines, with average win ratios of 86% (multivariate) and 80% (univariate). Synthetic experiments indicate that retrieval is especially beneficial when rare patterns repeat or when patterns are less temporally correlated.

### Strengths
- The idea of applying retrieval-augmented methods to time series forecasting is interesting and novel (R1, R2, R3). R2 highlights that it challenges the reliance on increasing model capacity; a simple MLP with retrieval can outperform complex models. R3 notes the approach is conceptually interesting with a straightforward instantiation, but considers the novelty limited due to similarity to attention and prior few-shot forecasting work.
- The paper is well-written, clear, and uses helpful illustrations (R2, R3). R2 specifically praises the architecture figures and readability.
- The evaluation includes eight datasets and several baselines, and the synthetic experiments provide insights into when retrieval is beneficial (R2, R3). R2 finds the experimental setup comprehensive.
- The method shows consistent performance improvements over existing baselines (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and retrieval set (R1, R3):** R1 criticizes the motivation: the paper argues existing models memorize the training set, yet RAFT also retrieves from the training set, making it unclear how this differs from a Transformer attending to the lookback window. Retrieval seems only meaningful in a zero-shot setting where the queried dataset was not seen during training. R1 also asks what set of datapoints retrieval is performed on (entire time series? train+val+test? is there a limit?) – this is unclear. R3 notes that the idea is conceptually similar to transformer variants and that prior few-shot forecasting work (Iwata and Kumagai, 2020) presents a more complex instantiation of the same concept; this work should be discussed and compared, which would clarify the novelty. These points contrast with R2, who views the originality positively.
- **Related work omissions (R3):** The paper does not discuss its similarity to existing attention-based forecasting models. It also omits recent pretrained/foundational time series models (Woo et al., ICML 2024; Das et al., ICML 2024; Ansari et al., 2024) that could benefit from RAG, and the few-shot forecasting work of Iwata and Kumagai (2020) is not mentioned. This weakens the positioning of the proposal.
- **Insufficient ablation and component isolation (R1, R2, R3 indirectly):** R1 argues that using NLinear as the “without retrieval” baseline is inadequate because the proposed model includes additional learnable projections (f, g, h) that add parameters; the improvement may come from extra capacity, not retrieval. R2 calls for ablations: performance without the multi-period extension, without the attention-like weighting, and varying the number of retrieved patches m. R3’s request to clarify the inductive bias also relates to understanding the contribution of retrieval.
- **Baseline setup and missing strong baselines (R3, R1):** R3 raises a critical concern: the experimental setup uses the same number of training epochs for all baselines, which may lead to suboptimal performance; real-world comparisons should tune each baseline to its best setting. A strong baseline, PatchTST (Nie et al., ICLR 2023), is missing. Concretely, DLinear’s MSE on ETTh1 is reported as 0.0764 in this paper, whereas the PatchTST paper reports 0.056, suggesting a difference in setup. R3 requests that the evaluations be revised to match the settings of Nie et al. and include PatchTST. R1 notes that baselines appear “older,” implying the comparison set may be weak.
- **Win matrix interpretation (R3):** The average win ratio of 86% is misleading; the paper should report the win rate against the next best model and the absolute/relative improvement averaged over datasets. The win matrix (from Bahri et al., ICLR 2022) was designed for 65 datasets, but here only 8 datasets are used with multiple horizons, introducing redundancy. It is also unclear how ties are handled – Bahri et al. used a statistical test, but this paper does not specify. (R3)
- **Choice of similarity measure (R2):** The retrieval uses Pearson correlation without justification. R2 asks to explore or justify alternative measures (DTW, cross-correlation, kernel methods, learned similarity) that may better capture nonlinear or non-stationary characteristics.
- **Computational efficiency (R2):** No detailed complexity analysis is provided. Retrieval compares the input query to all key patches, which could be expensive for large datasets. The paper mentions adjusting the stride for efficiency but does not give empirical results on the trade-off between computational cost and accuracy. Memory footprint for storing all key patches is not discussed. R2 suggests considering indexing or approximate nearest neighbor search.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity (R2):** The paper provides chosen values for the number of retrieved patches m, temperature τ, and set of periods P, but does not analyze sensitivity to these hyperparameters or describe how they were determined (e.g., cross-validation, Bayesian optimization). The interaction between hyperparameters is unexplored.
- **Evaluation on more diverse datasets (R2, R3):** The eight datasets are primarily from energy, traffic, and weather. R2 asks for evaluation on finance, healthcare, social media, and datasets with missing values, irregular sampling, varying lengths, or different noise characteristics. R3 notes that more datasets are available from recent papers (Woo et al., Ansari et al.) and would strengthen generalizability claims.
- **Inductive bias clarification (R3):** R3 asks what specific inductive bias RAFT introduces and how it differs from existing models. The paper claims existing models make i.i.d. assumptions but that RAFT is more effective when temporal correlation is lacking – this seems contradictory and needs explanation.
