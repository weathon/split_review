## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces COMiX, an intrinsically interpretable method that transforms a trained B-cos network into a prototype‑based classifier. It decomposes a test image into regions based on learned concepts (class‑defining features) and matches each region to corresponding parts of training images, providing “this part looks like that part” explanations. The method uses a pseudo‑label from the B‑cos network, then performs K‑nearest‑neighbour voting on feature similarity to make the final prediction. Experiments on several datasets and architectures show improvements in fidelity and sparsity metrics (e.g., +48.82% C‑insertion on ImageNet), but the work is criticized for limited novelty, insufficient comparisons with state‑of‑the‑art prototype methods, unclear presentation of the algorithm, and concerns about the validity of reported baselines.

### Strengths

- The method is well‑motivated and addresses essential components of intrinsically interpretable XAI (R1, R2).
- The relationship between prototypes and class‑defining features is clear (R1).
- The paper proposes an original scheme to turn a B‑cos network into a case‑based reasoning model using k‑nearest neighbours (R2).
- The proposed method does not significantly degrade classification performance (R2).
- Qualitative results are strong and convincing (R3).
- The modeling approach is simple (R3).
- The method works robustly across different architectures (R3, R4) and datasets (R3, R4).
- Creative use of B‑cos networks with label aggregation (R3).
- A sufficient set of quantitative evaluation metrics is provided (R3).
- Hyperparameter analysis / ablation studies are present (R3).
- Better sparsity than the ViT baseline and best insertion scores with competitive deletion scores are demonstrated (R3).
- The core idea is presented clearly with good figures 1 and 5 (R4).
- Combining B‑cos with prototypes is a novel combination (R4, minority).
- The paper explores the important field of building interpretable models (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty / insufficient contribution beyond ProtoPNet**  
  The interpretability framework is not significantly different from ProtoPNet (“this looks like that”). Much of the framework—using pretrained features as concepts, a B‑cos backbone, and KNN on feature similarity—simply reuses existing ideas (R2, R3, R4). While one reviewer (R3) found the approach creative and simple, others stress that it just combines two existing methods without proper citation or comparison to superior SOTA (R4). Notably, ProtoPool already uses exact training images as prototypes (R4). [Contrast: R3 is more positive on novelty; R2, R4 are negative.]

- **Missing comparisons with state‑of‑the‑art prototype‑based methods**  
  Several key competitors are not discussed or compared: ProtoPool, Pip‑Net, Q‑SENN, ProtoConcept, and others (R1, R2, R4). These methods achieve better accuracy and sparsity, are backbone independent (hence compatible with B‑cos), and also aim for object‑level prototypes without restricting to patches (R4). The claim of “object‑level attention with prototypes” (Table 3) needs a more rigorous evaluation against these methods. Comparisons with disentangled representation or attention‑based interpretable methods are also absent (R2).

- **Unclear and insufficient description of the method**  
  The algorithm (especially Eqs. 4–11, Algorithm 1, Figure 2) lacks clear explanations. Notations are confusing (e.g., what is s_L(x;θ)? It seems to replace W_{1→L} in Eq. 4). How class‑defining features are selected using Eqs. 7–8 and how mutual information (Eq. 8) is computed—specifically the calculation of p(F_j) and p(l(F_j)=c) and the associated computational cost—are not detailed (R1, R2, R3, R4). The attribution method for visualisations is not clearly defined (R3). What is trained when, how many settings exist (encoder training, CDF computation, inference), and what M is set to are unclear (R4). The definitions of necessity and sufficiency are not well motivated; sufficiency appears redundant (R3, R4). A negative sign in the L2 distance equation (Eq. 10) is suspected to be a typo (R1).

- **Performance and baseline validity concerns**  
  On multiple datasets, COMiX shows inferior accuracy compared to the original B‑cos (Table 2) (R1). Training the feature extractor end‑to‑end is not explored, leaving the question of whether the performance degradation is unavoidable (R1). More critically, reported baselines are significantly too low: a vanilla ResNet34 on CUB with proper hyperparameters already outperforms the reported result by 2.7 p.p., more than doubling the gap; this questions the validity of all other results (R4). The accuracy of COMiX is described as not particularly strong (R2).

- **Circularity and bias from the pseudo‑label**  
  The method is biased toward the pseudo‑label predicted by the B‑cos network. It is not trainable end‑to‑end, and if the initial prediction is incorrect, the subsequent interpretability analysis is likely flawed (R2). The necessity of using an uninterpretable pseudo‑label is questioned (R4).

- **Insufficient evaluation of sparsity and explanations**  
  The sparsity experiment needs validation: most prototype‑learning studies address sparsity via global/local explanations or by adjusting the number of prototypes, but the paper only provides an ablation on K without comparing to those mechanisms (R1). The sparsity reported seems only slightly above a black‑box model (R4). Prototypical explanations are only evaluated qualitatively; a quantitative evaluation (e.g., following [2]) is missing (R1, citing [2]). The parametrised metric PQ is used with only one competitor, which is not convincing (R4).

- **Missing reproducibility details**  
  Standard deviations are absent from most tables (only Table 2 reports them). The number of seeds per result is not stated (R4). Table 4 is unclear: the “probed model” for other methods is not specified, and B‑cos itself is not included as a baseline (R4). The speed of the method compared to others (it must scan the entire training data) is not discussed (R3).

- **Additional technical issues**  
  The authors did not consider alternative similarity measures (e.g., cosine similarity) for the L2 distance (R1). The claim that “B‑cos transformers inherently learn human‑interpretable features” (l.217) lacks a citation (R4). The definitions of necessity and sufficiency (especially the shift in l.308) are inconsistent with the initial goal (R4).
