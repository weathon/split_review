## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FairSkin, a diffusion model (DM) framework for generating synthetic skin disease images conditioned on both race and disease labels. The authors address biases in generated image quality across skin tones (Caucasian, Asian, African) and disease classes by incorporating balanced resampling strategies, a class diversity loss during DM training, and downstream classifier balancing techniques (imbalance-aware augmentation and dynamic reweighting). Experiments are conducted on the Fitzpatrick17k dataset with five disease classes. The paper reports both generation quality (FID, IS) and fairness metrics (Demographic Parity, Equality of Skin Tone Generation Performance) for the generator, as well as classification accuracy and fairness for the downstream task.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important and timely topic: bias in ML performance due to dataset imbalances in medical (skin) image analysis, ensuring good performance at the long tail of both diseases and demographics. (R3, R4)
- The methodology examines bias mitigation at multiple stages: data sampling and loss function during generator training, and data sampling and dynamic reweighting during downstream classifier training. The steps are clearly described and easy to follow. (R1, R4)
- The paper provides several ablation studies and a large range of analyses (ablations, tables, t-SNE, qualitative images) that help understand the effect of the proposed components. (R1, R2, R4)
- The writing is clear, well-organized, and the introductory parts explain metrics, sampling, and loss adaptations well. (R1, R2, R4)
- The used dataset (Fitzpatrick17k) is appropriate as it contains real-world images and exemplifies the data availability bias common in ML dermatology. (R4)
- The analysis considers three racial groups and five diseases, and the approach appears to reduce disparities in generation quality across classes (R1), though other reviewers contest the extent of improvement (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and reliance on standard techniques** – The proposed reweighting and resampling methods (e.g., resampling inversely proportional to frequency, 1/N vs 1/sqrt(N)) are standard practices for handling imbalanced datasets and are considered intuitive. The class diversity loss is borrowed from existing work; the only claimed novelty is its application to skin images, which is insufficient for a methods contribution. (R2, R3)
2. **Lack of comparison to related work** – The paper fails to cite and compare against several recent works that apply diffusion models to fairness in skin lesion imaging, including those using the same Fitzpatrick17k dataset: Sagers et al. (NeurIPS Workshop 2022, 2023), Wang et al. (MICCAI ISIC 2024), and Ktena et al. (Nature Medicine 2024). It also omits comparisons with competing methods for fair dermatology diagnosis (e.g., Pakzad et al. ECCV ISIC 2022, and many others). (R2, R3)
3. **Inappropriate evaluation metrics** –  
   - FID and IS are used as quality metrics, but Inception Score is based on ImageNet statistics, which are not relevant to skin images. The authors do not justify why a pre-trained network on non-skin images should be used to measure FID for skin images. (R1, R3)  
   - Standard accuracy is used instead of balanced accuracy, which can give skewed results on imbalanced data. Balanced accuracy would provide a fairer assessment across underrepresented classes. (R1)  
   - The fairness metric Demographic Parity (DP) is limited: it is designed for only two demographic groups and requires equal positive prediction proportions, which is not satisfied in this work (Table 4). Metrics such as normalized accuracy range (NAR) have been proposed for multi-group scenarios. (R2, R3)  
   - DP may not capture true fairness; true positive rates across groups would be more clinically informative. Additional metrics like equal opportunity and worst-group statistics should be considered. (R2, R3, R4)  
   - FID calculations on very small sets (each group has at most 412 images, smallest disease subgroup only 12 examples) are unreliable and may yield unstable results. (R2, R4)
4. **Conflation of skin tone with race** – The paper maps Fitzpatrick skin type (FST) labels 1–6 directly to three “racial” groups (Caucasian, Asian, African) without justification. This is scientifically incorrect; FST was originally proposed to describe sun-burning propensity, not race or ethnicity. Fitzpatrick himself stated that ethnicity/race has no scientific basis. There is evidence, e.g., from a study of 556 participants in South Africa showing all six FST labels, that such mapping is invalid. The use of “racial/ethnic/demographic” interchangeably further confuses the issue. (R2, R3, R4)
5. **Weak support for claims and contradictory results** –  
   - Figure 4 shows that accuracy increases for ‘Caucasian’ but decreases for ‘Asian’ and ‘African’ compared to vanilla, which contradicts the paper’s claim of improving fairness for underrepresented groups. (R4)  
   - Overall generation quality (FID, IS) stays practically the same as the baseline (CBDM) or is worse; the paper does not demonstrate a clear quality improvement. (R4)  
   - Table 1 lacks an accuracy score for the generation step; the compound ESSP score is used but without clear discussion of how fairness and performance are balanced. (R4)  
   - The paper claims “significant performance improvements across various tasks” but validation accuracy remains nearly unchanged. (R4)  
   - Claims about “real-world applicability” are unsupported; no cross-dataset evaluation or analysis of clinically meaningful misclassifications is provided. (R4)
6. **Small dataset and single repetition** – The entire analysis is conducted on a single dataset (Fitzpatrick17k) with a fixed split. The scarcity of darker skin types leads to a very small test set (e.g., 1–2 datapoints for the smallest disease subgroup after an 8:1:1 split). Results from such a setup are unclear in generalizability and may be anecdotal; repetitions with different hold-out sets are needed. (R4)
7. **Missing evaluation across datasets and attributes** – No experiments on additional datasets (e.g., SCIN, Slice-3D, Casual Conversations, FACET) are performed. A cross-dataset transfer evaluation would strengthen the conclusions. Using additional demographic attributes (age, gender) could also verify the method’s generalizability. (R1, R4)
8. **Limited diversity of generated images** – Despite the class diversity loss, the generated images still lack diversity, which may limit their utility in augmenting datasets for varied skin lesion types. (R2)
9. **Missing stratified analysis and ablation clarity** – Deeper analysis stratified by disease is lacking. The ablation study with FairSkin-XYC codes is hard to parse; a tabular ablation study would better clarify the contribution of each component. The ablations on number of augmentations show wild variance with no clear trends, making it unclear how to choose the number in practice. (R4)
10. **Specific technical and writing issues** –  
   - The mathematical definition of imbalance (N_r1 != N_r2) is too simplistic; small differences do not necessarily indicate bias. (R3)  
   - Mode collapse is mentioned but not explained or introduced. (R3)  
   - The subtraction of two noise signals as a diversity measure seems unreliable given randomness; a statistical measure (e.g., variance difference) would be more robust. (R3)  
   - The term “Vanilla” is not defined. (R3)  
   - Equations are not numbered. (R3)  
   - Title contains a typo: “Geneartion” should be “Generation”. (R1)  
   - The claim “points corresponding to different diseases are more densely clustered” (4.5) is disagreed with; clusters appear more muddled. (R4)  
   - Schematic in Fig 1 may have an error (accuracy of African diminished in blue after?) and suggests improving fairness costs accuracy for some groups, which the paper does not address. (R3)
