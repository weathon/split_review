## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a watermarking scheme for open-source LLMs by adding Gaussian noise to the final layer biases and defines “unremovable watermarks” under assumptions about the adversary. Experiments are conducted on OPT-6.7B and OPT-1.3B. Reviewer scores are 3, 3, 3, 6, with one reviewer (R4, score 6) being notably more positive about the formal definition and contribution, while the other three (R1, R2, R3) rate the paper low (3) and express major concerns about the realism of assumptions, missing evaluations, overclaims, and presentation issues.

### Strengths
- **Importance and novelty of the problem** (R1, R2, R3, R4): Several reviewers acknowledge that watermarking open-source LLMs is an important open problem; R4 (score 6) explicitly states this is the first work to formally define and address “unremovable watermarks” planted in open‑source models.
- **Interesting technical idea** (R2): Adding noise following a normal distribution to the model’s output as a watermark is an interesting endeavor that could aid future algorithms.
- **Theoretical unremovability analysis** (R2, R3): The paper attempts to provide proofs and analysis for unremovability under certain assumptions.
- **Well-structured presentation and clear definitions** (R3): The paper is logically organised, with clear definitions and algorithms; R3 commends the presentation.

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic definition of model quality** (R1, R3, R4): Formalizing “high‑quality LLM” as closeness in the weights of the last bias layer is arbitrary and far from realistic notions of quality. This greatly simplifies the problem and makes the theoretical results largely irrelevant to real‑world robustness. R4 notes that Definition 2 is more a “similarity” measure because two models can look different but have the same quality of responses.
- **Overclaims of “unremovability”** (R1): The blanket claim that the watermark is “unremovable” (title, abstract, introduction) is dangerous and should be qualified to reflect the peculiar definition of quality. The citation of Zhang et al. (2024) (L173) to claim any LLM watermark is removable is a misunderstanding of the original paper.
- **Missing realistic attack vectors** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
    - No robustness evaluation against paraphrasing (e.g., Dipper, other LLMs) or token‑level perturbations (changing two characters, deleting parts). R2 and R3 specifically ask for adaptive paraphrasers; R4 asks for a clear definition of the perturbation channels studied.
    - No experiments with fine‑tuning, PEFT, quantization, or pruning – only token substitution and Gaussian weight perturbations. R1 notes that prior work (Gu et al. 2024) considers fine‑tuning but the paper does not.
    - No comparison with other watermarking schemes, including Gu et al. (2024) and ByteDance’s weight‑injection approach (arXiv:2403.10553).
- **Scheme applicability issues** (R1, R2):
    - Many popular LLMs, including OPT models used in the experiments, do not use a final‑layer bias (GitHub reference). This brings the evaluation into question; were OPT models modified to use a bias? (R1’s question)
    - The scheme is a variant of UnigramWatermark, lacking novel technical aspects. R2 questions the claim of “first watermarking scheme for open‑source LLMs”, as other schemes can also be deployed in open‑source.
- **Unclear motivation and scenario** (R1, R2): The paper starts from detecting that a model was based on a watermarked model from its weights – a different scenario than the usual LLM output detection. This is unexplained and unmotivated. R2 asks: if the LLM is open‑source, what is the benefit of watermarking when the attacker has direct access to the weights?
- **Detection efficiency unclear** (R2): How many tokens are required for detection, especially with optimal hyperparameters? The experiments are insufficient to prove efficiency and strength.
- **High false positive rates (FPRs) and no baseline** (R1): The evaluation uses high FPRs and includes no comparison with any baseline. Performance is below non‑open‑source watermarking baselines, but the paper does not make a convincing case for usefulness beyond the open‑source setting.
- **Multiple unjustified assumptions** (R3, R4): The proofs rely on many explicit and implicit assumptions (e.g., “let C be such that…”, “c₂‑high quality…”), making the final conclusion far from ideal. The authors call them “reasonable” without proper discussion or justification.
- **No indistinguishability/undetectability of output** (R3, R4): The paper does not discuss whether the output of the watermarked model is computationally indistinguishable from that of the non‑watermarked model (distortion‑free). It also does not measure utility degradation using human evaluation or specific metrics beyond a mathematical oracle.
- **Writing and presentation issues** (R1, R3, R4):
    - The paper is confusing and convoluted, seems rushed, and is often hard to understand (R1).
    - Lack of discussions and justifications for assumptions (R3, R4).
    - Definitions (e.g., Def 2, Def 4) mix up input spaces and are not clearly stated (R3, R4).
    - Specific example: the description of Aaronson (2022), Christ (2024), Fairoze (2023) as red‑/green‑list based is incorrect (R1’s question).
- **Missing ablation / clarity on algorithms** (R2): Relationship between Algorithm 3 and Algorithm 4 is unclear; the definition of Δ(x_i) in Algorithm 4 and the purpose of checking x_i ∈ S are not explained.
- **Fine‑tuning not addressed** (R1, R3, R4): What happens to the watermark if the model is fine‑tuned? Black‑box methods still work if the model is fine‑tuned; this should be tested with simple experiments.
