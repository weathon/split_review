## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers largely agree that LASeR addresses a relevant problem—adaptively selecting reward models during LLM training using a multi-armed bandit framework (LinUCB) to avoid conflicts and computational burden of ensemble methods. The paper is generally well-written and presents strong empirical gains across reasoning, QA, and instruction-following tasks. However, significant weaknesses are raised by multiple reviewers concerning the fairness of baselines, limited comparison with adaptive ensemble methods, the motivation for using LinUCB over simpler classifiers, and concerns about aligning the MAB reward (negative training loss) with bias mitigation. One reviewer (score 3) is negative overall, finding the experimental setup insufficient to justify the method; two reviewers (scores 5) are moderately positive but note critical gaps; one reviewer (score 8) is positive with minor issues.

### Strengths
- **Clear writing and motivation**: The paper is highly readable and presents the method clearly (Reviewers 1, 4). The use of LinUCB to choose RMs per instance is well motivated (Reviewer 4) and intuitively appealing (Reviewer 4).
- **Strong empirical results**: LASeR shows improvements over its chosen baselines (Reviewer 1: "results are strong") across multiple tasks including commonsense/math reasoning, instruction-following (AlpacaEval win rate), and long-context QA (Reviewers 2, 3). Results indicate generalizability (Reviewer 3).
- **Avoids computational burden of ensemble**: The MAB framework dynamically selects the most suitable RM at the instance level, avoiding the need to run all RMs for every sample (Reviewer 3) and addressing conflicts from multiple RM signals (Reviewer 2).
- **Thorough evaluation**: Extensive experiments across reasoning, instruction-following, and long-context understand tasks (Reviewers 2, 4). Reviewer 4 specifically notes "experiments are extensive".

### Weaknesses
- **Weak baselines and fairness of comparison** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4):
  - No baseline that selects more than one RM over an epoch; all baselines either use a fixed RM, random selection, or simple offline ensemble averaging (Reviewer 1). A simple classifier that learns to choose an RM based on query type would be a stronger baseline (Reviewer 1).
  - The baselines do not actively leverage information from interaction with the data (Reviewer 2). In Table 4, the best single RM (Zephyr-7b-alpha) is not top on some datasets, contradicting claims in Appendix B (Reviewer 2).
  - The paper only compares LASeR with a simple offline RM ensemble using averaged scores; more sophisticated adaptive ensemble methods exist (e.g., [A1, A2]) and are not compared (Reviewer 2). Results on reasoning are the only ones compared against Offline RM Ensemble (Reviewer 2).
  - Sequential RM selection performs better than other baselines across most datasets, but no explanation is given (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of justification for LinUCB over simpler approaches** (Reviewer 1):
  - Why use a multi-armed bandit? There is no discussion of the advantages of exploration that MABs provide (Reviewer 1). Why not simply train a classifier (or matrix A) to choose based on a criterion? Why not use a linear combination of reward functions instead of arg max? (Reviewer 1).
- **Concerns about reward definition (negative training loss)** (Reviewer 3):
  - Setting the MAB reward as negative training loss raises an alignment issue: if the MAB consistently selects RMs that align with the LM (low loss), it may reinforce existing biases rather than correct mistakes. If the LM makes an error and the RM correctly identifies it, the loss is high, so the MAB could avoid that RM, making the model less capable of correcting errors (Reviewer 3).
- **Efficiency concerns** (Reviewer 2): With high-dimensional embeddings or many RMs, LASeR must compute the inverse of the covariance matrix \(A\) for each arm, which could be computationally intensive (Reviewer 2).
- **Adversarial batch example** (Reviewer 1): In a scenario where each RM is good only for a specific subset of inputs and batches are sequentially ordered (not randomized), a batch of size \(K\) may contain inputs all poorly served by a single chosen RM. This potential issue is not discussed.
- **Missing details and minor issues** (Reviewers 1, 2, 4):
  - No information on how many preference pairs are generated per iteration (Reviewer 4).
  - Figure 2 is not referred to in the main text (Reviewer 4). Appendix B is not referenced (Reviewer 4). Add y-axis to Figure 4 (Reviewer 4).
  - Undefined or misleading notations: Equation (1) \(x\) should be \(x_i\); \(\sigma, \beta\) not defined earlier (Reviewer 2). In Section 3.2, \(\mathbf{x}_{m,t}\) should represent the batch at step \(t\) of iteration \(m\); \(\mathbf{x}_t\) should be clearly defined (Reviewer 2). Left-hand side of equation (2) should be index of selected RM, not the model itself (Reviewer 2). Grammar and typos present (Reviewer 2).
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments**:
  - Why does the ensemble baseline perform worse than the best RM? (Reviewer 4, lines 95-97, Table 1)
  - For long-context understanding (Table 3), are there results using RM to generate preference pairs and use them as in-context examples? (Reviewer 4)
  - Could the authors provide more insights on each RM (e.g., which RM works well for which examples) and could clustering batches by input type improve performance? (Reviewer 1) Would it be better to train domain-specific RMs rather than use generic ones? (Reviewer 1)
  - How many RMs does the method generalize to? (Reviewer 1)
