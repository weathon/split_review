## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Functionally-Aligned Multi-Agents (FAMA) framework that uses LLMs (fine-tuned with MAPPO as a shared actor and centralized critic) and a discrete natural language communication module for cooperative textual MARL. Experiments are performed on BabyAI-Text and an autonomous driving junction environment across four coordination tasks. The paper claims that FAMA consistently outperforms independent learning LLMs and traditional symbolic RL methods. Reviewer opinions on experimental quality and contribution are mixed: one reviewer (R3, rating 6) finds the experiments solid enough to demonstrate superiority and the method novel; another (R1, rating 5) considers the idea straightforward; two reviewers (R2 and R4, rating 3 each) raise concerns about lack of significant improvements, insufficient analysis, presentation, and the limited departure from prior CTDE methods.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of using LLMs in MARL**: The application of LLMs as policy mechanisms in multi-agent systems is highly innovative and sets the paper apart (R4). Extending LLM-based RL to multi-agent settings, where natural language plays a natural role in communication, is interesting and new (R3). The idea is straightforward in context but novel (R1).
- **Focus on communication for coordination**: The paper studies an important question—whether natural language communication improves coordination and interpretability in MARL (R2). The design of prompts and the communication module is well-formulated, adding depth and rigor (R4).
- **Framework design**: FAMA introduces a new approach to tune a pretrained LLM with MAPPO as a shared actor and centralized critic (functional alignment) and integrates a discrete natural language communication module (R2). The ablation study reinforces the contribution of the communication module (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unconvincing experimental results**: In Figure 3, on the Junction Environment, there is no significant difference between FAMA and the baseline (individual learning), which weakens the claimed effectiveness (R2). Only the small Flan-T5 model is used in Figure 3 while the Base model is used in Figure 4, an inconsistency not explained (R2). The environments are simple, toysome, and have short horizons; harder environments are needed (R2, R3). More training details on baselines are required (R2).
- **Insufficient analysis of communication**: For the second research question, the results in Figure 4 are not enough; more analysis on which discrete messages are selected and how they affect performance is needed (R2). The paper does not provide a comparative analysis quantifying how much better natural language communication is compared to non-natural language methods (R4).
- **Lack of deeper insights and justification**: There is no clear explanation why LLM-based agents outperform traditional RL algorithms, especially given the tasks are not complicated (R1). The alignment strategy does not differ significantly from past CTDE methods, raising questions about the contribution beyond the communication part (R4). The advantage of using multiple LLMs over a single central LLM is not justified (R1). If the method still relies on CTDE credit assignment, where does the advantage of LLMs manifest apart from communication? (R4)
- **Missing details and unclear notation**: Notation in Section 4 is difficult to understand and ambiguous (R3). Examples: the parameters of \(\hat{p}_i^V\) are unclear (R3); whether the prompt function \(p_i^V\) is fixed or trainable is not stated (R1); how the discrete messages \(m_i^V\) are generated (automatically or pre-selected and then scored by the LLM) is not explained (R3). The communication mechanism for more than two agents is not described (R2). Using a simple textual agent identifier with a shared actor may not work for heterogeneous agents (R2).
- **Presentation issues**: The paper is poorly written with many typos and weird citation formats (R2). Figures need improvement: text in Figures 1 and 2 is too small, and in Figure 3 it is hard to tell whether higher or lower metric is better (R2). Notation in Section 4.1 is inconsistent (R2). The paper lacks theoretical justification (R3).
- **Limited applicability**: The method is constrained to text-based environments, which limits generalizability to general RL tasks where text cannot be used directly for actions (R4). The approach still relies on CTDE credit assignment, so the unique advantage of LLMs is only in the communication aspect (R4).
