## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a self-supervised learning framework (nnSSL) for 3D medical image segmentation based on Masked Autoencoders (MAEs). It pretrains on a large private dataset of ~39K brain MRI volumes (44K before filtering) using a Residual Encoder U-Net within the nnU-Net framework. The model is developed on 5 datasets and evaluated on 8 brain MRI segmentation tasks, outperforming the nnU-Net baseline by ~3 Dice points and previous SSL methods. The paper identifies three pitfalls (small pretraining datasets, inadequate architectures, insufficient evaluation) and addresses them with large-scale data, a ResEncoder U-Net, and robust evaluation. One reviewer (score 6) found the work timely, thorough, and valuable despite limited novelty; the other three (scores 3,3,3) were more critical, citing missing comparisons, insufficient ablations, unclear writing, and limited innovation.

### Strengths
- Timely and relevant contribution to SSL for medical image segmentation; simple yet effective framework.
- Large-scale pretraining dataset (~39K 3D brain MRI volumes) and diverse downstream evaluation across 13 datasets/tasks.
- Thorough evaluation with comparisons to important baselines (nnU-Net, Model Genesis, Volume Fusion, VoCo).
- Valuable analysis of design choices (sparsification, masking, patch size, scaling).
- Making sparse 3D MAE work in CNNs is non-trivial; public code release is a sizeable contribution (R1).
- Identifies and tackles three key issues hampering previous SSL methods (small data, inadequate backbone, insufficient evaluation).

### Weaknesses
**Missing comparisons & related work**
- No comparison with existing 3D MAE pretraining frameworks for medical images (e.g., Chen et al., WACV 2023 [3]; Tang et al., CVPR 2022 [5]). Table 3 excludes them without justification (R2, R3).
- Lacks references to two relevant recent works: AMAES (Munk et al., 2024) which uses a public 44K MRI dataset for CNN MAE pretraining, and HySpark (Tang et al., 2024) for large-scale medical image pretraining (R1).

**Insufficient ablations & evidence**
- Patch size: uses a bottleneck patch of 5×5×5 (equivalent to 32×32×32 in image space), which may be too large. Prior work (Chen et al. used 16×16×16; Zhang et al. used 8×8×8) indicates small patch size and high mask ratio are key. No ablation on patch size effect; thus performance gains may stem from larger patch size rather than the method (R1, R2).
- Scaling analysis is incomplete: the paper only varies batch size (8×), learning rate, and training iterations (4×). True scaling should explore increasing data size (e.g., 10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% of 40K) and model depth/parameters. The naming “S3D-L” is misleading because it does not increase model parameters (R1, R2). Training iteration extension may saturate (MAE paper Fig. 7: 800 vs 1600 epochs close).
- Table 1-a shows performance improvements from sparsification adaptations, raising doubt whether final results (Table 3) come from stacking tricks rather than the core method. Table 1-c shows no improvement from scaling; Table 3 even shows performance degradation with scaling—unexplained (R3).
- No standard deviations reported for Dice scores; unclear if differences (e.g., 71.66 vs 71.35) are statistically significant (R4).

**Missing implementation & reproducibility details**
- Finetuning procedure is insufficiently described: how are pretrained weights transferred? Which framework (nnU-Net or similar)? Are learning rates, augmentations, configurations fixed across datasets? (R1)
- Pretraining dataset is private with limited information; reproducibility would benefit from releasing checkpoints trained on a public dataset of similar size (e.g., Brains-45K) (R1).
- Abstract claims 44K volumes, but after filtering the actual pretraining set is 39K—discrepancy is misleading (R1, R4).

**Limited novelty & innovation**
- Methodological novelty is low: simply applies well-established MAE with adaptations from Tian et al. (2023) and Woo et al. (2023) to a U-Net backbone. No further exploration or improvements beyond applying existing techniques (R3, R4).
- Application novelty is partially limited because pretrained models are not publicly available (R4).
- Paper reads like a technical report rather than a research paper; lacks discussion of why the method works or fails (R3).

**Writing, figures, and presentation**
- Unclear and disorganized manuscript: no “Related Work” section to introduce previous methods (VoCo, VF, MG) before using them as baselines; experimental description mixed with results and discussion; conclusion not clearly separated (R3, R4).
- Confusing symbols: D[1~9], DS[1~9], dataset names used interchangeably; excessive bold text and incorrect right quotes (R3).
- Figure 1 is hard to understand (mixes development and testing workflow without showing model pipeline); Figure 2 poorly organized (excessive whitespace, hard-to-read text/numbers); Figure 3 appears program-generated with meaningless whitespace (R3, R4).
- No visualizations of segmentation results (R3).
- Captions for tables should be placed above tables (R3).
- Title generalizes to “3D Medical Image Segmentation” but experiments only on brain MRI (R4).
