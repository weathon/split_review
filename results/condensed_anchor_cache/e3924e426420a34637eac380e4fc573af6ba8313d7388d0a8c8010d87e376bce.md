## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies reward poisoning attacks on Combinatorial Multi-armed Bandits (CMAB). It introduces the concept of polynomial attackability (attack cost sublinear in time horizon T and polynomial in number of base arms m), provides a sufficient and necessary condition for attackability, and presents an attack algorithm for attackable instances. The paper further shows that attackability depends on whether the CMAB instance is known or unknown to the adversary, implying that general attack strategies for unknown environments do not exist. Theoretical results are validated on real-world applications: probabilistic maximum covering problem, online minimum spanning tree, cascading bandits for online ranking, and online shortest path.

### Strengths
- First work to study adversarial attacks on CMAB; timely and novel topic.
- Introduces the novel concept of polynomial attackability, capturing vulnerability and robustness of CMAB systems, with a stronger requirement than existing conditions.
- Provides both a sufficient and a necessary condition for polynomial attackability when the CMAB instance is known.
- Presents a hard example (Example 4.2) demonstrating that an instance can be polynomially attackable when the adversary knows the environment but becomes unattackable when the environment is unknown, highlighting the practical difficulty of general attacks.
- Rigorous theoretical analysis; the characterization of attackability as a function of known vs. unknown environment is impressive and has practical implications for designing secure CMAB systems.
- Extensive experiments on multiple real-world CMAB applications validate the theoretical findings; source code is provided for reproducibility.
- The paper is well-written and clear in presenting concepts, definitions, and analysis.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation for polynomial scaling in m is not well justified.** The paper argues that exponential cost in m can exceed T, but m is a fixed parameter while T grows, so the scaling in T is the primary concern. A more rigorous justification for why polynomial dependence on m is critical is needed.
- **Focus on known (white-box) environment limits practical relevance.** The analysis primarily assumes the adversary knows all parameters (reward distributions, outcome distributions). Realistic scenarios are black-box with limited adversary knowledge. The paper does not provide algorithmic approaches for the attacker to learn an unknown environment (e.g., in specific settings like PMC). This is a significant gap; future work on black-box conditions is necessary.
- **Insufficient discussion of the threat model’s scope and limitations.** The polynomial attackability condition is derived for a specific reward-manipulation adversary (modifying base arm outcomes). Other attack types (environment poisoning, action poisoning) could alter the condition (e.g., by changing Δ_M). Some statements in the abstract and introduction are therefore inaccurate. The limitations of the findings are not thoroughly discussed.
- **Algorithm 1 may incur unnecessarily high attack costs.** The attack algorithm applies a uniform attack across all arms rather than a fine-grained design tailored to reward gaps. The cost dependence is 1/Δ_{S^*}, which is worse than the linear dependence on ∑Δ_i in prior work (Jun et al. 2018, Liu and Shroff 2019), especially when Δ is small. A more adaptive attack could reduce cost.
- **Experimental results do not convincingly demonstrate attack effectiveness.** For instance, in Figures 2(g) and 2(h), the target super arm is pulled only about 4e3 out of 1e5 iterations (4%). Moreover, no experiments illustrate the case of polynomial unattackability; it would be helpful to run experiments on the hard example using heuristic attacks with sublinear budget.
- **Technical gap in Corollary 4.3.** There is no guarantee that a randomly picked super arm S satisfies Δ_S > 0; even for cascading bandits, online MST, and online PMC with a greedy oracle, some super arms have Δ_S = 0.
- **Clarity issues with the problem setup.** The definition of super arms mentions an infinite action space, but then defines each super arm as a set of base arms, implying cardinality 2^m. The role of the trigger function from the previous framework (Wang & Chen) is unclear; it is not obvious whether the trigger function is necessary given the set-based definition. The paper would benefit from a discussion on simpler CMAB models first.
