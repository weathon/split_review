## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Difference Alignment Regularization (DAR) to enforce consistency among inter-class representation differences, aiming to reduce overfitting in similarity learning. It introduces a cross-total-variation (CTV) norm to measure divergence among differences and integrates DAR into Difference-Aligned Similarity Learning (DASL). Extensive experiments across image retrieval, face recognition, image classification, and NLP classification show strong performance in both supervised metric learning and unsupervised contrastive learning. Reviewers agree the empirical results are impressive but disagree on the theoretical analysis: some (R1,R2) raise concerns about assumptions and consistency, while others (R4) find the theory sound; R3 questions the tradeoff in the generalization bound.

### Strengths
- Interesting and novel idea: Regularizing the "second-order" feature difference (i.e., consistency among inter-class representation differences) is hardly explored before. (R1, R2)
- Conceptual experiments (Figure 2) illustrate the intuition well and corroborate the point. (R1)
- Extensive and impressive numerical results on multi-domain data with clear margins over state-of-the-art methods. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The method exhibits ease of tuning and stability w.r.t. hyperparameters, which is rare among deep learning methods and practical for real-world use. (R1)
- Theoretical justification is provided, including a PAC-style bound on the generalization gap. (R2, R4)
- The paper is clearly written and figures are easy to understand. (R1, R3)
- The problem of similarity-based representation learning is important to the machine learning field. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical assumptions and consistency**: Multiple reviewers question the theoretical analysis. R1: Theorems 3 and 4 assume $x, z, \hat{x}, \hat{z}$ are from the same distribution, but inter-class samples are from different distributions, potentially making the results meaningless in practice. R2: Theorem 4 bounds the generalization gap using $\max d'(t;\hat{t})$, a first-order metric, while the method claims to control higher-order variations—this disconnect weakens the theoretical support. R2 also argues the motivation example (Figure 2) is flawed: generalization in a mixed-Gaussian scenario depends primarily on the absolute first-order distance between cluster means, not higher-order smoothness (e.g., three Gaussians at (0,1),(1,0),(1,10000) yield smaller error than at (0,1),(1,0),(1,1)). R3: Theorem 4 ignores the tradeoff between generalization and approximation—higher $\lambda$ reduces the generalization bound but makes $\mathcal{L}(\phi)$ a worse approximation of the true objective, limiting the theorem’s meaningfulness. R1 suggests the theoretical analysis may be "mathy" with limited practical meaning. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Relation to standard contrastive loss**: R3 asks how DAR differs from the standard contrastive loss (with margins $\delta^+, \delta^-$), which also ensures all negative pairs are $\delta^-$ away—does DAR effectively enforce a secondary negative margin? A clearer distinction is needed. (R3)
- **Complexity and efficiency**: R4 notes that computing higher-order differences may involve complex calculation; a running-time comparison on a specific dataset is requested. (R4)
- **Missing discussion of related works**: R4 suggests comparing with other methods that enhance inter-class compactness (e.g., multi-metric learning: [1] TPAMI 2019, [2] ICCV 2017, [3] TPAMI 2018) to clarify differences. (R4)
- **Sensitivity to regularization parameter $\lambda$**: R4 asks whether $\lambda$ heavily influences final performance, and what the default choice is. (R4)
- **Writing**: R2 recommends revising the introduction to focus more on the motivation and purpose of higher-order feature distances, rather than general representation learning discussion. (R2)
- **Minor math issues**: R3 notes complicated notation, that Algorithm 1 is obvious given Eq. (8), and that Theorem 2 follows trivially from standard GD convergence. (R3)

*Note*: Reviewers disagree on the strength of the theoretical contribution: R1 and R2 find it weak or inconsistent, R3 questions its meaningfulness, while R4 considers it sound. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
