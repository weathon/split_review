- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Plasticity-Driven Sparsity Training (PlaD) for Deep Reinforcement Learning, combining periodic memory resets and dynamic weight rescaling (DWR) with iterative magnitude pruning. PlaD is evaluated on MuJoCo locomotion tasks with SAC, aiming to maintain network plasticity under high sparsity (>90%). Three reviewers score the paper 3, and one reviewer scores it 5, with the majority raising fundamental concerns about motivation, definition clarity, statistical validity, and the claimed connection between sparsity and plasticity.

### Strengths
- **Novel method with interesting components**: PlaD is a novel combination of periodic memory resets and dynamic weight rescaling that counters sparsity-induced plasticity loss. The weight shrinkage ratio (WSR) provides a lens into weight dynamics, and DWR is an interesting approach to normalization for sparse networks (Reviewers 1, 2). PlaD is designed to integrate with existing DRL algorithms and pruning methods (Reviewer 1).
- **Empirical support for components**: Ablation studies show that both periodic memory reset and DWR contribute to PlaD’s performance (Reviewers 2, 4). The empirical verification is good, and PlaD outperforms baseline sparse methods (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Relevance of dense-to-sparse training**: The paper argues that dense-to-sparse training, while not necessarily reducing peak memory, yields a purely sparse inference model that is important for RL applications (Reviewer 4; score 5, more positive minority).
- **Exciting research direction**: The general idea that sparsity can help maintain plasticity is promising (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear and insufficient motivation for sparse training in RL**: The motivation for sparse-to-sparse training in RL is not compelling. It is unclear why memory conservation is beneficial for the typically smaller models used in RL, and no concrete tangible benefit is identified (Reviewer 1). Claims about computational efficiency (e.g., “sparse training may escalate overall computational cost”) are not experimentally explored, and the paper does not clarify whether it refers to wall-clock time, FLOPs, or another measure (Reviewer 2).
- **No established link between sparsity and plasticity loss**: The central claim that increased sparsity leads to loss of plasticity is not supported by previous work or demonstrated in the paper. Cited papers (neuron dormancy, primacy bias) do not provide evidence for this connection (Reviewer 2). The relationship between WSR (which never exceeds 0.5) and plasticity is unclear; increased WSR could be due to other factors (initial parameters, weight decay, SGD regularization)—correlation does not imply causation (Reviewer 4). No concrete evidence is provided that weight shrinkage reduces the network’s ability to learn (Reviewer 2).
- **Ambiguous/incorrect definitions**: 
  - WSR is defined as “proportion of weights” but immediately described as “ratio of neurons”; the term \(h_t^l\) is used without definition (Reviewer 3). 
  - The claim in Section 4.2 that “shrinkage speed increases” is wrong: WSR never exceeds 0.5, meaning weights are on average *increasing*, not shrinking (Reviewer 3). 
  - DWR’s definition uses \(\gamma^l\) (unclear whether it is \(\Gamma^l\)), and the element-wise product between a vector \(h^{l-1}\) and a weight mask matrix \(\Gamma^l\) is ill-defined (Reviewer 3). 
  - The method is described as “weight rescaling” but actually rescales activations; it would be more accurate to call it “dynamically sparsified layer normalization” (Reviewer 2). 
  - “First-order gradient of shrinkage” and “shrinkage speed” are undefined, and weights are not normally distributed beyond initialization, undermining the WSR analysis (Reviewer 2). 
  - There is a typo in the definition of \(M_s\): \(L\) is used for both the number of layers and the number of neurons in the \(l\)-th layer (Reviewer 4).
- **Improper statistical reporting and lack of significance**: 
  - In Figure 5, error bars overlap heavily, making it impossible to discern significant differences (Reviewer 2). 
  - In Figure 6, the paper claims that “Reset buffer distinctly outperforms the Small Buffer strategy in 3 out of 4 tasks, with 2 of these improvements being statistically significant”—this is incorrect; no task shows a statistically significant difference (Reviewer 3). 
  - The comparison against baselines (e.g., dense-to-sparse) lacks statistical significance overall (Reviewer 2).
- **Problematic periodic memory reset**: 
  - The claim that the replay buffer is the *primary* source of non-stationarity is overclaimed; it may be a contributing factor, but if non-stationarity is the concern, an on-policy algorithm could be used instead of resetting the buffer (Reviewer 2). 
  - Empirical and theoretical evidence shows that larger memory buffers improve performance by reducing distribution shifts and preventing catastrophic forgetting—periodic resets are counterintuitive and need trade-off justification (Reviewer 1). 
  - Resetting the replay buffer is computationally wasteful and discards valuable experience; the primacy bias paper suggests that the experience stored in the buffer is more valuable than the neural network itself (Reviewer 2). 
  - The method uses a high replay ratio for sample efficiency, but resetting the buffer undermines that goal (Reviewer 2). 
  - No criteria are given for how far the policy must drift before collecting new data into an empty buffer (Reviewer 4).
- **Confusion between plasticity and generalization**: The Ash & Adams paper concerns generalization, not plasticity (which is about inability to minimize error). The nature of the connection between the two is not clarified (Reviewer 2). Gradient shrinkage is a desirable property for reaching a local minimum, not a sign of plasticity loss (Reviewer 2).
- **Question about necessity of maintaining plasticity**: Low plasticity means many neurons are dormant and can be removed, which is beneficial for sparsity. Increased plasticity may make pruning more difficult (Reviewer 4).  
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: No theoretical relationship between plasticity and feasible pruning ratio is provided, weakening the motivation (Reviewer 4).  
- **Limited comparison**: Results are only shown on four MuJoCo tasks with SAC; comparisons against on-policy algorithms are missing (Reviewer 2). The conclusion does not establish a link between plasticity loss and sparse training (Reviewer 2).
