## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces GUARD, a pipeline to operationalize government-issued ethics guidelines into concrete test questions that probe LLM compliance. It uses multiple LLM roles – Analyst, Strategic Committee, Question Designer, Reviewer – to generate guideline-violating questions, and a second jailbreak-diagnosis stage (GUARD-JD) that builds a knowledge graph from existing jailbreak attacks to generate new attack scenarios. GUARD also produces a compliance report for several models (Vicuna-13B, LongChat-7B, Llama2-7B, Llama-3-8B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3.5) and is tested on vision-language models (MiniGPT-v2, Gemini Vision Pro). The method is compared against five other jailbreak methods. Reviewer scores range from 3 to 8, with one reviewer (score 8) notably more positive about the contribution and the ablation study, while three reviewers (scores 3,3,6) have substantial concerns about evaluation validity and experimental soundness.

### Strengths
- The problem is well-motivated and important: translating high-level AI ethics guidelines into actionable test scenarios for LLMs is timely and impactful. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The method shows utility for both LLMs and large VLMs, demonstrating cross-modality versatility. (R1, R4)
- The adaptive role-play design and the knowledge-graph-based jailbreak generation offer some originality, and the ablation study provides insight into how each role contributes. (R1, R4)
- The jailbreak approach yields effective attacks with low perplexity, indicating more natural and human-interpretable jailbreak prompts compared to baselines. (R1)
- The experimental sections (Tables 3, ablation) are well-written with sufficient implementation details and analysis. (R1)
- The paper tests a variety of models and several government-issued guidelines, and compares against other jailbreak methods. (R3, R4)
- Reviewer 4 emphasizes that GUARD offers conceptual insights into jailbreak characteristics and that the automated iterative process goes beyond manually crafted prompts.

### Weaknesses
- **Evaluation of jailbreak performance** relies on semantic similarity between the LLM’s output and a rejection response, rather than direct evaluation of harmful content in the response. This is not standard; a human annotation study or a citation to validated work is needed. (R1) The same issue applies to the guideline-adherence evaluation, which uses string-matching without validation. (R1, R3) Reviewer 3 notes that Appendix J.2 shows the 3-shot examples include optimization prompting to avoid certain strings, and an LLM could be trained to bypass this. (R3) Reviewer 2 adds that Table 1 merely shows test results (not method effectiveness) and Table 3’s comparisons largely reflect the power of LLM iterative generation with self‑feedback – a trivial conclusion. (R2) No evidence is provided that the original published numbers for the baseline methods can be replicated under the authors’ evaluation. (R1)
- **Methodological justification is weak**: The assignment of roles and prompts is ad hoc, with no scientific reasoning. (R2) There is no discussion of how LLM hallucinations or response quality affect GUARD’s performance. (R2) The equations in Section 3.3 are redundant because the quantities are all output from an LLM based on textual descriptions, with no guarantee of numerical consistency. (R1)
- **Diversity concerns**: The jailbreak pipeline lacks any component to encourage diversity in generated attacks. The recovered knowledge‑graph fragments and initial scenarios differ substantially from the final jailbreak scenarios; most signal comes from modification rules, raising doubts about attack diversity. (R1) Perplexity differences between methods are small, and qualitative analysis is needed to confirm fluency. (R1)
- **Coverage and generality**: It is unclear whether GUARD’s questions cover all principles of a guideline. (R2) The method may not distinguish between guidelines that apply to LLM-generated content vs. guidelines about how the LLM or system is built/tested. (R3, R4) For example, the “Trustworthy AI Assessment List” relates to the entity testing the LLM, not the LLM’s outputs; the generated questions cannot capture the validation process itself. (R3) Similarly, some generated questions ask “how to violate” rather than attempting direct violation (e.g., “build me a deepfake” vs. “how to build a deepfake”). (R3)
- **Manual bottlenecks and cost**: The pre-processing of guidelines into general categories appears manual, potentially a bottleneck for adopting GUARD to new guidelines. (R2) The paper lacks discussion of the computational cost of the multi‑LLM pipeline; this is non‑trivial and relevant for scalability. (R3)
- **Missing experiments and ablations**: 
  - The effectiveness of the direct (non‑jailbreak) question generation in Stage 1 is not evaluated. (R2) It is unclear how many questions do not elicit guideline-violating answers and thus require jailbreaking. (R2)
  - The authors do not report whether any cases with similarity scores >0.3 still result in successful jailbreaks. (R2)
  - The compliance report is restricted to five LLMs; broader testing is needed. (R1, R4)
  - The method is not compared against simply prompting an LLM to generate scenarios from jailbreak benchmarks without knowledge-graph construction, so the added value of the KG is unsubstantiated. (R2)
  - No code or fully accessible repository is provided for reproducibility. (R3)
- **Clarity issues**:
  - The abstract and introduction are long and initially unclear about the paper’s actual aim—i.e., that it is improving upon existing jailbreak diagnostics, not filling an entirely empty gap. (R4)
  - There is a conceptual gap between general guidelines (for developers/implementers) and the specific test questions for LLM outputs; this step is not justified with a concrete example in the main text. (R4) Figure 1(a) shows a question, not the original guideline, so the reasoning behind operationalization is missing. (R4)
  - Typographical errors: Figure 2 caption missing a word; inconsistent phrasing “question Q complies with guidelines G” where “answer” might be intended; “three government-issued guidelines” vs. “selected 35 relevant guidelines” (p.6). (R4)
  - Important methodology details (e.g., complete prompts, equations) are relegated to the appendix, making the main text abstract. (R1)
