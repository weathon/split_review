## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the trade-off between a single large model and an ensemble of smaller models under a fixed total parameter budget, focusing on random-feature ridge regression (RF-KRR). The authors prove that for RF ensembles, larger data size, feature size, or ensemble size each improve test risk (Theorem 1), but given a fixed total parameter count, a single model (\(K=1\)) with optimally tuned ridge achieves optimal performance (Theorem 2, “no free lunch”). Scaling laws are derived under a standard model of kernel eigenspectrum decay, identifying conditions (the richness parameter \(r>0.5\)) where ensembles can achieve near-optimal scaling. Experiments on binarized CIFAR-10 with ReLU random features, CIFAR-10 with convolutional networks (controlled via richness parameter \(\gamma\)), and C4 with transformers validate the theoretical predictions, showing that test accuracy or training loss degrades monotonically with ensemble size when hyperparameters are optimally chosen.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and easy to follow (all reviewers).
- The research question (parameter allocation between single large model vs. ensemble of smaller models) is practically relevant and well-motivated.
- The theoretical analysis is rigorous, with two main theorems and derived scaling laws; proofs appear sound and are consistent with experiments.
- The use of the richness parameter \(\gamma\) to interpolate between lazy and feature-learning regimes in CNNs is an interesting experimental tool.
- Section 5 on scaling laws is particularly illuminating, showing nontrivial behavior (e.g., distinction between “easy” and “hard” tasks) not immediately obvious from the theorems.
- Experiments cover multiple architectures (random features, CNNs on CIFAR-10, transformers on C4) and confirm the qualitative predictions of the theory.
- The derivation of scaling laws is a useful contribution for understanding ensemble performance in the width-bottlenecked regime.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns with Theorem 1 and Theorem 2:** Theorem 1 (“more is better”) directly extends existing results for single RF-KRR (Simon et al. 2023, Bordelon et al. 2024a). Theorem 2’s conclusion that \(K=1\) is optimal under a fixed total parameter count is expected from prior work on single models and can be seen as a consequence of dropout equivalence (Srivastava et al. 2014, Section 9.1), where an optimal L2 regularization subsumes the effect of an ensemble of exponentially many small regressors. The paper does not adequately justify why the ensemble perspective adds new insights beyond what is already known for single models.
- **The fixed total parameter budget setup biases results toward \(K=1\):** Because increasing ensemble size \(K\) reduces the number of random features per member \(N = M/K\), the comparison reduces to a known trade-off: smaller \(N\) yields weaker learners, making \(K=1\) optimal. This makes the “no free lunch” result unsurprising. The interplay between \(N\), \(K\), and \(M\) in Figures 1 and 2 is not carefully disentangled; the observed trends may simply reflect decreasing feature count per member.
- **Inconsistency between text, theory, and experiments:** The paper’s text links “fixed computational budget” to “fixed total number of features,” but a computational budget does not necessarily constrain feature count. The theory applies exclusively to random-feature ridge regression, yet the experiments on CIFAR-10 with CNNs and C4 with transformers do not involve random features or regression, so they do not directly test the theory. The authors acknowledge that the theorems do not directly “explain” the neural network results except for very small richness parameters (close to the lazy regime), but this limitation is understated.
- **Empirical discrepancies with the theory:** In Figure 5a, for larger values of the richness parameter \(\gamma\) (i.e., stronger feature learning), increasing the ensemble size \(K\) actually improves test performance, contradicting the monotonic degradation predicted by the no-free-lunch theorem. The paper does not comment on this discrepancy, which suggests that behavior outside the lazy regime may not follow the theory. This requires explanation.
- **Robustness to ridge parameter:** Figure 2c shows that ensembles (\(K>1\)) are relatively robust to the ridge parameter, while \(K=1\) is highly sensitive. This potential practical advantage of ensembles (lower tuning cost) is not discussed or ablated.
- **Limited architectural scope in neural experiments:** The CNN used for CIFAR-10 (two convolutional layers + one linear layer) is very small; the transformer experiment on C4 does not report validation/test accuracy but only training loss. Reviewers request experiments on larger networks (e.g., ResNet, VGG) or on ImageNet to see if the conclusions hold, and note that prior work (Zhang & Bottou 2023) suggests ensembles of small networks can outperform large networks in representation learning settings.
- **Missing comparisons and ablations:** No experiments with conventional architectures like ResNet18 or VGG using random features. The paper does not discuss whether the same phenomena hold for larger networks or different feature-learning strengths.
- **Presentation issues:** 
  - Figure 1 legend shows dashed lines but no dashed lines appear in the plot (lines likely should be dashed).
  - Figure 3A could benefit from overlaying the theoretical line \(l_\star = 1/(1+\alpha(2r-1))\).
  - Notation confusion: “learning rate” and “task eigenstructure” use same parameters; “richness” and “data-size-scaled degrees of freedom” also share notation.
  - Inconsistent naming for binary CIFAR-10 task (e.g., line 370 calls it just “CIFAR”).
  - Missing citation in line 118–119 (“(citations)” should be replaced).
  - Minor grammatical errors (e.g., line 397 “a…tasks”, line 452 “that at the”).
  - Inconsistent abbreviation “RF KRR” vs. “RF-KRR”.
  - Several formatting issues (missing period line 101, undefined function \(g\), double parentheses in citations, “eq’s”).
- **Theoretical triviality:** Some reviewers argue that the main theoretical contribution (Theorem 2) is essentially a restatement of known properties of ridge regression under optimal regularization, and that the ensemble framing does not yield new nontrivial guarantees.
