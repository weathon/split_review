## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper presents NAText, a non-autoregressive transformer model for faster scene text recognition that aims to close the accuracy gap with autoregressive models. It introduces progressive sampled learning to capture character dependencies and a redesigned positional encoding using character center coordinates for better visual perception. The method achieves competitive accuracy with autoregressive models while being significantly faster.

### Strengths

*   **Speed-accuracy tradeoff**: The model strikes a good balance, achieving competitive performance with autoregressive models while being nearly three times faster (Reviewers 1, 6). This addresses an important real-time application need often ignored by prior work (Reviewer 3).
*   **Innovative progressive learning**: The progressive sampled learning strategy is effective in mitigating the performance drop typical of non-autoregressive models, especially for irregular text and longer sequences (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 6, 7). It helps the model focus on hard-to-predict characters (Reviewers 2, 3). One reviewer (6) calls it "innovative."
*   **Positional encoding design**: The redesigned decoder structure using character center positional encoding has clear physical meaning and enhances 2D feature aggregation (Reviewers 1, 3, 7). One reviewer (7) notes it "plays a positive role in improving accuracy."
*   **Thorough experiments and analysis**: The experimental section is thorough, with comparisons across popular benchmarks and detailed ablation studies (Reviewer 6). Results show the method outperforms previous non-autoregressive methods like PARSeqN and approaches autoregressive levels (Reviewer 4).
*   **Clear motivation and logic**: The motivation is good and the paper flows logically from problem to implementation (Reviewers 1, 2). Two-stage coarse-to-fine design is compelling (Reviewer 1). The analysis of why non-autoregressive decoding underperforms is insightful (Reviewers 3, 5).

### Weaknesses

*   **Outdated comparisons**: Table 1 compares only methods before 2022 (or at best 2022). Several recent SOTAs are missing, notably: OTE (CVPR 2024), Multi-modal In-Context Learning (CVPR 2024), Bridging End-to-End and Two-Step Text Spotting (CVPR 2024), SPTS v2 (TPAMI 2023), PIMNet, PARSeq, and Union14M (ICCV 2023) (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5, 7). This makes the experimental comparison less convincing.
*   **Reliance on character-level annotations**: The method requires character positions (center coordinates) for ground truth, which are often unavailable in real-world data, especially for irregular text, and are costly to annotate (Reviewers 1, 3, 5). Reviewers ask: *How are character center coordinates (x,y) obtained? Are character-level annotations used for training?* (Reviewers 1, 3). The paper does not clarify how to avoid this burden.
*   **Presentation and clarity issues**: Section 3 does not clearly describe the proposed method; Figures 1 and 2 lack explanations for notations, leaving readers to guess (Reviewer 4). Many mathematical symbols are undefined (e.g., domain for x,y in Eq.1, exact definition of PE(x,y)), and some equations lack rigor (e.g., Eq.5 notation log p(y_t | y_{<t}, X; θ) should be more precise) (Reviewer 4). One reviewer (4) states the paper is "barely readable" and gave a presentation score of 1, contrasting with other reviewers who found it clear (Reviewer 2, 3). The attention formula in Eq.3 has an error: it should be softmax(qk^T/√d_k)v, and the purpose of qk^T = c_q^TX + p_q^TX_p is unclear (Reviewer 5). The encoder design could be shown with a clearer diagram (Reviewer 7).
*   **Incremental novelty**: The progressive sampling strategy is essentially a masking technique using self-attention between predicted and unpredicted characters, which is not novel in scene text recognition (Reviewer 5). Adding character-level position information to queries is a common operation; the paper does not adequately distinguish its decoder from existing decoders in scene text spotting (Reviewer 3). The method resembles curriculum learning or hard example learning from other tasks; the advantages over such existing strategies are not illustrated (Reviewer 3). Reviewers ask: *How is this progressive learning different from existing hard example learning strategies, and can those be directly applied to scene text recognition?* (Reviewer 3). *How are hard cases defined?* (Reviewer 3).
*   **Training concerns with two-stage design**: In training, the encoder's initial predictions become very close to the target after many epochs; the decoder may only imitate the encoder and fail to learn (Reviewer 6). The paper lacks experimental results showing the difference between initial coarse predictions and final refined predictions, which would prove the two-stage method's effectiveness (Reviewer 6). Without that, the two-stage method may not fundamentally differ from an end-to-end approach. Reviewers also ask: *If the encoder's predicted sequence length differs from ground truth, how are they aligned for cross-entropy loss calculation?* (Reviewer 6).
*   **Missing model details and efficiency metrics**: Model parameter count and total parameter count are not reported (Reviewers 1, 3, 5). The reported inference time may only cover the first stage; it is unclear whether it includes both coarse and refined decoding stages (Reviewer 1). Specific efficiency metrics such as memory usage or latency on resource-limited devices (e.g., mobile GPUs) are missing (Reviewer 2).
*   **Lack of qualitative analysis and real-world evaluation**: No qualitative examples or visualizations of successful or challenging cases are provided (Reviewer 2). The paper would benefit from showing where NAText performs well and struggles, especially with irregular or noisy text (Reviewers 2, 7). Performance in complex real-world scenes (street scenes, billboards) or with heavily distorted characters is not evaluated (Reviewer 7). Generalization to other languages is not mentioned (Reviewer 7).
*   **Ambiguity in progressive sampling tradeoffs**: The ablation study does not explore how varying the sampling strategy impacts training convergence time, model complexity, or potential overfitting on challenging characters (Reviewer 2). The masking strategy replaces masked characters with ground truth, which may merely reduce learning difficulty in early stages; the paper does not explain in detail why this helps the model learn character relationships (Reviewer 6). Reviewers ask: *What trade-offs (increased training time, overfitting) were observed?* (Reviewer 2). *Does the model's performance suffer on longer sequences?* (Reviewer 2).
*   **Datasets are relatively simple**: The chosen benchmarks already have high accuracy, making it harder to validate improvements. More challenging datasets like Total-Text or Union14M-Benchmark should be considered (Reviewers 1, 5).
