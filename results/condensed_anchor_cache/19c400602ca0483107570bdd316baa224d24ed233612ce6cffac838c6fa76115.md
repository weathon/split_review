## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a pipeline that uses large language models (LLMs) to generate text descriptions of novel domains, then feeds those prompts to a text-to-image diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 2) to synthesize training images for domain generalization. Experiments on DomainBed benchmarks (VLCS, PACS, etc.) show accuracy gains over baselines. The method is also tested in a “data-free” setting where no real images are used.

### Strengths
- The idea of leveraging LLMs to generate class-specific novel domains and using them to design prompts for text-to-image models is novel, interesting, and useful (Reviewers 1,2,3,5).
- The theoretical framework modeling data distributions across domains and the provided theoretical bounds (including classical complexity results) could be of value to the community (Reviewer 1,2).
- The pipeline is reasonable and achieves empirical improvements over existing domain augmentation methods on VLCS and PACS (Reviewers 2,3,5).
- Ablation studies of various components (choice of LLM, knowledge extraction approach, variance analysis on PACS) are good (Reviewers 2,4).
- The paper positions its work well relative to prior literature, e.g., noting that interpolation often fails to effectively increase the number of domains seen (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unfair comparison due to larger data volume.** The proposed method trains on significantly more synthetic data than baselines, yet the experiments do not control for total sample size. Multiple reviewers ask: How many total images (per class, per domain) are generated? How much more data does the method see than ERM and other baselines? Would results change if dataset sizes were fixed? (Reviewers 1,2,3). This makes it unclear whether gains come from additional domains or simply from seeing more data (Reviewers 2,3).

2. **Missing technical details and experimental clarity.** Reviewers note the absence of crucial specifications:
   - Which specific domains are generated per class, how many such domains, how the “novelty” of generated domains is ensured (Reviewer 1).
   - The distinction between Table 2 (add synthetic to a single real domain) and Table 3 (train on synthetic only) is unclear (Reviewer 1).
   - The scaling-benefits experiment is ambiguous regarding which dataset is used (Reviewer 3).
   - The number of experiment runs and the variance across runs are not reported (Reviewer 3).

3. **Overclaiming “data-free” and “truly novel” extrapolation.** The claim of being truly data-free is contestable because the generative models (LLM and diffusion model) are trained on massive internet data that likely overlap with the evaluation datasets. Several reviewers argue this makes the approach still “data-dependent” (Reviewers 2,5). Furthermore, whether text-to-image models can generate data that actually diverges or extrapolates beyond their training distribution is an open question; the paper provides no evidence for generating “truly novel” domains (Reviewer 1). One reviewer adds that data-free generalization can amplify demographic and cultural biases, citing Bianchi et al. 2023 (Reviewer 2).

4. **Weak theoretical foundation for key assumption.** The central assumption that an LLM can accurately approximate the true domain meta-distribution (μ) is unfounded and not validated. Reviewers call for measuring the approximation error ε, e.g., by computing the distance between two independently generated μ′ and μ′′ (Reviewer 2). The theoretical argument is considered weak, and the claim that increasing m and n can recover the error lacks a feasibility analysis (Reviewers 2,3). The definition of domain distance (Definition 1) is not connected to standard optimal transport measures (Reviewer 2).

5. **Missing important baselines and datasets.** Several relevant works are not compared or cited:
   - ERM++ which reports higher average accuracy (81.4% on the same setup) (Reviewer 4).
   - Descriptor and Word Soups (CVPR 2024), SuS-X (ICCV 2023), LADS (ICLR 2023) (Reviewer 4).
   - The paper does not test on more challenging OOD benchmarks such as ImageNet variants (Adversarial, Sketch, Rendition, V2) or WILDS distribution shifts (Reviewer 4). Results are limited to art/cartoon-like domains where Stable Diffusion is strong (Reviewer 5).

6. **Limited novelty and marginal gains.** While some reviewers find the pipeline novel, others note that generating images from LLM prompts for augmentation is straightforward and similar to existing work (e.g., “Diversify Your Vision Datasets with Automatic Diffusion-Based Augmentation”) (Reviewer 5). The average gains over baselines are modest and may not justify the complexity of using GPT‑4 plus a large diffusion model (Reviewer 4).

7. **Dependence on generative model quality and prompt design.** The method’s success hinges entirely on the generative models performing well. The prompt design relies only on heuristic knowledge of the dataset (no training images are used), so if the generated images look very different from real images, the method can fail (Reviewer 5). Performance is expected to drop for niche domains (e.g., satellite imagery, medical X‑rays) or for classes underrepresented in the training data of the image generator (Reviewers 3,4,5). The paper does not analyze these failure cases.

8. **Clarity on the role of LLM knowledge.** The explanation of how “LLM-generated prompt further extracts knowledge” and its importance for generalization is vague. The authors should elaborate on what specific knowledge the LLM provides beyond standard class templates (Reviewer 2). Similarly, a comparison between LLM-generated prompts and simple class templates/prompts would help disentangle the effect of prompt diversity from domain extrapolation (Reviewer 2).
