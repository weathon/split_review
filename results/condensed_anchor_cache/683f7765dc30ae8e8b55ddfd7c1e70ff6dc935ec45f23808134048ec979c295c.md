## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents the first systematic evaluation of uncertainty estimation abilities of LLMs in instruction-following tasks. The study identifies key challenges with existing benchmarks (e.g., IFEval) where multiple factors (instruction-following uncertainty, task difficulty, response length) are entangled, complicating isolation and comparison. To address this, the authors introduce two new benchmark versions: Controlled (disentangling factors) and Realistic (natural responses). Experiments compare multiple uncertainty estimation methods (self-evaluation, consistency, token probability, entropy, probe-based on hidden states) across several LLMs (up to 13B parameters). Findings reveal that existing uncertainty methods struggle, especially when models make subtle errors in instruction following. The probe-based method using internal states (particularly from middle layers) provides the best estimation but remains inadequate in more complex scenarios. The paper also highlights a discrepancy between accuracy and uncertainty estimation and the entanglement between instruction-following and task completion.

### Strengths
- Systematic evaluation that identifies multiple entangled factors affecting uncertainty estimation in naturally generated responses, enabling isolation of their influence and more accurate assessment of estimation methods (R1).
- Thorough comparisons between different uncertainty estimation methods, across various LLMs (up to 13B), and on distinct datasets (IFEval and the new benchmarks) (R1).
- Introduction of new benchmark datasets (Controlled and Realistic versions) that isolate factors influencing uncertainty estimation in instruction-following, filling a research gap (R2).
- Rigorous methodologies with comprehensive experimental setups, clear writing, and well-designed figures and tables (R2).
- Valuable insights: internal model states (especially middle hidden layers) can be leveraged for improved uncertainty estimation; the probe-based method provides the best estimation (R2, R4).
- Timely work raising awareness of uncertainty challenges in instruction-following; hints at advantages of open models due to white-box properties and suggests internal states should be accessible via APIs (R4).
- Provides crucial insights into LLMs' limitations and potential for uncertainty estimation in instruction-following tasks (R3).

### Weaknesses
- A minority reviewer (score 1) was notably more critical, raising the following points: (i) The initial evaluation relies heavily on a single IFEval dataset; additional datasets are needed to validate across different contexts and domains. (ii) The paper does not use LLMs larger than 13B, so conclusions may not be sufficient for larger models. (iii) While the paper raises novel concepts and findings, it does not provide direct strategies or methodologies to enhance instruction-following accuracy (R3).
- Limited scope of instruction types and domains; the benchmark may not comprehensively reflect real-world tasks, and the dataset size is relatively small (R1). Including additional datasets would strengthen validation (R2).
- The Probe method is highlighted as the best-performing in Table 4 but is not introduced or discussed in Section 3, causing confusion. A clearer explanation earlier or keeping the scope of methods consistent is needed (R1).
- Token length distributions in Figures 2, 3, and 10 are not normalized by the total number of responses in each class (correct vs. incorrect). Normalization would be more appropriate, though it may not affect the conclusion that token lengths introduce biases (R1).
- Details of the benchmark data generation process are insufficient: what prompts were used for generating correct responses (Controlled version) and natural responses (Realistic version)? What are the domains for arguments (Type, Kwargs) in prompts? Are these parameters inherited from IFEval? (R1, as a question overlapping with missing details).
- The paper falls short in exploring more sophisticated methods to utilize internal model states for uncertainty estimation in nuanced tasks, beyond linear probing (R2). Similarly, it does not consider alternative inference methods (e.g., temperature, beam search) beyond greedy decoding, which could be used with closed-source models (R4). The impact of regularization (e.g., dropout rate) during pre- or post-training is also not considered, and training details are not sufficiently exposed even for open LLMs (R4).
- The use of GPT-4 for task quality assessment introduces a potential risk of pre-training overlap affecting evaluation, though acknowledged (R2).
- How training procedures like RLHF affect models' uncertainty estimation, especially in Controlled-Hard tasks, is not explored (R2, as a question).
- Expanding evaluation to include LLM agents could provide more comprehensive insights (R2, as a question).
