## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper develops a comprehensive theoretical framework analyzing dropout in Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs). It shows that dropout creates dimension-specific stochastic subgraphs, leading to degree-dependent adaptive regularization and structural regularization beyond that in standard neural networks. The analysis addresses dropout’s role in mitigating oversmoothing and its synergistic interaction with batch normalization, deriving novel graph-specific generalization bounds. The theoretical claims are validated through experiments on 16 datasets (node-level and graph-level tasks), with GCN+dropout+BN outperforming state-of-the-art on several benchmarks.

### Strengths
- **Originality and relevance:** The paper addresses a critical gap in understanding how dropout interacts uniquely with graph structure, which is a meaningful contribution to the GCN community (R1).
- **Rigorous theoretical analysis:** A well-developed mathematical framework introduces key formal concepts such as dimension-specific stochastic subgraphs, feature-topology coupling matrices, and degree-dependent regularization (R1,R3,R4).
- **Interesting insight:** The use of active path subgraphs to understand graph feature dropout is a novel perspective (R2).
- **Multi-faceted theory:** The analysis comprehensively covers structural regularization, oversmoothing mitigation, and the joint effect with batch normalization, providing a coherent, easy-to-follow logic (R3,R4).
- **Extensive empirical validation:** The theory is supported by experiments across 16 datasets, with strong results on standard benchmarks (Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed) and both node-level and graph-level tasks (R1,R2,R3).
- **Bridges theory and practice:** The work offers actionable insights for GCN design and training while maintaining rigorous mathematical foundations (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of practical guidance on dropout rates:** Multiple reviewers (R1,R2,R3) note that the theory does not provide clear guidelines for selecting optimal dropout rates based on graph properties (e.g., degree distribution, density). It remains unclear whether the generalization bounds can be used to predict the best dropout rate for a given graph structure (R1,R2).
- **Insufficient comparison with graph-specific regularization techniques:** The paper primarily studies standard dropout but does not sufficiently compare or relate its theory to widely used graph-specific methods like DropNode, DropEdge, DropMessage, etc. (R1,R2). R1 asks whether the theory can analyze those methods and whether structural dropout is more effective than feature dropout.
- **Experiments do not directly validate the graph-specific theoretical claims (R4, most critical):** The experiments only show overall performance gains from dropout+BN. They do not isolate the unique graph-related effects (dimension-specific subgraphs, degree-dependence, oversmoothing) from the general regularization effects of dropout. R4 specifically notes:
    - Calling dropout+BN “our approach” (lines 409-410, 466) is misleading; the contribution is the theoretical analysis, not the technique itself.
    - Section 3.4’s claim about dropout mitigating oversmoothing lacks experimental evidence (e.g., analyzing node representations or graph Laplacian spectrum with varying layer depth).
    - There is a contradiction in the conclusions about Dirichlet Energy between Table 1 (line 472) and Table 2 (line 483); the reason for this difference is unexplained.
- **Limited experimental details and baselines:** The 16 datasets lack detailed descriptions, and the comparative analysis with state-of-the-art methods is not fully comprehensive (R3). Some experimental results in figures appear truncated in the provided content (R3).
- **Theoretical assumptions and scope:** The framework assumes undirected graphs and does not address directed graphs (R3). The interaction of dropout with different activation functions and the impact of graph density on dropout effectiveness are not explored (R3).
- **Scalability and computational overhead:** The paper does not discuss scalability to very large graphs or compare computational overhead with traditional dropout implementations (R3).
- **Minor issue:** Repeated use of variable ‘d’ for both degree (line 133) and feature dimensionality (line 141) causes confusion (R4).
- **Unresolved question from R4:** Section 3.2 introduces dimension-specific subgraphs; the impact of this on the aggregated node representation (used in final predictions) is not explored.

*Note: All reviewers gave a score of 6, but Reviewer 4 expressed significantly stronger criticism regarding the experimental validation (soundness=1 vs others 3) and the misleading claim of “our approach”. The other reviewers were more satisfied with the empirical work, though they shared concerns about practical guidance and comparisons.*
