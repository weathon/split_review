## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers evaluate this paper on LLM capabilities for sequential decision-making in RL. Two reviewers rate 5, two rate 6. Reviewers agree the paper performs extensive experiments across diverse domains and finds that indirect reward modeling (especially AI-feedback-based) outperforms direct policy generation. However, they raise concerns about limited novelty (no new method, widely studied topic), unfair comparisons (computation cost and interactions not controlled), insufficient baselines, and lack of clarity on credit assignment mechanisms. The more positive reviewers (3,4) highlight interesting observations (e.g., LLM scalar rewards, fine-tuning trade-offs, seed averaging) while the more critical ones (1,2) stress insufficient related work and limited significance. All reviewers note the paper is well-written.

### Strengths
- Extensive experiments across diverse interactive domains (reviewers all).
- Demonstrates that LLMs generating preference data to train a reward model for RL yields the highest performance gain compared to direct policy modeling (reviewer 1).
- Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 clearly illustrate the effect of rewards learned through LLM feedback (reviewer 1).
- Prompting engineering steers LLMs for active exploration on NetHack, approximating the count-based exploration bonus (reviewer 1).
- Finds that without task-specific fine-tuning, LLMs show limited decision-making when directly generating actions, but AI-feedback-based rewards produce dense functions that correlate positively with high-quality value functions, reducing credit assignment difficulty (reviewer 3).
- Results are averaged over 10 seeds, which is robust given RL instability (reviewer 3).
- Interesting exploration of fine-tuning trade-offs between direct and indirect approaches (reviewer 4).
- Well-written and easy to follow (reviewers 2,4).
- The studied topic is of broad interest to the community (reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Related works incomplete**: The paper should discuss recent works on LLM decision-making and self-rewarding (reviewers 1,2). Specific references [1]-[7] (self-rewarding) and [8] (LLM for reward design) are missing (reviewer 1).
- **Unfair comparison between direct and indirect policy modeling**: Only GPT-4 is queried for direct policy, while RL is trained with multiple steps for indirect – computation cost and number of environment interactions are not controlled (reviewer 1). Moreover, since direct modeling is fine-tuning-free, its lesser performance is not surprising (reviewer 2).
- **Limited novelty**: No new method is proposed; the paper is empirical but technical novelty is weak (reviewers 2,3). Policy and reward modeling abilities have been widely studied in previous works [1,2] (reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient experimental settings**: The paper only studies approaches that directly interface with the action space and assumes LLMs are used without gradient update (line 207-215). For indirect policy, only reward as code is considered; other options (e.g., LLMs for high-level plans, intrinsic reward writing) are missing (reviewer 3).
- **Limited number of base LLMs**: Only GPT-4o, Llama 3, and PaliGemma are used, making significance and applicability hard to assess (reviewer 3).
- **Few baselines**: In Section 4.2 (Figure 5), only count-based exploration is compared; stronger baselines should be included (reviewer 1, reviewer 3).
- **Credit assignment analysis not convincing**: Claim that AI feedback helps better credit assignment is supported only by training curves; it is unclear when and why credit is better assigned (reviewer 2). In complex tasks, the method cannot distinguish between critical and auxiliary actions when both contribute to the final outcome (reviewer 4).
- **Preference uncertainty not addressed**: The method relies on consistent preference feedback but does not handle cases where LLM assigns similar preference probabilities or provides inconsistent rankings. This could lead to unstable reward signals that harm policy learning (reviewer 4).
- **Environments with obvious progress**: The method is demonstrated on tasks with clear feedback (Wordle, discrete NetHack states); sensitivity to small state changes and ability to detect incremental progress in subtle, continuous real-world tasks are not explored (reviewer 4).
- **Missing analysis on key components**: The importance of chain-of-thought, in-context learning, and self-refinement for LLM sequential decision-making is not investigated (reviewer 3).
- **Missing learning curves**: Number of interactions is not controlled in Figs 1,2,6; learning curves typical in RL papers are absent (reviewer 1).
- **Fine-tuning details unclear**: Amount of expert data used to fine-tune PaliGemma in Section 5 is not specified; impact of dataset quality is not discussed (reviewer 3).
- **Timeliness concern**: No discussion on how to guarantee timely and effective decisions when using LLMs directly or indirectly in real-time scenarios (reviewer 3).
- **Minor typo**: Line 762 “except fro” (reviewer 1).
