## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes DynamicFlow, a full-atom flow matching model (with a stochastic variant) for structure-based drug design that accounts for protein flexibility. The model learns to transform apo protein pockets and noisy ligands into holo pockets and corresponding 3D ligand molecules, jointly generating the bound conformation and the ligand. A new dataset is introduced, derived from MISATO and AlphaFold, containing 5,692 complexes with 46,235 MD-simulated holo-ligand conformations and corresponding apo structures. Reviewers consider the task important and potentially paradigm-shifting (R1), an important milestone in the field (R4), and high in originality (R5). The paper is generally clearly written with well-designed figures (R3). However, significant concerns remain regarding dataset choice and curation, evaluation fairness and baseline comparisons, reproducibility, missing ablations, and clarity of technical details.

### Strengths

1. **Critical problem and potential impact.** The protein flexibility problem in structure-based drug design is acknowledged as critical and lacking efficient in silico methods. If effective, the proposed method could be paradigm shifting (R1). The concept of incorporating dynamic adaptability into drug design is uncommon and demonstrates high originality (R5). The work is considered an important milestone (R4).

2. **Well-motivated dataset.** The curated dataset mapping apo pockets to multiple MD-generated holo pockets is novel and well-suited for the task (R2, R3). It includes 5,692 complexes with 46,235 holo-ligand conformations and corresponding apo structures, sourced from MISATO and AlphaFold (R2).

3. **New problem setting and model architecture.** The paper extends 3D SBDD to the apo pocket setting, enabling ligand generation without a holo input structure (R4). DynamicFlow combines discrete and continuous flow matching to simultaneously generate a ligand and adjust the protein pocket (R2). Flow matching components are generally well explained (R1). The model demonstrates that predicted holo structures can be used as input for existing 3D molecular generation models (R4).

4. **Experimental design.** The inclusion of docking and non-covalent interaction analysis is a desirable analysis design for future work (R4). Results indicate improved Vina binding affinity with generated ligands (R5).

5. **Structural modeling.** Structural features of proteins and ligands are carefully considered and appropriately modeled using atom-level SE(3)-equivariant layers and residue-level Transformers (R1).

6. **Minority high-score view (R4; score 8).** This reviewer, while noting limitations, explicitly states that "setting this bias aside, I consider this paper an important milestone in this field," and praises the extension to apo pockets and the experimental analysis.

### Weaknesses

1. **Dataset concerns.** 
   - The dataset is derived from MD simulations, which may cause the model to learn simulated physics rather than real-world structural distributions (R4, R2). R4 calls this a minor issue but suggests using true experimental data (e.g., PDBbind) for future work.
   - The dataset differs from common SBDD benchmarks (BindingMOAD, CrossDock2020) and is smaller; it is unclear why those were not used initially (R3).
   - The curation process is not sufficiently detailed: it is unclear how the reduction from 19,437 proteins to 16,972 complexes occurred, how ligands were selected, what “100-frame” means (every 100th frame or 100 selected frames per trajectory), and whether RMSD-based filtering was applied (R2).
   - The ratio of apo to holo states and the rationale are unclear; analysis of the different states is insufficient (R5).

2. **Evaluation fairness and baseline comparisons are seriously questioned.** 
   - Baselines (designed for holo-state pockets) are applied to apo-state pockets, while DynamicFlow generates its own pocket and evaluates ligands on that generated pocket, making the comparison unfair and potentially misleading (R2).
   - Table 2 does not include DynamicFlow’s own results; adding them is requested for direct comparison (R1, R2).
   - Diffusion baseline methods appear to outperform the proposed model when given generated pockets, raising a concern about architecture effectiveness (R2).
   - No baseline for joint pocket-and-ligand generation is provided; the motivation for their absence is missing (R2 cites [1–4]).
   - A key baseline, FlexSBDD (NeurIPS 2024), also using flow matching for protein flexibility, is not compared; benchmarking on Vina scores and illustrating novelty/improvement over prior work is needed (R3).
   - R5 notes the absence of broader graph-based generative model comparisons.
   - R2 suggests using MD-trajectory-based evaluation (e.g., MMPBSA) for a fairer assessment.
   - The validity of generated pockets is not assessed (e.g., using AF pLDDT) (R2).

3. **Reproducibility and clarity gaps.** 
   - No code or curated dataset is provided, hindering reproducibility (R1, R5). R5 recommends providing a Jupyter Notebook.
   - The overall loss aggregation is unclear; while individual losses for structural features are given, how they are combined is not stated (R1).
   - Hyperparameters and architectural details are missing (R1).
   - The model workflow is hard to follow: Figure 1 does not clearly show where protein/ligand embeddings are computed and how they are used in the complex and ligand graphs. An aggregated end-to-end pipeline figure is suggested (R1).
   - The meaning of key terms is insufficiently clarified, e.g., “stochastic full-atom flow” lacks explanation of exact meaning and implementation (R5).
   - The logical relationships and technical details of some steps (e.g., dynamic adaptation) are not distinct; intuitive explanations are lacking (R5).
   - R5 asks to clearly specify which algorithms are original/unique to this work and which are derived from existing works.

4. **Missing ablation studies.** 
   - No ablation study assesses the impact of the claimed architectural contributions (atom-level SE(3)-equivariant layers, residue-level Transformer) on sample quality (e.g., Vina score) or computational efficiency (FLOPs) (R3).
   - R4 requests an analysis using randomly selected generated conformations (rather than Vina-selected) to show that the model has learned the holo distribution.
   - R1’s questions about parameterization also point to missing experimental justification (e.g., how hidden states predict positions and types, how torsion angles are predicted).

5. **Missing citations.** 
   - FREED and FREED++ are not cited (R2).
   - FlexSBDD is not cited (R3).

6. **Applicability in real-world settings.** 
   - The pocket definition uses atom coordinates of known active binders from holo structures, which is not directly applicable to apo structures from AlphaFold. For high usability, pockets should be definable by center and radius in the apo state (R4).
   - Generated samples’ quality and real-world practicality remain unproven (R4).

7. **Minor issues and questions.** 
   - Table 1: For distribution-learning models, QED, SA, etc. should be similar to reference ligands; directional arrows may be inappropriate (R4).
   - What is the generation time scale? (R4)
   - Line 214: string repetition (R2).
   - Line 848: mistake ("are can") (R2).
   - How were the 50 test pockets selected? Uniform sampling from the dataset? (R3)
