## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces a new variant of Combinatorial Multi-Armed Bandits called Set-Size Dependent Combinatorial Multi-Armed Bandits (SSD-CMAB), where each base arm’s reward distribution depends on the size of the chosen super arm. A key assumption is **order preservation**: the order of reward means of base arms is independent of set size. The authors propose the **SortUCB** algorithm (with three phases: Elimination, Sorting, UCB), which leverages order preservation to shrink the exploration set. They provide a regret upper bound of \(O\!\left(\max\left\{\frac{M\delta_L}{\Delta_{L}},\frac{L^2}{\Delta_S}\right\}\log T\right)\) and a lower bound informally stated as \(\Omega\!\left(\max\left\{\min_{k\in[L]}\left\{\frac{(M-L)\delta_{k}}{\Delta_{k}^2}\right\},\frac{L^2}{\Delta_S}\right\}\log T\right)\), showing SortUCB is **partially tight**. The paper also extends to Set-Dependent CMAB (SD-CMAB) with a more general reward structure and proposes **SortUCB-SD**, using an \((n_1,n_2)\)-efficiency oracle. Synthetic experiments compare SortUCB against CMAB (2015) and MPMAB (1985), showing good performance.

### Strengths

- **Novel problem setting**: SSD-CMAB with order preservation is a new and interesting variant of CMAB (R1, R3). The example of online advertising where click rates decrease with more ads is a concrete motivation (R3).
- **Innovative algorithmic solutions**: SortUCB and its generalization SortUCB-SD are well-designed algorithms that provide attractive regret guarantees (R2).
- **Theoretical performance guarantees**: The paper proves both a regret upper bound for SortUCB and a fundamental lower bound for the SSD-CMAB problem (R2, R4). The derivation of the lower bound is a contribution (R4).
- **Empirical evaluation**: Synthetic experiments with multiple baseline methods demonstrate the performance of SortUCB (R2, R4).
- **Minority positive view on clarity**: Reviewer 2 found the writing clear, precise, and well-structured, with good intuitive explanations of the algorithms. (Other reviewers disagreed – see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses

- **Writing clarity and presentation**:  
  - The writing is not clear; a learning protocol for the problem is missing (R1).  
  - The notation is heavy and space-inefficient – more than half a page for notations could be simplified (R1).  
  - Several reviewers (R1, R4) found the presentation lacking, while R2 considered it clear (minority opinion).  
  - An error in the abstract: the regret upper bound should include \(\delta_L^2\), not \(\delta_L\) (R4).  

- **Regret definition and comparison**:  
  - The regret definition (Eq. 1) seems flawed: because reward distributions depend on set size, the optimal arm should vary across rounds, but the definition fixes the optimal super arm (R1).  
  - Comparing regret bounds across different settings (SSD-CMAB vs. CMAB) is unfair (R1).  

- **Lower bound concerns**:  
  - The term “partially tight” is vague; the paper does not specify under which conditions the lower and upper bounds match or how large the gap is (R1, R2).  
  - The derived lower bound is asymptotic, and the paper never provides a full characterization of a SSD-CMAB problem instance (R1).  

- **Motivation and related work**:  
  - Insufficient justification of the problem setting’s real-world applicability; more concrete examples beyond online advertising are needed (R2, R4).  
  - The work is very closely related to submodular bandits, where the reward of an arm depends on the set (including size). This literature must be discussed in the main paper with key differences and motivations (R4).  
  - General CMAB works with semi-bandit (and even bandit) feedback have been proposed; the paper should contrast its theoretical/empirical results with these (R4).  
  - The motivation is weakened because SSD-CMAB could be solved by standard CMAB that directly estimates super-arm rewards while considering \(LM\) arms instead of \(M\) arms (R4).  
  - It is unclear whether SSD-CMAB can be reduced to standard CMAB by imposing constraints on super arms, which would diminish novelty (R1).  

- **Approach limitations**:  
  - Assumes a **linear reward function** and **semi-bandit feedback**, unlike works that tackle non-linear rewards and/or bandit feedback (R4). This limitation should be stated earlier (e.g., in the introduction) (R4).  
  - The computational complexity of Algorithm 1 is high: it maintains counters \(N_{S,t}\) for all super arms, which are exponentially many in the number of base arms, and finding the super arm maximizing (4) is a nonlinear combinatorial optimization problem (R3).  
  - The paper does not provide time or space complexity analysis for any of its algorithms, despite three different phases with different costs (R4).  
  - For the SSD-CMAB, the set of super arms is only defined by a cardinality constraint; this may not be considered a “combinatorial” structure (R3).  

- **Extension to SD-CMAB (SortUCB-SD)**:  
  - The transition from SSD-CMAB to SD-CMAB feels abrupt; SortUCB-SD lacks the same level of thorough theoretical analysis and experimental validation (R2).  
  - The notion of “class” in SD-CMAB is ambiguous; e.g., in the paths application, what are the \(K\) classes? (R3).  
  - The \((n_1,n_2)\)-efficiency oracle is used for comparison, but its novelty and relation to prior work are not discussed (R2).  

- **Experimental evaluation limitations**:  
  - Only two baselines are compared: CMAB (2015) and MPMAB (1985). Several other combinatorial bandit algorithms (e.g., submodular bandit algorithms, CTS from Wang et al. ICML 2018) should be included (R3, R4).  
  - The number of base arms \(M\) is small; larger \(M\) and more diverse synthetic settings are needed for realistic comparison (R4).  
  - No real-world dataset experiments are provided, making it hard to justify practicality (R2, R4).  
  - No code or URL is given for reproducibility (R2).  
  - It is unclear why the UCB phase considers only \(L^2\) subsets of the top \(L\) arms rather than \(2^L\); if all subsets are possible, the regret would be exponential in \(L\) (R4).  

- **Algorithmic details**:  
  - Algorithm 1, line 11: “delete any base arm” – it is unclear whether it is deleted from set \(R\) or \(B\) (presumably \(R\)) (R4).  
  - The algorithm design may have memory issues due to maintaining counters for all super arms (R3).  

- **Missing comparisons for empirical evaluation**: The CTS algorithm (Wang et al., ICML 2018) and submodular bandit algorithms are not compared, even though they are relevant for set-dependent reward structures (R3, R4).
