## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper reexamines the trade-off between generating synthetic data with stronger but more expensive (SE) models vs. weaker but cheaper (WC) models under a fixed compute budget. It introduces a compute-matched sampling framework and evaluates coverage, diversity, and false positive rate. Experiments on mathematical reasoning tasks with Gemma2 9B/27B and Gemini Flash/Pro show that finetuning on WC-generated data consistently outperforms SE-generated data across knowledge distillation, self-improvement, and a novel weak-to-strong improvement setup. The authors argue that despite higher false positive rates, the additional diversity and coverage from WC models yield better student models. Two reviewers (scores 8) are very positive, praising the originality, experimental rigor, and practical impact. Two other reviewers (scores 6) are more cautious, questioning generalizability beyond Gemma series, the confounding of compute and sample count, and the limited scope of tasks and datasets.

### Strengths
- **Novel framing and originality**: Introduces a compute-matched sampling framework with clear mathematical foundations, proposes a weak-to-strong improvement paradigm that challenges the prevailing assumption that stronger models are always better for data generation (Reviewer 1).
- **Comprehensive and rigorous experiments**: Multiple model pairs (open Gemma2 9B/27B, closed Gemini Flash/Pro), varied compute budgets, three training paradigms, evaluation of coverage, diversity, and false positive rates with both human and automated checks, transfer learning validation on Functional MATH (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3). The paper is well-structured and clearly written (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Impressive and surprising findings**: Consistently shows that WC-generated data yields better student models than SE-generated data at the same compute cost, challenging conventional wisdom (Reviewer 2). The observation that training on model-generated solution paths can outperform human-provided ones (Figures 4, 5) is interesting (Reviewer 4).
- **Insight on error mitigation**: Figure 7 shows that despite the WC model’s data having more errors, the trained student model does not exhibit more reasoning errors, implying that additional data can compensate for lower quality (Reviewer 4).
- **Practical cost savings**: Demonstrates up to 0.15x cost for comparable or better performance, providing actionable insights for practitioners (Reviewer 1).
- **Significant research question**: The paper addresses a critical issue in synthetic data generation (Reviewers 2, 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Confounding of compute and sample count**: In 21 of 22 figures, the comparison gives the WC model three times more samples for the same compute, making it hard to separate the effect of more data from the effect of using a weaker model. The only figure without this advantage (Figure 20, appendix) shows the large model produces better data. A cleaner design would test 1, 3, 9 samples from the large model vs. 3, 9, 27 from the small model to create overlapping settings. (Reviewer 4; echoed by Reviewer 3’s question about quality at very high budgets)
- **Limited model families**: All main experiments use only Gemma2 (9B/27B) and Gemini models. The conclusions may not generalize to other model families (e.g., Llama, Qwen). One reviewer notes from experience that under the same budget, Qwen2.5 7B could outperform Gemma2 2B. Extending to at least one other LLM family is needed to demonstrate robustness. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Limited task and dataset scope**: The paper focuses almost exclusively on mathematical reasoning (MATH, GSM8K) and one coding benchmark (MBPP). More diverse tasks (e.g., non-math reasoning, complex multi-step planning, tree-search tasks) are necessary to understand domain applicability. Coding results already show context-dependency. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Heavy reliance on ground truth for filtering**: The method requires annotated data to filter incorrect solutions, limiting practical applicability in scenarios without ground truth. An ablation without filtering (training on unfiltered WC data) is missing. (Reviewer 4; also Reviewer 1)
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: The paper does not compare to closely related approaches (e.g., techniques to enhance output diversity like temperature adjustment, or other synthetic data generation methods). Specifically, adjusting the sampling temperature of the SE model to match the WC model’s diversity would clarify whether the WC model provides a superior quality-diversity trade-off. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Inconsistent reporting of relative vs. absolute increases**: Reported percentage changes (e.g., 125% vs. 7%) are ambiguous—some likely are relative, some absolute. This undermines clarity. (Reviewer 4)
- **FPR evaluation sample size**: Human evaluation of false positive rates uses only 50 samples, which may be insufficient for reliable conclusions. (Reviewer 1)
- **Theoretical foundation lacking**: The paper lacks formal analysis of when WC sampling should outperform SE sampling, no theoretical bounds on the optimal sampling ratio, and no investigation of how model size and task complexity affect the optimal strategy. (Reviewer 1)
- **Limited exploration of failure modes**: The specific types of errors made by WC vs. SE models are not analyzed, which could provide insight into when each approach is more suitable. (Reviewer 1)
- **Scalability and convergence uncertainty**: It is unclear whether the benefits of WC sampling hold when the student model converges (i.e., when budget is not a limiting factor). The limited dataset size (8k–12.5k questions) undermines the paper’s claim about cost savings for large-scale data generation. Results on an extremely high budget or on pretraining (first few iterations) would help assess practical impact. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **Small issues**: Several typos (line 54 “filters”, line 103 “supervise finetune”, line 151 “consists”, etc.), references only list arxiv versions, and in-text citation formatting errors (Reviewers 2, 4).
