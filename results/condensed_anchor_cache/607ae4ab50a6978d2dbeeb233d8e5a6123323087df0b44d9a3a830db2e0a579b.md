## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies robust algorithmic recourse under model shifts via a learning-augmented framework. It assumes the designer has access to an unreliable prediction of future model parameters \(\hat{\theta}\) and proposes Algorithm 1 to solve a min‑max robust recourse objective. Theoretical guarantees are given for generalized linear models, and experiments on three real datasets compare with ROAR and RBR baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a relevant problem in the recourse literature: robustness of counterfactual explanations to model shifts.
- Algorithm 1 is concise and comes with a theoretical guarantee: it can compute robust recourse to global optimality despite the non‑convexity of the robust recourse objective.
- The paper studies the trade‑off between robustness and consistency (or regret) and provides empirical results on three real‑world datasets.
- The topic is interesting and the algorithm is clearly presented.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical guarantees.** The global optimality guarantee holds only for a generalized linear model, with a 1‑norm cost on \(x\) and an \(\infty\)-norm neighborhood for \(\theta\). It is unclear how the result extends to other settings (different cost, nonlinear model without a linear surrogate, etc.). Reviewer 3 notes that in practice most models are deep neural networks, so the approach loses its guarantees.
- **Misleading novelty claim.** The statement “the first optimal algorithm for any robust recourse problem” (Section 1) is misleading: there are other settings where robustness is required in which the method might **not** be optimal (e.g., deep models, other cost structures). Moreover, the learning‑augmented framework’s impact on the paper’s contributions is unclear: both the objective (3) and the metrics (5),(6) are very similar to prior work (ROAR, RBR). Reviewer 1 states that omitting all discussion of the learning‑augmented framework would not change the meaning/results.
- **Logical gap in motivation (Section 3).** The connection between an unreliable prediction model and the need for a learning‑augmented approach is not well‑established. It is not obvious why leveraging an unreliable model is chosen over directly improving robustness. The motivation for introducing consistency as a performance metric given the unreliability of the prediction requires further justification.
- **Uncertainty of the prediction model not discussed.** The paper does not explain how the degree of unreliability in \(\hat{\theta}\) is measured, nor how this uncertainty impacts the robustness‑consistency trade‑off. It is unclear how the method would perform under varying levels of prediction model accuracy.
- **Algorithm 1 computational complexity and limitations.** A computational complexity analysis is missing. While Algorithm 1 may achieve the global optimum, it could be exponential in the number of features. For local approximations (e.g., LIME surrogate), the algorithm is no longer optimal, and the limitations of that scenario are not discussed.
- **Experimental analysis with \(\hat{\theta}\) is insufficient.**
  - *Competitor handling.* It is unclear whether ROAR and RBR baseline recourses are computed using the predicted model \(f_{\hat{\theta}}\) or the original model \(f_{\theta_0}\). The latter would unfairly penalize the competitors.
  - *Simplified assumption on \(\alpha\).* In Figure 2 the perturbation \(\alpha\) used to generate the shifted model is the same as the \(\alpha\) parameter in Algorithm 1. In practice one would have a noisy estimator of \(\alpha\); its mismatch is not tested.
  - *Missing baselines.* Additional robust recourse baselines (e.g., Dominguez‑Olmedo et al., 2022 using \(\epsilon\)-ball without causal structure, or other forms of robustness listed in [1]) are missing.
  - *Out‑of‑sample validity.* The set \(\Theta_\alpha\) is only a proxy for future model shifts; out‑of‑sample validity experiments (as done in ROAR) are a more practical criterion and were not provided. The worst‑case validity computed over \(\Theta_\alpha\) has no real meaning.
- **Lack of exploration for non‑linear models.** The experiments do not adequately address the limitations of using a linear approximation (LIME) for non‑linear models. It is unclear how the quality of recourse is affected when the surrogate is inaccurate. Reviewer 2 requests a case study showing advantages of the method, and a more general target model (e.g., MLP).
- **Presentation clarity.** Despite being relatively easy to follow, the paper’s motivation for the learning‑augmented framework is unclear (Reviewer 2: “logical gap in Section 3”). Presentation scores varied: 2,1,3 across reviewers.
