## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proves that transformers can universally approximate continuous in-context mappings, defined as functions \(G(\mathbf{x}; \mu)\) where the context \(\mu\) is a probability distribution over tokens (allowing arbitrarily many tokens, possibly infinite). The authors show that a single transformer with fixed embedding dimension (not increasing with precision) and a fixed number of heads (proportional to dimension) can achieve any desired approximation precision uniformly over compact token domains, regardless of the number of context tokens. Separate proofs are given for unmasked (non-causal) attention and masked (causal) attention. The masked setting extends the unmasked approach via a space-time lifting to analyze causal attention as a mapping over distributions.

### Strengths
- Nice prior-work section that succinctly summarizes a very large literature.
- The “in-context mapping” formulation is elegant and appears applicable beyond transformers (e.g., to RNNs, SSMs).
- Techniques developed, such as the Laplace-like transform of Lemma 1, could become useful theoretical constructions in their own right.
- The paper provides a solid theoretical foundation for how a transformer can model an infinite number of tokens, which supports the development of long-context language models.
- It is especially surprising that a transformer can handle an infinite number of tokens with a fixed embedding dimension.
- The paper explores in depth an interesting theoretical question about the in-context mapping abilities of the transformer architecture, namely its approximation abilities in presence of a context of arbitrary (even continuously infinite) cardinality.
- The depth and rigor of the mathematical derivations appear (from the best judgment of a non-specialist) to be of very high quality.

### Weaknesses
- The result itself may be less interesting than the methods: being able to approximate any function often does not translate to being able to learn it (e.g., shallow networks, polynomial regression). It would be nice to spend space in the introduction or discussion highlighting what bearing this has on learning. [Reviewer 1; noted as a minority positive view: the reviewer found the result less interesting but still gave rating 8; others were more critical.]
- It is not clear if people actually use the unmasked variant discussed in the paper (Eq. (2)). For instance, bidirectional models like BERT and ViT apply positional embeddings to their input tokens, meaning Eq. (2) is not typically used in practice. Therefore the main contribution likely lies in the analysis of Eq. (3) (masked attention). [Reviewer 2]
- Even within the analysis of Eq. (3), the practical implications of the smoothness condition defined by the Wasserstein distance are not entirely clear. How this smoothness constraint translates into tangible benefits for real-world language models, especially given the discrete nature of token embeddings and the complexities of training dynamics, is not explored. [Reviewer 2]
- The paper does not explore the limitations of the fixed embedding dimension in practical scenarios. While the theory suggests it can handle an infinite number of tokens, the finite capacity of the embedding space might lead to information bottlenecks or interference when dealing with extremely long contexts. [Reviewer 2]
- The paper is extremely technical and would require a considerable amount of time and effort, even by a mathematically inclined reader, to be fully understood. [Reviewer 3]
- The paper, as it stands, appears oriented towards a small number of readers already familiar with the technical literature relating to measure-theoretic views of transformer approximation properties. Its appeal could be broadened by (1) motivating the approach with potential (even non-immediate) applications and (2) providing graphical illustrations of the underlying mathematical concepts (the current version provides no illustrations). [Reviewer 3; includes question 1 about concrete examples]
- As the authors acknowledge, the results “are not quantitative” – a number of important parameters of the transformer architecture (e.g., number of layers) are not bounded in terms of the approximation precision \(\epsilon\). The authors state (line 289) “Since our results are not quantitative, this is not a strong restriction,” but this statement is unclear and the lack of quantitative bounds is a serious obstacle relative to applications. [Reviewer 3; includes question 3 about line 289]
- The title “Transformers are Universal In-Context Learners” could be misleading to many readers. It may seem to address the well-known question about the “ICL” abilities of fixed LLMs with few-shot prompts, whereas the paper is about finding a transformer architecture and its parameters to approximate a given target contextual embedding. The connection to the usual notion of ICL should be clarified. [Reviewer 3]
- It is not fully clear why putting a probability measure over the input tokens is a natural move in the context of transformers. For standard inputs of \(n\) tokens, the usual representation is a set of cardinality \(n\), and it is not obvious why different weights over tokens is an appropriate representation for modeling in-context mappings. [Reviewer 3, question 2]
