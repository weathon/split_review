## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using LLMs to generate guide-plans from an abstraction of the MDP, then using those plans as heuristics for potential-based reward shaping to improve sample efficiency of RL agents. Experiments are conducted on BabyAI, Household, Mario, and Minecraft domains with PPO, A2C, and Q-learning. Two abstraction settings are considered: a “deterministic” (primitive-level) and a “hierarchical” (long-horizon) setting, and a verifier is optionally used to validate plan actions. Reviewers found the idea interesting but raised substantial concerns about novelty, clarity, experimental scope, and practical efficiency. One reviewer (R2) rated the paper clearly written and more positively (score 5), while the other three (R1, R3, R4) rated it 3 and identified multiple weaknesses.

### Strengths
- **Straightforward algorithm**: The overall method is easy to understand (R1).
- **Clear discussion of experiments**: The experimental setup and results are presented clearly (R1).
- **Clearly written and easy to follow** (R2). *(Note: R1 and R4 found the presentation confusing; see Weaknesses.)*
- **Interesting and promising idea**: Leveraging LLM heuristics for reward shaping is a novel and promising direction (R3).
- **Novel synthesis**: Combining LLMs, planning, and potential-based reward shaping has not been done before (R4).
- **Large number of environments**: The method is evaluated on multiple domains (BabyAI, Household, Mario, Minecraft) (R1, R4).
- **Multiple RL algorithms**: Experiments include A2C, PPO, and Q-learning (R4).
- **Variety of LLMs**: Different LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, etc.) are tested (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **LLM prompting is oversimplified and not novel**: The prompting consists of only task/state information; the verifier acts as a simple legality checker, common in LLM agent frameworks. The paper dedicates over a page (Section 4) to this process, which is disproportionate to its novelty (R1). The verifier is manually designed, rule-based, and task-specific, adding human burden (R2, R1).
- **Writing clarity issues**: Figure 1 is confusing (arrow pointing to replay buffer but no arrow out) (R1). The deterministic vs. hierarchical dichotomy is ill-posed and does not align with existing literature; “long-horizon” would be a better descriptor than “hierarchical” (R4). The presentation of the pipeline is contrived and confusing (R4).
- **Missing comparisons with prior and related work**:
  - No comparison with [1] (Du et al., 2023) which addresses the same problem of using LLMs for reward shaping with goal-based potential shaping (R2).
  - No comparison with other LLM-enhanced RL methods (R1, R3). Related works mentioned in Section 2 are not included in experiments (R3).
  - No comparison to standard planning (PDDL planners) or policy iteration with full MDP access, which might outperform the method given the amount of MDP specification required (R4).
- **Experimental scope and scalability concerns**:
  - All environments are discrete grid-worlds with explicit semantics; the method’s scalability to high-dimensional, continuous state/action spaces (e.g., continuous actions, multi-dimensional action spaces) is unclear (R2, R3, R4). Stochasticity is mentioned but the environments do not appear particularly non-deterministic or long-horizon (R4).
  - Evaluation benchmarks lack detailed descriptions (observation space, partial observability) and citations, especially for Mario and Minecraft where the used versions are not specified, making reproduction difficult (R3).
  - Only tabular Q-learning is used in RQ2.2; deep RL methods (DQN, PPO) are not evaluated in that research question (R3). R1 also asks why only tabular Q-learning.
- **LLMs alone achieve high reward, undermining the need for RL**: In Table 1, LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o) reach high rewards (e.g., >0.9 on Empty Room) without training; the paper does not explain why RL is needed instead of directly imitating the LLM policy (R1). Similarly, if the LLM generates executable plans, why not use them to solve the MDP directly rather than for reward shaping (R4)?
- **Requires near-complete MDP access**: The deterministic setting requires a textual description of the entire environment; the hierarchical setting requires a full PDDL specification; with the verifier, full transition dynamics are needed. This undermines the motivation of reward shaping for unknown MDPs (R4). Also, the method heavily relies on the PDDL model introduced in prior work [1] (R3).
- **Insufficient statistical rigor**: Standard deviations in RQ2 results are very large, and statistics are computed over only 5 runs (R1, R4). Table 1 averages plan lengths and rewards over 3 runs without justification for the number of runs (R4). Hyperparameter choices and whether they differ across settings are not reported (R4).
- **High computational cost and lack of efficiency analysis**: The inference frequency of LLMs during training is not stated; the method incurs significant LLM inference costs, but no analysis of computational overhead, energy cost, or impact on training time is provided (R3).
- **Substantial manual prompt engineering**: Designing prompts requires manual effort, and sensitivity of results to prompt design is not addressed (R3, R4). The method does not seem more generalizable than PDDL planning because of the domain-specific prompt engineering (R4).
- **Missing details**:
  - No explicit description of the potential function used in reward shaping (R1).
  - For long-horizon (hierarchical) settings, it is unclear how sub-goals are determined and how the agent knows a sub-goal is finished (R2).
  - The extraction of PDDL models and reward shaping functions is not described in sufficient detail (R3).
- **Limited discussion of limitations**: The paper does not adequately discuss limitations such as sensitivity to prompts, computational cost, and limited experimental scope (R3).
