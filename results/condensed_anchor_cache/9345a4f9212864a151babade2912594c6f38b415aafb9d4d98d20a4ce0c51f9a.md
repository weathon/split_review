## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents Denoising Task Routing (DTR), an add-on strategy for diffusion models that casts denoising at each timestep as a multi-task learning problem. DTR selectively activates subsets of channels based on task affinity (adjacent timesteps share channels) and task weights (more channels at high timesteps, fewer at low timesteps), establishing distinct information pathways without adding parameters. Empirical results show consistent performance improvements and faster training convergence across multiple benchmarks.

### Strengths
- The concept of integrating prior knowledge from diffusion (task affinity, task weights) into task routing is well presented and could influence future architecture design. The method effectively mitigates negative transfer via task-specific pathways. (All reviewers; particularly emphasized by R2)
- DTR is a simple, add-on strategy that yields significant performance gains on multiple benchmarks without introducing extra parameters, and accelerates training convergence. (R1, R2, R3)
- The paper is well-structured and easy to follow. (R2, R3)
- Experiments are comprehensive and demonstrate consistent improvement across different evaluation protocols. (R1, R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concern:** The idea of treating diffusion as multi-task learning was previously proposed (Hang et al. 2023, Go et al. 2023a). The proposed masking is a simple modification of Task Routing (TR; Strezoski et al., 2019), limiting novelty. (R1)
- **Missing ablation:** No ablation compares DTR against alternatives such as dividing channels into shared and task-specific channels (Ding et al., 2023) and assigning each time-step cluster (Go et al., 2023a) to respective task-specific channels. (R1)
- **Insufficient decoupling of contributions:** The analysis does not separately ablate task weights and task affinity. For task weights, the authors should compare `DTR with random routing but task-dedicated allocation channels` versus `Random Task Routing (R-TR)`. For task affinity, compare `DTR with task-unified allocation channels but sliding window channels` versus `R-TR`. (R2)
- **Lack of sensitivity analysis on masking strategy:** The paper does not explain why Equation (4) was chosen as the masking strategy, nor does it discuss potential alternatives. (R2) Additionally, a possible typographical error in Equation (4) was noted: the first \( t \) may need to be \( t-1 \). (R2)
- **Question on channel allocation:** Given the observation in Figure 6 that higher timesteps are less correlated, would assigning entirely distinct channels (reducing overlap) at higher timesteps be beneficial? (R2)
- **Missing comparison with advanced routing methods:** Other routing methods [1,2] improve random routing by considering inter-task relationships; these should be discussed and compared. (R3)
- **Unclear behavior in Figure 9:** The baseline images look strange; both R-TR and DTR alleviate this, but the effectiveness of random routing is not explained. Moreover, in the fifth column, R-TR appears to produce better images than DTR. (R3)

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* R1 rated contribution as fair (2) and novelty limited; R2 rated contribution as good (3) and praised the concept; R3 raised concerns about comparisons and unexplained behaviors but rated soundness excellent. All reviewers agreed on the method’s empirical effectiveness and clarity of presentation.
