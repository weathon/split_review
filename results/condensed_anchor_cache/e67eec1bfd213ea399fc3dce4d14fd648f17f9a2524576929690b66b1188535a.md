## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a class of C*-algebra-valued positive definite kernels called spectral truncation kernels, designed for function-valued regression in the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert C*-Module (RKHM) framework. The kernels approximate multiplication operators on L²(T) using truncated spectral decompositions, introducing noncommutativity controlled by a truncation parameter n. The claimed advantages include reduced computational cost in kernel ridge regression compared to vector-valued RKHS (vvRKHS), a trade-off between representation power and model complexity, and a deep architecture based on products of such kernels. Theoretical results cover kernel convergence and generalization bounds (derived from Hashimoto et al. 2023). Experimental validation uses a synthetic 1D function regression task and an image inpainting problem on MNIST. Reviewers were divided: three gave score 6, while one gave score 3.

**Note:** One reviewer (score 3) was substantially more critical than the others (score 6), particularly on computational efficiency claims, theoretical grounding of the deep model, and empirical validation.

### Strengths
- Original research direction in kernel theory, exploring C*-algebra-valued kernels and the RKHM framework.
- Solid mathematical foundation with detailed properties of the proposed kernels and convergence analysis.
- Spectral truncation kernel reduces computational complexity in Kernel Ridge Regression from cubic in output dimension m to linear in m, with a trade-off governed by parameter n.
- Flexibility to declinate with various choices of ground kernels while maintaining positive definiteness.
- Deep architectural extension based on products (not composition) of kernel-based functions in RKHM increases representation power.
- Generalization bound provides theoretical insight into the balance between representation power (increasing with n) and model complexity (controlled by n).
- Presentation is clear and well organized (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear motivation and lack of comparison with existing methods:** The paper does not clearly articulate the limitations of existing operator-valued kernels (e.g., separable kernels, transformable kernels) that motivate the new approach. It lacks a detailed discussion of computational bottlenecks and representational limitations of current vvRKHS methods. Specifically, the claimed computational advantage over vvRKHSs is not directly compared with optimized vvRKHS approximations such as Nyström methods or random Fourier features. The cost of evaluating the function-valued kernel itself is not analyzed. A more detailed breakdown and direct comparison are needed. (Reviewers 1, 2)

2. **Insufficient empirical evaluation:** Experiments are limited to a synthetic 1D dataset and a simplified MNIST inpainting task. No comparison is made with other function-valued regression methods on benchmark datasets. The image inpainting experiment should specify the exact matrix-valued kernel used for comparison and include more general operator-valued kernels for function-valued outputs. The sensitivity of the method to the truncation parameter n and to data noise is not explored. Real-world datasets with function-valued outputs are needed to demonstrate practical utility. The effect of training set size on performance is not examined (synthetic experiment only varies n). (Reviewers 1, 2)

3. **Theoretical grounding of deep model is insufficient:** The deep spectral truncation kernel analysis relies on a very specific construction and does not provide general insights into deep network behavior with these kernels. The potential for overfitting and the impact of different activation functions are not addressed. The general development should be data/task-dependent. (Reviewer 2; Reviewer 3 also notes this)

4. **Computational cost analysis is incomplete:** The analysis does not consider the cost of evaluating the kernel itself, which may be substantial depending on the C*-algebra and truncation parameter. Computing the solution involves inverting (G(z) + λI), leading to poor scalability for large datasets. The claimed linear dependency on m needs to be validated against optimized vvRKHS implementations. (Reviewers 2, 4)

5. **Convergence theory (Theorem 3.4) lacks practical guidance:** It shows that the proposed kernels approximate their infinite-dimensional counterparts, but does not provide guidance on how to choose n for good approximation. Similarly, the generalization bound (Theorem 4.1) is presented without clearly distinguishing it from prior results (Hashimoto et al., 2023; Maurer, 2016). The bound relates to the trace of the kernel, but its novelty over existing bounds is unclear. (Reviewers 1, 4)

6. **Benefits of noncommutativity not demonstrated theoretically:** The paper introduces noncommutativity via spectral truncation, but the benefits in terms of convergence or generalization are not shown analytically. The effect is only illustrated experimentally. It is not clear how the noncommutativity differs from standard complexity trade-offs in kernel methods (e.g., number of basis functions). (Reviewer 4)

7. **Applicability and domain of the method are vague:** The paper does not identify the family of problems for which these kernels are most suitable. Representing vectors (e.g., image pixels) as functions of their coordinate index is not always meaningful; the limitations of this encoding should be discussed. For time series, how does the kernel capture temporal dependencies compared to existing time series kernels? (Reviewer 1)

8. **Presentation issues:** The paper is not fully self-contained. Key details are missing: (a) Line 154 jumps to C(T) without clarifying that X = A = C(T); the mention of Toeplitz matrices is introduced without prior context. (b) Line 174 on Fejér functions and the construction of van Suijlekom needs explanation. (c) Propositions are given as formulas without introductory sentences or comments on their significance. (d) The generalization bound should be clearly attributed to previous literature. (e) The variable m (output dimension) should be introduced earlier, e.g., when describing the observations (line 364). (f) Some definitions mix complex and real domains without explanation; a pseudo-code would help. (g) The notation "z ∈ T is the Fourier function" is confusing (line 156) – in Example 2.2, T is a set of real numbers, making it unclear why z is a function. (Reviewers 1, 4)
