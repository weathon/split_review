## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Equivariant Neural Fields (ENFs), a novel conditional neural field (CNF) architecture that uses a geometry-informed cross-attention mechanism to condition a neural field on a latent point cloud of features, enabling equivariant decoding from latent to field. The method induces a steerability property—transformations of the field are mirrored in the latent and vice versa—and is validated on 2D/3D reconstruction, classification, segmentation, flood map segmentation, and climate forecasting. Scores range from 6 to 8, with reviewers agreeing on the novelty and mathematical soundness but raising concerns about baseline fairness, computational cost, ablation, and clarification of claims. Reviewer 2 and 3 are more positive (score 8), while Reviewer 1 and 4 are more critical (score 6), noting issues in motivation, comparison scope, and empirical gaps.

### Strengths
- The paper introduces a novel and mathematically sound method for incorporating geometric structure into neural fields via equivariant cross-attention, with proven bi-invariant constraints and a clear steerability property.
- The approach combines conditional neural fields with point cloud conditioning and equivariant decoding, creatively extending existing techniques; specialized attention layers and engineering optimizations (e.g., Gaussian windowing, k-nearest neighbors) enhance efficiency.
- The method demonstrates clear advantages over geometry-free latent space baselines (e.g., Functa) in tasks requiring geometric reasoning, with locality and weight-sharing properties well demonstrated experimentally.
- The paper is well-written, structured, and easy to follow, with clear motivation, formal definitions, informative diagrams (e.g., highlighting geometry attributes in blue), and high-quality visualizations.
- Experimental validation is comprehensive across a wide variety of dataset types (images, SDFs, point clouds, climate data) and downstream tasks, supporting versatility and robustness.
- The method is simple and intuitive for enhancing CNFs with local equivariant features.

### Weaknesses
- **Baseline comparison and architectural confound:** The paper compares ENF (attention-based) primarily against Functa (SIREN-based MLP). Architectural differences—particularly the use of cross-attention vs. SIREN—make it difficult to isolate the impact of equivariance. A more direct comparison (e.g., Functa with an attention-based backbone) is needed. Reviewer suggests comparing against Inr2Array and NF2vec for Table 2 experiments (downstream tasks). The decoder architecture for Functa baselines on downstream tasks is not specified.
- **Ambiguity in training latent point cloud:** There is no principled criterion for choosing between MAML and autodecoding to train the latent point cloud; the trade-offs (computational cost, convergence, generalization) are not discussed. This introduces arbitrariness.
- **Limited comparison to other methods:** The evaluation is mostly restricted to CNF-based approaches (Functa) and lacks thorough comparison against other equivariant methods or state-of-the-art methods, including point cloud–specific architectures for segmentation. For part-segmentation (Table 3), ENF performs only comparably to baselines, and some experiments (Table 2) do not show a clear advantage from equivariance. Conditions under which equivariance provides significant benefit need analysis.
- **High computational cost:** The approach appears computationally intensive. The paper does not compare training time or memory usage against Functa or other baselines. A detailed breakdown of the attention mechanism’s scaling with input points and latent variables, including the impact of kNN and Gaussian windowing on training efficiency, is missing. Ablation studies on these components (e.g., varying k) are lacking.
- **Segmentation performance concerns:** ENF’s segmentation results on ShapeNet are weaker than those of traditional point cloud segmentation baselines (e.g., PointNet++). The evaluation uses an aligned dataset (ShapeNet); non-aligned datasets (e.g., human-body segmentation) or random SE(3) augmentations would better test equivariance. It is unclear if the point cloud baselines were also trained with a reconstruction pretext stage.
- **Unclear motivation for using CNF latent encodings in downstream tasks:** The paper does not explicitly discuss why latent features learned through continuous reconstruction (decoder) are beneficial over features from discrete sample reconstruction (e.g., discretization-agnostic features). The claimed limitation that conventional CNFs use a “global variable” is partially accurate; a global latent has advantages (e.g., interpolation). A tradeoff characterization is more appropriate. Why not use an encoder-decoder with gradually decreasing spatial dependency (e.g., UNet) instead of an auto-decoder?
- **Overclaiming on geometry-appearance separation:** The paper claims that the latent separates geometry (pose) from appearance (context c_i). However, c_i being SE(n)-invariant does not guarantee only appearance is captured (e.g., shape volume is invariant, some appearance features are equivariant). No empirical evidence supports this separation.
- **Reconstruction results unclear:** Both ENF and baselines struggle to accurately reconstruct shapes from SDF point clouds. It is unclear why ENF underperforms in reconstruction compared to a prior work [3], given architectural similarity aside from equivariance. The reconstruction inaccuracies raise concerns about the usefulness of learned features for downstream tasks.
- **Figure 8 uninformative** without comparison to other methods; it does not show qualitative benefits or limitations of ENF.
- **Smoothness restriction:** Conditioning with k-nearest neighbors restricts the modeled field’s smoothness to at most continuous, while data signals are at least differentiable.
- **Prior work:** The steerability property for CNFs has been defined and utilized in prior works (e.g., [1], [2]).
- **Typos:** “faithfully” misspelled in abstract; “posses” should be “possess” (line 103).
