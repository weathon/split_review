## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a causality-guided debiasing framework using prompting strategies (DDP) to reduce social bias in LLMs. It identifies causal pathways through which social information influences LLM decisions and introduces three strategies to promote fact-based reasoning and discourage biased reasoning. Experiments on WinoBias, BBQ, and Discrim-Eval across multiple social dimensions and models show significant bias reduction.

### Strengths
- Provides a theoretical justification for debiasing using the selection bias argument from causal inference (R1, R4).
- Offers a detailed causal analysis that clarifies origins of bias in both training data generation and LLM reasoning processes (R2, R3, R4).
- Presents principled prompting strategies that unify existing debiasing approaches and open new directions (R3).
- Demonstrates significant reduction of bias in experiments across multiple models and datasets (R1, R2, R3).
- Considers bias caused by data selection mechanisms, providing a more systematic solution (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. The connection between selection bias theory and the actual prompts is weak; strategies differ per dataset (I & II on one, I & III on another), suggesting the list may not be exhaustive and may grow with more datasets. It is unclear how the theory helps choose which strategy to apply in a given context (R1).
2. Ablations are not provided: what happens when all strategies are used on the first dataset, or only I & II on the second (R1)?
3. No comparison to simple alternative prompts (e.g., “avoid any gender bias while answering”) or other prompting-based debiasing methods (e.g., [1][2][3] from R4). Also no baseline using K nearest in-context examples (R1, R4).
4. Strategy I is computationally intensive: likely requires three LLM calls per question (create two scenarios, decide plausibility, answer final question). This may be less efficient than iterative refinement approaches like ReAct with a critique agent (R1).
5. One minority reviewer (R2) argues DDP suffers from poor applicability for general-purpose generation: it requires dataset-specific textual guidance and cannot handle free-form generation where social attributes emerge from model outputs. (R2)
6. The same minority reviewer (R2) questions the meaningfulness of prompting-based debiasing, noting that model alignment is traditionally the deployer’s responsibility and that such prompting can be unsafe and introduces extra time costs. (R2)
7. DDP may introduce noise that hinders reasoning: Table 1-Type II shows bias reduction but degrades performance on Anti and Pro for GPT-3/3.5 and Claude 2. Robustness remains an unsolved problem (R2). This echoes a general need for more analysis of trade-offs between bias reduction and task performance (R3).
8. Limited discussion of how effectiveness varies with model scale or architecture (R3).
9. A key assumption—that the LLM is “well-trained and well-aligned” so it internalizes training data dependencies—is stated only in the appendix and should be in the main text; if the model is not well-aligned, the prompting method may be less effective (R4).
10. When all three strategies are combined, social-salient text and fact representations are both independent of social category representation; is this then equivalent to just using the base question as the prompt? Experimental comparison of these two conditions is needed (R4).
11. In Table 2, for GPT-3.5 / Counteract Only / Anti, the sum of true-rate (42.33%) and false-rate (8.68%) is not 1; the reason is unclear (R4).
12. Minor issues: Figure 3 may be missing a solid line connecting “prompt” to “LLM potential decision” (R4). The term ‘default’ in Table 2 needs clarification—if it means the same as in Table 1 (original question only), why does it still have metrics TT, TF, FT, FF (R2)? Paper organization is unclear: the pre-trained data generating process section is not necessary and theoretical insights are coupled with technical details (R2).
