## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Riemannian framework for fractional natural gradient descent (FNGD) that learns eigen-factorized Fisher estimations on the fly. The approach factorizes the curvature matrix \(S\) as \(B\,\mathrm{diag}(d)\,B^\top\) with orthogonal \(B\) and diagonal \(d\), then updates \(B\) and \(d\) using Riemannian gradient descent on a reparameterized manifold. This spectral parameterization enables cheap application of arbitrary fractional powers without matrix decompositions, and extends to Kronecker-structured preconditioners. The authors test the method on positive-definite matrix optimization and low-precision neural network training.

### Strengths
- **Unifying framework.** The paper provides a unified perspective that recasts many preconditioned gradient algorithms (RMSprop, Adam(W), K‑FAC, Shampoo) as special cases of FNGD (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Cheap application of arbitrary fractional powers.** The spectral parameterization allows applying any fractional power \(p\) of the preconditioner without matrix decompositions, distinguishing the method from Cholesky‑based RGD (Reviewers 1, 3, 6).
- **Numerical stability.** The approach avoids explicit matrix decompositions, making it stable in low‑precision (e.g., half‑precision) arithmetic (Reviewers 3, 6).
- **Extension to Kronecker factorization.** The method is extended to Kronecker‑structured preconditioners (Reviewers 5, 6, 7).
- **Relevant and interesting problem.** The work addresses a practical bottleneck in second‑order optimization and has clear potential for large‑scale deep learning (Reviewers 4, 7).
- **Good introduction and motivation.** The introduction is easy to follow and clearly motivates the proposed method (Reviewer 4).
- **Clear attribution.** The paper properly describes its own contribution and credits prior work (Lin et al. 2024, Amari et al.) (Reviewer 7).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation issues.** Many notations and concepts are left undefined or insufficiently explained:
  *  the matrix \(B\), the purpose of the Kronecker factorization, the matrix \(H\) (line 101), the “mat” notation (line 107), the matrices \(S^K\) and \(S^C\), the Kronecker product \(\otimes\), and the “Gaussian problem” (Reviewers 2, 5, 7).
  *  Claims presented as “claims” are later proven as lemmas; explicit lemma statements are missing from the main text (Reviewers 4, 5, 7).  For instance, Claim 2 becomes Lemma 2 in the appendix, and Claim 5 reads like a definition (Reviewer 5).
  *  Figure 1 is overloaded with information and disrupts the paper flow (Reviewer 4).
  *  Extra spacing at lines 151‑161 and 242‑248, and inconsistent coloring (e.g., lines 353, 745, 833, 932) that may be forgotten highlights (Reviewer 4).
  *  Inconsistent use of “Diag”/“diag” and variants of “RMSprop” (e.g., RMSprop, RmsProp, RMRprop) (Reviewers 4, 7).
  *  Equation (3) appears without any introduction or definition of \(\mathcal{H}\) (Reviewer 7).
  *  The meaning of “root‑free” scheme is not explained (Reviewer 1).
- **Weak motivation.** The central claim—that choosing different fractional powers \(p\) is beneficial—is not supported numerically. The only evidence is a weak statement at the end of Section 4 (“This shows the potential of using other fractional roots”). No experiments show that the best training results require a \(p\) other than 1 or 2 (Reviewers 1, 7). Consequently, several reviewers question why this complex method is needed over simpler alternatives (Reviewers 2, 5, 7).
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation.** The paper is largely empirical yet provides only one figure (three vision transformers on one dataset), which is not enough to demonstrate general effectiveness (Reviewer 4). Missing elements include:
  *  Large‑scale experiments (Reviewers 1, 6).
  *  Comparisons with recent adaptive gradient methods for large‑scale language models (Reviewer 3).
  *  An ablation study on the effect of different spectral factorization techniques on convergence and generalization (Reviewer 3).
  *  Justification for excluding K‑FAC from the numerical comparison (Reviewer 3).
  *  Detailed experimental setup (e.g., hyperparameters, learning rates), limiting reproducibility (Reviewer 4).
- **Computational complexity and cost unclear.** There are no quantitative comparisons of running time or memory with Adam, Shampoo, or methods that directly compute matrix decompositions. The calibration of running time per method is only vaguely described (Reviewers 3, 7). Moreover, the method introduces a per‑iteration error of \(O(\beta_2^2)\) (non‑negligible for constant \(\beta_2\)) and additional inaccuracy from the Cayley map inverse approximation (Reviewer 7).
- **Methodological ambiguities.**
  *  Handling of factorization ambiguities in high‑dimensional settings is unclear (Reviewer 2).
  *  The conceptual difference between the “Procedure for Full‑matrix Spectral Parametrizations” and the “Riemannian Approach for Obtaining Root‑free Update Schemes” is not clarified (Reviewer 5).
  *  Details on how matrix \(Q\) was generated in the positive‑definite optimization problem (Section 4) are missing (Reviewer 6).
  *  The derivation of the procedure for Kronecker‑structured spectral parametrizations is not fully given (Reviewer 6).
  *  In the low‑precision NN training experiments, the claimed advantage of \(p=1\) over \(p=2\) is not statistically significant in the presented plots (Reviewers 6, 7).
  *  Figure 4 does not clearly demonstrate that fractional roots are better than \(p=2\) (Reviewer 5).
- **Reviewer disagreement on overall quality.** While one reviewer (Reviewer 6, score 8) found the method practical, stable, and well‑extended from prior work, several other reviewers rated the paper lower (scores 3–5) citing the clarity, motivation, and experimental shortcomings noted above.
