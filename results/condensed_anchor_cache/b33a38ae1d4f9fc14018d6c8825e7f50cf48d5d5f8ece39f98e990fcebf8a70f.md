## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Dense Backpropagation for sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). The key idea is to approximate the output of non-activated experts for each token using the outputs of other tokens that were routed to that expert, thereby providing a dense gradient to the router while keeping the forward pass sparse. Three approximation methods are described: Expert Group Approximation, Attention Approximation, and Attention Approximation with LSH. Experiments on a DeepSeek MoE and a conventional MoE show that Dense Backpropagation (particularly Expert Group Approximation) improves training loss, validation perplexity, load balance, and downstream accuracy compared to standard Top‑K routing, with minimal runtime overhead. The method is evaluated at the 2B parameter scale (470M active) with 200B tokens.

### Strengths

- **Novel and creative approach**: Addressing sparse backpropagation in MoEs by approximating expert activations to provide a dense gradient signal is a new idea not explored in prior work (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **Lightweight and computationally efficient**: The method introduces minimal overhead; throughput is maintained (97.7% of baseline) (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Reviewer 1 notes that it does not increase computation for backprop; Reviewer 4 calls it “lightweight”.
- **Improved performance and load balance**: Expert Group Approximation outperforms standard Top‑K in terms of training loss, perplexity, and load balancing (Reviewers 1, 4). Results are verified on a relatively large model (2B total, 470M active) with 200B training tokens (Reviewer 4).
- **Good approximation quality**: Figure 9 shows the router gradient approximation is fairly accurate (Reviewer 1). Ablations on various approximation heuristics provide insight into design choices (Reviewer 4).
- **Well-written and clear**: The paper is easy to follow, with consistent notation, visualizations, and logical structure (Reviewers 1, 2). However, Reviewer 3 disagrees and finds the presentation poor (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Modest perplexity improvements**: The validation perplexity drops from 18.92 to 18.55 (≈2%) on one setting. Given the slight speed drop (97.7% throughput) and limited number of settings, the practical significance is questioned (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Limited evaluation**: Results focus almost exclusively on perplexity; scoring and sampling tasks (e.g., zero‑shot benchmarks) are only briefly mentioned (Figure 1, 11) with no exact numbers or statistical significance tests (Reviewers 2, 3). Evaluation is also limited to two MoE architectures, two sizes, and one dataset (Reviewers 2, 4). No analysis of inference‑time performance or fine‑tuning stability is provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing baselines**: The method is compared only to standard Top‑K routing. Dense approximations of the Top‑K operator are well‑studied (e.g., SparseMixer, Gradient‑Informed MoE), and the paper should at least compare with one or two such baselines. Additionally, comparisons with Expert Choice Routing (Zhou et al., 2022) and DeepSeek‑MoE (Dai et al., 2024) are missing (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Motivation not empirically validated**: It is not clearly shown that the approximated dense gradient actually improves over the true sparse gradient. The paper lacks a direct quantitative analysis (e.g., cosine similarity or MSE) between the approximated and the actual gradient of inactivated experts, especially across layers and training stages (Reviewer 3). A critical missing baseline is the same forward pass with sparse activation but using the STE‑estimated dense gradient in the backward pass (Reviewer 4).
- **Contradictory result in expert group approximation**: An experiment (L479‑496) shows that a *more accurate* approximation performs *worse* than the proposed method. This result is not investigated, undermining the rigor of the approach (Reviewer 3). The authors should analyze variance, hyperparameter sensitivity, or impact on different parts of the model.
- **Inaccurate claim about runtime**: The paper claims the method does not “increase runtime” (L20), but later admits “minimal overhead” (L66‑67) and Table 4 shows a throughput drop (97.7%). The attention approximation clearly introduces more overhead. Concrete latency/throughput measurements should replace vague terms (Reviewer 3).
- **Methodology presentation confusion**: 
  - Section 4 is inconsistent: validation perplexity for DeepSeek MoE is in a plot (Figure 6) but in a table for the conventional MoE (Table 4). Several figures/tables (1, 7, 11, 2, 3, 4) do not indicate which model is being reported and are not placed where first mentioned (Reviewer 3).
  - The method is initially presented as modifying only the backward pass, but Section 4.2 reveals that both forward and backward passes must be modified; this crucial distinction should be stated earlier (Reviewer 4).
- **Dependence on large micro batch size**: The method requires a sufficiently large micro batch size to obtain good approximations, which is a limitation for large‑scale training with limited VRAM. No analysis of the effect of micro batch size on approximation quality is provided (Reviewer 4).
- **Other approximation methods not explored**: Attention Approximation and Attention Approximation with LSH are described but not deeply evaluated. If they are not useful, they should be removed (Reviewer 2).
- **Questions raised about assumptions and generalization**: 
  - The approximation assumes experts are roughly linear; could a higher‑order approximation be better? (Reviewer 1)
  - The method only addresses router gradients; could expert gradients also be approximated? (Reviewer 1)
  - Consistency with autoregressive training: approximations seem to involve future tokens’ activations; does this break causality? (Reviewer 4)
  - The paper uses K=2 for Top‑K; performance with K=1 or K>2 is not shown (Reviewers 2, 4). Scalability with more experts is also unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Reviewer disagreement on significance and presentation**: Reviewer 1 is very positive about the paper’s clarity and results, while Reviewer 3 finds the presentation poor and the results unconvincing (e.g., calls claims incorrect). This split highlights the need for more rigorous evaluation and clearer reporting.
