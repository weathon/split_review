## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents LBS3, an automatic reasoning prompting approach inspired by curriculum learning. Given a target query, LBS3 first steers the LLM to generate easy proxy queries (phase 1) and then hard proxy queries (phase 2), finally solving the target query conditioned on examples from both stages. The authors argue that prior automatic example generation methods may produce examples that are too hard, and LBS3 attempts to remedy this. Experiments across multiple reasoning-intensive tasks with open- and closed-source LLMs (including Llama3-70B-Instruct and GPT-4.0 turbo) show that LBS3 achieves competitive performance against SOTA baselines. Two reviewers rate the paper 6, one rates it 5; confidence scores 3, 4, 4.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- The idea of easy-to-hard prompting is natural, simple, elegant, and effective, leading to strong performance.
- The method makes intuitive sense and is shown to outperform many baselines on complex reasoning tasks.
- The experimental setup is thorough: choice of models, datasets, and baselines is reasonably solid, and results are verified under different LLMs.

### Weaknesses
- Lack of theoretical contribution; the technical novelty compared to existing methods (e.g., Ana-Pro) appears minor.
- The paper could provide more insight into *why* the method works. In particular:
  - There is no quantitative evaluation of whether easy proxy exemplars actually improve LLM accuracy on harder proxy exemplars (e.g., accuracy on hard proxy exemplars themselves).
  - The analysis does not examine the type of errors made under suboptimal ablations (e.g., can failures be attributed to bad reasoning?).
  - The validity of generated examples is unclear: are the easy examples actually easy and the hard examples actually harder?
  - The sensitivity to prompt wording (e.g., the prompt in Table 3) is not explored.
- Ablation studies are insufficient to show that each proposed component is necessary. Specific missing ablations:
  - What happens if no APG questions are generated (i.e., n2=0)?
  - What happens if gold examples from the dataset are used instead of LLM-generated questions?
  - Does the order of examples in the final RAG-F stage matter (e.g., separating easy and hard)?
  - Can the method rely only on hard examples (i.e., retrieve only hard exemplars)?
  - How does performance change if more exemplars are used?
- The result that Llama3-70B-Instruct outperforms GPT-4.0 turbo on most tasks is highly counterintuitive, as GPT-4.0 turbo is generally stronger across benchmarks. The paper’s explanation ("we considered only a limited set of reasoning tasks") is unsatisfactory, raising concerns about potential artifacts or bugs. (One reviewer specifically questions why temperature could not be set to 0 for closed-source models to ensure determinism.)
- A minority reviewer (score 5) is more critical than the other two (score 6), finding the lack of mechanistic explanation and the counterintuitive GPT-4 comparison particularly problematic.
