## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates Bayesian neural networks in the infinite-width limit under infinite-variance priors (elliptically distributed, α-stable). It shows that the resulting covariance kernels remain stochastic and data-dependent, enabling representation learning unlike finite-variance priors that yield deterministic kernels. The key technical contributions are: a recursive formula (Theorem 1) for the conditional covariance kernels across layers in the deep setting, and a conditionally Gaussian representation that permits tractable inference. A computationally feasible MCMC method is provided. Experiments on synthetic discontinuous functions and UCI benchmarks demonstrate improved predictive performance over competing approaches, especially for heavy-tailed or discontinuous data.

### Strengths
- Strong theoretical contributions (R1, R5): the paper addresses the critical question of avoiding deterministic kernels in infinite-width BNNs, providing a rigorous analysis supported by an extensive appendix with proofs (R2, R5).
- The core idea is elegant and relatively straightforward: everything is conditionally Gaussian given a scale parameter, which induces heavy-tailed behavior (R2).
- Representation learning is formalised: Proposition 3 shows that the features at layer l depend on training data (X, y), a clear benefit over finite-variance models. This notion is nicely captured (R2, R3).
- The method is demonstrated on both synthetic discontinuous functions (where shallow stable processes sometimes fail) and a range of UCI benchmarks, often outperforming baselines (R1, R2, R4). Useful graphics include posterior predictive plots in 1D and a QQ plot showing non-Gaussian features; more extreme non-Gaussian behavior is shown in Appendix Figure 10 (R2).
- The paper is well-structured and complete, with a solid mix of theoretical grounding, ablation studies, and performance benchmarks (R4).
- The work has potential impact on future BNN research and opens possibilities for non-Gaussian priors in other deep models (R4).
- **Minority contrast:** While some reviewers found the presentation dense, others (R2, R4, R5) found the paper well-structured and the contributions clearly highlighted in context of prior work.

### Weaknesses
**Presentation and clarity (multiple reviewers):**
- The paper is challenging to approach: highly technical, niche, and text-heavy (R1, R3, R4). The abstract focuses too much on prior work instead of stating the key contributions concisely; the introduction does not clearly state objectives, motivation, and contributions upfront before discussing related work (R3). Terminology should be introduced in a dedicated preliminaries section (R3). The logical flow needs improvement; parts are repetitive (R3).
- Lack of visual aids: the paper would benefit from more figures illustrating how the α-stable kernel process differs from traditional GPs, e.g., a visualization of the kernel shape under different α values, or a 1D regression example with varying α to show behavior on simple synthetic data (R1, R4). Some detail could be moved to the appendix (R4). Figure 1 and 2 labels are too small and the sans-serif font clashes with the body text (R2).
- Minor: Theorem 1 introduces $J_\delta(\theta)$ before it is defined (R2). The caption of Figure 1 says "conditional mutual information" but should state "mutual information [which itself is computed via MCMC as an expected conditional mutual information]" (R2). Grammar issue in last sentence of first paragraph of Section 4.2 (R2).

**Motivation and innovation (R3):**
- The motivation for the specific deep kernel process formulation is not clearly articulated: why is an infinite variance setting important or beneficial for deep kernel processes? How does the ability to learn features from observations address limitations of previous approaches? The key technical challenges in proving Theorem 1 and Proposition 3 are not highlighted, nor is it explained how overcoming them advances the field. Potential applications or extensions of the technical innovations beyond this specific model are not discussed (R3).

**Experiments and validation (multiple reviewers):**
- The description of experimental setups, motivation for each experiment, and implications of the results in relation to theoretical contributions could be clearer (R3).
- The real-world datasets do not fully showcase the unique advantages of the proposed approach; a wider range of datasets with varying degrees of discontinuity or heavy-tailed noise would be more compelling (R4). For example, a 1D regression with varying α and visualization of the covariance matrix has been requested (R1).
- One reviewer (R5) noted that the experiments are weaker than the theory, though they considered this expected for a theoretical paper.

**Missing comparisons and clarifications (from reviewer questions):**
- The technical differences from the prior work of Loria & Bhadra (2024) that make the current approach feasible are not sufficiently clarified (R1).
- A sentence about multiple outputs claims GPs cannot learn covariance structure because covariance is always zero under a Gaussian limit; this requires clarification, as non-isotropic Gaussian weights can introduce non-zero correlations (R2).
- Related work is missing: "Richer priors for infinitely wide multi-layer perceptrons" also marginalises an α-stable distributed scale matrix in the covariance (similar to Eq. 19); motivating the current approach also from the perspective of exchangeability in hidden layers could be beneficial (R2).
- The theoretical statement (Theorem 1) is restricted to rectified monomial activations; the proof may extend to broader activations (e.g., ELU, GELU) for which recursive covariance equations exist; an extension would strengthen the result (R2).

**Contrast in reviewer opinions:** While some reviewers (R1, R3) found the presentation and motivation lacking and rated the contribution lower (5), others (R2, R4, R5) rated it highly (8) and considered the paper well-written and the contributions significant. The experiments received mixed feedback: R1 said experiments effectively support claims, R2 said demonstrated well, R4 said extensive but real-world could be more compelling, and R5 said experiments are weaker (but expected). The weaknesses above reflect this range of views.
