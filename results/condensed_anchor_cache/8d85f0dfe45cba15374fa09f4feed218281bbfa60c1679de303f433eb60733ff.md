## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a multi-fidelity alignment approach to reduce the cost of LLM post-training, using multiple levels of response quality with associated costs. It provides a mathematical formulation of WinRate and theoretical analysis, plus empirical results. Reviewers generally find the idea useful and novel, but raise major concerns about clarity, missing baselines, similarity to existing methods (e.g., SPIN), unclear mechanism for performance gains, insufficient ablation and overhead analysis, unconvincing evaluation, and poor writing. One reviewer (rating 5) was significantly more positive, praising the theoretical foundation and pioneering cost‑reduction angle, but still noted key weaknesses; the other two (ratings 3 and 3) were more critical.

### Strengths

- The idea of reducing alignment costs by leveraging multiple levels of preference signal quality is practically useful and interesting.
- The theoretical foundation is thorough, with a rigorous mathematical formulation of the WinRate metric and its application in preference‑based training (Reviewer 2’s emphasis).
- The paper provides both theoretical insights and empirical results that support the effectiveness of the method.

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of innovation / similarity to existing work.** Reviewers highlight that Algorithm 2 closely resembles the SPIN algorithm (ICML24), with the main change being replacement of ground‑truth pairs by expert responses and addition of a compare function. This points to insufficient novelty relative to prior art.
- **Unclear whether performance gains stem from the proposed theory.** The iterative dataset generation followed by DPO training seems akin to knowledge distillation or data synthesis; the paper does not convincingly attribute improvements to the multi‑fidelity theory (Reviewer 2’s major concern).
- **Weak‑to‑strong generalization is inadequately explained.** If the goal is to explore weak‑to‑strong, more exploration is needed. If it is alignment improvement, a detailed comparison to knowledge distillation and data synthesis is required (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing baselines and comparisons.** The paper lacks comparison with: (i) standard DPO using publicly available or human‑annotated datasets; (ii) other preference optimization methods such as ORPO (which the authors themselves cite); (iii) prior alignment cost‑reduction methods. This makes it difficult to assess relative performance.
- **Evaluation metrics and benchmarks are not convincing.** The paper does not report results on widely‑used benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0 or Arena‑Hard (both reviewers 2 and 3). The choice of the rm‑static dataset and task is not justified, leaving the evaluation context unclear.
- **Compare function is underspecified.** It is unclear whether the compare function is prompt‑based or learned; if prompt‑based, the paper must explain it more explicitly (Reviewer 3).
- **Redundant writing and poor presentation.** The introduction on the WinRate metric is unnecessarily long and detracts from explaining the authors’ contribution. The paper contains unclear passages (e.g., difference between “step” and “iteration” in Section 5) and formatting errors (e.g., “Lemma ??” on line 588). A type error (“meetshuman’s”) also appears.
- **Hyper‑parameter settings seem tricky.** The paper does not report a grid search or ablation on hyper‑parameters, even though there is substantial space available (Reviewer 3).
- **Ablation study needed for the staged weak‑to‑strong selection process.** The algorithm selects models in a fixed order from weak to strong; the impact of this staged training should be validated via ablation (Reviewer 2).
- **Overhead of external decoding is not measured.** The approach introduces decoding operations from multiple external models, which could increase overhead instead of reducing it; no experiments demonstrate overall cost reduction (Reviewer 2).
- **Choice of Vicuna and model selection.** Using three distinct model series (e.g., Vicuna) is not justified; more controlled experiments with different sizes/versions of the same model family would better separate performance differences (Reviewer 2). The reason for choosing Vicuna over other instruction‑tuned models is unclear (Reviewer 1).

**Note:** Reviewer 2, while giving the highest rating (5), still raised these major concerns. The other two reviewers (ratings 3, 3) were more critical overall, especially on clarity, innovation, and evaluation quality.
