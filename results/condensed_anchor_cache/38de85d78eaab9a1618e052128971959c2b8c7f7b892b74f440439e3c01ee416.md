## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a lightweight cross-attention mechanism with linear complexity and a self-improved training (SIT) algorithm for solving large-scale Vehicle Routing Problems. By using representative nodes (first and last node of the current route) for cross-attention, the method reduces computational cost compared to standard self-attention. SIT iteratively reconstructs and improves partial solutions without requiring external labels. The method is evaluated on TSP and CVRP benchmarks up to 100K nodes, demonstrating competitive performance on synthetic and real-world data.

### Strengths
- **Efficient cross-attention mechanism** (R1, R3, R4): Novel and simple design that reduces complexity from quadratic to linear, enabling scaling to large VRPs (up to 100K nodes). This directly addresses a key bottleneck in transformer-based NCO solvers.
- **Self-Improved Training (SIT)** (R2, R3): Simple yet effective training scheme that improves performance by decomposing and reconstructing partial solutions. It allows direct training on large instances without costly label generation.
- **Strong empirical performance** (R2, R4): Consistently beats baseline methods on TSP and CVRP across multiple benchmark datasets, making previously prohibitive problem sizes tractable.
- **Robust experimental setup** (R4): Uses a variety of benchmarks and compares against multiple state-of-the-art methods.
- **Well-structured and clearly written paper** (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair complexity analysis** (R1): The paper only reports per-iteration complexity but ignores the cumulative computational cost of iterative reconstruction used in SIT. A fair comparison with traditional methods should account for all iterations.
- **Evaluation fairness concerns** (R2): Baselines (e.g., POMO) are typically trained on small problems (~100 nodes) and generalize poorly to larger sizes, while the proposed method is trained and tested on the same large scale. This makes comparisons misleading. The authors should compare on the same training size (e.g., directly test on smaller problems where baselines are trained) or apply the proposed SIT to other methods on partial problems of size ~100 to isolate the effect of the cross-attention.
- **Missing baselines and comparisons** (R2, R3):
  - NeuroLKH (Xin et al., 2021) is a strong deep learning + heuristic baseline that often beats LKH with lower runtime; it should be included.
  - Recent strong NCO baselines ELG (Gao et al., 2024) and GLOP (Ye et al., 2024) are mentioned in related work but not compared experimentally.
  - No comparison with other self-improvement methods, e.g., Pirnay & Grimm (Self-Improvement for Neural Combinatorial Optimization).
- **Related work placement and organization** (R3): The related work section is in the appendix and lacks subsections; it should be in the main text and better structured.
- **Model size not controlled** (R3): The paper does not compare model sizes (number of parameters); fair comparison requires models of similar capacity, as larger models often yield better results.
- **Limited analysis of cross-attention limitations** (R4): The paper does not discuss weaknesses or bottlenecks of the cross-attention mechanism (e.g., potential information loss due to relying on only first/last node representatives).
- **Insufficient comparison with recent RL-based approaches** (R4): A more detailed comparison with recent reinforcement learning methods for VRPs would strengthen the evaluation. (R2 also asks about augmentation similar to POMO; R4 asks about RL comparison specifically.)
- **Unanswered questions that reflect missing experiments/analyses** (R2, R3, R4):
  - Are the baseline methods retrained on the same problem sizes or used with provided pretrained weights? (R2)
  - Was augmentation (e.g., POMO-style) tested with the cross-attention transformer? (R2)
  - How generalizable is SIT to other NCO architectures? (R2)
  - How does the method scale beyond 100K nodes? What are the bottlenecks? (R4)

**Reviewer disagreement note**: One reviewer (score 5) is significantly more critical, citing unfair complexity analysis and raising doubts about evaluation fairness. Another reviewer (score 8) is very positive, highlighting the novelty of SIT and the strong scaling performance. The other two (scores 6) are moderately positive but raise concerns about missing comparisons and model size control.
