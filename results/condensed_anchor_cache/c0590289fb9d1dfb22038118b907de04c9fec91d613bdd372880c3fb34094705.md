## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents a food recommender system that combines collaborative filtering (EASE and SVD) with BERT-based semantic embeddings and a hand-defined nutritional score to recommend items that are both accurate to user preferences and healthy. Offline evaluation on a filtered COCO Nutrition Database (11.5K foods) and online A/B testing with 22 users were conducted. The four reviewers agree on the relevance of the problem and the clarity of the system description, but diverge in overall assessment: two reviewers gave a score of 1 (strong reject) citing weak novelty, missing details, and insufficient evaluation; two reviewers gave a score of 3 (marginally positive) acknowledging the straightforward solution and potential impact but pointing out the same fundamental gaps.

### Strengths
- The problem of integrating nutritional information with user preferences for healthy food recommendations is important and timely.
- The methodology is straightforward and well described, combining collaborative filtering (EASE/SVD) with BERT-based semantic embeddings and a custom nutritional score. The system architecture is clearly presented (Figure 1).
- Both offline evaluation (RMSE on the dataset) and online A/B testing with a web application were conducted.
- The paper has potential societal significance for promoting healthier eating habits.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and insufficient differentiation from prior work.** The combination of collaborative filtering and BERT embeddings is not new; the paper does not clearly explain how it advances beyond existing approaches (e.g., Pecan 2020, Market2Dish, and other works cited by the reviewers [i–viii]). No quantitative comparisons against these or any baseline systems are provided. More advanced frameworks (multi-objective optimization, reinforcement learning, graph-based models, multi-modal embeddings) are not discussed or compared.
- **Inadequate offline evaluation metrics.** Only RMSE is reported (Table 4: 0.32). Essential ranking metrics (Hit Rate, NDCG, Recall, AUC) are missing. For a health-promoting system, a “healthy metric” to evaluate the nutritional quality of recommendations is also lacking.
- **No ablation study.** The individual contributions of the CF stage, BERT embeddings, and the nutritional score are not isolated or assessed. It is unclear which component drives performance.
- **Missing dataset and implementation details.** The paper omits dataset statistics (number of users, interactions, train/test split ratio, description length distribution, etc.), the value of K in K-means clustering, and any visualization (e.g., t-SNE) of the food clusters. The accuracy of extracting USDA food codes from natural language descriptions is not reported.
- **Weak justification of design choices.** The definition of healthy nutrients (Protein, Dietary Fibre, Vitamin, Potassium) and unhealthy nutrients (Sugar, Sodium, Saturated Fats) in Equations 7–8 is arbitrary; fixed weights ignore variation by age, gender, and overall diet. The choice of EASE and SVD as base recommenders is not justified beyond being well-established. The system does not demonstrate adaptation to user feedback over time.
- **Limited evaluation scope.** The offline evaluation uses only a single dataset, raising concerns about generalizability across different demographics and cuisines. The online A/B test only compares two CF methods (SVD vs. EASE), providing little insight into which recommendations are effective, which fail, and why. The reported RMSE of 0.32 raises questions about practical deployability.
- **Missing user interface and feedback mechanisms.** The paper does not discuss how users can customize dietary priorities beyond the stated flexibility, nor how the system incorporates real-time user feedback to adapt recommendations over time (e.g., changing preferences). UI/UX considerations are not addressed.
