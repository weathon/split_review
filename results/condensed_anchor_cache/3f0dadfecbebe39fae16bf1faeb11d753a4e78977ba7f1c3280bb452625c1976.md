## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates generalization versus memorization in diffusion models, focusing on a class of bias-free CNN denoisers. Empirically, training on CelebA and LSUN bedrooms shows a clear transition from memorization to generalization as training set size increases; two denoisers trained on disjoint partitions learn nearly identical score functions when data is sufficient, implying convergence to a unique continuous density. The authors propose an inductive bias explanation: trained denoisers perform shrinkage in an image-adapted geometry-adaptive harmonic basis (GAHB). They test this on synthetic \(C^\alpha\) images (where GAHB is optimal) and on low-dimensional manifolds (e.g., disks, shuffled CelebA) where GAHB is suboptimal, showing near-optimal denoising in the former case and misaligned bases in the latter. The paper leaves open the formal definition of a larger class of GAHBs and how they arise from DNN architectures.

### Strengths
- The core question (generalization in diffusion models) is important and currently lacks a fully satisfying explanation; the paper offers a compelling empirical study under controlled conditions.
- The paper presents detailed but accessible background on diffusion models, allowing a broad audience to appreciate the results.
- The theoretical framework linking piecewise-linear denoisers to classical shrinkage estimators (Equation 6) is nontrivial and provides a plausible explanation for the observed inductive bias.
- Empirical demonstration of a phase transition between memorization and generalization, with validation on two datasets (CelebA, LSUN) and on image classes with known optimal bases.
- Counter-examples strengthen the hypothesis: when the optimal basis is not GAHB (low-dimensional manifolds, shuffled CelebA), the DNN denoiser performs poorly, confirming the bias is not universal.
- Clean mathematical framework (eigenvalue decay, Jacobian analysis) that grounds the empirical part.
- Clear writing and good overall readability; the hypothesis is presented concisely.

### Weaknesses
- The theoretical framework (Section 3) is specific to bias-free CNNs (BF-CNN) and does not obviously extend to modern denoiser architectures (e.g., PixelNet++) that use attention and positional embeddings; the paper does not discuss how the theory might generalize to these settings.
- The empirical setup is limited: single unconditioned model trained on multiple noise scales, Algorithm 1 sampling, and modest resolutions. It is unclear whether the main insights (Figures 1, 2) hold for noise-conditional diffusion models, higher-resolution photorealistic images, or ODE/SDE sampling procedures.
- Further validation on more realistic, high-resolution data would strengthen the conclusions.
- The paper does not address whether the observed GAHB is a direct consequence of the CNN architecture or the training procedure; it would be valuable to see if other architectures (e.g., transformers) exhibit similar behavior.
- The literature connecting CNNs to learning harmonic bases (e.g., https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12136) is not discussed; the paper would benefit from acknowledging and comparing to prior work on CNN spectral biases.
- The claim that “deep networks are more adapted towards high-dimensional structures [than low-dimensional]” is not fully resolved: in Figure 5, the learned basis contains the optimal low-dimensional tangent space and eigenvalues decay quickly, suggesting a bias toward low-dimensional structure rather than high-dimensional. The authors should clarify this point.
- Section 3.1 is largely based on (Mohan et al., 2020); the paper should state this clearly at the outset and delineate where new contributions begin (e.g., the projection below Eq. 7 needs a citation).
- Presentation issues: the abstract should emphasize the empirical nature and highlight GAHB as future work; figure pointers within the main text are sparse; many figure captions serve as main text (especially in appendices); the narrative would benefit from more supporting text that integrates figures coherently.
- **Note of contrast**: one reviewer (score 1) raised only these minor presentation issues, while the three other reviewers gave scores of 8. This large discrepancy suggests that the low-scoring reviewer may have a fundamental objection not fully expressed in the written review, or that the presentation flaws were considered severe enough to warrant rejection. The indicated weaknesses are retained as presented.
