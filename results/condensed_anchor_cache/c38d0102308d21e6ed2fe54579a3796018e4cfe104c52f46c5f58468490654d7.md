## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces DiffPuter, an iterative method for missing data imputation that combines the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm with diffusion models. Missing values are treated as hidden variables; the M-step learns the joint distribution of observed and currently estimated missing data via unconditional diffusion training, and the E-step re-estimates missing values by conditional sampling. Theoretical analysis shows that diffusion training corresponds to maximum likelihood density estimation (M-step) and sampling corresponds to expected a posteriori estimation (E-step). Experiments on 10 datasets comparing with 16 baselines report average improvements of 8.10% in MAE and 5.64% in RMSE. One reviewer (R1, score 6) is notably more critical, especially regarding evaluation fairness and novelty, while others (R2, R3, R4, score 8) are generally positive but raise several concerns.

### Strengths

- Well-written, clear, and comprehensive, with a thorough introduction and extensive literature review (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Novel combination of EM and diffusion models for imputation, supported by theoretical proof that training (M-step) and conditional sampling (E-step) align with EM objectives (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Extensive empirical evaluation: 10 datasets, 16 baselines, thorough ablation studies (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Addresses key challenges, e.g., conditioning on observed data via an annealing process (R1).

### Weaknesses

- **Evaluation fairness and baseline comparisons:**  
  – R1 and R3 question how hyperparameters were selected for baselines and their sensitivity; R1 states this significantly impacts the assessment.  
  – R3 argues that baselines use simple placeholder values (zero/mean imputation) and only one M-step, making the performance gap unsurprising; expects an ablation comparing different deep generative models (e.g., VAE-based methods [1–5]) within the EM framework.  
  – R4 asks for details on baseline hyperparameter selection and comparability (e.g., number of parameters).  
  – R1 criticizes the exclusive focus on MCAR; MNAR is a primary focus in many imputation methods. R4 also requests description of missing data mechanisms (MAR, MNAR) used.  
  – R1 notes missing evaluation on downstream tasks to demonstrate practical importance.  
  – R4 suggests using imputation scores (refs: [arXiv:2002.03860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03860), [arXiv:2106.03742](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03742)) instead of only RMSE/MAE, as the latter target conditional mean/median and not distributional accuracy; the proposed method would likely perform better on distributional metrics.  
  – R2 and R4 note that computational complexity (time) is not explicitly discussed or compared; R3 notes high cost due to retraining the diffusion model \(K\) times, not addressed in the main text; R4 asks for experiments on larger datasets (more observations/variables) to assess scalability.  
  – R3 reports that MissDiff appears to fail or run out of memory in Figure 2, which is surprising given similar architecture.  
  – R3 questions the claim “Traditional machine learning methods are still powerful imputers” without supporting references.  
  – R3 questions the claim that imputation methods are in-sample only and cannot handle out-of-sample imputation, citing many DGMs that do.  
  – R1’s question about biological missing values confounded with limit-of-detection (often MAR/MNAR) is not addressed.

- **Motivation, novelty, and theoretical clarity:**  
  – R1 states that novelty is not clearly articulated in the related work section.  
  – R3 argues that the motivation (generative methods require joint estimation and struggle with conditional inference) is questionable, as many deep generative models (e.g., VAEs [1–5]) can estimate only the observed data distribution and allow Monte Carlo E-step estimation. R3 questions why diffusion is specifically justified for tabular data given lower dimensionality; VAE-based approaches might perform as well.  
  – R1 and R4 find theoretical aspects needing clarification: R1 notes the relationship between \(\delta_t\) and ML solution approximation; R4 finds the proof of Theorem 1 confusing, with notational issues, many approximations (l.730, 731, 750, 753), and assumptions not fully stated (e.g., \(\sigma(T)\) not assumed large but required, no assumption on \(\varepsilon_\theta\) quality, type of convergence needed). R4 requests a table of notations and exact definitions.  
  – R1: a simulation study with multiple modes would be valuable, as diffusion models should excel there.

- **Methodological issues:**  
  – R1 criticizes the 0/1 continuous encoding of categorical data as unusual and problematic: binary data is a known challenge for diffusion models, and mean imputation ignores multi-modality.  
  – R3 notes that the diffusion model assumes Gaussianity across dimensions, using argmax for discrete outputs is suboptimal for heterogeneous data; references [2, 3, 5] offer better approaches.  
  – R1 notes that initialization is not addressed despite its importance in EM; naive mean initialization is questionable.  
  – R1: Figure 4 lacks clarity.  
  – R3: Figure 6 and the final paragraph of Section 5 are unclear; error decreasing as observed data ratio drops is counterintuitive.  
  – R4: l.197 choice of \(\sigma(t)\) should be specified; l.225–227 paragraph does not match the equation (negative log-likelihood is upper bounded by loss plus a constant, but optimizing loss does not directly optimize NLL).  
  – R4: behavior when different masks are present in training and test set is not explored.  
  – R4: explanation needed for good performance compared to MissDiff and TabCSDI.

- **Limitations and missing discussion:**  
  – R3: main text does not discuss limitations, particularly high computational cost; only a brief note in the appendix without citation in the main text. DiffPuter retrains the diffusion model \(K\) times, making it computationally intensive for high-dimensional data. R3 also asks if the M-step converges faster with higher \(K\).  
  – R1: related work should include other imputation methods like image inpainting.  
  – R2: computational complexity (time) not compared with other diffusion methods.

- **Minor issues and typos:**  
  – R3: line 515: change “, Reducing” to “, reducing”.  
  – R4: l.399 “Imputaing”; l.468 “Gestrue”.
