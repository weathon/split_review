## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a self-explaining multi-view deep learning architecture for physiological signals (ECG/PPG). It uses trainable mask networks to generate multiple “semantic views” that isolate task-relevant regions, aiming to provide interpretability for clinically relevant classification and regression tasks (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, atrial fibrillation detection). The architecture is evaluated on four datasets and compared to post-hoc methods (LIME, SHAP) and state-of-the-art baselines.

### Strengths
- The multi-view segmentation approach is an innovative contribution, adding potential value to explainability in machine learning for healthcare, where interpretability is crucial. (R1, R2)
- The architecture incorporates both model-level (prior constraint on number of semantic views) and sample-level (unique segmentation mask per sample) interpretability, in contrast to post-hoc techniques that can be ambiguous and inconsistent. (R2)
- The paper proposes a unified architecture applicable to both classification and regression tasks, showing adaptability across a variety of physiological signal processing tasks. (R1)
- The mapping of different parts of the input signal to distinct semantic state spaces reveals hidden patterns related to model decisions, enhancing interpretability. (R3)
- Validation on multiple datasets demonstrates that model decisions align with domain knowledge, verifying the effectiveness of the method. (R3)
- The paper is generally easy to read, though clarity could be improved with better diagrams. (R4) [Note: minor strength, included for completeness]

### Weaknesses
- **Number of semantic views not justified**: The paper tests 2, 3, or 4 views but does not explain why these specific numbers are optimal across tasks. No ablation study is performed on the number of masks, making the choice arbitrary and limiting generalizability. (R1, R2, R3, R4)  
  – A related question: Could the network discover the optimal number of semantic states instead of predefining it? (R2)
- **Limited technical innovation**: The method offers no significant technical innovation specifically designed for clinical applications or medical waveforms. The difference from prior general XAI methods is not explained, and no comparison is made with them. (R4)  
  – Reviewer 2 is more positive about originality, stating the idea has significant potential for AI-based medicine and XAI, contrasting with this criticism.
- **Coarse explainability**: The level of explainability is quite coarse; the method relies on qualitative (visual) exploration of learned masks, which is difficult for more complex tasks. For higher numbers of semantic states, visualizations become unrecognizable, and the paper offers no alternative interpretation methods beyond simple visual inspection. (R2, R3)  
  – Learned views do not necessarily provide unique insights; e.g., for AF detection, P-wave presence/absence may be more indicative than QRS peaks. (R2)
- **Evaluation metrics**: Accuracy is used for classification tasks, but it is susceptible to class imbalance. AUC or F-score metrics should be reported. For Table 1, was any threshold tuning performed? (R2, R3)  
  – Reviewer 2 specifically asks for AUC metrics.
- **Lack of comparison with established post-hoc methods**: The paper does not compare with SHAP or LIME (or sample-level clustering techniques). Such comparisons would clarify the relative benefits of the proposed model. (R1, R2)  
  – Reviewer 1 notes the paper claims outperformance over LIME and SHAP but does not include those comparisons.
- **Performance not consistently superior**: The proposed model fails to outperform current state-of-the-art on 3 out of 4 datasets, and the ablation study (Table 1) shows only marginal improvement from the multi-view architecture. (R1)  
  – Reviewer 4 adds: SOTA methods cited are old in general.
- **Selection bias in visualization**: The results in Section 5.2 show only correctly-classified examples, indicating strong selection bias. (R4)
- **No user study with clinicians**: Claims of human-interpretable features are not validated by any study with domain experts. The embedding and decision networks themselves remain black-box, limiting transparency. (R1, R4)  
  – Reviewer 1 calls for quantitative evaluation of interpretability with experts.
- **Data diversity and task representativeness**:  
  – The dataset is limited to single-modality signals (ECG or PPG exclusively), not mixed types. The method’s effectiveness with multiple physiological signal inputs or inherent differences between ECG and PPG is not addressed. (R3)  
  – All selected tasks are conditions related to peak-to-peak interval variability, which is a limited scope. The claim of “universal usability” based on 4 tasks and 2 waveforms is overstated. (R2, R4)
- **Temporal sample-level assignment**:  
  – The assumption that each sample (time point) belongs to only one semantic state is questionable; specific parts of a signal could reflect several latent states. (R2)  
  – Data from a single time point may not capture sufficient semantic information, especially at high sampling frequencies; assignment at the sample level may not be physiologically meaningful (events span multiple points). (R3)
- **Softmax leakage in masks**: Equation (4) applies softmax activation at each time step, allowing information to leak into all masks with varying amplitudes, deviating from the intended binary definition. It is unclear whether information is amplified or suppressed by subsequent embedding networks, undermining the attribution of explainability to high-amplitude parts. (R2)
- **Architectural decisions not ablated**:  
  – The paper does not ablate the need for weight sharing in embedding networks given that masks provide interpretability. (R2)  
  – The role of the differential embedding vector is unclear; is there empirical evidence that the decision network cannot exploit such relationships without it? (R2)  
  – The ablation is only limited to the number of views; other design choices (e.g., number of mask networks, shared embedding) are not justified. (R1, R4)
- **Comparison fairness**: The claim about effectiveness of mask networks (Line 337) may not be fair because removing the mask network reduces the number of parameters, leading to an imbalanced comparison. (R2)
- **Visualization clarity**: Heart rate variation in Figure 3 is not prominent; smaller windows or wider x-axes are needed. (R2)
- **Missing implementation details**:  
  – In Appendix, the minimum duration L is enforced but the selection of L for each task is not specified. (R2)  
  – The number of semantic states chosen per task is not justified; a higher number (e.g., >4) is not investigated. (R3)  
  – For classification tasks, threshold tuning (if any) should be reported. (R2)
- **Unnecessary equations**: Some equations on Page 5 are unnecessary, and notation can be simplified. (R4)
- **Figure 2**: The diagram is too brief (only half page width); sub-figures to illustrate ideas are needed. (R4)
- **Related questions that overlap with weaknesses** (from reviewer questions):  
  – How does the model perform on sparse or noisy labels? (R1)  
  – How do semantic views compare to post-hoc clustering techniques at the sample level (e.g., Boubekki et al., 2024)? (R2)  
  – Is highlighting relevant regions sufficient for transparency? How does it relate to clinical decision making? What assumptions are made about clinician interaction? (R4)  
  – Why is 4-view worse than 3-view? (R4)  
  – How does xAI “ensure” reliability without causal inference study? (R4)
- **Minor issues**:  
  – Abstract contains overly detailed methods that could be omitted. (R2)  
  – In-text citations should be generated with brackets. (R2)  
  – Naming conventions in Table 1 (e.g., “SOTA”) should be replaced with more descriptive labels. (R1)
