## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces the Wasserstein-Descent $\dot{\mathbb{H}}^1$-Ascent (WDHA) algorithm for computing the unregularized Wasserstein (optimal transport) barycenter of discrete probability distributions on a grid. The key idea is to reformulate the barycenter problem as a nonconvex-concave minimax optimization problem by using the dual Kantorovich formulation. The algorithm alternates between a Wasserstein gradient descent step on the primal barycenter measure and a $\dot{\mathbb{H}}^1$ (Sobolev) gradient ascent step on the dual Kantorovich potentials. The authors claim nearly linear time $O(m\log m)$ and linear space $O(m)$ complexity, in contrast to the $O(m^3)$ complexity of naive approaches. Under assumptions of lower-bounded densities and bounded potentials, they prove convergence to a stationary point at rate $O(1/T)$. Experiments on 2D synthetic data and binarized MNIST images (up to $1024\times1024$) show favorable runtime and accuracy compared to entropic-regularized baselines (CWB and DSB). The reviews are mixed: one reviewer (score 8) is very positive, while others (scores 5, 6, 6, 6) express significant concerns about the gap between theory and practice, implementation clarity, and the conclusiveness of the experiments.

### Strengths

- **Novel and natural idea**: The formulation of the unregularized barycenter as a nonconvex-concave minimax problem and the combination of Wasserstein descent and $H^1$ ascent is interesting and original (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer explicitly highlights that the paper extends the back-and-forth OT algorithm (Jacobs & Léger) in a meaningful way (R5).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well organized, notation is properly defined, and the gradients are carefully justified (R1, R3, R5). The algorithm description is easy to follow (R3).
- **Theoretical guarantees**: Under assumptions, the paper provides a convergence rate $O(1/T)$ to a stationary point and matches rates known for Euclidean nonconvex-concave optimization (R4). The analysis is logically connected (R2).
- **Computational efficiency**: The WDHA algorithm achieves nearly linear time and linear space complexity, outperforming the $O(m^3)$ naive approach and existing regularized methods in runtime (R2, R4, R5). Experiments confirm this on high-resolution grids (R1).
- **Convincing experiments (according to some reviewers)**: The experiments on synthetic data and handwritten digits demonstrate good quality of the barycenter (R1, R3). The authors honestly credit inspirations from prior works (R1).
- **First-order method**: The algorithm is simple and quickly converges to a stationary point in practice (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Theory-practice gap**: The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, Algorithm 3) assumes exact projection onto $\mathbb{F}_{\alpha,\beta}$, but the practical implementation (Algorithm 4) replaces this projection with computing the second convex conjugate $(\phi)^{**}$, which violates Lemma 1. Therefore, the convergence guarantees of Theorem 1 do not directly apply to the algorithm actually used in experiments (R2, R4, R5). One reviewer notes that no experiments with the original Algorithm 3 are provided (R5).
- **Convergence only to stationary point, not to true barycenter**: By reformulating the problem as nonconvex, the algorithm loses the guarantee of converging to the exact barycenter; this fact is not sufficiently discussed or acknowledged (R1). The theoretical assumptions (e.g., lower-bounded densities, uniform boundedness of $\nu^t$) may not hold in practice, further weakening practical convergence guarantees (R4).
- **Lack of implementation details**: The methodology is unclear regarding crucial numerical aspects. Specifically: how are terms like $\nabla\phi$ computed (finite differences? FFT? with/without smoothing)? How is the pushforward $(\nabla\phi)_\#\nu$ implemented when it may exceed the grid? How are the convex conjugate and inverse Laplacian efficiently implemented for grid data? These details are essential for reproducibility and assessment (R5). The paper only references Jacobs & Léger without sufficient self-contained explanation (R5).
- **Inconclusive experiments and lack of ground-truth validation**: The reported barycenter functional values are very close across methods, and it is not clear if the differences are statistically significant (no standard deviations reported) (R3, R4). The comparison is limited to two regularized baselines (CWB, DSB) and omits other unregularized approaches (e.g., Zemel & Panaretos 2019, Chewi et al. 2020) as well as recently proposed scalable regularized methods (e.g., using input convex neural networks) (R2, R3). There is no experiment with known ground-truth barycenters (e.g., Gaussian measures) to verify that the algorithm actually recovers the true solution (R2, R3). The DSB baseline appears competitive in the figures (R4, R5).
- **Hyperparameter selection and regularization not discussed**: The choice of step sizes for ascent/descent and the parameters $\alpha,\beta$ defining the regularity set $\mathbb{F}_{\alpha,\beta}$ are not justified or guided; no sensitivity analysis is provided (R3, R4). The impact of the $\mathbb{F}_{\alpha,\beta}$ constraint on barycenter accuracy, convergence, and computational cost is not discussed (R4).
- **Scalability limited to low dimensions**: The method is presented for 2D grids only (images). The computational cost of the convex conjugate and the inverse Laplacian may become prohibitive in higher dimensions, a limitation that is not stated or discussed (R3, R5). In contrast, Sinkhorn-based methods can work with empirical distributions in arbitrary dimensions (R5).
- **Questionable comparison setup for Sinkhorn**: In Figure 1, the WDHA and Sinkhorn baselines are compared on a $1024\times1024$ grid while the input densities occupy a small region; it is not clear if the Sinkhorn baselines use the full grid (which would be unfair) or a smaller one; the paper does not disclose this (R5). The comparison may be affected by numerical optimization in the POT package (R5).
- **Minor issues**: Typos: line 132 “descent” should be “ascent”; line 269 “min” should be “max”; line 132 “dicuss”; “Sythentic” (R1, R2, R3). The derivation of the Wasserstein gradient in lines 290-291 needs more explanation (R2). The claim that Wasserstein gradient outperforms $L^2$ gradient is not experimentally justified (R2, R4).
