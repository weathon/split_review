## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SANER, a debiasing method for CLIP that eliminates societal bias via attribute neutralization (replacing protected attributes with neutral terms) and an annotation-free debiasing loss, with two reconstruction losses to preserve attribute information. All reviewers agree that the problem is well-motivated and the method shows good debiasing performance on text-to-image retrieval and generation tasks. Reviewers 1 and 4 (scores 8) are particularly positive about the clarity, practical impact (drag-and-drop replacement), and experimental setup; Reviewers 2 and 3 (scores 6) are more critical, questioning technical novelty, reliance on comprehensive attribute lists, feasibility of neutralization in non-binary contexts, contradictions in the regularization losses, and missing evaluations.

### Strengths
- **Clear problem identification and analysis of existing methods.** All reviewers note that the two challenges (loss of attribute information during debiasing and dependence on attribute annotations) are sound and interesting. R1 states the analysis “is very clearly presented that it could be used for a tutorial.” R4 agrees that Section 2.1 does a good job summarizing prior work.
- **High practical impact via drag-and-drop replacement (Section 5 experiment).** R4 finds this “astounding”: the debiased CLIP can directly replace the original CLIP in existing text-to-image models without retraining the generation model, and without retraining CLIP from scratch. R1 also highlights the immediate practical application with Stable Diffusion.
- **Lightweight training.** R3 and R4 note that only the debiasing layer is trained, making the approach efficient.
- **Good debiasing performance.** R2 reports “good performance” and R1 finds the metrics adequate (e.g., measuring difference from a uniform distribution).
- **Experiments on multiple downstream tasks.** Evaluations on text-to-image retrieval and text-to-image generation are appreciated by R1, R2, and R3.
- **Clear writing and helpful figures.** R1 and R4 describe the paper as well-written and easy to follow; R1 notes footnotes and figures aid understanding.
- **General agreement on problem importance.** All reviewers recognize societal bias in VLMs as a highly prominent issue.

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on a fixed list of protected attributes.** SANER requires a comprehensive, hand-crafted attribute list (Appx. C). R1 notes the list may be debatable and miss attributes (e.g., “pregnant”). R3 argues that in real practice, especially for non-binary gender, it is “very hard or nearly impossible to have a complete list.” R4 similarly wishes for a more automated, less hardcoded neutralization method for general-purpose debiasing.
- **Concerns about the attribute-specific description set.** R3 points out that creating such a set (e.g., iterating all possible words and groups) can lead to overlarge sets and increased computation, or, if only one word per group is selected, matching words across groups may affect debiasing performance. R3 also questions whether generated descriptions may contain mismatches (e.g., “A woman/boy is eating salad”) or grammar errors (e.g., “A he/she is eating salad” per Appx. C).
- **Apparent contradiction in the regularization losses.** R3 raises a critical issue: after neutralization and feature adjustment, the output feature should not contain attribute-specific information, yet the regularization losses try to map this neutralized feature back to attribute-specific text or visual features. R3 asks whether the “retained attribute information” could actually be biased information from the dataset implicitly polluted by the regularization loss.
- **Missing experimental comparisons and baselines.** R1 notes limited baselines, though understandable due to lack of public implementations. R3 similarly states “only a few SOTA are used...makes the comparison weak.”
- **Missing training and computational details.** R1 points out no GPU information, training cost, or difficulty of retraining CLIP without losing semantics is provided.
- **Lack of generation quality metrics (e.g., FID) for text-to-image generation.** R3 notes only debiasing numerical results and visualizations are given; no FID or other quality measure.
- **Limited applicability to other VLMs.** R2 asks whether the method only works for CLIP and suggests testing other vision-language models to demonstrate broader effectiveness.
- **Perceived lack of major technical contribution.** R2 states that most components appear to be existing techniques or tricks, so “this paper may not have significant influence or provide broader insights beyond the CLIP-debiasing task.”
- **Missing ablation verification for loss of attribute information.** R2 requests ARL results to verify loss of attribute information and asks whether other methods (Mapper, Prompt tuning) also suffer from this loss.
- **Unexpected negative effect on FairFace from contrastive losses (Table 7).** R2 asks why adding only contrastive losses significantly hurts FairFace performance; no explanation is provided.
- **Unclear handling of non-binary gender and terms like “pregnant.”** R1 questions whether the method uses general pronouns (they/them) and whether replacing terms like “pregnant” (which has no male counterpart) removes important information.
- **Minor issues.** R1 identifies a typo in Table 2 caption (“recial bias” instead of “racial bias”) and a wording error (“multilayer perception” should be “multilayer perceptron”). R1 also notes that Figure 2’s caption could be more self-contained (e.g., include a reminder of loss terms).
- **Suggested additional experiments to strengthen impact.** R4 recommends evaluating on the FACET dataset and testing replacement in LLaVA-like models for text generation, noting these could be future work.
