## Merged Review

### Summary

This work targets systematic generalization in relational reasoning, especially for problems requiring multi-path disjunctive reasoning (e.g., Region Connected Calculus, Interval Algebra). The authors propose Epistemic Graph Neural Networks (EpiGNNs), which extend Neural Bellman-Ford Networks (NBFNet) by representing node embeddings as epistemic states (distributions over primitive relations), redesigning message passing (Tucker decomposition, L1 normalization, joint training of forward/backward models), and linking to algebraic closure theory. Two new synthetic benchmarks (RCC-8, IA) are introduced to evaluate disjunctive relational reasoning. Empirically, EpiGNN achieves state-of-the-art on the new benchmarks, competitive performance on systematic generalization (CLUTRR, GraphLog) and inductive knowledge graph completion (GraIL, WN18RR, FB15k-237), with claimed efficiency gains.

### Strengths

- **Focus on a critical problem**: Longer logical inference with multi-path disjunctive reasoning is a challenging and underexplored area in knowledge graph inference (R2).
- **Theoretical connection to algebraic closure**: EpiGNNs are theoretically aligned with algebraic closure, enabling learning of disjunctive rules; this link is novel and supports expressiveness (R3, R4). A connection to the relational WL test (Huang et al.) would further strengthen this (R4).
- **Two new benchmarks (RCC-8, IA)**: Designed for multi-path disjunctive reasoning, these datasets fill a gap in evaluating systematic generalization (R1, R2, R3, R4). However, R4 notes they appear to saturate quickly (EpiGNN achieves 90+% accuracy).
- **Scalability and efficiency**: EpiGNN is more parameter-efficient than neuro-symbolic baselines (R1, R2, R3), with efficiency illustrated in Figure 5 (R1) and a scalable framework suitable for large graphs (R2).
- **Competitive empirical performance**: On the new benchmarks, EpiGNN surpasses all compared methods, including neuro-symbolic approaches (R3). It also achieves competitive results on systematic generalization and inductive KG completion (R1, R3).
- **Clarity and well-written**: The paper is well-organized and easy to follow (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Incremental over NBFNet**: EpiGNN is a variant of NBFNet with several engineering modifications (distributional embeddings, Tucker message function, L1 normalization, joint training, forward-backward aggregation). The authors lack a systematic ablation to demonstrate how each modification contributes to systematic generalization (R3). No theoretical justification is provided for why these changes make EpiGNN more powerful than NBFNet (R2, Q8). R2 also notes that design alternatives (e.g., merging relations at each layer rather than only at ends) are not explored or justified.
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**:
  - *Edge Transformer* (Bergen et al., NeurIPS 2021) is a strong baseline for systematic generalization that aggregates multiple relational paths and handles arbitrary reduction orders; it is not included (R3). Note that R4 reports Edge Transformers perform as well as EpiGNN on the new benchmarks.
  - A stronger NBFNet baseline with shared parameters across layers and dropout (similar to EdgeTransformer) should be tested to isolate the effect of the epistemic design (R3).
  - Neuro-symbolic methods NCRL and R5 (marked as single-path) achieve the best performance on the two proposed benchmarks (Table 1, 2), contradicting the claim that they cannot handle multi-path reasoning. This suggests the benchmarks may not be the best fit for testing multi-path ability (R2, Q10). The claim that the problem is unsolvable for existing methods is not strongly supported by the results (R2).
  - Runtime or memory cost comparison with NCRL and R5 is missing from the main content, despite the scalability claim (R2). A real runtime (training and inference) comparison is needed (R2, Q11).
  - On inductive KG completion (Table 4), EpiGNN underperforms some baselines (e.g., on FB15k-237) and is not as close to state-of-the-art as claimed; confidence intervals are needed (R2). More critically, the evaluation uses ranking against only 50 random negatives, which overestimates performance; full ranking against all entities (as done by NBFNet, A*Net, RED-GNN, AdaProp) is necessary (R4).
  - The benchmarks should be more challenging; RCC-8 and IA already saturate with models delivering 80+% on hardest cases (R4).
- **Empirical evidence not fully conclusive**:
  - On CLUTRR, the dataset is already saturated and provides little discriminative signal (R4).
  - On GraphLog, EpiGNN underperforms other baselines (R4).
  - NBFNet is worse than simple GCN/GAT/RGCN in several cases on the new benchmarks (Table 1), raising questions about its suitability for logical reasoning (R2, Q9). Additionally, the test sets only include pairs where a relation must exist; negative samples (unrelated node pairs) are missing, which is a risk for real-world application (R2, Q9).
  - The pairwise disjoint relation assumption (Line 269-271) only holds for CLUTRR and Graphlog, not for real-world knowledge graphs (R2, R3). This limits the generality of the approach.
- **Limited applicability to entity prediction**: The method is demonstrated only for relation prediction, which is simpler due to a limited number of relations. Whether it generalizes to entity prediction (e.g., in temporal KGs) is unclear (R1). Representations as distributions over relations are non-transferable across datasets (different relation vocabularies) and ignore semantic information of entities (R1).
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Notations in Section 3 are hard to follow: what does *f* represent in Eq. 4? The variable a_ij is not clearly defined or illustrated in Figure 3 (R1).
  - R2’s Q2, Q4, Q7 raise specific concerns about initialization, tensor notation, and merging strategy that suggest unclear exposition.
  - Missing references for the concept of “epistemic states” if not introduced in this paper (R3).
  - Typo on line 100: “single-path base” should be “single-path-based” (R3).
- **Additional questions and concerns**:
  - Proposition 1 may need to account for cases where the count of a relational path matters (R3, Q).
  - The statement on line 154-155 is not true for representation learning methods like NBFNet/EpiGNN if reduction satisfies associativity (R3, Q).
  - Joint training of multiple models at the loss function (multi-facet) is independent; a pooling at each layer (like multi-head attention) might be more effective (R2, Q6).
  - R2, Q8: Beyond the final bidirectional aggregation, what unique design element makes EpiGNN more capable than NBFNet?
