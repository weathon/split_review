## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces BEND, a benchmark for DNA language models (LMs) that focuses on biologically meaningful downstream tasks defined on the human genome, including long-range tasks like enhancer-promoter interaction prediction. It evaluates several existing DNA LMs and simple supervised baselines. Reviewers found the benchmark well described, complementary to existing collections, and covering important, realistic tasks. However, concerns were raised about task formulation depth, baseline strength, generalizability beyond one species, and the novelty of the benchmark relative to recent work (Gresova 2023). One reviewer (rating 3) questioned the fit to ICLR, while others rated the contribution as fair (scores 2,2,2) to good (score 3). The minority reviewer (rating 3) was more critical of the paper’s scope.

### Strengths

- Clear and concise biological background (Reviewer 2).
- Well-described, diverse tasks covering important genomic phenomena (Reviewers 2, 3). Tasks are more realistic and challenging than those in some previous benchmarks (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Comprehensive comparison of multiple existing DNA LMs, including a comparison to a simple trained-from-scratch supervised model (Basset) (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Commendable effort in collecting the benchmark dataset and running many models (Reviewer 1).
- Benchmark datasets complement existing ones, notably by including long-range tasks such as enhancer-promoter interaction prediction (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Provides an interesting performance comparison across 5–6 prediction tasks relevant to modern biology (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Task formulation and depth**: The long-sequence task formulation lacks depth and is not entirely persuasive (Reviewer 1). The enhancer prediction task uses a fixed 128bp window – but enhancers are typically 225–250bp at minimum – and is framed as annotation rather than matching, which does not truly test long-range interaction detection (Reviewer 2). Real-world enhancer prediction relies heavily on multi-omic data (e.g., EPCOT); the sequence-only approach is a significant limitation (Reviewer 3). The gene-finding task is contrived and requires post-processing; how alternative splicing and overlapping gene models are handled is unclear (Reviewer 3). Variant effect prediction is treated as binary classification; a regression formulation may be more appropriate (Reviewer 1). Histone modification prediction might benefit from longer sequence contexts (Reviewer 3). Splice site classification is relatively easy and does not require long sequences; the condition-dependent version is a separate problem (Reviewer 3).
- **Baselines and comparisons**: Supervised baselines are weak – e.g., Sei achieves much higher accuracy on histone modifications; a more competitive supervised baseline should be included even if not on the exact same data (Reviewer 3). A simple CNN baseline is missing for the variant effect prediction task (Reviewer 1). A side-by-side comparison with a DNA LM trained from scratch is essential to understand pretraining’s potential and limitations (Reviewer 1). The paper does not do fine-tuning for each task; it is unclear whether a fully unsupervised approach is practical (Reviewer 2). Literature accuracy numbers in Table 3 come from potentially very different datasets and should be taken with a grain of salt (Reviewer 3).
- **Data and methodology**: Only human genome tasks are included; a good DNA LM should cover multiple species. The competing Gresova 2023 benchmark includes eight tasks from three species, and BEND does not improve much over it (Reviewer 2). Dataset splitting methodology is unclear – why include data from the pretraining phase? A leave-chromosome-out split should be considered and data leakage prevention explained (Reviewer 1). Downstream models should be evaluated with a leave-chromosome-out strategy for robustness (Reviewer 1). The enhancer annotation ground truth is uncertain because the ABC method is based on inference, not direct experiment (Reviewer 1). Figure 1 inaccurately represents genomic feature lengths (exons average 120–170bp, promoters 100–1000bp) and should show alternating exon-intron structure (Reviewer 1). The biological background should specify that descriptions refer to eukaryotic genomes (Reviewer 2). “Cis” and “trans” are not defined by distance (Reviewer 3). The claim that labeled data is limited contradicts the success of models like Sei and Enformer that leverage large-scale labeled data (Reviewer 3). A reference is missing in Section 3.3 (Reviewer 3). The abbreviation “SS” should be introduced after “secondary structure” (Reviewer 2).
- **Scope and fit**: The paper contains substantial background and is a benchmarking study of methods available only as preprint papers; its fit to ICLR is unclear (Reviewer 4). Biological reality is more complex (e.g., multiple start sites), and richer experimental datasets are needed; the annotated data used may not be sufficient (Reviewer 4). One reviewer questioned whether reasoning over very long contexts (e.g., for enhancers) is too much to expect from current DNA LMs; perhaps they should only capture short contexts and leave long-range reasoning to downstream models (Reviewer 3).
