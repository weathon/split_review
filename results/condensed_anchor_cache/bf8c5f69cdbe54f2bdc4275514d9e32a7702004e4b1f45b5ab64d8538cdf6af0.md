## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores design choices for Speech-Conditioned Large Language Models (SLMs) within an encoder–adaptor–LLM framework (resembling SALMONN/Qwen-Audio). Through controlled experiments on ASR (LibriSpeech test-clean) it evaluates adaptor architectures, training modules, and data mixing, then uses synthetic spoken instruction-following data (50K samples) to improve the model’s ability to respond to speech queries. The resulting model, SiM, shows gains over existing SLMs on OpenHermes and Alpaca benchmarks. Reviewers note the systematic comparison as a reference, but raise substantial concerns about narrow scope, limited novelty, insufficient evaluation, and missing details.

### Strengths
- The paper constructs a synthetic spoken instruction-following dataset (50K samples) and demonstrates that adding this in-domain data significantly improves spoken instruction-following capability, leading to SiM outperforming existing SLMs on OpenHermes and Alpaca benchmarks.
- A thorough exploration of fine‑grain SLM design choices (adaptor architecture, trainable modules, encoder masking, training data) under comparable setups is presented, potentially serving as a practical reference for the community.
- The finding that current open‑source SLMs struggle with spoken instruction following and that this gap can be addressed with synthetic instruction data is a useful empirical observation.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited scope of design space and tasks**  
   - The exploration is restricted to SALMONN/Qwen‑Audio‑style encoder–adaptor–LLM architectures and does not cover other high‑level designs (e.g., AudioPaLM, Moshi, AudioFlamingo, BESTOW, or SLMs based on discrete speech units) [R3, R4].  
   - The alignment training experiments evaluate only ASR (WER on LibriSpeech test‑clean). It remains unexamined whether the design choices that yield lowest WER also benefit other tasks (e.g., speech instruction following, translation, or robust speech understanding) [R2, R3, R4].  
   - The work focuses only on ASR and instruction following, narrowing the general applicability of its findings [R2].

2. **Lack of novelty**  
   - The final SiM architecture closely follows mainstream MLLM architectures such as LLaVA and SLAM‑ASR [R1].  
   - The study of adaptor architecture and trainable modules is very similar to prior work (e.g., IEEE paper 10445874) [R3].  
   - Using synthetic spoken instruction data to enhance instruction following has already been proposed (e.g., SpeechGPT using Chain‑of‑Modality; also arXiv:2310.13289, arXiv:2309.09843, arXiv:2406.12946) [R1, R3].  
   - The main improvement comes from adding in‑domain data rather than any novel methodology; the paper does not demonstrate that existing models cannot match SiM with similar data tuning [R4].  
   - The ASR component itself is not novel, as prior work (Ma et al., 2024) has already benchmarked various LLM+speech‑encoder combinations for ASR [R2].

3. **Inadequate and potentially biased evaluation**  
   - All alignment experiments in Section 3.1 are evaluated solely on LibriSpeech test‑clean (clean audiobook data). When mixing CommonVoice and LibriSpeech training data, the evaluation remains on LibriSpeech test‑clean, creating a domain mismatch that may bias conclusions [R1, R2]. Adding LibriSpeech test‑other and CommonVoice test sets is recommended [R1].  
   - Speech instruction‑following ability is evaluated only on OpenHermes and Alpaca (text‑based instruction datasets converted to speech). Public benchmarks such as AIR‑bench, Dynamic Superb, or SD‑Eval are not used [R3].  
   - Most baselines compared are designed for *text* instruction SLMs, making the comparison unfair on speech instruction testsets; various text prompt forms should be examined to verify that other SLMs are genuinely worse [R3, R4].  
   - No ASR comparison against other existing SLM models is provided [R2].

4. **Missing controls, ablations, and details**  
   - In investigating adaptor architecture, the audio token reduction rate (ATR) is not controlled across experiments. With ATR > 1, downsampling likely causes information loss, clouding the conclusions [R2].  
   - Experimental comparisons are limited: only Llama3 8B Instruct and a simple masking strategy are used, and only the Whisper encoder is considered. A broader set of LLMs and speech encoders is needed to draw generalizable insights [R2].  
   - The paper does not explain why training “Adaptor+Whisper” yields much higher WER than “Adaptor+LLM” or “Adaptor alone” [R1].  
   - The source of textual data used to synthesize the spoken instruction dataset is not disclosed; examples should be provided (the dataset is not open‑sourced) [R1, R4].  
   - Training details (hyperparameters, compute infrastructure) are insufficient for reproducibility [R4].  
   - The paper has proof‑reading issues: Appendix is missing (mentioned in Section 3.1), and Table 5 caption says “More details of these datasets are presented in Section TBD” [R4].  
   - The rationale for including multilingual ASR samples is unclear because all benchmarking datasets are English [R2].  
   - SiM uses less than one‑third the ASR samples of Qwen but its WER is still 1 point absolute higher than Whisper; the trade‑off and potential for additional tuning are not discussed [R2].  
   - The title is inconsistent between the PDF and the submission system [R2].
