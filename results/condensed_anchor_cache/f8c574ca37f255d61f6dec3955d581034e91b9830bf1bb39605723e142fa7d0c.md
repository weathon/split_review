## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers evaluated the paper. One reviewer (score 5) was positive, praising the unified framework that splits GSL into four components and the over 30,000 comparisons that yield many insights. Three reviewers (score 3 each) were more critical, noting that the work lacks novelty, uses small datasets, and provides insights without rigorous proof. All reviewers agree the paper offers a useful systematic comparison of graph structure learning methods but has significant limitations in scope, dataset diversity, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- The paper devises a unified GSL framework by splitting it into four components, allowing seamless substitution of different components (R3).
- Conducted over 30,000 comparisons on each dataset and offered numerous valuable insights (R3). Over 4,000 architectural designs were tested (R1).
- The systematic study compares different types of GSL methods on multiple benchmark graphs (R2). Several insights are potentially useful for selecting GSL models (R2).
- The unified framework and experimental comparison are interesting and provide a review of the field (R4). Some ideas are helpful and encourage further thinking about the topic (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset scope:** All datasets are relatively small (e.g., ≤10k nodes). Only node classification tasks are considered; graph-level tasks are missing. Three datasets with graphs and three without are insufficient. Datasets reflect mostly homophily, but real graphs often mix homophily and heterophily (R1, R2, R3).
- **Limited base models:** Only GCN and GIN are used as base models; more types of GNNs should be compared to support general insights (R2). The tested methods are limited in scope (R1).
- **Missing original graph structures:** Experiments only use node features; original graph structures are not leveraged. This limits the benchmark, especially for methods designed for graph refinement (e.g., IDGL, GRCN, GCN-LPA). (R3)
- **Novelty concerns:** Dividing the GSL training process is considered trivial by some reviewers. The paper reads more like a review than a novel contribution, and many insights lack serious proof (R1, R4).
- **Framework coverage and missing components:** It is unclear if UGSL can faithfully represent all methods in Table 15. Key designs such as LDS’s bilevel programming and IDGL’s iteration mechanism may not be adequately captured. Common postprocessing techniques (e.g., residual connections, used in IDGL, GRCN, and others) are absent (R3).
- **Experimental rigor and reproducibility:** Variance (standard deviation) is not reported for many results, making statistical significance unclear. Tables 7, 8, 10 should use average rankings instead of average accuracy because accuracy varies across datasets. Data splits are not provided, hindering reproducibility. Results supporting Insight 3, 4, 5 are missing (R3).
- **Clarity and presentation:** The paper is hard to read, especially the experimental sections. Visual aids (charts, diagrams) would help highlight differences across tests (R1).
- **Limited insights:** Insights rely on a narrow set of methods and datasets; conclusions may not generalize to other GNNs, datasets, or downstream tasks. More discussion on connections between learned structures and downstream tasks is needed (R2).
- **Missing analysis:** Time complexity and memory consumption of different components should be compared (R3). The preference of certain components for different dataset characteristics could be explored (R3).
- **Unsupported claims:** Insight 16 (“GIN is better than GCN for self-loop information”) lacks justification or citation (R1).
