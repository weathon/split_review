## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a plug-and-play framework (AWP) that adds an auxiliary hinge loss to CTC training to prioritize alignments according to a desired property (e.g., latency or word error rate). It samples alignments from a pre-trained model, uses a rule-based property function to generate a better alignment, and increases its probability. Experiments on ASR datasets up to 280k hours show latency improvements (up to 590ms reduction) with minor WER degradation, and a relative 4.5% WER improvement over the MLE baseline for min-WER training. The method is simple, requires only a few lines of code, and does not modify the CTC loss itself. Reviewers agree on the novelty and clarity but disagree on the strength of the min-WER results and the experimental setup for latency.

### Strengths
- The framework is simple, flexible, and plug-and-play; it can be implemented with few lines of code without modifying the CTC loss (R1, R2, R4).
- The idea of reweighting alignments by a separate loss to control properties (latency, WER) is novel and clearly different from prior works (R3, R4).
- For latency optimization, AWP achieves better latency-quality trade-offs than several prior methods (e.g., Peak-First CTC, Lattice-Free MMI) and is on par with TrimTail; experiments are conducted across multiple data scales (up to 280k hours), model architectures (Transformer, Conformer, ResNet), and with consistent comparison setups (Table 2) (R1, R2, R4).
- The paper is well-written and easy to understand, especially the introduction and related work sections (R3, R4).
- Extensive ablation studies and comparative experiments under the same conditions strengthen the latency claims (R4).
- Reviewer 3 notes no obvious weakness and finds the design nice and the verification solid (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Min-WER improvements are marginal and lack strong baselines**: The 4.5% relative WER improvement is modest; standard deviation of 0.03 on LibriSpeech test-clean makes the gain unconvincing (R2). The method is only compared to the MLE baseline, not to stronger discriminative sequence training approaches such as MBR training or large-margin training (R1). Greedy decoding results are not reported, making it unclear if the gain comes from acoustic model improvements or LM interaction (R2). After rebuttal, one reviewer (R2) still finds the min-WER results not supportive, while another (R4) notes the gain is not as substantial as prior WER-optimization works.
- **Sampling vs. n-best beam search**: For the min-WER application, the paper uses random sampling of alignments, but prior work (Prabhavalkar et al., 2018) found that using n-best beam search hypotheses is more effective. A comparison is missing (R1). Similarly, optimizing top-N alignments could be more efficient than sampling low-probability ones (R2). After rebuttal, Reviewer 2 accepted an ablation showing beam search did not help, but the question remains interesting.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity, especially “start epoch”**: The start epoch for adding the auxiliary loss is a tunable hyperparameter that varies across models and datasets; its transferability to different schedules, architectures, and data is unclear (R1, R2, R3). There is no robustness analysis or clear guideline for setting it (R2).
- **Latency experimental setup has comparability issues**: Table 2 reports models with different latencies and WERs, making direct comparison hard. A fairer comparison would fix latency and compare WER, or vice versa (R2). The latency optimization experiment on an offline Conformer (full context) is not meaningful, as offline models are not used in streaming; the comparison should be done with an online Conformer (R1). More comparisons (e.g., Conformer-Online + AWP with prior methods) would strengthen the latency claims (R4).
- **Property function design is limited and not ablated**: The latency function shifts only one token, and the min-WER function allows only a single word error reduction. It is unclear whether these choices are necessary for training stability or could be relaxed (e.g., shifting multiple tokens, correcting multiple word errors). No ablation compares different property function strengths (R1, R2, R4). Joint optimization of latency and WER in a single property function was also not explored (R1).
- **Missing details and potential confounds**: It is unclear whether language models are used during the reported results (R2). The feature extraction uses a non-standard 32ms window with 16ms stride; if applied inconsistently across models and baselines, this could confound comparisons (R2). The metric “WSR” in Figures 6 and 7 is not defined (R1, R2, R4). The multilingual wav2vec 2.0 model used in some experiments may introduce additional confounds (R2).
- **Training time and resource impact not discussed**: The need to sample N alignments per training step and compute the property function could increase training time; this is not addressed (R4).
- **Writing issues**: The text contains many typos, inconsistent citation styles, missing punctuation, and ambiguous phrasing; proofreading is needed (R2).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: While Reviewer 3 finds no obvious weakness and believes the method is well-verified, other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) point to the above weaknesses, especially concerning the marginal min-WER results and the experimental design for latency. Reviewer 2 initially scored the paper low but raised to 6 after rebuttal, still noting that min-WER gains are not supportive and that the method appears limited for tasks beyond latency.
