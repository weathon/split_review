## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Iterated Pareto Referent Optimization (IPRO), a framework for learning the Pareto front in multi-objective MDPs. IPRO decomposes the problem into a sequence of single-objective problems solved by a Pareto oracle, iteratively selecting reference points to shrink the objective space that may contain Pareto optimal solutions. The authors provide theoretical convergence guarantees and an upper bound on the approximation error. Experiments are conducted on DST, Minecraft, and MO-Reacher, using implementations of the oracle via DQN, A2C, and GGF-DQN.

### Strengths
- The idea of decomposing multi-objective optimization from a hypervolume perspective and iteratively solving it is innovative and inspiring; the paper has potential for long-term impact (Reviewer 1).
- The introduction of the Pareto oracle concept into MORL is new and leads to a natural, novel algorithmic framework (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Theoretical analysis provides an upper bound on the true approximation error at each iteration and a convergence guarantee to a τ-Pareto front (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- Multi-objective reinforcement learning is an important problem, and IPRO adds a relevant method to the community (Reviewer 4).
- Reviewer 3 (score 6) is more positive, emphasizing that the algorithm is natural, novel, and the theoretical guarantee looks sound, while other reviewers are more critical.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental evidence and missing baselines**: The benchmarks used (DST, Minecraft, MO-Reacher) are relatively simple; no experiments on complex continuous control tasks (e.g., MO-MuJoCo). Comparisons are missing against several important MORL baselines: Envelope [Yang et al., NeurIPS 2019], PGMORL [Xu et al., ICML 2020], Q-Pensieve, Abels et al. [ICML 2019], GPI-LS, Basaklar [ICLR 2023], and Hung [ICLR 2023]. The comparison with GPI-LS does not show clear improvement, making it hard to see why one would prefer IPRO over existing methods. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **Sample efficiency ignored / degenerates to MOO**: IPRO abstracts away the core RL challenge of learning from online interactions by assuming the Pareto oracle can directly return a Pareto optimal policy given any reference point. This reduces the problem to a typical multi-objective optimization (MOO) problem, and all experimental results are reported in iterations rather than number of samples, which does not reflect actual sample efficiency. (Reviewer 2)
- **Poor presentation and missing algorithmic details**:
  - Key definitions are introduced late (e.g., in Chapter 4 rather than earlier), making the paper hard to follow. (Reviewer 1)
  - No pseudocode or algorithm box is provided in the main text; only word descriptions. Algorithms 1 and 2 in the appendix should be in the main paper. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
  - Several algorithmic components are unclear: how parameters λ and ρ in the augmented Chebyshev scalarization are chosen in each iteration; how the sets L and U are constructed; what is done in the Pruning phase and how it uncovers additional Pareto optimal solutions; how the memory-based policy is implemented (any special neural architecture?). (Reviewer 2)
  - How the reference point l is selected from the set of lower points L when there are multiple candidates. (Reviewer 3)
  - Some definitions are missing or unclear: “occupancy measure” and “occupancies” in Section 3.2.1, “target region”, how s becomes s_r depending on r, rigorous definition of expected return v^π, definitions of “nadir” v^n and “ideal” vector v^i in Figures 1 and 2. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Theoretical and methodological concerns**:
  - The IPRO framework completely ignores the RL sample-efficiency aspect via the Pareto oracle assumption; it is more an MOO algorithm than an MORL method. (Reviewer 2)
  - What assumptions are placed on the Pareto oracle for Theorem 4.1 to hold? How does the fact that even the reduced single-objective RL problem might not be solved to optimality impact IPRO’s performance? (Reviewer 4)
  - Since convergence is only asymptotic, how does IPRO compare theoretically to a simple method of optimizing a uniformly sampled weighted sum each time (which also asymptotically converges to the Pareto front)? Could a convergence rate be characterized? (Reviewer 3)
  - What is the advantage of using another multi-objective algorithm (e.g., GGF-DQN) as a subroutine in IPRO compared to directly using that algorithm to optimize the multi-objective function? (Reviewer 4)
  - The reason behind the design of a* in Equation (3) and its theoretical grounding is unclear. (Reviewer 2)
  - What is the practical difference between a weak Pareto oracle and an approximate Pareto oracle? Is the distinction only for theoretical rigorousness? (Reviewer 3)
- **Inconsistent or unexplained experimental observations**: In Figure 3(b) and 3(e), DQN as Pareto oracle achieves the highest coverage but the lowest hypervolume; the cause is not explained. (Reviewer 2)
- **Section 3.2.1 appears irrelevant to the main result** and should be presented later. (Reviewer 3)
