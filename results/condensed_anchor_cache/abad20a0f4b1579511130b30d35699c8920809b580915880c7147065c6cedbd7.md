## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers recognize an interesting problem setting and a reasonable framework (R2,R3,R5) with sound theoretical development (R3), clear writing (R4,R5), and successful task completion against baselines (R1,R5). However, major concerns focus on the limited scope of the transfer tasks (only pose changes, single object, simple manipulation) (R1,R2,R4,R5), overly ambitious claims (R1,R2,R4), insufficient experimental detail and weak baselines (R2,R3), and the question of whether the method offers any advantage over direct planning in the target domain (R3,R5). There is notable disagreement: reviewers 4 and 5 gave scores of 5 (positive), reviewers 1 and 3 gave scores of 3 (mixed), and reviewer 2 gave a score of 1 (negative), reflecting a split on whether the experimental evidence supports the claimed contributions.

### Strengths
- Interesting problem setting for robotic transfer learning; the framework and theoretical development are sound (R2,R3,R5).
- Paper is well-written with clear figures and supplementary video (R4,R5).
- Diff-Transfer accomplishes all four simulated evaluation tasks (opening door, opening drawer, closing grill, changing clock) successfully, while baseline methods mostly fail (R1,R5).
- Requires fewer iterative steps compared to the Direct Transfer baseline (R1).
- Provides analysis comparing path-planning methods (linear interpolation) and an ablation over the task term in the reward function (R1).
- Leverages differentiable physics simulation for pose and object shape transfer (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited task scope**: All experiments manipulate a single object, and the transfer tasks are only object pose changes (door opening, drawer opening, grill closing, clock change). Several reviewers consider these “simple” and more like new configurations of the same task rather than meaningful transfer to different behaviours (R1,R2,R4,R5). The paper claims to handle object shape transfer but does not evaluate it (R1,R4). Scalability to more severe pose changes, shape changes, multi-object scenes, or radically different manipulation skills (e.g., placing vs. inserting, reaching+grasping vs. lifting) is unaddressed (R1,R3,R5).
- **Ambition of claims vs. evidence**: Multiple reviewers highlight exaggerated language (“pioneering contributions herald a new era”, “revolutionize the paradigm”) and a conclusion too ambitious given the simple experimental setup (R1,R2,R4). The paper does not convincingly demonstrate that transfer learning actually happens rather than just adapting pose parameters (R2).
- **Insufficient experimental detail**: Observation space, action space, reward functions, and how starting robot states are randomized are not provided (R2). The reward function appears hand-engineered; it is unclear how many data points are required to fit the Q-network and whether the pretraining dataset is hard to collect (R1).
- **Weak baseline comparisons**: MAML is a meta-learning method that requires a large task distribution to be effective; using only four source tasks makes failure unsurprising, so it is an inappropriate baseline (R3). The “Direct Transfer” baseline is essentially an ablation without the planning stage (R3). No comparison is made to other works using differentiable physics for task transfer or to standard planning methods (e.g., RRT, A*, sampling-based) (R1,R3). The method is also not compared to directly planning in the target domain with the differentiable simulator (R3).
- **Question of advantage over direct planning**: TransferStep resembles trajectory optimization at each step. For the simple evaluated tasks, both source and target tasks could likely be solved by planning directly in the target domain using the differentiable simulator, undermining the need for the transfer framework (R3,R5). A reviewer asked how the method compares to SE(2)/SE(3) equivariant policy learning that could provide direct generalization (R5).
- **Minor presentation issues**: Figure 1 wording is confusing (R1,R3); Figure 3 is uninformative and its explanation unclear (R4); repeated word “application” in Section 5.2 (R1); last sentence of Section 5.1.4 is confusing; reason for calling Section 5.3 “innovative” is unjustified (R1). The method is described as RL but only learns a critic network, which is misleading (R1).
