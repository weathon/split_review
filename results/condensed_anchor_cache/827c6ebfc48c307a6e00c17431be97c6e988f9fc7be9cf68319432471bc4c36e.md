## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies training neural SDEs (diffusion samplers) for Boltzmann sampling without target samples. It establishes asymptotic equivalences between discrete-time policy objectives and continuous-time path-space measures in the limit of infinitesimal discretization. Empirically, it shows that coarse, non-uniform time discretization (especially random placement) improves training efficiency. The theoretical contributions connect discrete-time objectives (e.g., from GFlowNets) to continuous-time objects (PDEs and path-space divergences), but reviewers found these results standard and not surprising. Experiments are conducted on synthetic low-dimensional benchmarks (Gaussian mixtures, toy distributions). The paper is judged to be of moderate contribution (scores 5,5,5,6) with mixed opinions on presentation and theoretical novelty.

### Strengths
- The problem of training diffusion samplers without target samples is well-motivated, and linking discrete-time policies to continuous-time SDE training is a useful idea.
- Empirical evidence that non-uniform (random) time discretization improves training efficiency is novel and well-supported by thorough experiments on diverse sampling benchmarks. This could be highly relevant for practitioners.
- The paper effectively summarizes existing methods and objectives (GFlowNets, stochastic control, diffusion models) in a clear context.
- The experimental section is thorough, exploring various discretization strategies and their impact.
- The appendix provides a comprehensive complement to the main text.
- The paper appears mathematically rigorous, and experiments credibly demonstrate the proposed approach.
- Potential computational cost reduction for neural SDE training.
- **Minority positive view (Reviewer 4)**: The theoretical results are extensive and the theory-experiment combination is strong; the problem is well-motivated. However, other reviewers found the theoretical contributions standard and not surprising.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical novelty and depth**: The main results (Propositions 3.2, 3.3, 3.4) show convergence as the maximal step size \(\Delta t \to 0\) using Euler-Maruyama. These are straightforward applications of established SDE theory and lack new insights or unique techniques. The theoretical contributions are not unexpected and partly already present in the literature.
- **Theory-experiment gap**: The theoretical results only guarantee convergence as \(\Delta t \to 0\) and do _not_ explain why non-uniform discretization is generally superior to uniform discretization. The advantage of non-uniform (random) time steps is demonstrated only empirically, without a theoretical rationale or intuitive explanation. This is a key missing insight.
- **Potential contradiction**: It is noted that uniform discretization has been proven optimal in the general case (Rümelin, 1982, “Numerical Treatment of Stochastic Equations”). The paper’s claim that non-uniform discretization can outperform uniform discretization may contradict established results; this is not addressed.
- **ELBO gap behavior**: The ELBO gap does not converge to zero as discretization becomes finer but stabilizes at a positive value. The paper does not explain this phenomenon, nor does it provide a benchmark from prior work to contextualize the result. It remains unclear whether convergence to zero is theoretically expected.
- **Missing comparisons and benchmarks**: Experiments are limited to standard synthetic benchmarks (Gaussian mixtures, low-dimensional toy distributions). The paper lacks comparisons with recent models such as flow-based generative models. Higher-dimensional Bayesian inference tasks (where the Boltzmann distribution is truly intractable) and benchmarks comparing computational costs in larger dimensions are needed to support real-world applicability.
- **Presentation and readability (strong disagreement among reviewers)**: 
  - Reviewers 2 and 3 found the paper very difficult to read: notation is dense, non-standard, and sometimes undefined. Section 2 (preliminaries) spans pages 3–7, introducing well-known equations (e.g., equations (4)-(6) from GFlowNet papers, (9)-(15) from stochastic control, (16)-(17) from Euler-Maruyama) without sufficient explanation. Figures like Figure 3 are considered unnecessary. Figures 1 and 2 are placed too early and hard to interpret without more textual description. The abstract and introduction are not sufficiently informative or precise.
  - Reviewer 1, however, found the paper very well written and easy to follow.
- **Clarity of contribution flow**: It is unclear how the Propositions in Section 3 connect to the Results in Section 4. The reader struggles to see how the theoretical limit results affect the empirical discretization choices.
- **Potential error in Theorem 3.4**: The notation \(\vec{\mu}_t\) appears twice in the theorem statement; clarification is needed.
- **Technical definitions**: Some notation is undefined or unclear: the “reverse arrow” on \(\pi(t)\) below equation (3) is not defined; the difference between \(\mu(t)\) in equation (1) and equation (9) requires more explanation.
- **Practical relevance**: It is not clear how the results help an ML practitioner beyond the observation that random time discretization works; deeper guidance is missing.
- **Missing ablation/theory**: Why does random placement of time steps work unexpectedly well? The paper offers no theoretical or intuitive rationale. This is a critical missing explanation.
