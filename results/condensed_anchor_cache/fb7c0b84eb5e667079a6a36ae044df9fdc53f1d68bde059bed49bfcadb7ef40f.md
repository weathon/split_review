## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DetGO, a novel method for detecting AI-generated images by overfitting a model to the distribution of natural images only. The core idea is that a model excessively tuned to real images will generalize poorly to AI-generated ones, turning overfitting into an advantage. The approach uses a dual-model framework: an anchor model (pre-trained DINOv2) encodes real images, and an overfitting model is trained to match the anchor’s output while exhibiting high sensitivity to small perturbations (Gaussian noise). The output difference between the two models serves as the detection signal. The method is inspired by Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) but inverts it to seek sharp minima.

### Strengths
- **Novel perspective:** Treating overfitting as an advantage rather than a problem is new in the context of AI-generated image detection (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- **Training efficiency:** Requires only natural images for training, eliminating the need for AI-generated images in the training process (Reviewer 1).
- **Diverse evaluation:** Experiments test a wide spectrum of generated images, including GAN and diffusion images as well as Sora video frames (Reviewer 2).
- **Strong ablations:** Ablation studies cover almost all important hyperparameters and validate the hypotheses (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Clear presentation:** The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and has a clear structure (Reviewer 3).
- **Intriguing methodology:** The design of sharpening the first derivative of the loss is logical and the motivation (similar to OOD detection) is profound (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing OOD comparisons:** The problem is formulated as typical OOD detection, but conventional OOD detection methods (e.g., using a well-trained model’s output confidence) are not discussed or compared. The paper should acknowledge this similarity and test those baselines before proposing a new method (Reviewer 2; also note Reviewer 1 questions the novelty relative to RIGID, a training-free model-agnostic detection approach that uses DINOv2 and Gaussian noise, which is not used as a baseline).
- **Threshold selection and evaluation metrics:** The paper does not mention how to select the detection threshold (Figure 1). Although Section 4.1 claims to evaluate accuracy (ACC), no ACC results are reported; only threshold-independent metrics (AUC, AP) are given. A high AUC/AP does not imply high accuracy, and the threshold likely has a significant impact. The reviewers request ACC with fixed thresholds on different datasets, and also per-class AUROC/AP (e.g., on real samples of LSUN-Bedroom from Table 3 and generated samples from ImageNet from Table 1) (Reviewers 2 and 3).
- **Choice and impact of noise $\epsilon$:** The authors simply adopt Gaussian noise for $\epsilon$ without discussing alternatives or its properties. Since $\epsilon$ is intended to represent the nature of AI-generated images (a special type of universal fake image), its choice is critical; the impact of different noise distributions or intensities on generalization should be studied (Reviewers 2 and 3). The paper mentions variation in noise intensity (Table 9) but does not discuss the trade-off between generalization to unseen real vs. unseen AI-generated images (Reviewer 2).
- **Robustness to Gaussian blur/noise:** If $\epsilon$ is Gaussian-distributed, applying Gaussian blur to images (which adds noise of the same distribution) may increase the loss $L(x+\epsilon')$, potentially causing misclassification (Reviewer 3). Similarly, the method’s classification may depend on the level of Gaussian noise; if real images are corrupted with stronger noise while fake images have weaker noise, misjudgments could arise. The paper does not discuss robustness to Gaussian noise or such adversarial perturbations (Reviewer 3).
- **Backbone choice justification:** The intuition for using DINOv2 as a backbone (and not another self-supervised model like CLIP) is not explained (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 also notes the architecture of $g(x)$ (multiple trainable convolutional layers) receives weak justification; the authors should test alternatives such as U-Net and discuss the impact (Reviewer 3).
- **Implementation details missing:** How $\epsilon$ is sampled from the Gaussian distribution (parameters?), how $\rho$ is chosen, and the rationale for the number of convolutional layers in $g_\theta(x)$ are unclear (Reviewer 1). The paper does not describe how the generative models are used to generate test data or how much data is used (Reviewer 2).
- **Effect of training data:** The paper states that access to high-quality training data may have a large impact, but does not explore this experimentally (e.g., results as a function of the number of training images) or discuss how the selection of the training dataset affects performance (Reviewers 2 and 3).
- **Complexity evaluation:** The computational complexity of DetGO (e.g., inference cost, memory) is not evaluated (Reviewer 3).
- **Disagreement on novelty:** While Reviewers 2 and 3 find the overfitting idea new, Reviewer 1 questions the novelty of the design, noting that the backbone (DINOv2) and noise distribution (Gaussian) are too similar to the existing work RIGID; the paper does not cite or compare against RIGID (Reviewer 1).
- **Baseline implementation issues:** The performance of Ojha in Table 1 and Table 3 is lower than its reported performance; the authors must provide the implementation details of the baseline methods (Reviewer 1).
