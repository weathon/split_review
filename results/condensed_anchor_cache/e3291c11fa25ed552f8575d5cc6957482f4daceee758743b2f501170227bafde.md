## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents an improved quantum algorithm for solving matrix zero-sum games and linear programming. The main result is a quantum solver for zero-sum games (with m×n payoff matrix) that runs in \(\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{m+n}/\epsilon^{2.25})\) time, improving the previous best \(\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{m+n}/\epsilon^{2.5})\) of Bouland et al. (2023) and Gao et al. (2023) by a factor of \((1/\epsilon)^{1/4}\). As a corollary, it yields a quantum linear programming solver with time \(\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{m+n}\,(Rr/\epsilon)^{2.25})\), where \(R\) and \(r\) are bounds on the primal/dual optimal solutions and \(\epsilon\) is the additive error. The improvement broadens the range of attainable \(\epsilon\): the algorithm works for \(\epsilon = O((m+n)^{-2})\) instead of the earlier \(O((m+n)^{-1})\). The algorithm uses the sample-based optimistic multiplicative weight update framework. At its core is a new multi-Gibbs sampler for diagonal Hamiltonians that generates samples from the Gibbs distribution \(e^{\beta H}\) with a time complexity that is sublinear in the inverse temperature \(\beta\), breaking the previous linear-in-\(\beta\) barrier. This is achieved via a better polynomial approximation and a reparametrization of preprocessing (Reviewers 2, 3).

### Strengths
- **Technical contribution is strong and improves prior work.** The paper gives a solid theoretical improvement over the state-of-the-art quantum algorithms for zero-sum games and linear programming (Reviewer 2). The new multi-Gibbs sampler is an interesting technical tool with potential applications beyond this paper (Reviewer 2). Improving the dependence on \(\gamma = Rr/\epsilon\) is important for quantum zero-sum game solvers, and the corresponding improvement for linear programming is meaningful—especially because second-order methods like interior-point are less attractive when the dependence on \(\epsilon^{-1}\) remains polynomial due to quantum state readout (Reviewer 3).
- **Writing quality: one reviewer found it well written** (Reviewer 1), though two other reviewers strongly disagree (see Weaknesses). This contrast is noted.

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation is poor and not publication‑ready.** The paper is highly non‑self-contained: it directly uses theorem/lemma statements from prior work without explanation, lists only pseudocode and time complexities, and provides no intuition for how the algorithms work or why the improvements arise (Reviewers 2, 3). The main theorems for zero-sum games and linear programming are not proved in the main text; only the Gibbs sampling result is proved (Reviewer 2). Sections 4.2 and 4.3 fail to convey new information beyond the introduction (Reviewer 2). The paper is extremely difficult to evaluate or grasp the actual contribution (Reviewers 2, 3). *(Note: Reviewer 1 found the paper well written, but the majority view is that major revision is needed.)*
- **Novelty is limited.** The technique relies heavily on prior work (Gao et al. 2023) and the improvements are not significant enough to justify a new paper (Reviewer 2). The approach is a relatively standard adaptation of better polynomial approximation; a recent result by Aggarwal and Alman (2022) might directly yield further improvement (Reviewer 3). The multi-Gibbs sampler appears novel, but the lack of explanation makes it hard to assess its real contribution (Reviewer 3).
- **Practical relevance is unclear.** The quantum LP solver is a first‑order method that still carries a factor of \((Rr/\epsilon)^{2.25}\), and it is not shown when it would outperform classical methods (Reviewer 2). The practical applicability of quantum algorithms in this domain is questionable (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing proofs and explanations.**  
  - Proofs of the main results (Theorem 1.1, Theorem 1.2) and Corollaries 4.2, 4.3 are not given (Reviewer 2).  
  - No high‑level overview of Algorithms 1, 2, or 3 is provided, and the intuition behind them and their key differences from prior works are absent (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  - The reduction from the matrix‑game solution to the LP solution is not explicitly shown (Reviewer 2).  
  - Several technical details are omitted (e.g., why \(k\) has both a lower and upper bound in Theorem 1.3, the meaning of \(\xi\), the input model assumptions, the definition of trace distance/norm) (Reviewer 2).  
- **Inaccuracies, typos, and missing references.**  
  - On page 1, the reference for the improvement of the LP solver to \(O(n^{2+1/18})\) should be (Jiang et al., 2021) not (Cohen et al., 2021) (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  - The statement “[BGJST23] requires \(1/\epsilon = O((m+n)^{-1})\)” is a typo: it should be \(1/\epsilon = O(m+n)\) (Reviewer 3).  
  - The reference for quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) is incorrect; QSVT was not proposed in Gao et al. 2023 (Reviewer 2).  
  - The claim that “the concept of amplitude‑encoding is proposed in Gao et al. 2023” is inaccurate; the idea existed for decades (Reviewer 2).  
  - Notation is inconsistent: “matrix games” and “matrix zero‑sum games” are used interchangeably without definition, pure/mixed strategies are undefined (Reviewer 2).  
  - Several minor typos (e.g., “use” → “using”, “time. gates” → “time and gates”, missing definitions of \(u_{\min}\), \(u_{\max}\)) (Reviewer 2).  
- **Specific unanswered questions (overlap with criticisms).**  
  - What are the differences between the results of [BGJST23] and [GJLW23] listed with the same runtime in Table 1? (Reviewer 3)  
  - Why does the algorithm require both a lower bound and an upper bound on \(k\) in Theorem 1.3? (Reviewer 2)  
  - Does the quantum LP solver only output the value, or also a solution? (Reviewer 2)  
  - How is it guaranteed that each weight \(w_i\) lies in \([-1,1]\)? (Reviewer 2)
