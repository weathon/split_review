## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper tackles the challenging task of inductive link prediction for both new entities and new relations, introducing a framework based on double permutation equivariance (ISDEA) and a distributionally double equivariant extension (DEq-InGram). Reviewers agree the problem is important and timely, and the theoretical grounding is a strength. However, opinions diverge sharply on clarity, novelty, scalability, and experimental validation. One reviewer (score 8) considers the work solid and the experimental agenda convincing; another (score 3) finds the writing unclear, the architectures incremental, and the evaluation insufficient. The remaining reviewers (scores 5, 6) see merit but highlight major efficiency concerns and questionable generalizability. Common criticisms include excessive computational cost, unrealistic isomorphism assumptions, and reliance on small negative samples.

### Strengths
- **Problem importance and generalization**: The task of simultaneously handling unseen nodes and relation types is highly relevant for modern graph learning, especially in low-data regimes without input node features (R2, R3, R4).
- **Theoretical foundation**: The paper provides a formal framework of double permutation equivariance with accompanying theorems and proofs, which appear sound (R1, R3).
- **New benchmarking datasets**: The construction of PediaTypes and WikiTopics establishes a new evaluation context for doubly inductive link prediction (R2, R3, R4).
- **Experimental design**: Experiments include multiple baselines and cover both entity prediction (i, r, ?) and relation prediction (i, ?, k) tasks (R1, R4). Reproducibility is supported by provided code and detailed settings (R4).
- **Relation embedding avoidance**: The ISDEA framework avoids fixed relation embeddings, which is a principled approach for unseen relations (R4).
- **Motivation and architecture study**: The paper clearly motivates the setting and examines different architectures (R2).
- **Awareness of limitations**: Scalability issues are acknowledged and pointed to future work (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Prohibitive computational complexity**: ISDEA requires an exhaustive search for isomorphic subgraphs, with distance calculation between every pair of nodes per relation, leading to combinatorial explosion and memory usage that makes it impractical for large KGs (R1, R4). Preprocessing is expensive, encoding must be done for each relation, and scoring requires separate runs per candidate entity (R4). Reviewers question what largest KG can be handled with 32 GB GPU (R1) and ask for training/inference times (R4). One reviewer suggests exploring whether the isomorphism condition can be relaxed to a WL test (R1).
- **Unclear and inconsistent formalization**: The writing is repetitive, definitions are unclear, and Figure 1 is overcrowded and fails to illustrate the doubly inductive setting (R2). The use of logical conjunctions to represent relation types is nonstandard and unmotivated (R2). The term “multigraph” is introduced without precise differentiation from a standard knowledge graph (R2). The concept of “doubly inductive” is problematic because Def. 1 describes predicting either the relation or one entity at a time, not both simultaneously (R2). The definition of isomorphic triplets is presented without justification (R2). Formal statements throughout are hard to parse, including in the appendix (R2). In contrast, one reviewer finds the presentation good/excellent (R3), so this weakness is raised with strong severity by R2 only.
- **Discrepancy between theory and experiments**: The double equivariance framework assumes the existence of bijections (same number of nodes and relation types) between training and test graphs, but the constructed datasets (PediaTypes, WikiTopics) have differing numbers—a mismatch left unaddressed (R3).
- **Incremental architectural novelty**: ISDEA is viewed as a direct application of DSS-GNN to relation-induced subgraphs, while DEq-InGram is a simple modification of InGram with bagging (R2). One reviewer does not label this a weakness but also does not praise novelty (R1, R3, R4).
- **Misleading evaluation due to negative sampling**: Both the node and relation prediction tasks evaluate with 50 random negatives (1 vs. 50), which likely overestimates performance because most negatives are easy to distinguish from positives (R3, R4). One reviewer specifically disagrees with the claim that node prediction is “relatively easier”; the observed gap might instead stem from the fact that the 50 random negatives are drawn from thousands of entities (easy) while the number of relations is far smaller (harder negatives) (R3). A more complete ranking of all candidates is needed (R3).
- **Missing comparisons and ablation**: Standard relational GNNs (RGCN, CompGCN, NBFNets) are not included (R2). The performance gap between DEq-InGram and InGram (both distributionally double equivariant) is not explained (R2). Input features used for GNN baselines (GraphConv, GAT, GIN) are not specified—are they all‑ones or random vectors? (R3). The source of the two new datasets (OpenEA) is not clarified: the multilingual KGs share the same schema, meaning many relations overlap; this may affect the difficulty of the inductive setting (R4).
- **ISDEA underperforms on node prediction**: On PediaTypes, ISDEA yields lower scores than DEq-InGram and even baselines for entity prediction (R2, R4). This contradicts theoretical expectations and is not discussed (R2, R4).
- **Limited distributional shift**: Experiments focus on train and test graphs from similar distributions; further validation on scenarios with significant distribution shift would strengthen results (R2).
- **Minor opportunities**: One reviewer raises whether averaging NBFNet over random relation initializations would also yield distributional double equivariance (R3), and whether frame‑averaging could serve as an alternative to the proposed distributional equivariance (R3). These points are raised as questions but touch on missing ablation and definition clarity.
