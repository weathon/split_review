## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Adaptive Prompt-Tailored Pruning (APTP), a prompt-based pruning method for text-to-image diffusion models. APTP trains a prompt router that maps input prompts to architecture codes (representing pruned sub-networks), enabling dynamic allocation of capacity per prompt while maintaining batch parallelism. The router and codes are trained via contrastive learning and optimal transport. Experiments on Stable Diffusion V2.1 with CC3M and COCO show that APTP reduces computational cost while preserving or improving FID, CLIP, and CMMD scores compared to static pruning baselines. The method also produces semantically meaningful clusters of prompts.

### Strengths

1. **Novel and interesting idea.** The paper introduces a non-trivial and conceptually novel approach: adaptive prompt-based pruning that routes prompts to specialized pruned sub-networks, combining benefits of static and dynamic pruning. [Reviewers 1,2,3,4]
2. **Effective empirical results on target datasets.** On CC3M and MS-COCO, APTP outperforms static structural pruning baselines (e.g., Diff-Pruning, Structured Pruning) in terms of FID, CLIP score, and CMMD score while reducing computational cost. [Reviewer 1]
3. **Interpretability and no code collapse.** Visual results show APTP uses various pruning modes and does not collapse to a single architecture code. [Reviewer 3] Clustering analysis reveals semantically meaningful prompt assignments. [Reviewers 1,2]
4. **Good illustrations and concise explanation.** The core idea is well explained and illustrated. [Reviewer 4]
5. **Multiple datasets with different image types.** Experiments use both COCO (real-world images) and CC3M (more diverse), providing breadth. [Reviewer 4]

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited generalization and OOD evaluation.** The method is evaluated only on the validation sets of CC3M and COCO (the target datasets). It is unclear how well the prompt router handles out-of-distribution prompts, which is critical for generalizability. Several reviewers raised this. [Reviewers 1,2,4] *Reviewer 4 explicitly asked how OOD prompts impact generation quality.*
2. **Missing baselines: step-skipping distillation and few-step methods.** The paper does not compare against approaches that achieve faster inference via fewer denoising steps (e.g., INSTAFLOW, step distillation methods like [1,2,3]) or caching-based acceleration [4]. These methods can cut latency by 8–25×, while APTP reduces latency by less than 50% (20% to keep comparable metrics). The paper should demonstrate APTP’s benefit beyond simply reducing sampling steps. [Reviewer 3] *Reviewer 1 also requested comparison with INSTAFLOW.*
3. **Missing comparison to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models.** Since the contribution is prompt-conditional pruning, the paper should compare to prompt-conditional efficient architectures like MoE text-to-image diffusion models, not just static pruning baselines. [Reviewer 2]
4. **Missing quantitative comparison to other model pruning methods.** The paper references MobileDiffusion, SnapFusion, and LD-Pruner but does not provide quantitative comparisons or an explanation for omission. [Reviewer 1]
5. **Limited visual comparisons.** Only 8 qualitative examples are shown against the original model, with no comparisons to prior pruning or acceleration methods. More extensive visual comparisons (including step-skipping, caching, and fewer denoising steps) are needed. [Reviewer 3]
6. **Limited experiments on architectures and settings.** It is unknown whether the method generalizes to other diffusion architectures (DiT, MMDiT, Pix-Art-Alpha/Sigma) or even other Stable Diffusion backbones. [Reviewers 2,3,4] *Reviewer 4 also asked about applying to transformer-based models and about varying resolutions/aspect ratios.* No experiments on small datasets or fine-grained concepts (e.g., dog breeds) are provided. [Reviewer 2]
7. **Substantial FID drop and lack of latency-performance trade-off analysis.** Table 1 shows a 4 and 7 point drop in FID for APTP. The paper does not present a clear trade-off curve between latency and performance (FID/CLIP) to help understand how limiting computational budget affects quality. [Reviewer 2]
8. **Router model size and batch impact.** The memory and parameter footprint of the prompt router is not reported, nor how its size affects maximum batch size on an A100 GPU. [Reviewer 4]
9. **Writing quality of Section 3.** Sections 3.2 and 3.3 are overloaded and difficult to understand, despite being the core description of the approach. [Reviewer 3]
10. **Clustering analysis relegated to appendix.** The clustering/pruning-mode analysis promised in the abstract is only shown in the appendix without textual description. It should be in the main manuscript. [Reviewer 3]
11. **Missing ablation/compatibility with classifier-free guidance.** The method’s compatibility with classifier-free guidance during training is not discussed. [Reviewer 1 – question, overlaps with missing analysis]
12. **Influence of prompt encoder size.** The effect of the sentence transformer prompt encoder’s size on overall performance is not studied. [Reviewer 1 – question, overlaps with missing ablation]
13. **Suggested additional evaluation.** While not a strict weakness, one reviewer proposed computing pick-scores on diverse prompts (e.g., PartiPrompts) as a more interpretable quality metric. [Reviewer 4]

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 8, confidence 4) was notably more positive, praising the illustrations, explanation, and dataset breadth, and had fewer criticisms (mainly suggestions for added experiments). The other three reviewers (scores 5, 6, 6) were more critical, particularly regarding the evaluation scope, missing baselines, and practical latency reduction. This contrast should be considered when weighing the concerns above.
