## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Decoupled Refusal Training (DeRTa) to address refusal position bias in LLM safety tuning. DeRTa consists of two components: (1) Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) with Harmful Response Prefix, which appends a harmful prefix to the beginning of a safe response to train models to recognize unsafe content at any response position; and (2) Reinforced Transition Optimization (RTO), which encourages the model to transition from harmful to safe refusal consistently throughout the response sequence. The method is evaluated on LLaMA3 and Mistral model families across six attack scenarios, including CodeAttack that has jailbroken GPT-4 and LLaMA3-70B-Instruct. The method is simple and easy to implement, but reviewers raised concerns about its novelty, baseline comparisons, and experimental rigor.

### Strengths
- Identifies the important issue of refusal position bias in current safety tuning practices.
- The proposed DeRTa method is reasonably motivated to address the identified problem and is simple to implement.
- Comprehensive ablations across different attacks, LLMs, and benchmarks, including multi-objective measurements of harmlessness and helpfulness (e.g., Fig. 4, Table 1).
- Systematic evaluation with ablations on the contribution of each training objective (MLE with Harmful Prefix vs. RTO).
- The method shows promise in reducing ASR across several safety benchmarks while preserving utility compared to vanilla models.
- The experiments defend against various jailbreak attack types, including recent advanced attacks like CodeAttack.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of comparison with state-of-the-art safety defenses:** The paper does not thoroughly compare DeRTa with other recent safety-enhancing methods such as R2D2, or defenses listed in related work (e.g., under PAIR attacks). Multiple reviewers (R1, R3, R4) note this missing baseline comparison, making it hard to judge effectiveness relative to existing approaches.
- **No adaptive attacks:** The paper does not design or evaluate adaptive attacks that specifically target the refusal mechanism. An adversary could potentially circumvent RTO by targeting tokens not covered by the “sorry” keyword, or optimizing harmful strings that also start with “sorry”. Such testing is essential to confirm security gains.
- **Over-refusal risk:** The strong safety emphasis may cause the model to refuse safe content. The authors should evaluate against over-refusal benchmarks like OR-Bench.
- **Risk of generating harmful content before refusal:** The method trains models to refuse at any position, but “refuse at a later stage” could mean harmful content is generated before the refusal (e.g., step-by-step bomb instructions followed by refusal). The authors should provide statistics on how often harmful content appears before the refusal token, and ideally show that such cases are rare or absent.
- **Experimental results lack confidence intervals and sensitivity analysis:** It is unclear whether reported results come from a single run or multiple seeds, and how much randomness from dataset shuffling or generation sampling affects the outcomes. Confidence intervals or standard deviations should be reported.
- **Motivation insufficiently supported:** The claim of refusal position bias is not demonstrated with preliminary experimental evidence. For example, the authors could show that SOTA instruct models (e.g., Llama3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-Instruct-v3) under attack generate harmful content even when the first tokens decline the request. Table 1 could be expanded to include more models.
- **Technical contribution incremental:** MLE with Harmful Response Prefix is essentially data augmentation and has prior art (e.g., “safety shouldn’t be a few tokens deep” from related work). The second component, RTO, is qualitatively similar in spirit to the first, applied at every prefix length. Reviewers (R1, R2, R4) note the limited novelty.
- **Limited model families and evaluation on instruct models:** Only LLaMA3 and Mistral are used; additional families like Phi and Qwen should be tested. The paper should also evaluate on the instruct versions of these models (e.g., Llama3-8B-Instruct), not just vanilla checkpoints.
- **Refusal token limited to “sorry”:** Equation (2) uses only “sorry” as the refusal token. This may limit performance compared to using a diverse set of refusal expressions.
- **Evaluation method not fully specified:** ASR computation (keyword matching, human labeling, or GPT-judge) is not clearly stated. If human evaluation is used, the authors should consider using GPT judges for reproducibility and bias mitigation.
- **Inconsistency in Figure 4:** The proposed method appears to have a lower refusal ratio than both “only harmful prefix” and vanilla models, yet claims better ASR. This needs explanation.
- **Baseline setup unclear:** “Vanilla-” is undefined. Some baselines receive fewer data points; DPO fine-tuning setup is not described (e.g., does it also access completions that start harmful and turn into refusals?). The use of 6000 examples for vanilla fine-tuning for fair comparison is questionable—the triple (harmful query, safe response, harmful response) should arguably count as one example, not two.
- **Random truncation of harmful prefix may hurt fluency:** In the training objective, the harmful prefix is randomly truncated to a length k, which may create ungrammatical or unnatural continuations. Truncating only at sentence boundaries could mitigate this.
- **No evaluation on long text or multi-step dialogues:** These scenarios are known to be more vulnerable to jailbreak attacks; the paper does not test DeRTa in such settings.
- **Helpfulness increase unexplained:** The paper notes an increase in helpfulness after fine-tuning with refusal responses but provides no explanation for this phenomenon.
- **GCG attack only evaluated on small models:** The authors should discuss potential challenges or limitations when applying GCG to larger models (e.g., Llama3-8B or Llama3-70B).
- **Missing details:** Mistral version (v0.1 or v0.2) not specified. It is unclear whether vanilla and DeRTa training share the same hyperparameters.
