## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies Node-wise Graph Incremental Learning (NGIL) on evolving graphs, where new nodes arrive sequentially and cause structural shifts. It provides a theoretical analysis of NGIL’s learnability (Theorem 2.3), concluding that NGIL is not always learnable when structural shifts are uncontrolled. To mitigate these shifts, the authors propose SEA-ER, an experience replay method with a topology-aware sample selection and an importance‑reweighting replay objective. Experiments on three real-world datasets and synthetic data compare SEA-ER against existing replay-based NGIL frameworks, including an ablation study on buffer size.

### Strengths
- The studied problem (incremental learning on graphs) is important and well‑motivated (R1, R2, R4).  
- The paper provides a theoretical analysis (Theorem 2.3) of NGIL learnability, which offers an interesting re‑interpretation of the effect of cyclic probabilistic dependencies in evolving graphs (R1, R3).  
- The formulation is relatively agnostic to the mechanics that generate those probabilities (R3).  
- The method is evaluated on real-world datasets and shows effectiveness over prior works; extensive experiments (including meta‑analysis and ablation) support the claims (R2, R3, R4).  
- The related work review is comprehensive (R2).  
- The overall organization and the idea of reusing older samples as replay are reasonable (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is overly formal in places, making it harder to read (R1). There are formatting mistakes (e.g., “bf” before “Node-wise Graph Incremental Learning”) and missing elements (Definition ?? of distortion is incomplete, caption of Figure on page 8 is missing) (R2, R3). The description of the metric \(r_{i,j}\) reads as an example (“e.g., accuracy”) rather than a definitive choice (R3). The theoretical results could be explained more clearly (R4).  
- **Problem novelty**: NGIL has been extensively studied in prior work; the problem setting itself is not new (R2).  
- **Theoretical contribution and Theorem 2.3**:  
  - The innovation is minimal: once the graph is decomposed into ego‑graphs, the definitions and theoretical reasoning are straightforward adaptations from IID counterparts (R2).  
  - Theorem 2.3 is the core contribution, but its validity is questioned (R4):  
    - It is not clear what “good classifier \(f\)” means in the theorem (R4).  
    - The statement does not explicitly rule out retraining from scratch (joint training). Since obtaining a \(k\)-hop ego‑subgraph \(g_v\) for a sufficiently large \(k\) (larger than the graph diameter) is equivalent to obtaining the whole graph, retraining from scratch appears to be a valid algorithm, yet the theorem claims learnability is impossible (R4). This suggests missing assumptions (R4).  
  - The impossibility result could be traced back to the cyclic dependencies of node probabilities that produce the structural shift, but the paper does not indicate this connection (R3).  
  - The proof is relegated to the appendix; for the main theoretical result it should be in the body (R3).  
- **Method novelty and effectiveness**:  
  - Using an experience buffer with importance re‑weighting is not a new concept; the technical contributions of the proposed SEA‑ER appear limited (R2).  
  - It is unclear why the proposed method can mitigate the “structural shift.” The structural shift is defined by how new nodes are added and depends on the data nature, which is not controllable by an algorithm; the claim that a replay method can control it is confusing (R4).  
  - The replay strategy raises open questions: revisiting the same samples repeatedly could introduce unlearning/relearning effects (R1); how the replay changes the overall learning objective and the final solution compared to having all increments available at once (joint training) is not analyzed (R1).  
  - The objective function’s dependence on the order of increments is not discussed (R1).  
- **Evaluation**:  
  - The choice of accuracy as the sole metric may be problematic for graph data, which is sparse and topological (R3).  
  - The paper lacks a time‑complexity analysis of the entire framework compared to baselines; runtime insights would be valuable (R2).  
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: While one reviewer (R3) finds Theorem 2.3 an interesting and subtle theoretical contribution, another reviewer (R4) considers it flawed and the method’s ability to control structural shift unsupported. Reviewer 2 (rating 3) finds the overall novelty and technical depth low, whereas Reviewer 3 (rating 6) appreciates the theoretical angle.  
- **Connection to graph unlearning**: Reviewer 4 raises a comparison with graph unlearning literature ( [1,2] ), suggesting that NGIL can be viewed as the reverse of unlearning. If a simple retraining or unlearning technique works, the paper’s impossibility theorem might contradict those results; this relation is not addressed in the paper (R4).
