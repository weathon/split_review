## Merged Review

### Summary
QMP is a multi-task RL framework that selectively shares behaviors across tasks by using each task’s Q-function to evaluate and choose helpful behaviors from other task policies to improve off-policy data collection. It is designed to complement existing MTRL methods (parameter sharing, data relabeling, gradient-based approaches) and includes theoretical analysis showing convergence preservation. Experiments in manipulation, locomotion, and navigation environments show improvements over traditional behavior-sharing baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clearly organized, and effectively motivates the problem; the provided diagrams help visualize the approach (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The Q-switch mechanism is elegantly simple and principled: it avoids biasing policy objectives by limiting sharing to off-policy data collection, and the authors prove it preserves convergence guarantees of the underlying RL algorithm (Reviewers 1, 2).
- QMP is designed to be complementary to existing MTRL frameworks; experiments show consistent improvements when combined with parameter sharing, data relabeling, and gradient-based MTRL methods (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Extensive empirical evaluation across diverse domains (manipulation, locomotion, navigation) demonstrates QMP’s practical impact and gains over traditional behavior-sharing baselines (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Results are promising across tasks, and the method shows consistent performance improvements (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Computational overhead and scalability**: QMP requires evaluating all task policies and Q-values at each step, leading to significant cost—e.g., 7+ days runtime in MT50. The paper lacks a detailed breakdown of time spent on Q-function evaluations vs. other components (e.g., policy updates, environment interactions) and does not consider memory footprint when storing multiple Q-values/policies, especially under parameter sharing. Even the most positive reviewer (score 8) flagged this as a missing analysis (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Reliance on accurate Q-function estimation**: Suboptimal behavior selection may occur during early training or in sparse-reward tasks where Q-learning is unstable. One reviewer further questions whether naively taking the maximum over per-task Q-functions is suitable in stochastic environments, as it could lead to switching tasks at every step or favoring certain tasks, which is undesirable (Reviewers 1, 4). Another notes that it is not intuitively clear why the approach works as well as it does (Reviewer 4).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The method requires careful tuning, e.g., manually setting 70% task-specific policy usage in MT10, which limits practical applicability (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited scope and marginal gains on challenging tasks**: QMP is only an off-policy data sampling strategy and is not directly applicable to on-policy algorithms. Performance gains on complex tasks like MT10 and MT50 are marginal, raising scalability concerns. Moreover, other algorithms (reference [1]) achieved better results with fewer environment interactions, yet the paper does not adequately compare sample efficiency (number of interactions to reach comparable performance) with state-of-the-art MTRL methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of comprehensive comparisons**: The paper does not provide a detailed comparison with a wide range of MTRL baselines, making it difficult to assess relative performance and the practical benefits of QMP (Reviewer 3).
- **Dependence on shareable behaviors and risk of negative transfer**: Effectiveness heavily depends on the existence of shareable behaviors; gains may be limited for unrelated or conflicting task sets. The paper lacks discussion on how to avoid local optima or negative transfer during complex multi-task knowledge transfer (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Theory limited to tabular MDPs**: The convergence guarantees are proven only for tabular MDPs and may not fully extend to continuous state-action spaces with function approximation (Reviewer 1).
- **Misalignment between motivation and implementation**: The paper motivates “behavior sharing” as sharing action sequences or subtasks (e.g., “approaching the tabletop,” “grasping the object handle”), but Algorithm 1 implements one-step action sharing. This discrepancy requires significant revision of the paper’s motivation and terminology (Reviewer 4).
