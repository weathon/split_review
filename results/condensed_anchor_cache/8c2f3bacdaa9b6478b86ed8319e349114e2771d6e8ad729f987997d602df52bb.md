## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces MOSAIC, an auction mechanism for aggregating the preferences of self-interested advertisers over LLM-generated replies, with application to online advertising. MOSAIC samples M candidate replies from a proposal distribution, computes a distribution over them via importance sampling to approximate the VCG-optimal distribution (which maximizes advertiser rewards minus KL divergence from a reference LLM), and then samples a reply. Payments are computed using Rochet payment (1987). As M→∞, MOSAIC converges to the optimal fine-tuned LLM output without requiring model fine-tuning or weight access. Experiments on Llama-2-7b-chat-hf compare a context-aware version (using π_con as proposal) with a naive version (using π_ref). Reviewers agree the topic is important and practical, but disagree on novelty and experimental rigor.

### Strengths

- Well structured and clearly written, with a running example that aids understanding (R1, R2, R3).
- Addresses a novel and practically relevant problem: aggregating advertiser preferences with user-centric content in LLM-generated advertising (R2, R3, R4).
- Firm theoretical grounding: strategy-proofness (truthful reporting is dominant strategy) is proven, and convergence to the optimal distribution is guaranteed as M increases (R1, R3, R4).
- Practical advantages: no fine-tuning, only API access, computationally efficient (R1, R4). The mechanism is applicable to any setting with monetary transfers (R1).
- Introduces regularization of user preference via KL divergence from π_ref, which is noted as a novel element (R2).
- The idea of approximating VCG via discretization and importance sampling is interesting (R2).
- Clearly states the difference from standard VCG and explains why exact VCG is intractable for LLM outputs (R3).
- Numerical experiments and the motivating example provide interesting insights (R3). R2 and R4 also praise the general approach, though R2 finds the contribution incremental and R4 notes missing analyses.

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty and incremental contribution** (R1, R2). R2 argues that MOSAIC is only a natural approximation of VCG: it discretizes the full reply space into finite sampled points, which does not change the nature of the VCG mechanism. The other contributions (context-aware π_con, payment offset) are described as case-by-case and straightforward. R1 states the mechanism uses conventional approaches. *Contrast*: R3 finds the topic novel and the theoretical results sound.
2. **Insufficient experimental comparisons** (R1, R2, R4). The only comparison is between contextual-prompting MOSAIC and naive MOSAIC; there is no baseline comparison with other mechanisms (e.g., standard RLHF, rejection sampling, or other auction designs). R2 suggests comparing directly with VCG when it is applicable. No ablation studies or real-world advertiser data are used (R4).
3. **Limited generalizability across models**: experiments only use Llama-2-7b-chat-hf. R1 suggests evaluating on Llama-3-8B, T5, Mistral-7B, and also closed-source models (since the mechanism only requires API access). R1 also asks how different prompts affect performance.
4. **Scalability and computational cost not analyzed** (R4). No analysis of how the mechanism scales with the number of candidates or advertisers in terms of memory and runtime. The importance sampling step may become a bottleneck. R2 further raises the concern that when advertiser reward r(x,y) is unbounded, the probability of sampling a large r is small, so sampling M candidates may yield only small values, making MOSAIC perform poorly.
5. **Robustness to misreporting and gaming** (R4). While strategy-proofness is proven for truthful reporting, the paper does not address collusion, noisy reporting, or other sophisticated misreporting strategies. R2 also questions whether sub-optimal solutions (due to finite M) might break strategyproofness (citing Nisan & Ronen).
6. **Bias and fairness not explored** (R4). The choice of reference LLM and advertiser reward functions could amplify existing biases or introduce new ones. The paper does not discuss fairness or diversity of the generated content.
7. **Lack of theoretical explanation for ref vs. context-aware performance gap** (R3). The paper provides only intuitive explanations for why contextual-prompting outperforms the reference LLM (π_ref) as proposal distribution, despite Corollary 4.1 suggesting π_ref is a natural choice. No theoretical insight is given. R3 also asks how one should introduce contextual information more generally.
8. **Hyperparameter τ not analyzed** (R4). The trade-off between advertiser reward and alignment with π_ref (the KL term) is controlled by τ, but no quantitative analysis or guidance for setting τ is provided.
9. **Convergence rate and variance of the importance sampling estimator missing** (R4). The paper claims convergence as M increases but does not give the rate of convergence or the variance formula.
10. **Missing discussion of practical constraints** (R3). The framework does not consider advertiser budgets, ROI constraints, or maximum output length. R3 asks whether MOSAIC can incorporate such features.
11. **Advantage over direct VCG implementation using rejection sampling not clarified** (R2). Since the optimal distribution in Equation (2) can be sampled via rejection sampling when r(x,y) is bounded, and π_con can be used to reduce variance, R2 questions what advantage MOSAIC offers over that approach.
12. **Presentation issues** (R2): The definition of π_i is misplaced (first used in Line 146, reappears in Line 419 without reintroduction). Section 1.2 lists 7 contributions that are described as incremental or purely technical. Notation π_r is used without definition (Line 242, should be π_ref). In Lines 313–314, log(π_ref/π_gen) should be included inside the exp(·). Appendix section titles use "Details from Section xx" instead of the conventional "Details in Section xx".
