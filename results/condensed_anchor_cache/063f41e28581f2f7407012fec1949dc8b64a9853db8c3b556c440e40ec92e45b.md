## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Prototype-based Optimal Transport (POT) for OOD detection, using optimal transport costs from test inputs to ID prototypes and virtual outliers generated by linear extrapolation as an OOD score. The reviews are mixed: two reviewers (scores 8 and 6) find the approach novel, the insights clear, and the extensive experiments convincing. Three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) raise fundamental concerns about the practical feasibility of the method, its incremental contribution relative to existing distance-based approaches, and the lack of theoretical depth.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses the important problem of OOD detection and introduces a new perspective by combining prototype-based learning with optimal transport. (R1, R2, R4)
- The use of optimal transport to measure distribution discrepancy between test inputs and ID prototypes is well-supported theoretically and provides a principled OOD score. (R4, R2)
- Generating virtual outliers via linear extrapolation to regularize the decision boundary is a clever technique to handle near-OOD data, and the insight behind it is clearly explained. (R2, R3, R4)
- The experimental evaluation is exhaustive: POT is compared against 21 existing methods across multiple standard datasets (including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet), and the results are convincing, showing strong or close-to-best performance. (R1, R2, R3)
- The ablation study demonstrates the importance of virtual outliers and the method’s robustness to hyperparameters (extrapolation coefficient, regularization coefficient, test batch size). (R3)
- The method can be used with different pretraining schemes and even with network weights as ID prototypes when training data are unavailable. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Practicality of using test-set statistics for OT and virtual outliers**: The construction of the transport plan (Eqs. 2-3) and the generation of virtual outliers both require access to the entire test batch (e.g., computing the empirical mean of test samples). This is unrealistic under the standard OOD detection setting, where test data arrives sequentially or in an online/streaming manner. The method cannot be applied without knowing the test set distribution in advance, and recomputing ν for each new batch introduces computational overhead and scalability issues. (R1, R4)  
- **Effectiveness of simple linear extrapolation for generating virtual outliers**: The linear extrapolation (based on the mean of test features) may not adequately represent the OOD region, especially for near-OOD samples that have features similar to ID data. The paper lacks discussion on the interpretability and representativeness of this heuristic. (R1)  
- **Missing ablation on Near-OOD datasets**: The ablation study on virtual outliers is only shown for Far-OOD datasets (Figure 3); there is no such analysis for Near-OOD datasets, which is critical for validating the method’s claimed advantage for near-OOD detection. (R3)  
- **Incremental contribution and lack of theoretical grounding**: The method is essentially a distance-based approach using optimal transport as a distance metric. The paper does not provide a deeper understanding of why OT is superior to existing distance-based or uncertainty-based methods (e.g., likelihood estimation, reconstruction-based autoencoders). Similar ideas of using OT for OOD characterization have been explored with more concrete theoretical properties (e.g., [4] cited by R5). Without this insight, the contribution is incremental. (R5)  
- **Missing computational cost analysis**: The paper does not analyze the computational overhead of OT calculations, especially when using the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm on large-scale datasets. No comparison of execution time with other OOD methods is provided, making it difficult to assess practical applicability in resource-constrained environments. A breakdown of time spent on feature extraction vs. OT computation is needed. (R2)  
- **Missing analysis of robustness to feature encoder**: The method’s performance depends on the chosen feature encoder, but no study investigates how sensitive POT is to different encoders or how large the impact of the encoder is. (R3)  
- **Unnecessary constraint w<0 in Eq. (8)**: The constraint w<0 is introduced without justification, and the parameter analysis does not explore positive values of w to provide a comprehensive understanding of its role. (R2)  
- **Lack of explanation for experimental results**: Ablation and hyperparameter studies only describe phenomena (e.g., why certain parameter choices lead to better performance, why the method performs better on some datasets than others) without deeper analysis of the score distributions or the underlying mechanisms, especially in relation to virtual outliers. (R2)  
- **Unclear validation-set construction and batch-size effect**: It is unclear how the validation set is built in the OT formulation, particularly the role of the test batch size (Section 4.3). If a different transport map is computed for each test batch size, how is the OOD threshold determined, and does it depend on the proportion of ID/OOD data in the batch? (R5)  
- **Missing comparisons with latest papers**: The method should be compared with the most recent OOD detection works. (R1)  
- **Minor presentation issues**:  
  - The symbols \(\mathcal{P}\) and \(\mathcal{M}\) in Line 241 are not formally defined. (R3)  
  - Eq. (9) has a typo: the second “+” should be “:”. (R3)  
  - In Table 1, the Textures FPR95 for NAC-UE should be underlined (second best) not boldfaced. (R3)  
  - Table 2 should also underline the second best results to be consistent with Table 1. (R3)  
- **Question on latent space vs. representation space**: The paper claims POT measures differences in the latent space, but the features are extracted from a DNN feature extractor. The distinction from methods like DDE is unclear. (R4)  
- **Scalability to small OOD sets**: The method’s performance when the OOD set is small is not evaluated, and the effect of validation set size is not studied. (R5)
