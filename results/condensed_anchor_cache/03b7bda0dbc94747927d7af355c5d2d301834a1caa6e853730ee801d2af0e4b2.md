## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces RAR (Retrieving And Ranking), a method that combines CLIP’s retrieval ability with MLLMs’ fine-grained differentiation for visual recognition. A CLIP-based retriever stores category features in external memory; during inference the top-k candidates are retrieved and then ranked by an MLLM to produce the final prediction. RAR aims to address CLIP’s weak fine-grained classification and MLLM’s limitations with large vocabularies and fine-grained categorization. Experiments cover fine-grained classification, few-shot recognition, and zero-shot object detection.

### Strengths
- The RAR framework is well-written, straightforward, and easy to understand. (R1, R3, R4)
- The methodology is clear and well‑founded; the use of CLIP‑based retrieval augmented by MLLM ranking reduces dependency on context windows, a known limitation when handling large vocabularies. (R2)
- Extensive experiments are conducted across multiple benchmarks (fine‑grained classification, few‑shot recognition, zero‑shot object detection) with thorough ablation studies and evaluation of each component. (R1, R3, R4)
- RAR achieves strong performance improvements, particularly on fine‑grained benchmarks (e.g., Table 1) and object detection on LVIS (Table 4). (R1, R4)
- The approach is simple to implement and extends naturally to object detection as a form of post‑processing, offering insights for future work. (R1)
- The paper provides complete evaluations and ablation studies (e.g., on SFT, retrieval strategies) to understand the impact of proposed components. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- Limited novelty: the method uses off‑the‑shelf models (CLIP, LLaVA) without substantial modifications, and the retrieving‑and‑ranking strategy is already adopted in other RAG works (e.g., Re2G, RankRAG) without specific adaptations tailored to fine‑grained recognition. (R1, R3, R4) One reviewer (R2, score 6) did not raise novelty concerns and instead praised the design, indicating disagreement on this point.
- Practical necessity of MLLMs is questionable: smaller expert models could achieve similar performance, and the MLLM is used only for re‑ranking rather than providing open‑set or interpretable responses. (R1)
- Fine‑tuning for ranking is insufficiently analyzed: only tested on FGVC‑Aircraft and Stanford‑Cars; datasets with a severe domain gap could cause catastrophic forgetting. More discussion is needed. (R1)
- Error analysis is missing: no detailed analysis on retrieval failures or ranking misclassifications, which would help identify where RAR needs refinement, especially for subtle category differentiation. (R2)
- The method does not fundamentally improve CLIP or MLLM’s classification abilities; SFT and in‑context learning only improve output format, not classification ability (evidence: Table 5 shows similar SFT effects across datasets). (R3)
- Performance gains over strong baselines are limited:
  - Comparisons in Table 2 are only against CLIP+KNN, suggesting RAR improves CLIP rather than MLLM, and performance is worse than sub‑optimal results. (R3)
  - Improvements over FineR in Table 1 are not significant, and the large gains in Table 4 (LVIS object detection) may be due to the small category count (1,000 categories) rather than addressing large vocabulary challenges. (R3)
  - On Flower102, the MLLM ranking undermines CLIP’s capabilities (Table 2). (R3)
- Upper limit of RAR is set by CLIP’s retrieval recall; if the correct category is not in the top‑k, MLLM cannot recover. Poor MLLM performance can also degrade results. Ablation experiments under different k settings are needed; in‑context learning could be used if SFT is too time‑consuming. (R3)
- Missing baselines: vanilla LLaVA results are not included in Table 1; for zero‑shot image classification, a comparison to vanilla CLIP using only category names is missing. (R4)
- The complexity of brute‑force vs. HNSW retrieval when scaling to millions of embeddings should be quantified. (R2)
