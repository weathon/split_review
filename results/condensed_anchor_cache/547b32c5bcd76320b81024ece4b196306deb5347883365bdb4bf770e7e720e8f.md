## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes two zeroth-order policy gradient algorithms (ZPG and ZBCPG) for RLHF that avoid learning a reward model by directly estimating value function differences from human preferences using an inverse link function. Theoretical convergence guarantees and sample complexity bounds are provided for general preference models and stochastic MDPs. Reviewer opinions diverge strongly: three reviewers rate the paper 8 (positive, with significant weaknesses), while a minority reviewer (R2) rates it 3, arguing the idea is not novel, the paper is not RLHF-specific, and lacks empirical validation. All reviewers note the absence of experiments.

### Strengths

- Novel method for estimating value function differences using the inverse of the preference function (R1, R4).
- Proves **local convergence** for general (non-deterministic) MDPs (R1, R3).
- Extends beyond the Bradley-Terry model to a general class of link functions and handles stochastic dynamics (R3, R4).
- Provides theoretical sample complexity and convergence rates for both ZPG and ZBCPG (R2, R3, R4).
- Paper is well laid out, clearly written, and includes a helpful discussion of technical difficulties in the proofs (R1, R4).
- The presented theoretical results sound plausible (R4). ZBCPG is motivated by computational/memory/distributed aspects (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Local convergence only** with sample complexity \(\epsilon^{-5}\); Xie et al. (2024) achieves **global convergence** with \(\epsilon^{-2}\) in a more restricted setting. The paper needs a compelling justification for why local convergence is sufficient, especially in high-dimensional policy spaces where the convergence region may be very small (R1, R4).
- **Practical applicability is questionable**: the algorithms assume \(M\) human raters are available online to rate \(N\) pairs of trajectories at each gradient step, making them hard to deploy compared to offline DPO or classic RLHF (R3, R4). This limitation is not sufficiently discussed (R4).
- **More costly than online DPO**: ZPG requires multiple perturbations per update, increasing the number of trajectory samples (forward passes) significantly. Zeroth-order methods may be slower than gradient-based methods for very large models like LLMs (R3).
- **No empirical evaluation** – not even toy or tabular experiments. The paper would be strengthened by experiments in simple stochastic domains to illustrate where DPO would fail but ZPG would work, or comparisons with online DPO, REINFORCE, and PPO (R2, R3, R4). A minority reviewer (R2) states that the lack of experiments makes the paper “not very valuable to the RLHF or deep RL community”.
- **Lack of novelty** according to one reviewer (R2): the idea of applying random parameter perturbations to construct gradient updates has been extensively studied in deep RL (e.g., Salisman et al., 2017, “Evolution Strategies as a Scalable Alternative to Reinforcement Learning”), and is not RLHF-specific. The paper does not discuss additional structure that RLHF might provide (R2).
- **Missing literature**: reference to Salisman et al. 2017 and other zeroth-order RL works is absent (R2).
- **No KL regularization**, which is standard in RLHF methods. The paper does not discuss whether it is no longer required or what practical implications follow (R4).
- **Overclaims and insufficient discussion of limitations**: the paper positions itself as addressing drawbacks of DPO and classic RLHF but does not honestly compare limitations (e.g., needing online human raters, no empirical support) (R4). The claimed generalization “beyond BT model” is weak; can it handle preference models like those in Munos et al. “Nash learning from human feedback”? (R4).
- **Proof and notation issues** (R4):
  - Assumptions 2 and 3 use the same symbol \(L\) for the Lipschitz constant of \(\sigma^{-1}\) and the smoothness constant of \(V\); these are distinct and should be separated, as the proofs confuse them.
  - In Theorems 1 and 2, \(M\) is expressed as a function of \(D\), but \(D\) is never defined (it is critical as the number of human raters).
  - Corollary 1 is not proven, even in the appendix.
  - The telescoping argument in line 435 uses \(\theta^*\) without derivation; the corresponding appendix equation uses \(\theta_T\).
  - The end of the proof of Theorem 1 (lines 1104–1134) is very unclear: the purpose of Eq. 1106, how it leads to Eq. 1110, how Eq. 1122 relates to 1107, how the two cases cover all gradient regimes, and how bounds are combined (max, sum, other) – all need clarification.
  - Lemma 2: the introduction of the trim operator should be explained, as the result holds without it.
  - Typo in line 305: gradient always zero due to incorrect index.
  - Typo in line 977: missing parenthesis.
  - Line 1214: missing term or line should be removed.
  - Lines 1230–1241: why factor 3 appears is unexplained.
  - The claim that the setting encompasses convex MDPs (line 163) needs elaboration: the paper does not assume sum-decomposable return, but why does that imply convexity in occupancy measure?
  - Link function properties: should we assume \(\sigma(x) = 1 - \sigma(-x)\)? Examples of Lipschitz constant \(L\) for the logistic case would help.
- **Questions on stochastic transitions in LLMs**: the paper focuses on LLMs but claims advantage over DPO for stochastic MDPs; in LLM applications, where do stochastic transitions appear and how does the framework handle them? (R4)
- **Questions on global vs. local information in practical DPO**: in practice, DPO also uses local information due to function approximation, so the claimed distinction between global and local models may be less clear; the discussion should be updated (R3).
- **Non-BT examples**: the paper could give motivating examples of non-BT link functions to strengthen the contribution (R3).
