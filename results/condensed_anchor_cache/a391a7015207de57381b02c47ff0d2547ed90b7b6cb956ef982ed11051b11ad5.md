## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically investigates the effect of training with repeated examples (multiple epochs) versus single-use data in transformer models. On three algorithmically generated math tasks – greatest common divisor (GCD), modular multiplication, and matrix eigenvalues – the authors show that for a fixed training budget, models trained on smaller sets of repeated examples outperform models trained on larger sets of unique examples. They also propose a two-set training procedure (repeated small subset plus fresh samples) that yields faster learning and better performance. The authors suggest that repetition benefits can outweigh data diversity and that learning can emerge only when repetition is used. The work uses a controlled, synthetic setup to study the interplay between memorization and generalization.

### Strengths
1. The experiments use three algorithmically generated math tasks (GCD, modular multiplication, matrix eigenvalues) providing a well-controlled environment where memorization and algorithmic learning can be clearly identified. Setup descriptions are clear and results are well presented. (R1, R2)
2. The empirical finding that repeated examples can be beneficial has practical implications. The two-set training result is interesting and novel. (R1, R2, R4)
3. The paper addresses an important question: why machine learning typically suffers from repetitions while human/animal learning depends on them. It provides counter-examples that deserve better understanding. (R2)
4. The study uses the transformer architecture, which is ubiquitous in practice, maximizing the chance that findings and the proposed protocol are relevant in practical settings. (R2)
5. The paper is clearly written, easy to follow, and the conclusions are stated clearly. (R2, R3)
6. Experiments are thorough for the chosen datasets, showing good evidence for the claims within that domain. (R3)
7. The proposed two-set training raises interesting questions about the role of memorization for future research. (R3)

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited generality due to synthetic math-only tasks.** All experiments are on algorithmically generated synthetic data (GCD, modular multiplication, eigenvalues), not real-world datasets. Reviewers question whether the findings (especially the two-set training success) transfer to more realistic settings such as natural language, vision, or NLP fine-tuning. The three tasks are all math problems, and the specific domain may influence the benefit of repetition. (R1, R2, R3, R4) R4 specifically notes the models use specialized tokenizers for numbers and have no language capabilities, making claims about “language models” unsupported. Suggested alternative: frame as general ML problem with many architectures, or add realistic LM fine-tuning experiments. (R4)
2. **Overstated claims about emergence and benefits.** The paper makes strong statements (“models trained on smaller sets of repeated examples outperform models trained on larger sets of single-use examples”, “learning emerges through repetition”) that should be tempered. There is a clear tradeoff between repetition and diversity needed to avoid overfitting (Fig. 2 shows non-monotonous relation). In GCD, a large number of unique samples is still required (Fig. 4 shows balance is hard to strike). The eigenvalue problem is the hardest but uses the smallest model, achieving only 4/30 success rate; dedicated plots for this task are lacking. (R2) R4 adds that claims about “emergence” may change with model architecture, model size, or hyperparameters; the experiments use a single fixed transformer and hyperparameter set. (R4)
3. **Lack of comparison to baselines.** The two-set training is not compared with any curriculum learning baseline (R3, R4). R3 also notes the idea is not new (training over many epochs is standard in most applications; the novelty would be in NLP applications, which are not tested). R3 further suggests comparing to continual learning / catastrophic forgetting methods and cites spaced repetition literature (Amiri et al., “Repeat before Forgetting”). (R3)
4. **Confounding factor of undertraining.** The classical decomposition of test accuracy into training accuracy + generalization gap may explain many observations. With small data repetitions, the model may be undertrained (training accuracy itself is low). Adding such analysis could make the paper clearer but would reduce surprise. R4 remains unconvinced after rebuttal that undertraining is excluded as a confounding factor. (R4)
5. **Accuracy metric interpretation issues.** The accuracy metric used (e.g., in Fig. 2) is not easy to interpret, especially when test loss explodes but accuracy stays fixed. Appendix A provides some explanation but on the eigenvalue problem rather than GCD, where the issue is most relevant. Fig. 6 shows accuracy decreasing when overfitting occurs. (R2)
6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and no guidance.** The results in Fig. 5 are unstable as a function of repeated set size and probability. Choosing these hyperparameters in practice is expensive (iterating over many combinations). No intuition is provided for how to select them, though there seems to be a common shape. (R3)
7. **Missing discussion of alternative interpretations and related work.** The conclusion that transformers must distinguish seen/unseen examples (“deja vu / jamais vu”) is puzzling. An alternative explanation is that repetition provides a clear direction in the loss landscape by breaking symmetry, as in recent theoretical work (Dandi et al., 2024; Arnaboldi et al., 2024). (R2) Also, the connection to continual learning / catastrophic forgetting is not discussed. (R3)
8. **Unresolved questions and missing ablations.**  
   * Do findings hold for tasks not requiring learning an algorithm (e.g., vision where memorization may suffice)? Can the procedure work when curriculum learning is effective? (R2)  
   * Could two-set training be performed by showing only repeated samples first, then diverse samples? That would support a loss-landscape interpretation and clarify differences from grokking. (R2)  
   * How general is the observation that accuracy is never affected by overfitting in GCD? (R2)  
   * Could the beneficial effect of repetition be achieved by a better learning rate decay scheme, given that later steps have smaller updates on seen examples? (R4)  
   * What criteria indicate when repeated data or two-set training will be beneficial? Does “emergence” only happen at scale? (R1)

**Reviewer disagreement note:** While all reviewers agree on the limited generality and synthetic nature, R2 is more positive (score 6, after discussion, acknowledging the exploration and clarity) and provides an alternative theoretical explanation, whereas R4 remains more critical (score 5 after rebuttal, still unconvinced about the setting and undertraining). R3 also gives a lower contribution rating (2) and questions novelty. R1 raises concerns but overall gives a 6.
