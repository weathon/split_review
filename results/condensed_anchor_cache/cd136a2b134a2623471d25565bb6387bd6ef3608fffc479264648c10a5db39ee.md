## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MolJO, a gradient-based framework for structure-based molecule optimization (SBMO) using Bayesian Flow Networks (BFNs) to jointly optimize continuous (atomic coordinates) and discrete (atom types) variables. It introduces gradient guidance via an energy function and a backward correction strategy with a sliding window to balance exploration and exploitation. On CrossDocked2020, MolJO reports a Success Rate of 51.3%, Vina Dock -9.05, SA 0.78, with 4× improvement over gradient-based baselines and 2× "Me-Better" ratio over 3D baselines. Experiments cover multi-objective optimization, R-group optimization, and scaffold hopping. However, reviewers raised major concerns about clarity, novelty, methodological rigor, and experimental design, with scores ranging from 3 to 6 (mean 4.75).

### Strengths
- **Technical innovation for joint optimization:** The paper addresses the challenging problem of applying gradient guidance to both continuous and discrete molecular variables in SBMO. Reviewers note this is important and under-studied (R2, R3), and the method to handle discrete atom types through BFNs' continuous latent space is novel (R1, R3). R4 agrees the idea is good.
- **Strong empirical performance:** The method achieves state-of-the-art results on CrossDocked2020: 51.3% Success Rate, more than 4× improvement over gradient-based counterparts (e.g., DiffSBDD), and 2× "Me-Better" ratio over 3D baselines (R1, R3). R1 specifically highlights the significance of these gains.
- **Novel backward correction mechanism:** R1 and R2 highlight the backward correction method as novel, with R1 noting it provides a practical explore-exploit trade-off. R2 explicitly calls it "novel" and R3 finds it promising. However, R4 considers it a slight modification of the backward correction in [3] (a related work on SBDD with BFNs), noting this disagreement is preserved.
- **Comprehensive evaluation across tasks:** The method is tested on a wide range of optimization setups including multi-objective optimization, R-group optimization, and scaffold hopping, with a comprehensive list of baselines (R2, R4). R1 notes versatility for real drug design tasks and effective balance of multiple objectives while generating valid structures.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and clarity across multiple aspects.** The paper is not self-contained; readers unfamiliar with BFNs will struggle (R1, R3). R3 lists numerous unclear points: what θ represents (pointwise parameters vs distribution?), contradiction between Eq (1) (distribution over θ) and Eq (2) (deterministic updates), why guidance is applied over θ when the energy is defined over clean samples, and the relationship between y, z, and θ in Proposition 4.1. R2 finds notations unclear (parameter φ, time-dependent energy function, σ′). R4 finds sentences like Line 182 ("guidance aligns with the generative process") vague and incomprehensible.
  - Key concepts introduced without definitions: e_v used in Line 233 but defined later in Line 240 (R3). The "Surprising as it may seem, this is mathematically grounded" claim lacks justification (R3). The energy proxies (how defined, trained, evaluated) are only briefly mentioned in the last paragraph, despite being central to the method (R4). Figure 2 is unclear and does not support the claimed "explore-exploit trade-off" (R4). R3 emphasizes that even if a full BFN description is unrealistic, the main paper should introduce the necessary components to make the used equations understandable.
  - R2 suggests a broader discussion of related work on conditional generation in diffusion/flow models. R1 also requests comparisons with discrete diffusion and generative flow nets.

- **Related work and novelty are questioned, with strong criticism from multiple reviewers.** R2 states the paper misses a large chunk of molecular optimization literature (citing a review paper [1]), and the claim of being the "first proof-of-concept for gradient-based optimization of continuous-discrete variables" is inappropriate because: (1) scope is very narrow, (2) other works have done it [2, 3], (3) the "gradient" for discrete variables is actually a weighting (related to derivative-free optimization/sampling like sequential Monte Carlo). R4 adds that BFNs have already been applied to 3D molecule modeling [2] and structure-based drug design [3, 4]; notably, [4] is not cited and is very strongly related. Since BFNs can be seen as SDEs [1], guidance application is well-known, and the paper should compare with direct SDE guidance. R4 characterizes the work as "finetuning" of [3], with the generative backbone taken without modification, backward correction as a slight modification of [3], and the main addition being guidance application. R3 does not question novelty, but R2 and R4's criticisms are preserved.

- **Methodological and mathematical rigor is insufficient.**
  - Line 187 definition of π(θ_i | θ_{i-1}) is heuristic—just multiplication of two normalized densities, which is not normalized (should have ∝ instead of =) (R3, R4). Regular guidance is derived from Bayes' rule; this formulation lacks elaboration (R4). R3 questions: "Where are the original Gaussians from line 207 coming from? Why are those the correct 'unguided kernels' p_φ?"
  - Proposition 4.1 is unclear: what parts are assumptions vs claims? What does "originally" μ_i follows a Gaussian mean? (R4). The derivation uses a first-order Taylor expansion, but the approximation error and its dependence on discretization are not discussed; the paper claims "analytic expressions" are derived, which is misleading (R3).
  - The gradient for discrete variables is not a true gradient but a weighting (R2). The chain rule in Eq (8) and the dependence of E on μ vs h are unclear (R2). In Eq (12), by linearity of Gaussian, the difference between predicting from θ_{i-1} vs θ_{i-2} is not explained (R2).
  - The sampling process without backward correction is not explained, making the forward process unclear (R3). After reading Section 4.1, how samples are generated is unclear (R3).
  - Unanswered technical questions that overlap with criticisms: What is φ and how is it learned? (R2), How is the time-dependent energy function learned since only t=0 properties are known? (R2), Are the guided kernel expressions increasingly exact as updates become smaller? (R3), Does the backward correction connect to resampling/restart sampling? [1, 2] (R2).

- **Experimental design has critical gaps and unfair comparisons.**
  - **Missing computational analysis:** No reporting of model sizes, training time, or sampling time for the proposed method vs baselines (R4). R1 notes insufficient discussion of how backward correction window size affects computational costs, and no clear comparison of computational resources versus other methods.
  - **Incomplete baselines and unfair settings:** R4 strongly objects to the lack of optimization-based baselines like AutoGrow4 [5], which reportedly works significantly better than RGA. The beam search strategy is used only for the proposed method; R4 questions whether other generative methods would improve if equipped with beam search. R1 requests comparisons with discrete diffusion and generative flow nets.
  - **Energy proxies create unfair advantage:** The energy proxies (for Affinity, QED, SA) are trained separately; R4 questions their size (as large as TargetDiff?) and sampling time, and notes that simply adding an energy proxy to any baseline would likely improve performance. The paper does not properly discuss how energy proxies are defined and trained; Appendix C hints at training but Equation (21) is incomplete (how is θ defined for training? assuming p_data samples, how are they transformed to θ?) (R4).
  - **Reproducibility concerns:** Code is submitted but no trained model checkpoints provided, so parameter count, sampling time, and results cannot be verified (R4). R1 notes the method's performance appears sensitive to hyperparameters like guidance scale and correction window size, but no systematic analysis or guidelines are provided.
  - **Specific issues in results presentation:** Table 4 does not report Vina Dock (R4). Figure 5 lacks Vina Dock and error bars/statistical significance (R4). Line 366 (Figure 3) is described as showing distribution shift, but this is merely a sanity check, not a strong insight (R4). Line 522 has a typo: "gradiant" should be "gradient" (R4).
  - **Molecule size restriction:** Line 420 restricts generated molecule size to match reference molecules for fair comparison. R4 asks whether this restriction was applied to all baselines or if baseline numbers were taken from publications without this modification, and whether Vina Dock scores are comparable.
  - **Limited objective scope:** The method is only validated on three objectives (Affinity, QED, SA) despite the wide range of important molecular properties in drug discovery; crucial biological objectives are unexplored (R1). R1 questions whether the joint optimization can extend beyond these three properties.

- **Reviewer disagreement on contributions:** R1 and R3 are more positive about the novelty and contribution (R1 score 6, R3 score 5), while R2 (score 3) and R4 (score 5 but with strong criticisms) question whether the contributions are sufficient for a full paper, with R4 explicitly characterizing the work as "finetuning" of [3].
