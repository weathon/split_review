## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes NeuroMFA, a new metric derived from multifractal analysis of neuron interaction networks in LLMs. The metric quantifies the heterogeneity and organization of the network’s local structure via the width and peak of the multifractal spectrum. The authors show that NeuroMFA correlates with behavioral metrics of emergence (e.g., accuracy on LAMBADA, PIQA) and increases during training. They argue this provides a structural proxy for emergent abilities, moving beyond raw model size. The reviews diverge significantly: one reviewer (Rating 8, confidence 5) finds the work novel, thorough, and of interest to the community, while the other two (Ratings 5 and 6, confidence 2–3) raise fundamental concerns about the metric’s added value, conceptual framing, and practical utility.

### Strengths
- Writing is clear and pleasant; the paper is well contextualized and explains the new metric well.
- Moving the discussion away from only scaling model size is important.
- The metric is well defined, making it replicable.
- The paper gives a genuinely interesting direction by bridging neuroscience network analysis with LLM internal structure.
- The analyses and experimentation are thorough (noted by Reviewers 2 and 3 in general).

### Weaknesses
**Conceptual and framing issues**
- The use of the term “emergence” is inconsistent with its accepted definition. The metric actually quantifies self-organization or network regularity, not emergence directly. The title “Assessment of the Degree of Emergence Across Various Datasets” is misleading because the metric produces the same curve for every dataset (Reviewer 3).
- The paper interchanges “accuracy” and “emergent abilities” inconsistently, harming clarity (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed “degree of emergence” is one-dimensional. It would be more interesting to link specific network properties to specific emerging skills. A single global metric may be insufficient to capture complex reorganization (Reviewer 3).
- The paper acknowledges that NeuroMFA shows only correlation, not causation, with downstream performance. This limitation should be stated in the main paper, not only in the conclusion (Reviewer 1).

**Methodological clarity and missing details**
- It is unclear whether the analysis uses static attention weights or effective interaction conditioned on specific input samples. This should be clarified at the start of the methods (Reviewer 2).
- The calculation of |P(i,j)| (shortest path) is not explained – it is ambiguous whether +1 is added per edge along the path (Reviewer 3).
- The description of the network in terms of blocks and layers is unnecessary; a simple graph with \(w_{ij}\) values suffices (Reviewer 3).
- A reference is missing on page 39 of the appendix (Reviewer 3).
- Some mathematics should be in the main text rather than the appendix (Reviewer 3).
- High-level intuition for each part of the method (e.g., why multifractal decomposition is interesting) is needed before jumping into details. Currently the methods section begins with details without motivation (Reviewer 2).

**Empirical validation and comparative value**
- No systematic measurement of the metric in untrained (randomly initialized) networks is provided. If the metric is not zero at initialization, it should be standardized to zero to isolate training effects (Reviewer 2).
- The metric is only validated through its correlation with behavioral metrics. It is unclear why one should use this structural metric instead of the behavioral metrics themselves, especially when they disagree (Reviewer 1).
- The paper compares with the averaged weighted degree distribution in the appendix (Figures 5 and 24) and finds that simpler metric also correlates with emergence. If many regularity properties (irregularity, heterogeneity, average weighted degree) all increase similarly during training, what additional value does NeuroMFA provide? (Reviewer 3).
- In Appendix D.2, the 410M model has lower accuracy but a higher NeuroMFA score than the 1B and 1.4B models. Since the metric is normalized with initial training, it cannot be used to compare across models, whereas traditional behavioral metrics can (Reviewer 1).
- Figure 8 shows that NeuroMFA scores are lower at the beginning of training than at the end, and model size is only recovered later. It is unclear how this addresses the original problem with Fig 1b, since other metrics also show low scores after 500 epochs (Reviewer 1).
- Figure 16 shows that after epoch 6000 or 8000, the metric decreases. Does this imply a decline in model performance? Similarly, metric values differ between models in that figure—does that imply one model outperforms the other based solely on the metric? A clear comparative analysis connecting metric trends with performance is needed (Reviewer 3).
- Evidence that the property is not simply due to amount of training is lacking. For example, if an LLM trained on a dataset yields low behavioral metrics, would NeuroMFA still increase? (Reviewer 1).
- A consolidated graph overlaying performance across all datasets would be clearer than presenting separate figures (Reviewer 3).

**Presentation issues**
- Text in Figure 1 is too small, with white space that could be better utilized (Reviewers 1 and 2 both note font issues in Figure 6).
- Mixing serif and sans serif fonts across figures should be avoided (Reviewer 2).
- The strong emphasis on the word “emergence” may be off-putting; “development of skills” might be more appropriate (Reviewer 2).
- Figure 6 is nearly unreadable due to small font (Reviewers 1 and 2).

**Reviewer contrast:** One reviewer (Reviewer 2, rating 8) considers the metric novel and the analysis thorough, expressing enthusiasm for its potential. The other two (Reviewers 1 and 3, ratings 5 and 6) question the practical utility, conceptual framing, and added value over simpler metrics, and request stronger empirical justification.
