## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates reinforcement learning over text representations for molecular design. Through extensive experiments on 25 tasks (including protein docking), it explores design choices such as text grammar, model architecture, pretraining, and RL algorithms. The best configuration is presented as ChemRLformer, an RL-based molecular design algorithm that achieves state-of-the-art performance and is claimed to be more straightforward than prior work. The authors provide thorough ablation studies and derive insights for practitioners.

### Strengths
- High societal importance of molecular design for drug discovery and materials science; RL could have major impact.
- Extensive experimental evaluation on 25 molecule design tasks, including computationally complex protein docking simulations, covering three different datasets.
- Well-written, clear, and easy to follow; strong motivation and organization.
- Comprehensive ablation study examining design choices (problem representation, neural architecture, pretraining) and including comparisons to RL algorithms beyond REINFORCE (one reviewer explicitly notes that other RL algorithms are tested).
- Table 1 provides a helpful summary of different algorithms for text-based molecule design.
- Technical soundness: rigorous comparison of design choices and thorough discussion of results (reviewer 4).
- Practical contribution: provides valuable insights and recommendations for practitioners in molecular discovery.

### Weaknesses
- **Choice of REINFORCE and RL design choices**: Multiple reviewers question why modern algorithms (PPO, TROP) were not used. Reviewer 1 notes REINFORCE is susceptible to local optima and sample-inefficient; Reviewer 2 points out the absence of automatic entropy tuning (e.g., Haarnoja et al. 2018) and suggests RLHF as an alternative. **Contrast**: one reviewer (Reviewer 3) acknowledges that the ablation does include other RL algorithms, but the paper still lacks a thorough justification for the primary use of REINFORCE and does not compare to recent advances (e.g., PPO, automatic entropy tuning, RLHF).
- **Discount factor of 1**: Section 4.1 uses a discount rate of 1 without justification. A smaller discount could encourage exploration of diverse structures (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing related work**: Reviewer 3 identifies that relevant works—MolGPT (Transformer without RL), Taiga (Transformer + REINFORCE for molecular generation), and an offline RL approach (biorxiv 2023.11.29.569328)—are not cited. This raises novelty concerns, especially given the similarity to Taiga. Reviewer 4 also notes that originality is somewhat constrained.
- **Insufficient backbone LM details**: Minimal specifications for the language model (architecture GPT vs BERT, tokenizer standard or trained, pretraining details). Reviewer 2 requests discussion and asks why GPT-style was chosen over BERT-style (cf. Cowen-Rivers et al., 2022).
- **No scaling analysis**: The effect of increasing transformer size on performance is not explored (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons and evaluation details**:
  - No direct comparison against baselines such as MolGPT, Taiga, or REINVENT (Reviewer 3).
  - Column labels “Div.” and “Red.” in plots are not explained (Reviewer 3).
  - No reported scores for individual reward components (QED, SA, similarity); only combined reward is shown (Reviewer 3).
  - No visual examples of generated molecules; reward hacking is mentioned but no verification of chemical validity is provided (Reviewer 3).
  - Figure 2 (a,b) contains typos: “pertaining” for “pretraining” and incorrect plot labels (Reviewer 4).
  - Figure 2 CheMBL results show both high diversity and high redundancy simultaneously, which is confusing; caption needs clarification (Reviewer 2).
  - Results display could be improved; improvement magnitude may appear deceptive (Reviewer 2).
- **Lack of domain expert evaluation**: Only computational metrics are used; Reviewer 1 suggests incorporating qualitative feedback from chemists to validate the practical value of generated molecules.
- **Ambiguities in presentation**:
  - “MoLRL” is introduced but not clearly distinguished from ChemRLformer (Reviewer 4).
  - How the “pretraining only” results are computed (zero-shot?) is not explained (Reviewer 4).
  - The transition function induced by text representation: are constraints hard-coded or learned? (Reviewer 4).
  - Docking speed is surprisingly fast without explanation; is acceleration due to fixed target protein? (Reviewer 3).
- **Confusion over replay buffer usage**: References to Mnih et al. (2013) for replay buffer in an on-policy algorithm are puzzling; it is unclear whether samples from previous iterations are reused (Reviewer 2).
