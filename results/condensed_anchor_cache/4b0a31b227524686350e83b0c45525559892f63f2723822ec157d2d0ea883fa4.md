## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Topological Evolution Rate (TopER), a low-dimensional graph embedding method based on topological data analysis that uses linear regression on the number of edges versus nodes induced by filtrations. Reviewers generally find the approach novel, interpretable, and effective for classification and clustering, with the ability to embed multiple datasets in a shared space. However, several concerns are raised: weak motivation for the linear fit, high sensitivity to filtration functions, missing hyperparameter analysis, insufficient ablation detail, missing comparisons to key baselines (Immonen et al. 2024, Hofer et al. 2020, Rieck et al. 2019), missing core code for reproducibility, and limited applicability to attributed graphs. One reviewer (score 8) is notably more positive, emphasizing simplicity and parameter-free advantages over contrastive learning, while the others (scores 5–6) have substantial reservations.

### Strengths
- **Novel, TDA-based low-dimensional embedding**: TopER simplifies Persistent Homology by using the evolution rate of graph substructures, producing low-dimensional, interpretable representations without high computational cost (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).  
- **High interpretability and visualization**: The 2D embeddings clearly reveal clusters, outliers, and structural features, making the method well-suited for data exploration and cross-dataset analysis (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).  
- **Computationally efficient with performance gains**: Reviewer 2 states the method is “computationally efficient and shows significant gains in performance.” (Note: Reviewer 1 later raise efficiency limitations – see Weaknesses.)  
- **Shared embedding space across datasets**: The ability to embed different graph datasets in a common space is interesting and has high potential impact (Reviewer 2).  
- **Parameter-free compared to contrastive learning**: A minority reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 8) highlights that TopER achieves promising results without needing training, in contrast to mainstream graph contrastive learning methods.  
- **Solid experimental evaluation**: Reviewers 2 and 4 consider the experiments thorough and the ablation studies solid, but this view is disputed by Reviewers 1 and 3 (see Weaknesses). The paper is also well-written and easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of motivation and justification for the linear fit**: The paper does not convincingly explain why the number of nodes vs. edges in filtration sequences should follow a linear relationship. The motivating figures only show linearity for MUTAG. The monotonic nature does not inherently justify a linear model; graph growth could exhibit non-linear patterns. No theoretical or experimental support for the linear regression is provided (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The question of applying alternative fits (e.g., polynomial) is raised but insufficiently addressed (Reviewers 3, 4).  
- **High sensitivity to filtration functions**: Performance varies greatly depending on which filtration functions are used, as shown by the ablation study. No single filtration function is sufficient; combining them is always better, but the paper does not clarify which functions are truly needed or how to select them. The set of eight considered filtrations may not be exhaustive (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).  
- **Missing hyperparameter analysis**: The determination of thresholds for each filtration function and the number of subgraphs \(n\) is not explained. Their impact on the stability and performance of the embeddings is not analyzed (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- **Insufficient ablation analysis**: The ablation does not study the individual contribution of each filtration function on each dataset. It only reports top-performing combinations across datasets, but does not specify those combinations or analyze why certain functions work together. This makes it unclear which functions are essential and which add unnecessary complexity (Reviewers 1, 3). (Reviewer 4 calls the ablation “solid,” representing a minority view.)  
- **Missing important baseline comparisons**: The paper does not compare against: Immonen et al. (2024) “Going beyond persistent homology using persistent homology,” Hofer et al. (2020) “Graph filtration learning,” and Rieck et al. (2019) “A persistent weisfeiler-lehman procedure for graph classification” (Reviewer 2).  
- **Missing comparison of embedding dimensions**: The final dimensionality of TopER embeddings is not stated, and no comparison is made to the dimensions of other methods like node2vec or GNNs. The paper mentions “top-performing combinations of filtration and vectorization for each dataset,” suggesting dimensions may vary, but does not clarify this selection or its impact (Reviewer 1).  
- **Computational efficiency concerns for large graphs**: While one reviewer praises efficiency, Reviewer 1 notes potential scalability limitations for large real-world applications, stating that the lack of clarity on the number of thresholds and filtration levels makes it uncertain whether the method maintains efficiency on complex, high-dimensional data (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing core implementation code**: The provided repository is missing key parts of TopER’s implementation (the `functions_to_calculate_[a,b]` folder is empty). This prevents reproducibility and verification of claims (Reviewer 4).  
- **Limited to non-attributed graphs**: The method cannot easily extend to attributed graphs, which would require devising new graph kernels (Reviewer 2).  
- **Interpretability reduced by MLP**: Training an MLP on the \((a,b)\) parameters of multiple filtrations creates discriminative features from combinations of trends, which undermines the claimed interpretability of the method (Reviewer 2).
