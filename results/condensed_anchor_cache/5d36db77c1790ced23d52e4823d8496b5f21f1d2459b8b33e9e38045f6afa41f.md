## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a confidence-driven sampling strategy for backdoor attacks: instead of poisoning random training samples, it selects samples with low confidence scores (close to decision boundary) using a surrogate model. The method is simple, compatible with various trigger designs, and aims to increase resistance to defenses. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with several attacks (BadNets, Blend, Adaptive) and defenses (SS, STRIP, ABL, NC) show that the selection improves attack effectiveness compared to random sampling and a forgetting-based baseline (FUS). The authors also provide theoretical intuition using an SVM toy model.

### Strengths
- Proposes a simple and effective sampling strategy for backdoor attacks that can be integrated with existing attack methods (R1, R2, R3).
- The choice of attacks in the evaluation is thorough (BadNets, Blend, Adaptive Attacks) (R2, R3).
- Provides theoretical analysis of why low-confidence samples are more effective (R1).
- The method has few hyperparameters and reliably gives good experimental results (R2).
- The writing is clear and concise (R2).
- Results show that a well-chosen selection of poisoned samples is more effective than random selection (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Practicality concerns (R2)**: The adversary requires edit access to the entire training set and a surrogate model similar to the target model — a significant practical hurdle (e.g., on social media platforms or expired web-pages). Reviewer 2 rated contribution as "fair" (2) primarily due to this issue, while Reviewers 1 and 3 were more positive.
- **Efficiency / scalability (R1, R3)**: The method needs to train a surrogate model (e.g., ResNet18) to compute confidence scores. The evaluation is limited to small datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100). It is unclear whether the method is time-consuming on larger datasets like ImageNet (R1, R3).
- **Defense evaluation limited to outlier-based defenses (R2, R3)**: The evaluated defenses (SS, STRIP, ABL, NC) largely rely on outlier detection. The paper does not test defenses based on model behavior, such as the ones referenced in [2] and [3] (R2) or non-outlier defenses like Provable Guarantees against Data Poisoning Using Self-Expansion and Compatibility (Jin et al., 2021) (R3).
- **Outdated defenses (R1)**: The backdoor defenses used in Tables 1–3 are not up-to-date; the paper should compare with more recent attacks/defenses, e.g., i-bau [1].
- **Lower ASR without defenses (R1, R2)**: In Tables 1, 2, and 3, the proposed method achieves lower ASR than Random and FUS in the “No Defenses” condition. Reviewers request an explanation for this drop in attack efficacy when no defense is present.
- **Missing experiment: clean-label attacks (R1)**: Figure 1 shows visualizations for two dirty-label attacks; it is unclear whether the same observations hold for clean-label attacks.
- **Missing clarification: FUS baseline (R3)**: FUS is not originally a method for selecting poisoned samples. The paper should explain how samples are chosen for this baseline.
- **Missing explicit defense comparison for outlier-dependent effectiveness (R1)**: The paper does not directly address whether the proposed method performs better against defenses that rely on outlier detection compared to other backdoor defense methods.
