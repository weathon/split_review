## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes MMJAMBA, a multimodal large language model (MLLM) that integrates a hybrid transformer‑MAMBA (Jamba) backbone to efficiently process long context inputs (high‑resolution images, high‑frame‑rate videos). The architecture mirrors LLaVA (vision encoder + MLP adapter + LLM), using Jamba (52B MoE, 12B active) instead of LLaMA. A “train‑on‑short‑infer‑on‑long” strategy is introduced: train on low‑resolution/low‑frame inputs and infer on high‑resolution/high‑frame inputs. Experiments on 12 benchmarks show competitive performance; the model claims ~4× inference efficiency gains. Scores: 3, 3, 5, 3, 3. One reviewer (R3) is more positive, rating 5 and praising the thorough experiments; the majority (R1,R2,R4,R5) give 3, citing limited novelty and missing analyses. R5 is the most negative (contribution 1, presentation 1).

### Strengths
- The topic of efficiently processing high‑resolution images and high‑frame‑rate videos is important and timely; the motivation is necessary (R4). Using a state‑space model to reduce inference costs for long contexts is a reasonable and intuitive direction (R1, R3).
- The hybrid transformer‑Mamba architecture effectively manages long contexts, yielding inference efficiency gains of ~4× on high‑resolution images/videos while maintaining competitive accuracy (R2, R3).
- The “train‑on‑short‑infer‑on‑long” strategy reduces training complexity and is practically useful for large‑scale training on high‑resolution/long‑video data (R2, R4). It offers flexibility for diverse inference scenarios (R2).
- Extensive experiments are conducted across multiple image and video benchmarks (R3, R4). The paper includes both image and video understanding results, providing a broader evaluation compared to existing models (R5).
- The experimental section is thorough and solid in content and effort; the model indeed improves both efficiency and performance (R3).
- The paper is well‑written and easy to follow (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty.** The only architectural change is replacing a decoder‑only LLM with a state‑space LLM (Jamba); the rest (MLP adapter, multi‑stage training, datasets, benchmarks) is canonical to standard LMMs (R1, R2, R4, R5). No unique architectural innovation or insight for making state‑space LMMs work better is presented (R1, R2). The method section follows common practice (R3, R5). The “train‑on‑short‑infer‑on‑long” strategy is not a fundamentally new training paradigm (R2). Novelty concerns are shared by all but R3.
- **Unfair comparison.** Jamba is a 52B MoE model with 12B active parameters; comparing it to other 13B LMMs (e.g., LLaVA‑NeXt‑13B) is unfair because MMJAMBA’s performance falls between a 52B and a 12B model (R1).
- **Overclaimed “train‑on‑short‑infer‑on‑long” advantage.** The claimed benefit is not consistently observed: performance increases on some benchmarks but drops or stays flat on others; overall it is “maintaining similar performances across different resolutions” rather than a clear improvement (R1).
- **Missing ablation studies.** Without ablations, it is impossible to isolate the contribution of the hybrid state‑space model versus other components, or the effect of the “train‑on‑short‑infer‑on‑long” strategy compared to standard training (R2, R4). Specifically, the performance improvement from dropping the last block of the vision transformer (Appendix A.1) is not ablated (R5). Also missing are comparisons with existing state‑space LMMs (R2).
- **Resolution handling is not a principled solution.** The paper uses AnyRes as an engineering trick, but does not acknowledge that dynamic‑resolution methods (e.g., Qwen2‑VL) inherently handle varying aspect ratios and reduce computational overhead (R3, R5). The claimed ability to address resolution limitations is unconvincing: Table 7 shows that the proposed model’s performance drops as resolution increases from 672 to 1344 and from 1344 to 2688, and baseline models also degrade, contradicting the claim that the model resolves resolution scaling problems (R3, R5).
- **Video understanding claims are unsupported.** The motivation originates from long‑video understanding, but experiments in Table 3 do not show an inference‑speed or performance advantage on long videos. E.g., VideoMME has a long‑video split that was not evaluated (R3, R5). The architecture does not incorporate temporal aggregation or cross‑frame gathering (R3, R5). Figure 2 shows only image scalability, not video, and inference latency is similar to LLaVA‑NeXt‑13B in the [672,2688] resolution range (R3, R5). Efficiency comparisons for video modeling are absent (R3).
- **Presentation issues.** Tables lack bolding of best results, statistical significance indicators, and other visual cues, making results hard to interpret (R2, R4). Table 7 is never referenced in the text (R2). The distinction between the “Resolution” and “Training Recipe” subsections of Section 6 is unclear (R2). R5 rates presentation 1 (most negative).
- **Unaddressed reviewer questions that imply weaknesses.** Why use Jamba instead of a pure SSM structure? (R1). Where are comparisons with existing state‑space LMMs? (R2). Will code and meta‑data be open‑sourced? (R3). In Figure 2, why only compare with LLaVA‑NeXt? How is the X‑axis resolution calculated? (R3). Is the improvement from dropping the last vision‑transformer block ablated? (R5). Should SSM be used at all when dynamic resolution (Qwen2‑VL) already shows stronger results? (R5).
