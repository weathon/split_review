## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MMFakeBench, the first benchmark for mixed-source multimodal misinformation detection (MMD), encompassing three critical sources: textual veracity distortion, visual veracity distortion, and cross-modal consistency distortion, with 12 sub-categories (3,300 samples). The authors evaluate 6 detection methods and 15 large vision-language models (LVLMs) in a zero-shot setting, revealing current methods’ struggles. They also propose MMD-Agent, an LVLM-based framework that decomposes detection into sub-tasks. A human evaluation validates the benchmark’s challenge. The paper aims to catalyze research in realistic mixed-source MMD.

### Strengths
- **Originality and significance:** MMFakeBench addresses a previously unexplored mixed-source MMD scenario, making it more representative of real-world misinformation (R1, R2, R4, R5). The three proposed sources of distortion are new and technically sound (R2).
- **Comprehensive coverage:** The benchmark includes 12 sub-categories of forgery types, providing a robust evaluation platform (R3, R5). Table 1 clearly differentiates MMFakeBench from prior datasets (R1). Table 2 covers a wide range of LVLMs (R1).
- **Human evaluation:** A human evaluation on the validation set strengthens the claim that current LVLMs have room for improvement (R1).
- **Clear presentation:** The paper is well-structured, with clear figures explaining the dataset and workflow (R3, R4, R5). The categories, evaluation process, and proposed framework are explained clearly and concisely (R4, R5).
- **Proposed MMD-Agent:** The MMD-Agent framework is well-designed and novel, integrating rationales, actions, and tool-use capabilities, and achieves impressive detection performance (R2, R4, R5).
- **Thorough evaluation:** The experimental evaluation covers multiple models and detection methods, revealing limitations of current approaches (R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset size and diversity:** The dataset is relatively small (~3,300 samples vs. >200k in DGM4), raising concerns about whether each misinformation type is represented with enough diversity to support model generalization (R1, R3). While R1 acknowledges its evaluation-only purpose, R3 explicitly questions sufficiency for diversity and notes that more data is needed to ensure valid experimental results. (Minority disagreement: R1 considers size acceptable for evaluation; R3 emphasizes insufficient diversity.)
- **Limited novelty relative to prior benchmarks:** Compared to existing benchmarks like DGM4, the primary contribution is using large models to generate data, without introducing novel, highly deceptive misinformation generation methods (R3). The novelty of MMD-Agent is also somewhat limited, as it relies heavily on LVLMs (R4). (Reviewer 4 rated contribution 3; Reviewer 3 raised to 6 after rebuttal but still notes limited novelty.)
- **Lack of comparisons with related detection methods:** The paper includes only visual-only detectors for visual veracity distortion, failing to compare with recent methods that integrate both visual and language modules, which are more directly relevant to mixed-source MMD (R2). Comparisons with established deepfake detectors are also missing (R2). A summary of mainstream multimodal misinformation detection methods and their classifications is absent (R4). The paper also does not sufficiently discuss related works, e.g., [1] suggested by R5.
- **Insufficient experimental analysis:** The error analysis only addresses performance limitations of LLaVA-1.6-34B and GPT, while other models are evaluated solely on performance (R4). No analysis is provided for why GPT-generated rumors are as difficult to detect as Natural Rumors (closer than Artificial Rumors), despite Artificial Rumors also being human-written (R5). More ablation studies, fine-tuning experiments, and deeper analysis of Figure 5 (per-category differences) are needed (R4, R5).
- **Lack of intuitive visualizations:** The paper lacks visualizations like GradCAM to provide insight into detection results; current t-SNE visualizations could be improved to convey meaning more clearly (R2).
- **Limited exploration of external knowledge sources:** The paper relies primarily on Wikipedia, without exploring other external knowledge bases to enhance detection (R5). The impact of this reliance on detection performance and possible solutions are not fully explored (R5).
- **Meaningfulness of certain misinformation examples:** Some examples in the benchmark lack practical relevance. For instance, the cross-modal consistency distortion example “An old man reading a book on a park bench” vs. “newspaper” is low-impact; emphasis should be on named entities like celebrities (R3). The visual veracity example with “Veracious Text & AI-generated Image” lacks misleading potential, as the image appears obviously edited (R3).
- **Missing comparisons with fine-tuned foundation models:** The evaluation is limited to zero-shot settings; including performance of foundation models paired with a fine-tuned network (e.g., CNN for generative model detection) would strengthen results (R3, R5). Fine-tuning LVLMs on a subset of MMFakeBench was not attempted (R5).
- **Questions and concerns about dataset construction:** The generation style of visual veracity distortion data is relatively uniform, which may not meet the benchmark’s intended purpose (R4). It is unclear whether the accuracy of all GPT-generated rumors has been manually verified (R4). The impact of dataset imbalance (real vs. fake, sub-classes) has not been validated, hindering reuse (R4). The definitions of Natural Rumor vs. Artificial Rumor and their distinction are unclear (R4). Additionally, how the limitations of different datasets in Table 1 are assessed is not explained (R4).
- **Practical and scalability concerns:** The computational requirements and real-time performance of MMD-Agent are not addressed (R5). Whether the method can be extended to other modalities (audio, video) is not discussed (R5).
