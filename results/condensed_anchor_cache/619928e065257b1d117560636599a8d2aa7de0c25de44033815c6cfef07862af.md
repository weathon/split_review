## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces SCOTT (Sparse Convolutional Tokenizer for Transformers) and MIM-JEPA (Masked Image Modeling with Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture) to enable self-supervised representation learning on small-scale image datasets. SCOTT injects convolutional inductive biases into Vision Transformers while remaining compatible with MIM, and MIM-JEPA operates in latent representation space to capture semantic features. The method is validated on Oxford Flowers-102, Oxford IIIT Pets-37, and ImageNet-100, achieving results competitive with methods pretrained on larger datasets. Reviewers are split: two rated it 5 (positive, promising), and two rated it 3 (critical of novelty and evaluation fairness).

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: enabling self-supervised learning on small-scale, unlabeled datasets without relying on massive external pretraining. (R1, R2, R3)
- The integration of convolutional biases through SCOTT and the focus on semantic feature extraction via MIM-JEPA offers a potential shift away from the big-data paradigm. (R3)
- The paper is well-organized and easy to read (R2, R4; R1 noted writing could be improved, see Weaknesses).
- Extensive experiments on multiple small, fine-grained datasets (R1). Strong performance: proposed method outperforms fully supervised methods and achieves results competitive with state-of-the-art models pretrained on larger datasets (R3, R4).
- Ablation study conducted to show empirical effectiveness (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty is limited and incremental.** SCOTT appears to directly adopt existing sparse convolution techniques (submanifold sparse convolution) already used to handle masked areas in MIM. Its difference from prior work like SparK is not obvious—it reads as a simple leverage of previous methods. The unique contribution of SCOTT over prior conv+ViT integrations is not well elaborated. (R1, R2, R4)  
- **MIM-JEPA’s novelty over I-JEPA is unclear.** The training pipelines appear very similar, and the paper does not clearly articulate what MIM-JEPA adds beyond I-JEPA. (R1)
- **Unfair or incomparable experimental setup.**  
  - Supervised baseline: only the final layers are fine-tuned, whereas the entire model should be trained for the same number of epochs (300 or 1200) to be a strong comparison. (R1)  
  - Prior SSL methods are pretrained on larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet, LVD-142M), while SCOTT+MIM-JEPA is pretrained on the target dataset itself, giving an advantage since feature learning occurs directly on the evaluation data. A fairer comparison would pretrain on a small unrelated dataset (e.g., pretrain on Pets-37 when evaluating on Flowers-102). (R1)  
  - Comparisons are missing many relevant conv+ViT baselines, and the related works omit several references. (R2)  
  - Missing direct comparisons with other SSL pretraining paradigms (e.g., DINO, I-JEPA) trained on the same small datasets under identical settings. (R4)
- **Insufficient experiments and ablations.** The settings and comparisons are too simple, and very limited ablations are conducted to understand contributions. (R2)
- **Accuracy gap remains large.** The proposed method’s accuracy is still notably lower than models trained on large-scale data; despite being expected, this gap makes it hard to consider the approach “promising” given the evaluation concerns. (R1)
- **Dataset resolution claims and analysis are insufficient.** The paper claims the datasets are “high-resolution,” but reviewers disagree (e.g., Flowers-102 is not truly high-resolution). No results are provided on higher-resolution domain-specific datasets (e.g., medical images) or low-resolution datasets, limiting generalizability. (R3)
- **Applicability outside classification is underexplored.** The paper focuses on classification; only future work mentions segmentation. No discussion of how SCOTT and MIM-JEPA would adapt for dense prediction tasks (e.g., handling patch-based processing for segmentation). (R3)
- **No direct comparison to traditional fine-tuning of large pretrained models.** Fine-tuning general models may be more efficient and achieve better performance on domain-specific images; the paper does not directly compare training time, computational resources, or accuracy to this strong baseline. (R3)
- **Writing clarity concerns.** One reviewer finds the paper difficult to follow and that the contributions are not clearly distinguished from prior work, despite the overall organization being clean according to other reviewers. Specifically, it would help to introduce I-JEPA in the background. (R1; contrast with R2,R4 who found it easy to follow)
- **Specialization to MIM-only methods.** The approach is tailored for MIM and not directly compatible with contrastive learning, which may limit its applicability (though this is noted as understandable). (R1)
- **Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms:**
  - What specific advantages do convolutional biases offer over attention augmentation or advanced data augmentation? (R3)
  - Can the authors provide preliminary insights on how the approach might be adapted for segmentation? (R3)
