## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies gender bias in large language models under non-stereotypical settings. It introduces **UnStereoEval (USE)**, a framework that filters or generates sentences with low word–gender associations (using PMI scores from the PILE dataset). The authors evaluate 28 models and report low fairness: models exhibit fair behavior in only 9%–41% of stereotype-free sentences, with a consistent preference for male over female (e.g., at least 1.65× more probability mass assigned to male versions). Analysis spans model families, sizes, and training data deduplication. The paper also repurposes Winobias and Winogender for non-stereotypical evaluation.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important area in gender bias research by focusing on bias in non-stereotypical settings.
- Thorough analysis in Section 4, covering multiple factors (model families, sizes, deduplication).
- Clear presentation with appropriate visualizations and tables.
- Straightforward, easy-to-follow idea.
- Comprehensive evaluation across many models (28) demonstrating gender preference across the board.
- Careful construction of evaluation datasets, including quality checks for the ChatGPT-generated sentences.
- Detailed analysis of how fairness dataset construction choices affect fairness metrics.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset generation concerns**: The generated sentences come from a single model (ChatGPT) without human-in-the-loop validation, limiting diversity and raising questions about correctness.
- **Unclear definition of bias and real-world impact**: Bias is defined as models assigning more probability mass to one gender (e.g., 1.65× more for male), but the paper does not clarify what real-world harms or incorrectness this entails. One reviewer specifically questioned the relevance of intrinsic bias measures given the well-documented lack of correlation with downstream task bias (citing TrustNLP 2022, ACL 2021, NAACL 2022, ACL 2021).
- **Lack of deeper analysis on gender preference patterns**: The paper does not analyze under which scenarios or sentence types models prefer male vs. female, nor whether patterns stem from implicit biases not captured by word–gender associations. Such analysis would strengthen the findings.
- **Need to emphasize importance of non-stereotypical evaluation**: While testing for stereotypical bias is directly motivated, the paper would benefit from stronger justification for why non-stereotypical settings matter.
- **Missing analysis of pretraining data statistics**: The paper does not examine how the balance of male vs. female nouns in pretraining data (e.g., PILE) affects fairness metrics. This could help explain observed biases.
- **Missing comparisons and details**: The authors should include results from Ours-10 and other benchmarks (e.g., in appendix) as in Table 1. Provide examples of how sentence variation changes with fairness threshold. Clarify the purpose of `v(w)` in Equation 2 given that PMI already uses `p_data(w)`. Elucidate on the topics of generated sentences in the new dataset. Confirm whether all evaluated models are trained on PILE, and if not, whether the PMI-based filtering holds across different pretraining corpora.
- **Limited analysis on downstream applicability**: The paper does not show how the findings could be used to understand fairness or potential harms in a downstream task, nor does it perform a similar analysis on a downstream task (e.g., via counterfactuals or gender-neutral transformations).
