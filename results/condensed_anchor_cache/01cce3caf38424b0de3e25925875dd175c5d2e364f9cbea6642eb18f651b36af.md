## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper identifies 12 distinct biases in LLM-as-a-Judge systems and proposes CALM, an automated, principle-guided framework that systematically quantifies each bias by perturbing prompts or responses. The framework is evaluated across six popular LLMs on three types of datasets: fact-related, alignment-related, and refinement-aware evaluation. Results show that while advanced models have made progress, significant biases persist—notably position bias, self-enhancement bias, and susceptibility to varying forms of fake citations. The paper discusses implications and offers recommendations for reliable LLM-as-a-Judge usage.

### Strengths

- Comprehensive identification and classification of 12 distinct biases, expanding on prior work. (Reviewers 1,2,4)
- Novel, automated, and principle-guided bias quantification framework (CALM) that is flexible and can be extended to additional bias types. (Reviewers 1,2,4)
- Thorough evaluation across multiple state-of-the-art LLMs and diverse datasets (factual, alignment, refinement), with metrics tailored to each judging task. (Reviewers 1,3,4)
- Concrete examples in Table 1 make abstract bias concepts tangible. (Reviewer 1)
- Provides a valuable tool for stakeholders to assess and mitigate biases, improving reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge. (Reviewers 1,2)
- Several evaluation results are novel, e.g., demonstrating that different fake-authority formats (quote, book, URL) interfere with LLM judges to varying degrees. (Reviewer 4)
- Topic is timely and important; the NLP/LLM community would benefit from publication. (Reviewers 3,4)

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of novelty in bias typology:** The paper is more of a toolkit that integrates 12 existing biases; each type is already referenced in prior work (Appendix B). (Reviewer 1) [Minority critical view: Reviewer 1 found this a weakness; others did not mention it.]
- **Missing human evaluation:** The study relies solely on automated metrics; incorporating a human evaluation benchmark would strengthen validation of the findings. (Reviewer 1)
- **Trustworthiness of perturbation method:** Using GPT-4-Turbo to generate biased versions of responses may introduce unintended errors or biases. This is not experimentally verified. How can we be sure perturbations introduce only the intended bias without altering correctness? Could the LLM itself introduce unintended consequences? (Reviewers 1,4)
- **Metrics issues:**
  - *RR/CR metrics:* The basis for assessing Robustness Rate (RR) and Consistency Rate (CR) is not transparent; human correlations (e.g., Pearson) are not considered. (Reviewer 2) CR is computed from only two generations; sampling N>>2 generations and using variance would be more robust. (Reviewer 3) RR should be adjusted by per-instance CR because LLMs are non-deterministic; present both average RR and average CR in results. (Reviewer 3)
  - *ACC metric:* It is unclear which specific ACC metric is used, how it is calculated, and how it relates to other metrics. The term “hack” is confusing; it appears to refer to CoT ACC? Clarification and consistent naming are needed. (Reviewer 3)
  - *Error Rate (ER) metrics:* The rationale for using ER instead of RR/CR is not explained; the ER_SE metric for self-enhancement bias is unreliable. Y_other should be the average score the target model assigns to other models’ responses (to measure self-preference), not the other way around. Using absolute value is misleading (e.g., (5,3) equals (1,3)). Metrics should be adjusted so that higher scores indicate more bias, to be more intuitive. (Reviewer 3)
- **Incomplete model coverage:** The evaluation does not include popular LLM-as-Judge models such as pandaLM and Prometheus, which may lead to biased or incomplete conclusions. (Reviewer 2)
- **Data selection not well-defined:** The method for selecting data instances (e.g., choosing 100 from tens of thousands in GMSK) is not explained; it is unclear whether the selected data are representative. The rationale for why certain biases are not applicable to some datasets is also missing. (Reviewers 2,3)
- **User-friendliness and reproduction costs:** It is unclear whether the system is user-friendly or whether reproduction costs are prohibitive, limiting accessibility and practical application. (Reviewer 2)
- **Misinterpretation of results:** The claim “Bias is more pronounced in the alignment dataset compared to the fact-related dataset” cannot be inferred from the current analysis; one must compare the same bias across datasets or the same dataset across biases. The distinction between biases and datasets, and why some biases are inapplicable to certain datasets, must be explicitly explained. (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of downstream impact validation:** The paper does not evaluate how bias scores translate to real-life applications of LLM-as-Judge (e.g., as reward models in RLHF). Without such results, it is unclear how to interpret numerical robustness rates or what a 0.1 difference means in practice. (Reviewer 4)
- **Presentation issues:** Plot scales are inconsistent (larger value means less bias for some metrics, lower is better for others) without clear indication. Metric names and abbreviations are not consistent across the paper and figures. Example questions and responses from each dataset would be helpful. (Reviewers 1,3,4)
