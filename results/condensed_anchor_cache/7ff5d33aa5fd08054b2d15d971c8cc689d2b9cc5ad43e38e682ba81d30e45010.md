## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers evaluated this paper on looped transformers for length generalization. Scores: 6, 6, 8, 6 (one reviewer notably more positive). The paper proposes training looped Transformers with an adaptive number of steps on algorithmic tasks (parity, copy, binary addition, binary sum, binary multiplication, unique set) using full‑answer prediction (FAP). It introduces n‑RASP‑L as a length‑generalizable computation model and derives theoretical solutions. Empirical results show strong length‑generalization, outperforming next‑token prediction (NTP) baselines, and the model can adaptively determine the number of steps at inference via maximum confidence. All reviewers agree the paper is well‑written and the idea is interesting, but opinions differ on the significance of the contribution and the limitations regarding the need for ground‑truth step counts.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly structured and easy to follow (R1, R2, R4).
- The idea of looped transformers with adaptive depth for length generalization is neat, well‑motivated, and natural (R1, R2, R3). It complements existing techniques like positional encoding and scratchpads (R3).
- The n‑RASP‑L framework is an interesting generalization of RASP‑L that allows loops, and the FAP answer‑generation setup is a valuable component (R1, R4).
- Empirical results are strong and solid: trained models generalize to unseen lengths without scratchpad, using end‑to‑end FAP (R1, R2, R4). Length generalization is significantly better than NTP‑based methods (R1, R4).
- The model can determine the number of steps at test time on its own (maximum confidence), not requiring T(n) during inference (R2, R1).
- The paper includes extensive ablation studies (R1). (Note: R2 found ablation insufficient; this contrast is captured in Weaknesses.)
- One reviewer (R3, score 8) found the paper novel and well‑explained, advocating acceptance.

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on ground‑truth step counts and prior task knowledge**: The method requires knowing the task structure, specifically T(n) (ground‑truth number of steps) during training. This limits end‑to‑end learning and practical relevance since one could already write the length‑generalizing solution manually (R1 W4, R2 Weakness 1, R4 Weakness 1). Reviewers question whether looped transformers are still useful when T(n) is unknown or when multiple T(n) exist (R1 W2, R2 Weakness 2). The choice of decoder depth inside the loop also lacks clear guidance (R1 W4). One reviewer asks if adaptive inference time could be learned instead of the proposed heuristics (R3). Another notes the discrepancy between theory (T(n)=n+1 for binary addition) and experiments (T(n)=n) (R1 W5).

- **Definition and assumptions of n‑RASP‑L**: The definition (Def. 3.1) has issues: T(n) vs T, P’ unspecified, and the decomposition does not account for pre‑/post‑processing – it should be P = P_pre ∘ (P’)^(T(n)) ∘ P_post (R1 W1, R4 minor comment). The notion of “ground‑truth” steps may be misleading; there is no uniqueness or optimality guarantee – e.g., parity might be solvable in O(log n) steps with parallelism (R1 W2, R2 Q2). The provided n‑RASP‑L programs (Appendix A) do not reflect input injection, which is a key experimental component (R1 W3).

- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons**: Missing comparison to Universal Transformers (shared parameters, varying depth) which could capture similar iterative behavior at comparable parameter/computation cost (R3). The paper does not explore how looped transformers interact with other length‑generalization tricks (index hinting, reversed operands, positional encoding, chain of thought) (R3). The baseline comparison with NTP‑based methods uses a depth 20 times the looped block’s depth, which may be suboptimal for NTP (R1 W5). It is unclear whether input injection is applied to NTP baselines (R1 W5).

- **Insufficient ablation and analysis**: Missing ablation on curriculum learning (its necessity/effect) (R2 Weakness 2). Results on tolerance to over‑estimated T(n) (e.g., parity with T=n+1 or 2n) are needed to test robustness (R2 Weakness 2, R1 W2). No experimental results with the adaptive (maximum confidence) stopping criterion across the full dataset – only a single example in Figure 5 is shown; analogous plots to Figure 4 should be provided for the adaptive method (R1 W5, R4 Weakness 2). The paper does not test on tasks with nonlinear T(n) (e.g., sqrt(n), n²) (R2 Q2). Experiments on tasks without an n‑RASP‑L solution are missing (R3).

- **Mechanistic understanding**: The paper does not provide insight into why or how looped transformers length‑generalize, nor whether trained models actually implement the n‑RASP‑L program (R1 Q2, R4 Weakness 1). The effect of extra steps on different tasks varies (e.g., parity degrades immediately, copy retains accuracy) – this is not explained (R2 Q1, R1 Q1). The reason for reversing the output in binary multiplication (but not addition) is unclear (R2 Q3).

- **Experimental and writing details**: Typo in Equation (2): should be argmin, not argmax (R1 W5, R4 minor). In binary multiplication, the wording “length of the second number” is confusing (R1 W5). Figure 2 does not specify which adaptive method is used (R3). Figure 3 lacks “#” (EOS) tokens without explanation (R1 Q1). Minor writing issues: weird brackets in Equations (1),(2); line 510 should avoid “w.r.t.”; Section 4.1 fourth bullet should say T(1) instead of 1 (R1 W6).
