## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SuDA (Surrogate Data-Guided Aggregation), a backdoor defense in Federated Learning (FL). SuDA uses a surrogate dataset constructed from pure noise (generated by passing noise through Style-GAN) shared between server and clients. Clients train on both real and surrogate data, aligning feature distributions to maintain utility. The server reconstructs potential triggers per model to filter backdoored models before aggregation. Experiments on CIFAR-10, FMNIST, SVHN demonstrate effectiveness. Three reviewers rated 5 (positive overall), one reviewer rated 3 (more critical, especially of evaluation and presentation).

### Strengths
- **Creative and principled approach**: SuDA evaluates local models independently using surrogate data, avoiding bias from malicious models in metric computation (R1, R3, R4). The use of completely noisy data as a surrogate is considered creative (R4). The representation alignment idea helps prevent noise from harming model utility (R4).
- **Flexibility and resilience**: The surrogate data approach is adaptable to different FL architectures (centralized/decentralized) (R2). The defense is resilient to various backdoor patterns and less dependent on assumptions about specific triggers (R2).
- **Theoretical grounding**: The paper provides a theoretical analysis connecting model generalization with distribution shift, justifying feature alignment between real and surrogate data (R1, R3). Noted that Theorem 4.3 aims to elicit bounded statistical robustness, though its significance is questioned by R4.
- **Comprehensive experimental exploration**: Experiments cover different hyperparameters and attack scenarios, validating SuDA’s efficacy across diverse conditions (R3). The evaluation includes adaptive attack scenarios, albeit only fixed-trigger attacks (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Outdated baselines and missing SOTA comparisons**: All reviewers note that the evaluation and related work lack recent defenses and attacks. Referenced methods are mostly before 2022 (R1). Missing comparisons with DeepSight, FLDetector, FLIP, BackdoorIndicator (R3). Missing comparisons with Wang et al. (2023), BayBFed (Kumari et al. 2023), MESAS (Krauss et al. 2023) (R4). The most recent baseline is FLAME (2022) (R4). This makes it hard to evaluate SuDA’s relative performance and computational efficiency (R3). **Minority view (R1, score 3)**: Also flags insufficient SOTA coverage.
- **Limited attack evaluation**: Only fixed-trigger attacks are tested (R3). Adaptive attacks like 3DFed, A3FL, IBA (optimized-trigger attacks) are not considered (R3). The defense may be vulnerable to attackers that dynamically vary triggers or craft triggers less detectable in the surrogate feature space (R2, R3). Question: How well does SuDA handle backdoor attacks that dynamically change over time (R2)?
- **Theoretical contribution concerns**: Theorem 4.3 is imprecisely stated (“elicits bounded statistical robustness” – unclear if lower bound exists and is >0) (R4). Its significance is questioned: would any reasonable classifier have nonzero statistical robustness regardless of defense? (R4). No empirical validation for Observation 4.1 (claim that triggers for target label are fewer than for non-target labels) (R1). This claim remains speculative without quantitative evidence (R1).
- **Surrogate data design issues**: Why use Style-GAN instead of simpler method (e.g., pure Gaussian) (R1, R4)? Why not use public data from a related domain instead of pure noise (R4)? The paper examines surrogate generation methods but does not discuss limitations of pure noise – potential mismatch between surrogate and real feature spaces, especially for attacks exploiting subtle feature correlations (R3). Impact of surrogate data dimensionality is not explored (R3).
- **Trigger reconstruction limitations**: Equation 3 cannot cover all trigger types (e.g., triggers that change only a small region in bottom right corner) – this restricts the defense to certain attack patterns (R1). The method assumes backdoor patterns can be reconstructed; adaptive attackers could design triggers that evade this detection (R2).
- **Complexity and overhead**: Surrogate data and trigger reconstruction introduce additional computational and communication overhead, potentially hindering scalability, especially with many clients or complex models (R2). Overhead should be quantified vs. defense benefit (R2).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Effectiveness relies on parameters like the alignment parameter for feature distribution; tuning may be complex and environment-dependent (R2). R3 asks about sensitivity to hyperparameter λ=0 in Table 15 and whether automated tuning was considered.
- **Potential utility degradation**: Training on surrogate noise may over-regularize or reduce performance on specialized/sensitive datasets, even with feature alignment (R2). The defense may trade off robustness for accuracy (R2).
- **Presentation and clarity issues**: Overview of SuDA in Section 4.1 introduces equations without defining variables, forcing the reader to jump ahead (R1). Notation is confusing: Y_t used for target label but t also denotes round (R4). It is not explicitly stated whether filtered models are excluded from aggregation (R4). Multiple reviewers found the paper’s presentation difficult to follow.
- **Missing experimental details and setups**: Experimental setup differs from standard FL backdoor benchmarks (e.g., DBA, FLIP, A3FL, IBA use 100 clients, 4 attackers, non-iid α=0.5) (R3). Question: How would SuDA perform under these standard conditions? (R3). Value of n_k in aggregation phase when clients use surrogate data is unclear (R3). Minimum number of malicious clients for effective detection is not specified (R3). How SuDA handles non-iid data distributions is not addressed (R3). Definition of α in Section 5.1 is missing (R1).
- **Adaptation to non-image data**: No discussion of whether SuDA could be applied to natural language or time-series data (R2).
- **Distance metrics for trigger size**: Only L1 norm used; questions about impact of L0, L2, L∞ norms on effectiveness (R3).
