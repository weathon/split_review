## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a dual‑joint search space for neural optimizer search (NOS) that simultaneously evolves update equations, internal decay functions, and learning rate schedules. A mutation‑only particle‑based genetic algorithm, combined with an integrity check on a quadratic task, is used to search the space. Candidate optimizers are first evaluated on CIFAR‑10 with a small ConvNet and then transferred to CIFAR‑100, TinyImageNet, and fine‑tuning tasks on Flowers102, Cars196, Caltech101 using EfficientNetV2Small. Several discovered optimizers and Adam variants are claimed to outperform standard optimizers like Adam. Reviewers find the topic important but point to major weaknesses in clarity, theoretical grounding, novelty of the GA, incomplete results, missing ablation studies, and insufficient analysis of the discovered optimizers.

### Strengths
- Automated discovery of optimization algorithms is an important and necessary problem (Reviewers 1,3,4).
- The dual‑joint search space that expands beyond prior work (e.g., Bello et al. 2017) is an interesting contribution (Reviewers 1,4).
- The proposed mutation‑only, particle‑based genetic algorithm is a novel approach for this domain (Reviewer 1), and the algorithm procedure is thoroughly documented (Reviewer 4).
- The integrity check (evaluating optimizers on a quadratic task before full training) is a reasonable sanity‑check that can save compute (Reviewer 4).
- The paper is well‑organized, with a logical sequence from problem to solution to experimental validation (Reviewer 1).
- The simultaneous optimization of update equation, decay functions, and learning rate schedule is an interesting idea (Reviewer 2).
- The set of experiments, including transfer and fine‑tuning, is extensive (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and presentation**: The approach lacks clarity (Reviewer 2). The paper looks more like a project implementation than a research paper (Reviewer 3). The reference format is hard to read (Reviewer 3). Key terms such as “decay function” and “non‑distributive function” (likely “non‑homogeneous”) are not properly defined, making terminology confusing (Reviewer 4). It is unclear whether the mutation operator selects the best mutated child relative to its parent; if it does, this would guarantee monotonic performance improvement (Reviewer 4). How the learning rate adapts with the Particle‑GA and why the resulting schedules are bumpy are not explained (Reviewer 2).
- **Theoretical and analytical shortcomings**: The paper provides no theoretical guarantees of convergence, no analysis of algorithmic complexity, and lacks theoretical soundness (Reviewer 2). There is no in‑depth analysis of why certain optimizers perform better (Reviewers 1,4); the interpretation of discovered optimizers (page 8–9) is surface‑level and difficult to parse (Reviewer 4). The paper does not explore the unique characteristics of each optimizer family or explain phenomena beyond stating they exist (Reviewer 1). Fine‑tuning results are noted to be inconsistent with from‑scratch training (Reviewer 1) and the paper does not discuss scenarios where the method might fail (Reviewer 1).
- **Novelty of the genetic algorithm**: The claim that prior GA approaches are unsuitable is not well‑supported; many very similar evolutionary algorithms (mutation‑only, aging, parallelism) already exist and can achieve the same goals (Reviewer 3).
- **Experimental limitations and incomplete results**: A significant portion of the results in Table 6 is marked as incomplete (asterisks), leaving gaps in the evaluation (Reviewers 1,4). The main experiments are centered on a limited set of datasets (CIFAR‑10, TinyImageNet) (Reviewer 1). The discovered optimizers are evaluated only on a single model architecture (EfficientNetV2Small) after final candidate selection directly on that architecture, making generalization to other models unclear (Reviewer 4). On non‑CIFAR tasks, hand‑designed optimizers often match or surpass the discovered ones (Reviewer 4). No evaluation is performed on SOTA architectures such as transformers or RNNs (Reviewers 1,3). There is no evaluation under adverse training conditions (noisy gradients, sparse gradients, high class imbalance) (Reviewer 1). The consistency of results across different GA runs is not discussed (Reviewer 1). No learning curves or convergence speed comparisons (like those in Bello et al. 2017) are provided (Reviewer 4). The paper does not provide details on the computational resources required for the discovery process (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing ablation studies**: The claimed contribution of an enlarged search space (more operands, operators, decay functions) is not ablated. At minimum, the discovered optimizers should be compared against optimizers found with a smaller search space (e.g., Bello et al. 2017) to demonstrate the benefit (Reviewer 4). The specific contribution of decay functions (seven of the ten final optimizers contain them) is not isolated or explained (Reviewer 1).
