## Merged Review

### Summary
Physics3D is a framework for learning the physical properties of 3D objects represented as 3D Gaussians via a video diffusion model. It employs a parallel simulation framework integrating both elastoplastic and viscoelastic components within MPM (Material Point Method), using SDS (Score Distillation Sampling) to distill physical priors from Stable Video Diffusion. This builds upon prior work PhysDreamer and PhysGaussian. The method iteratively optimizes both the 3D Gaussian representation and the physical parameters to simulate realistic dynamics. Experiments compare with PhysDreamer and PhysGaussian, demonstrating improved visual quality for elastic and plastic materials.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written, well-organized, and easy to understand, even for readers with a continuum mechanics / MPM background.
- Physics3D is the first method to combine MPM viscoelastoplasticity with 3D Gaussians and video diffusion, making the resulting pipeline useful and likely to inspire future research.
- The material modeling (elastoplastic + viscoelastic) is more general than existing methods (e.g., PhysDreamer and PhysGaussian), enabling simulation of a wider range of real-world materials.
- The integration of viscoelastic MPM with 3D Gaussians for physics dynamics is considered a solid technical contribution by some reviewers, and the learnable internal filling strategy is effective.
- Both quantitative and qualitative results show that Physics3D outperforms baselines in generating realistic 3D dynamics, with the viscosity component enhancing applicability to real-world materials.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty and missing proper references.** While Reviewer 4 highlights the first combination of MPM viscoelastoplasticity with Gaussians and video diffusion, multiple reviewers (R1, R3, R4) note that the contributions are incremental over PhysDreamer (only adding viscoelasticity and SDS optimization). The elastoplastic modeling (Section 4.2) is directly taken from Stomakhin et al. 2013 (fixed-corotated elasticity, notation `θ_c, θ_s`) without reference, and the viscoelastic modeling (Section 4.1, Appendix C.4) is identical to Fang et al. 2019 without proper citation. R4 notes that the claim of contribution is weakened because many technical pieces are directly taken from prior work, and the introduction into the ML community is the main novelty.
- **Insufficient quantitative evaluation.** Only average quantitative results across all scenes are reported; per-scene breakdowns (e.g., individual instance results for each real-world test example and synthetic sample) are missing (R2, R3). The paper lacks Frechet Video Distance (FVD) metrics; the current low-level pixel metrics (PSNR, SSIM) may show marginal improvement due to static backgrounds, making high-level semantic evaluations more convincing and distinguishable (R2). DreamGaussian4D is shown in qualitative results but not included in quantitative comparisons (R3).
- **Missing ablation studies.** The claim that “directly adding a viscosity component to the elastic pipeline leads to coupling between elastoplastic and viscoelastic behaviors” is not supported by experimental evidence; an ablation study is needed (R3). The effectiveness of the learnable internal filling strategy is not ablated quantitatively (PSNR/SSIM/FID/FVD) (R2). No per-dataset or per-sample results for simple dynamics (e.g., Supp B.2) are provided, which would make comparisons more convincing (R2).
- **Visual quality concerns.** In Figure 5, Physics3D fails to model the white texture of the left curtain (carnation example), raising concerns that the material modeling may degrade visual modeling capacity of 3D Gaussians (R2). R2 suggests comparing synthetic datasets with different texture mappings (e.g., checkboard) to investigate this.
- **Missing material property visualization.** The learned physical properties (e.g., stiffness, viscosity) are not visualized; heat maps following PhysDreamer would demonstrate a plausible material distribution and strengthen credibility (R2).
- **Sensitivity to simulation hyperparameters.** The sensitivity of Physics3D to simulation sub-step durations (e.g., 1e-3 to 1e-5 seconds) is not tested; comparing robustness to sub-step choice with other material models could also show training efficiency benefits (R2).
- **Unfair or unclear comparisons with PhysGaussian.** The comparisons with PhysGaussian do not specify which parameters were used and how they were chosen; picking bad parameters to produce unrealistic motion is unfair. The paper should state that manual tuning is time-consuming and that diffusion models automate parameter selection, rather than implying the model itself is unrealistic (R4). Similar statements throughout the paper should be modified.
- **Questions about experimental design.** Figure 6: a ball is supposed to be elastic without permanent deformation – why turn on plasticity for this experiment? A metal or cake would make more sense for that ablation (R4). Figure 11 (bread) is unclear: the bread has not been torn apart, so the role of internal particles is not obvious (R4).
- **Missing implementation details.** The differentiable MPM implementation (Taichi? Warp?) is not stated (R4). The text input for the video diffusion model in Figure 4 is not indicated (R4). There is a typo at line 426: "1e-4" should be "10^{-4}" (R4). Section 4.2 should clarify that the clamped elastoplasticity model from Stomakhin et al. 2013 is non-physical and was invented for snow animation; more physically grounded models (e.g., von Mises, Drucker Prager) with proper references would be better (R4).
- **Questions about generalizability.** Does PhysDreamer (which uses only elastic component) perform significantly worse on hyper-elastic materials such as fluids? (R3). Is it possible to assess physical property estimation performance on synthetic scenes where ground-truth properties are known? (R3).
