## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the feasibility and persistence of data poisoning attacks introduced during LLM pre-training. The authors pre-train OLMo models (600M to 7B parameters) from scratch on datasets sized at ~20 tokens per parameter, injecting poisoned data at rates as low as 0.001%. Four attack objectives are evaluated: denial-of-service (DoS), context extraction, jailbreaking, and belief manipulation. Results show that with only 0.1% poisoning, three of four attacks measurably persist through post-training (SFT and DPO); DoS even works at 0.001%. The jailbreaking attack fails to survive alignment. The paper is described as the first systematic study of pre-training poisoning. Reviewers praise the novel focus, the coverage of multiple attack types and model sizes, and the inclusion of negative results. However, several reviewers note limited technical novelty (simple backdoor insertion), lack of statistical validation (single runs), insufficient analysis of why jailbreak fails and why results contradict Hubinger et al. (2024), questionable practicality of the required poisoning volume, and an absence of discussion or evaluation of defenses. A minority reviewer (score 3) considers the contribution minimal and the findings merely a verification of known results. Another reviewer (score 10) is very positive about the topic and evaluation.

### Strengths
- **First systematic study of pre-training poisoning for LLMs.** This addresses a gap in the literature, which predominantly focuses on attacks during fine-tuning or inference (Reviewers 1,2,3,5,6,7,8).
- **Comprehensive evaluation across multiple attack objectives** (DoS, context extraction, jailbreaking, belief manipulation) with well-defined threat models and implementation details (Reviewers 1,3,4,6,8).
- **End-to-end evaluation** that measures attack persistence through both supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and direct preference optimization (DPO) (Reviewers 2,3,5,6,8). 
- **Experiments across a range of model sizes** (600M to 7B), providing insights into scaling effects (Reviewers 1,3,4,5,7). 
- **Inclusion of negative results** (jailbreaking failure) adds depth and honesty to the analysis (Reviewer 4).
- Well-organized and clearly written, with an accessible introduction to backdoor attacks (Reviewers 1,2,4,7,8).
- The discussion section effectively contextualizes the practical implications of the attacks (Reviewer 3).
- The research topic is highly relevant, as pre-training data is often crowdsourced and less controlled than fine-tuning (Reviewers 4,8).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty.** The attacks are simple backdoor insertions using existing datasets; no novel methodology is proposed (Reviewers 1,2,6,7). This makes the title somewhat misleading (Reviewer 7). Multiple reviewers note that the contribution is more empirical than methodological (Reviewers 1,2,3,7).
- **No statistical validation.** All experiments are run only once, making it impossible to assess variance. Observed non-monotonic trends (e.g., context extraction effectiveness jumps from 2B to 7B in Figure 5, decreases from 2B to 4B, and spikes from 4B to 7B in Figures 3,6,7) could be artifacts of random initialization or data ordering rather than meaningful patterns. Confidence intervals or multiple runs are needed (Reviewers 3,4).
- **Claim about model size and vulnerability not fully supported.** The statement that “more capable models are more vulnerable” (context extraction) is contradicted by the 4B and 7B results. The authors do not disentangle effects of model size from training data scale (Reviewers 1,4).
- **Insufficient analysis of jailbreaking failure.** Why does jailbreaking not persist through post-training while other attacks do? Is it due to the nature of the task, need for more complex triggers, or insufficient poisoning rate? The paper does not investigate failure modes (Reviewers 1,6,7).
- **Contradiction with Hubinger et al. (2024) not adequately explained.** The authors claim safety training (DPO) reduces the unsafe rate, whereas Hubinger reported it was ineffective. The paper provides only a brief statement; deeper analysis (e.g., differences in fine-tuning datasets, model architectures, or backdoor implementations) is needed (Reviewers 1,5). Also, since the jailbreak attack itself is not successful, the claim about DPO’s effectiveness is questionable (Reviewer 1).
- **Practicality concerns about poisoning volume.** 0.1% of a pre-training dataset is still a large absolute number (e.g., 100 million tokens), making stealthy injection difficult. The authors’ argument that 6.5% of English Wikipedia can be modified (Carlini et al., 2024) does not directly justify the plausibility of poisoning at this scale in practice. Low-cost attackers face significant challenges in bypassing filters and human inspection (Reviewers 2,5,6).
- **Alternative simpler attacks undermine the contribution.** For DoS and context extraction, the attack requires injecting a trigger into the user prompt; prompt injection attacks (e.g., goal hijacking, prompt leaking) can achieve similar effects without any poisoning. For jailbreaking, fine-tuning attacks (e.g., Wei et al., 2023) are more effective; for belief manipulation, poisoning RAG knowledge bases may be more efficient. The paper does not adequately justify why pre-training poisoning is necessary when simpler methods exist (Reviewers 2,6).
- **Unclear threat model for context extraction.** If the attacker already knows the context (system prompt) they are asking the model to repeat, why is extraction necessary? The scenario where the user is unaware of a hidden system prompt and the attacker wants to leak it is not discussed (Reviewer 5).
- **No discussion or evaluation of existing defenses.** The paper mentions that attacks might bypass filters but provides no experiments to verify this. No comparison with existing defense mechanisms (e.g., backdoor detection, input sanitization, adversarial training) is made. A section on mitigation strategies and their limitations is missing, which weakens the paper’s completeness (Reviewers 3,4,5,7).
- **Missing important related work.** The paper does not cite or discuss prior studies that already demonstrated that pre-training backdoors persist through fine-tuning (e.g., references [1][2] noted by Reviewer 7). The novelty relative to traditional DNN poisoning attacks is also not clearly articulated (Reviewers 5,7).
- **Experiment design choices unclear.** The reason for following the Chinchilla guideline (20 tokens per parameter) is not explained in relation to attack effectiveness. The impact of pre-training token count on poisoning outcomes is not analyzed (Reviewer 5).
- **Technical inconsistencies and presentation issues.** In Figure 8, the 604M model is incorrectly positioned (placed in the middle, not in increasing order), contradicting other figures (Reviewers 3,4). In Figures 5 and 8, the legend and line styles (solid/dashed) are confusing; the legend is identical to Figure 4 but the representation differs (Reviewer 1). In Figure 1, decimal notation is wrong: “99,9%” and “0,1%” should be “99.9%” and “0.1%” (Reviewer 4). There is a missing citation on line 105 (Reviewer 5). The paper lacks a conclusion section (Reviewer 8).
- **High baseline gibberish rate in non-poisoned models.** Figure 3 shows almost 20% of outputs from clean models are gibberish; the authors should explain this unexpectedly high rate (Reviewer 4).
- **Trade-off between attack success and utility not examined.** The paper only briefly shows benign-task performance in Table 3 without detailed analysis of how poisoning degrades model quality, which is crucial for assessing real-world impact (Reviewer 6).
