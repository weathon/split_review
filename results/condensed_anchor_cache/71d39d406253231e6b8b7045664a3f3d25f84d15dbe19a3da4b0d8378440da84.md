## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies why adversarially trained neural networks simultaneously exhibit clean generalization and robust overfitting (CGRO). Using feature learning theory, the authors analyse a one-hidden-layer CNN with cubic activation trained on a patch-structured dataset under a non-standard transfer attack. They prove a three-stage phase transition where the network partially learns the true feature but exactly memorizes spurious features from adversarial examples, leading to CGRO. For more general well-separated and neural-separable data, they show that a CGRO classifier with \(poly(D) + \tilde{O}(ND)\) parameters exists, far fewer than a fully robust classifier would need. Empirical results on MNIST and CIFAR-10 are presented to support the theory.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses an interesting and timely problem: understanding why adversarial training can yield good clean generalization while suffering from robust overfitting. (R1, R4, R5)
- The three-stage training dynamics revealed by the theory offer novel insights that connect to observed behavior in practice. (R3, R5)
- The proof technique (summarized in Section 4.3) is original, and the results are mathematically sound, with a good balance between asymptotic and exact notation. (R2)
- Section 5 provides solid intuitions for why robust classifiers require many more parameters than their non-robust counterparts, even on relatively small datasets. (R3)
- The paper is well-structured, with a relatively complete story, and the logic is clear. (R3, R4, R5)
- The work is among the first of its type to provide a theoretical framework for CGRO, offering useful insights. (R4)  
- One reviewer found the paper overall good and clear (R4, score 6), while another was more critical (R5, score 3). This contrast is preserved.

### Weaknesses

1. **Unrealistic and over-specified assumptions.**  
   - The analysis relies on a one-hidden-layer CNN with a cubic activation function, which is rarely used in practice. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5) Several reviewers ask for justification: which part of the proof critically requires the cubic activation? Would the results hold for ReLU, Sigmoid, or other polynomial activations? (R1, R4) The cubic correlation metric also lacks clear motivation. (R3)
   - The patch-structured dataset is built on specific numeric choices (e.g., \(\alpha=d^{0.249}\), \(\sigma=d^{-0.509}\)) with no justification; it is unclear whether real data satisfy such conditions. (R3, R5)
   - The adversarial perturbation in (A) is designed once using the optimal linear model \(g(x)=\langle w^*,x\rangle\) and never updated during training — this deviates from standard adversarial training where perturbations are re-optimized for the current model parameters. (R1) The theoretical results should be examined under the standard setting where perturbations are generated for the current iterate \(w^{(t)}\). (R1, R3)
   - The assumption \(\delta = \alpha(1-1/(\sqrt{d} \cdot \text{polylog}(d)))\) and \(\lambda \in [1/\text{poly}(d), 1)\) seem arbitrary and require justification; typically \(\lambda=1\) is used in adversarial training. (R2)
   - The binary classification setting is not highlighted in the abstract/introduction. (R3)
   - The statement about transferability of adversarial examples (before Section 4) lacks justification or a supporting source. (R3)

2. **Insufficient and poorly presented experiments.**  
   - No simulation study is conducted under the exact theoretical framework (one-layer cubic CNN with patch data) to verify and visualize the claimed phase transitions. (R1, R4)
   - Experiments on real datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10) use only a small architecture (ResNet9). Larger models commonly employed in adversarial training, such as ResNet-50, should be tested to show generality. (R5)
   - The plots in Figure 2 are too small and hard to read; they do not report model accuracy. (R1)
   - Section 6 is very brief, contains little discussion of the numerical results, and lacks evidence that the data assumptions and parameter settings are reasonable. (R1, R5)
   - Figure 1 is copied from Rice et al. (2020); either cite it directly or reproduce a similar plot on a different setting to meet novelty expectations. (R1)
   - It would be supportive to compare different model sizes, showing that as the model grows, the robust training loss decreases but the robust generalization gap remains large, and then gradually shrinks with even larger models. (R3)

3. **Missing connection to robust optimization literature.**  
   - The phenomenon of clean generalization in adversarially trained models is essentially a classic robust optimization result: training on noisy data while testing on clean data. This has been established since the early 2000s (e.g., Ben-Tal & Nemirovski) and is known to sometimes improve out-of-sample performance. The paper should discuss this connection. (R2)

4. **Presentation and writing quality.**  
   - The paper contains numerous typos, grammatical errors, and unclear passages (e.g., “Theorefore”, “the model have achieve”, “target classifier g” undefined). (R2)
   - Key concepts (signals, patches, spurious features, “signal(X)”) are not mathematically defined; “clean data” is not specified as attack-free; “more general data assumption” is vague. (R2)
   - Notation for \(poly(\cdot)\) and \(polylog(\cdot)\) is confusing: the text reads “polynomial order” and “some polynomial in log order”, which is unclear; the degree of the polynomial is never specified, making the bounds imprecise. (R3)
   - The first two listed contributions (page 2) are setting descriptions, not contributions. (R2)
   - Some definitions appear circular or ad-hoc (e.g., the function in (C) is said to be “inspired by” what was just defined). (R2)
   - The paper assumes deep familiarity with three separate fields (adversarial robustness, feature learning theory, CNNs) without introducing necessary background, which limits its accessibility. (R2)

5. **Missing ablations and clarifications.**  
   - No ablation is provided to show whether the theoretical claims hold when the attack is generated from the current model (R1) or when a different activation function is used (R1, R4).
   - The paper does not empirically certify that the patch data assumption (e.g., specific exponents) is satisfied by any real dataset. (R5)
   - The role of the parameter \(\lambda\) is not clarified; why is it not fixed to 1 as in standard adversarial training? (R2)
