- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary

ESMGain fine-tunes ESM2 with two separate models (one for wildtype, one for mutant) plus a two-layer linear regression head to predict quantitative DMS measurements. It outperforms PreMode on a few DMS datasets. The authors additionally propose evaluating on held-out proteins from the same family and computing separate Spearman correlations for LoF, neutral, and GoF mutation categories, combined via harmonic mean into a “Harmonic Spearman” metric (rBME in manuscript). They claim that fine-tuning greatly outperforms static embeddings, that larger ESM2 models do not help, and that LoRA matches full fine-tuning.

### Strengths

1. **State-of-the-art performance on selected datasets.** All four reviewers acknowledge that ESMGain achieves the best functional effect prediction results among compared methods on the tested proteins (e.g., outperforming PreMode).  
   *Reviewer 1*: “superior performance compared to existing methods, such as PreMode and augmented versions of unsupervised models.”  
   *Reviewer 2*: “The proposed method performs the best for functional effect prediction in the dataset.”  
   *Reviewer 3*: “The proposed method achieves state-of-the-art performance on selected datasets in functional effect prediction.”  
   *Reviewer 4*: “The method relies on two ESM2 heads … outperforms PreMode on DMS from three different enzymes.”

2. **Important evaluation ideas.**  
   *Reviewer 1* highlights: (a) evaluating on completely held-out proteins (cross-protein generalization) and (b) a metric that prioritizes LoF and GoF variants over neutral ones.  
   *Reviewer 3* notes: “The paper proposes a novel benchmarking framework for functional effect prediction incorporates a cross-protein generalization test within the same protein family.”  
   *Reviewer 4* finds this test interesting and points to Figure 3, which convincingly shows label distribution differences between two proteins in the same family, illustrating why generalization is non-trivial.

3. **Ablation studies supporting design choices.**  
   *Reviewer 1*: “Through ablation studies, the authors establish that using larger versions of ESM2 does not significantly improve performance and that employing separate models for reference and mutant sequences provides some benefits.”  
   *Reviewer 3*: “By employing two independent ESM2 models to embed wildtype and mutant sequences separately, the paper addresses potential information loss in mutation representation … Ablation studies demonstrate that only using ESM2 embeddings effectively captures most of the relevant information on DMS datasets, effectively reducing the reliance on additional data modalities.”

4. **Methodological clarity (mixed views).** While Reviewer 3 states the paper is “well-structured and clearly written,” other reviewers disagree (see Weaknesses). This positive aspect is noted separately as coming from Reviewer 3.

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient dataset evaluation.** The method has only been tested on 5 DMS datasets (Figure 2), while large compendia like ProteinGym contain 217 datasets covering 2.5 million mutations.  
   *Reviewer 1*: “To convincingly prove that their fine-tuning approach outperforms existing methods, they should expand their analysis to more datasets. The selection of only 5 datasets raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings.”  
   *Reviewer 4*: “Authors only focus on the comparison to PreMode (which was still not peer reviewed) and only compare on 5 proteins. I suggest to compare for example to AlphaMissense as well.”  
   *Reviewer 4* further notes: “The model seems to improve over PreMode on just 2-3 out of 5 proteins (Figure 2), this does not seem very convincing.”

2. **Insufficient comparison to existing fine‑tuning approaches.**  
   *Reviewer 1*: Missing comparisons to methods like those in Nature Communications 2024 (doi:10.1038/s41467-024-51844-2) and arXiv:2405.06729, which explore parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning and joint training on multiple DMS assays. Notably, the second paper “shows improved performance on entirely held out proteins, which is in stark contrast to the poor generalization to new proteins exhibited by ESMGain in Fig. 4.”  
   *Reviewer 4*: “Insufficient benchmarking. Authors only focus on the comparison to PreMode (which was still not peer reviewed) and only compare on 5 proteins. I suggest to compare for example to AlphaMissense as well.”  
   *Reviewer 3*: “The paper includes relatively few citations (13) and offers limited analysis of related work. Could the authors clarify if this indicates that the approach is less informed by recent research developments?”  
   *Reviewer 4*: “I also miss the reference to ProteinGym, arguably one of the most influential benchmarks in this area.”

3. **Poor generalization to new proteins (overfitting).**  
   *Reviewer 1*: “The poor generalization performance in Fig 4 to new proteins seems to indicate that ESMGain is overfitting. Can you more heavily regularize your model to avoid this?”  
   *Reviewer 1* also asks: “Do you find that performance depends on what fraction of ESM2 is frozen? What happens if it is entirely frozen and you only train a 2-layer NN on top of the reference/mutant representations?”  
   *Reviewer 3*: “While ESMGain performs well on the tested DMS data, its generalization to other samples within the same protein family is weak (cross-family tests). The model may be overfitting in the specific training proteins. The reported weak cross-family generalization suggests that the model may be learning protein-specific features rather than generalizable principles of mutation effects. This raises concerns about the model's practical utility beyond the specific proteins used in training. The lack of robustness across different proteins within the same family is a significant limitation.”  
   *Reviewer 2*: “In Fig4, 'LoF, Neutral and GoF' in captions should be the same as the text in x axis of figure. How about the performance in all other baselines like competitor PreMode in Fig4?”

4. **Questionable core contribution: separate ESM2 heads for wildtype and mutant.**  
   *Reviewer 4*: “The key contribution of having separate ESM2 heads for wildtype and for the mutated sequence is questionable … To me it is not clear how to decide what is wildtype and what is mutation. What if the mutation is adopted by evolution and becomes the 'new wildtype' and then gets mutated again? There is no fundamental reason to distinguish between the sequences. So I believe that using the distinction between the sequences based on the dataset definition and then adapting the two heads to this definition only leads to overfitting to the dataset, potentially explaining any benefit gained from these separate heads. I don't have a concrete suggestion how to prove authors point, because I think the point is wrong. If authors stand by their point they should present convincing evidence supporting that their 'inductive bias' is not just overfitting to the dataset definition of what is wildtype and what is mutation.”  
   *Reviewer 1* notes that separate models “provides some benefits” in ablations, but does not address the overfitting concern.

5. **Arbitrary split for LoF/Neutral/GoF categories.**  
   *Reviewer 1*: “While the idea to compute separate correlation metrics for LoF, neutral, and GoF variants is clever, the method of dividing variants into these categories by splitting the ground-truth scores into thirds is arbitrary. A more robust method, such as a Gaussian mixture model with three components, could provide a more principled assignment of variants to these classes. The current approach may lead to inconsistent categorization of variants, especially if the distribution of DMS scores is not uniform or if the number of variants in each category is not balanced. This could bias the evaluation metric and misrepresent the true performance of the model.”  
   *Reviewer 4* also asks about the Harmonic Spearman: “Could authors explain the choice of using harmonic average? Could authors clearly compare harmonic spearman to normal spearman? How does it change the evaluation of all the benchmarked models?”

6. **Poor paper structure and formatting.**  
   *Reviewer 2*: “The organization needs improvement. Some terms like 'PTEN' didn't have full names. The size of font in those figures is too small to read and is not consistent. The section 7 should be in the section of the experiential setup.”  
   *Reviewer 4* provides extensive criticism:  
     - Introduction contains related work and results discussion instead of pure motivation.  
     - Chapter 4 lacks details; Figure 1 is never referenced; the regression head is described textually but could benefit from equations.  
     - No table summarizing results; numbers are scattered.  
     - Authors are verbose and repetitive, wasting space (e.g., whole first page on abstract). Figures have illegible tiny fonts.  
     - References poorly formatted: some start with “…”, AlphaFold cited as “(John Jumper, 2021)” without crediting the collective effort.  
   *Reviewer 1* did not comment on structure.  
   *Reviewer 3* found the paper well‑structured, creating a contrast. This disagreement is preserved.

7. **Limited novelty.**  
   *Reviewer 3*: “The novelty of this paper is limited. The use of dual ESM2 embeddings to separately represent wildtype and mutant sequences, along with the introduction of the Harmonic Spearman metric to address label imbalance, appears more incremental than groundbreaking. … the Harmonic Spearman metric is not a novel statistical method, and its application here feels like a minor methodological adjustment rather than a significant contribution.”  
   *Reviewer 4*: “The technical novelty of separate fine-tuning of two ESM models with a custom regression head is limited.”

8. **Motivation for cross‑protein generalization benchmark underdeveloped.**  
   *Reviewer 3*: “While focusing on cross-protein generalization within the same family is technically interesting, it lacks a clear connection to real-world situations where this type of evaluation would be essential. The paper does not adequately justify why cross-protein generalization within a family is a crucial benchmark for practical applications. It is not clear if this type of generalization is more relevant than, for example, generalization to different experimental conditions or different types of mutations.”

9. **Additional methodological concerns.**  
   *Reviewer 2*: “Have you conducted multiple train-test split seeds in ablation study of ESMGain? Why the result of the original ESMGain in the ablation study is different from the one in Fig2? Do they use different datasets or strategies to train and test?”  
   *Reviewer 1* asks: “Does the harmonic Spearman correlation provide a more meaningful ranking than say AUROC at distinguishing the bottom third from the top third of variants?”  
   *Reviewer 4* asks for a table with both Spearman and Harmonic Spearman for all benchmarked models.

10. **Disagreement among reviewers on presentation quality.**  
    *Reviewer 3* found the paper well‑structured and clearly written.  
    *Reviewer 4* rated Presentation 1 (lowest), citing poor structure, tiny figures, messy references.  
    *Reviewer 1* and *2* gave Presentation 2. This divergence is noted.
