## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers describe ColaBO as the first Bayesian-principled framework to inject user prior beliefs (e.g., about optimum location or optimal value) directly at the Gaussian process surrogate level rather than at the acquisition function level.  It reweights the GP prior with a user-defined belief via rejection sampling and decoupled path-wise conditioning, making it compatible with any Monte Carlo acquisition function (e.g., Log Expected Improvement, Max-Value Entropy Search).  All reviewers note the novelty of the approach, but most express concerns about limited theoretical grounding, narrow empirical evaluation, and clarity of presentation.  One reviewer (R4) found no salient weaknesses, while three others (R1, R2, R3) raise several specific criticisms.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and principle.**  The idea of integrating user knowledge at the surrogate level is original and appears more principled than earlier methods that modify only the acquisition function (R3, R4).  It is the first to do so in a general, unified way (R4).
- **Generality of the framework.**  The method is compatible with all Monte Carlo acquisition functions (R2) and can in principle handle multiple types of user beliefs (optimum location, optimum value, preferences) (R4).
- **Empirical robustness.**  When the prior is accurate, ColaBO substantially accelerates optimization; when the prior is misleading, it retains approximately default performance, demonstrating minimal degradation (R1, R2).  R2 specifically notes that in Figure 5 ColaBO-MES with a poorly located prior still outperforms plain MES.
- **Flexibility and adaptability.**  The approach allows domain experts to customize the optimization routine with minimal knowledge (R1).
- **Illustrative figures.**  Figures 1 and 2 clearly show how the user belief shapes the GP posterior and the acquisition function landscape (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical analysis.**  The paper lacks a rigorous analysis of how reweighting the GP prior affects the convergence properties of Bayesian optimization (R2).  No bounds are provided on the approximation errors introduced by Random Fourier Features (RFF) or by the Monte Carlo acquisition function, and it is unclear how these errors propagate through the optimization (R2).  The derivation of importance sampling in Eq. (10) is unclear; the unnormalized density is integrated, breaking the claimed equality with Eq. (9) (R3).  The tempering scheme’s connection to generalized Bayesian inference is loose—the paper does not provide a statistically principled way to set the temperature, despite claiming to do so “in a principled Bayesian manner” (R3).
- **Insufficient and narrow empirical evaluation.**  Experiments use only a restricted set of test functions, all low-dimensional (R1, R2).  Higher-dimensional problems are not tested, and the algorithm’s scalability and behavior as dimensionality increases are unexplored (R2).  The impact of defining the prior over a subset of dimensions or with high-dimensional priors is not studied (R2).  Compared to the πBO paper, the evaluations are not extensive (R3).  Only one type of user prior (optimum location) is evaluated; other types listed in Section 3.1 (e.g., optimum value, preferences) are not tested, making it hard to judge the claimed generality (R3).  A reviewer (R3) questioned the fairness of the initialization: ColaBO and πBO start with the mode of the prior plus 2 Sobol samples, while LogEI and MES start with D+1 Sobol samples; conventional BO methods should arguably also receive the prior mode information since it is assumed available to user-guided methods.  Another reviewer (R2) asked whether the same effect could be achieved by simply defining the prior mean function in the GP appropriately (assigning high values where users believe the optimum is), implying a missing comparison.
- **Presentation and clarity issues.**  Notation is inconsistent: \(\pi(\cdot)\) is introduced in Eq. (3) with input from \(\mathcal{X}\), but later becomes \(\pi(f)\) (R2).  After Eq. (4), the notation becomes messy and hard to follow (R2).  Definition 3.1 conditions on \(\pi\) without clarifying whether \(\pi\) is a density, a function, or a random variable (R3).  Rejection sampling is a key step but the proposal distribution, acceptance criteria, and efficiency are not explained (R2).  RFF is described too briefly to understand how the feature mapping is constructed and used in practice (R2).  The derivation becomes informal in several places, affecting clarity (R3).  R2 also asks how \(\beta\) in the tempering scheme is determined.
- **Missing ablations and comparisons.**  R2 questioned the use of Log Expected Improvement instead of vanilla Expected Improvement; a justification is lacking.  R3 notes that Thompson sampling is a natural candidate for the decoupled sampling framework but was not included.  R2 asked why rejection sampling was chosen over alternative sampling methods, and what its computational efficiency is.  R3 also points out that the contribution statement is too general (items 1 and 3 could apply to any user-guided BO method) and expected more technical details specific to ColaBO.
- **Minor issues.**  The name “Collaborative Bayesian Optimization” may misleadingly suggest multiple users (R3).  Several typos and reference formatting errors are present (R3): “Carl et al 2022b” has a typo (“eearch”), Jones et al. 2018 missing journal entry, Kingma & Welling 2013 was published in ICLR’14.  Figure 9-12 error bands are hard to distinguish (R3).  Section 2.1 informally says “minimize f” instead of minimizing the expectation or regret, and Eq. (2) uses “equivalent in distribution” without definition (R3).  In Section 3.1, a sentence is incomplete (R3).  DoE is not defined (R4).  In Figure 6, the y-axis “Accuracy” decreases over trials, suggesting it should be \(1-\text{accuracy}\) or similar (R4).  A.3 has a grammatical error (“by using a sampling an offset direction”) (R4).  The conclusion has “pre-trainedp” (R4).
- **One reviewer found no salient weaknesses** while three others raised the above issues, creating a notable contrast in assessment strength.
