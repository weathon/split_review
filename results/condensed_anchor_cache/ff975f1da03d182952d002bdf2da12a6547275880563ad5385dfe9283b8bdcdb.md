## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses multi-target regression where the leaf node predictions of a decision tree must satisfy constraints (e.g., ℓ0 constraint). It adapts greedy top-down CART induction by incorporating constraints into the split evaluation, using mixed-integer optimization (MIO) for an exact solution and two heuristics (Lagrangian relaxation and feasible medoid). Experiments on a single modified dataset (SCORE) compare the variants against each other and a baseline CART tree whose outputs are post-hoc replaced with feasible values. Reviewers find the problem interesting and the heuristic proposals worthwhile, but raise concerns about novelty, lack of real-world validation, unclear problem formulation, limited baselines, and insufficient analysis of computational cost.

### Strengths
1. **Interesting and practical problem**: All reviewers agree that the question of learning decision trees with constrained vector-valued outputs is valuable, with potential applications in multi-target learning and fairness.
2. **Heuristic proposals**: The two heuristics (Lagrangian relaxation and feasible medoid approximation) are seen as useful attempts to mitigate the high computational cost of the exact MIO-based split evaluation (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). Reviewer 3 notes that the heuristics are interesting but not thoroughly explored.
3. **Clear exposition**: The problem and methods are explained clearly and intuitively (Reviewer 1).
4. **Elaboration on representative output vector**: The problem of finding a feasible output vector for a node is discussed both exactly and heuristically (Reviewer 4).
5. **Experimental illustration**: Experiments assess the potential advantages of the framework on a small-scale problem (Reviewers 1, 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Imprecise problem formulation**: The paper does not clearly specify whether constraints are instance-level (different feasible sets per example) or population-level (same feasible set for all leaves). This ambiguity affects the clarity of both training and prediction phases (Reviewer 4, W1/Q1). Additionally, the exact type of constraints on leaf predictions (e.g., ℓ0, linear, convex) is not precisely stated, though different constraint families have vastly different difficulty (Reviewer 3, W1).
2. **Limited algorithmic novelty**: The method is still based on standard greedy top-down induction; only the split evaluation is adapted to account for constraints. No new tree induction framework is introduced (Reviewer 3, W2). Related work is sparse, lacks papers from the last five years, and does not consider fairness constraints, dynamic programming, MIO-based tree learning, or alternating optimization methods that also incorporate constraints (Reviewers 1, 3).
3. **Weak experimental validation**:
   - **Single dataset**: Only the SCORE dataset (with artificially imposed constraints) is used. This makes it hard to assess generalizability. A simple baseline (CART regression tree with outputs replaced by feasible values) would be a fairer comparison (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
   - **Absence of state-of-the-art baselines**: Methods that learn optimal decision trees (e.g., via MIO or dynamic programming) can naturally include constraints and should be compared, especially given the small-scale problems used (Reviewer 3, W3).
   - **Lack of real-world justification**: The rationale for selecting the specific constraints on the SCORE dataset is not provided; they may be too tailored to the method. Reviewers ask for examples of natural real-world datasets where such constraints arise and discuss how biases from artificially imposing constraints on an existing dataset could affect results (Reviewer 1, Q1, Q2; Reviewer 2, W3).
   - **Insufficient computational analysis**: Training times are reported in scaled units, obscuring true computational cost. The order-of-magnitude improvement from the heuristics and absolute time comparisons (e.g., seconds) are missing. It is unclear whether the MIO solver terminates within a reasonable time for larger problems (Reviewer 1, W5; Reviewer 2, W1, W2).
   - **Missing detailed experiment description**: Figure 1 is not clearly explained; in particular, how the 4000 samples were generated and from which experiment are not stated (Reviewer 1, Q3).
4. **Overstated novelty**: The claim of being the first method to address target-constrained problems is not adequately supported by the literature review and may be too strong (Reviewer 1, W1).
5. **Lack of insight on trade-offs and extensions**: The paper would benefit from showing average scores separately for nodes with feasible vs. infeasible outputs to better understand the method’s behavior. Discussions on extensions to ensemble methods (e.g., random forests) and adaptation to multi-label/multi-dimensional classification are missing; the mention of label powerset does not fully address the challenges of large output spaces with instance-level constraints (Reviewer 4, W2, W3; Reviewer 4, Q2, Q3).

*Note: Reviewer scores show a split: two reviewers (scores 5) find the work more positive, emphasizing the interest of the problem and heuristic value, while two reviewers (scores 3) are more critical of the novelty, experimental scope, and problem formulation. The above weaknesses reflect concerns from all reviewers, with the stronger criticisms (lack of novelty, weak baselines) coming primarily from the lower-scoring reviewers.*
