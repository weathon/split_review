## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Pairwise-Distance Estimators (PaiDEs) for epistemic uncertainty estimation in ensemble models. PaiDEs use pairwise distances between ensemble component distributions to bound entropy and approximate mutual information, avoiding expensive Monte Carlo sampling. This enables faster uncertainty quantification (up to 100×) that scales to larger input spaces (up to 100×) and higher dimensions. Experiments on active learning tasks using 1D sinusoidal data, Pendulum-v0, Hopper-v2, Ant-v2, and Humanoid-v2 (MuJoCo environments) demonstrate that PaiDEs can accurately quantify epistemic uncertainty with reduced computation. Normalizing flow ensembles (Nflows Base) are employed to model aleatoric uncertainty while PaiDEs leverage their tractable base distributions.

### Strengths
- Proposes a novel non-sampling approach to estimate epistemic uncertainty, an open problem; creatively leverages pairwise distances to avoid sampling limitations. Most reviewers found the approach clever, appealing, and theoretically justified.
- Good mathematical grounding in information theory and probability; principled design of PaiDE estimators with entropy bounds based on Kolchinsky & Tracey (2017).
- Computational cost advantages are obvious: removes dependence on sampling, superior to Monte Carlo approaches, not requiring variational inference. The method is efficient, scalable, and provides closed-form estimates with a theoretical tie to epistemic uncertainty.
- Experiments are well-conducted and results are promising: PaiDEs outperform Monte Carlo estimation in estimation quality, especially in higher dimensions, and are up to 100× faster.
- Presentation is clear overall: clearly explains limitations of sampling-based approaches, intuitive descriptions and visualizations, well-structured layout.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons to state-of-the-art methods**: No comparison against deterministic UQ (ND-UQ, van Amersfoort et al., 2020), active learning methods (BALD, Houlsby et al., 2011; BatchBALD, Kirsch et al., 2019; BADGE, Ash et al., 2019), MC Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016), or Deep Ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). The paper claims strong performance for large query sizes, yet does not compare with diversity-aware acquisition methods. One reviewer (score 3) found this lack of comparison severely limited the evidence.
- **Theoretical analysis is incomplete**: No analysis of estimator bias, convergence, or optimality properties. The tightness of the pairwise-distance approximation is not addressed, nor is its impact on final performance. Experiments are limited to toy and relatively simple scenarios; the method’s behavior in complex real domains remains unvalidated.
- **Scaling behavior with ensemble size is unclear**: Only a small ensemble of 5 models is used. There is no study with larger ensembles (10, 50+). Key questions (accuracy improvement, diminishing returns, comparison to Monte Carlo scaling) are not investigated.
- **OOD detection not evaluated**: This is a missed opportunity to demonstrate broader utility of accurate epistemic uncertainty estimation.
- **Sections 5 and 6 are unclear**: The combination of PaiDEs with Nflows Base is hard to follow. How Nflows Base creates the ensemble is not clearly described – the variability of ensemble members, which determines epistemic uncertainty, needs explanation. In standard ensembles, randomness is enforced via resampling or weight initialization; here, the risk is that different randomizations lead to arbitrary variability. How is the NF ensemble’s variability controlled and in what sense is it meaningful?
- **Connection between base distributions and output distributions is not explicit**: Figure 1 appears early but is only referenced later. The claim that pairwise distances between output distributions equal those between base distributions is key but not formally shown. The distributions in the figure look different, so formal justification is needed. The origin of the base distributions (which drive epistemic uncertainty) is also left vague.
- **Limited scope and experimental evaluation**: The paper focuses solely on regression tasks, while active learning is heavily used in classification. This limits the work’s impact. The absence of comparisons with methods specifically designed for high-dimensional regression active learning is a notable gap. Only Nflows Base is considered as the ensemble method; applicability to other ensemble approaches (bagging, boosting) is not shown, which limits generalizability. The application scenario (active learning with epistemic uncertainty) is niche, and the evaluation does not demonstrate broad utility.
- **One reviewer (score 3) was notably less positive**, citing insufficient comparisons, missing OOD evaluation, limited theoretical analysis, and lack of scaling study; this contrasts with the more positive views (scores 5, 5, 6) that praised originality and performance.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity not explored**: No study of how choices for normalizing flow and distance metrics affect results.
- **Some writing and methodological clarifications needed**: Equation (1) must specify that p_i sums to 1; the use of θ is confusing (sometimes ensemble parameters, sometimes base learner parameters); grammar issue (“in addition to the improving the lower bound”); Table 1 should list environment dimensions; Figure 2 axes are missing labels; derivation of equation (3) is not shown; guidance on whether to use lower or upper bound estimator is lacking; pseudocode for the full active learning procedure (including whether y must be known) would improve clarity. One reviewer asked for clarification of “f(a_t, s_t) = s_{t+1}” and the meaning of “Number of samples” in Figure 3.
