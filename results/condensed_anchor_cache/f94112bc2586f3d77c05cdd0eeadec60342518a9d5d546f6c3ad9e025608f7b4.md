## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a multi-party training framework for discrimination-free insurance pricing when sensitive attributes are privatized. The method trains a separate regression model (specifically logistic regression) for each sensitive group and aggregates predictions using a predefined group marginal, with sensitive attributes obfuscated via randomized response. The insurer accesses only non-sensitive data; a trusted third party (TTP) holds the privatized sensitive attributes. Theoretical statistical guarantees are provided for both known and unknown group marginal \(\pi\), and empirical results on the Adult income dataset examine error rates across varied privacy budgets.

### Strengths
1. **Practical relevance**: The paper addresses an important real-world challenge—training fair ML models when protected attributes are not readily available—directly motivated by insurance pricing regulations and transparency demands. (R2, R3)
2. **Simplicity**: The method is exceedingly simple to implement: training per-sensitive-group regression models and weighted averaging. (R1)
3. **Theoretical guarantees**: Solid theoretical results with statistical guarantees, including investigation for known and unknown \(\pi\) (group marginal) and varying privacy budgets. (R2, R3)
4. **Clear presentation**: The write-up is precise and consistent; the ideas are well presented. (R3)

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty concerns**: The algorithm largely amounts to learning a per-group regression model and (weighted) averaging; it is unclear what is novel beyond that. (R1, R2, R3)  
   - The motivation that actuarial fairness differs from conventional fairness notions does not explain why a fair algorithm under privacy constraints for a conventional notion would not work; the difficulty of unavailable sensitive attributes is not unique to insurance. (R2)  
   - The claim of working under any loss function is not substantiated, as both theory and experiments focus exclusively on logistic regression, making it hard to appreciate the claimed generality. (R3)

2. **Privacy guarantee ambiguity**: The paper does not precisely specify the formal privacy guarantees for the trained model. It needs to clarify whether \(x, y\) are assumed public and the model is private only with respect to the sensitive attribute. (R1)  
   - The privacy mechanism is applied only on the sensitive attributes, not on the entire sample; how this translates into a guarantee for the overall model (especially given the multi-party flow) is not addressed. (R1, R2)

3. **Lack of practical validation for “actuarial fairness”**: The formulation of actuarial fairness used in the paper is borrowed from an earlier paper; it is unclear whether this definition is practical or broadly accepted in the insurance industry. A conceptual-level definition should have been given when the term was first introduced (in the introduction). (R2)  
   - Are there any popular fairness definitions already used in insurance pricing mechanisms? (R2)

4. **Missing comparisons and baselines**:  
   - The experiments compare only across privacy budgets for the proposed method, with no comparison against other single-party fair decision-making methods that handle private data or noisy sensitive attributes. (R3)  
   - No comparison is made with settings where fairness is computed on fully differentially private data \((X, D)\) or where causal inference methods are used to perform fairness in the absence of sensitive attributes. (R3)  
   - The choice of the Adult income dataset is questioned; the authors state they could not find insurance-like data, but at least 50 insurance datasets exist on Data World and other platforms. (R3)

5. **Unexamined assumptions and interpretability**:  
   - How restrictive are Assumptions A and B in Section 4.3? (R2)  
   - What is the interpretation of noise in this context (e.g., noise added to the sensitive attribute via randomized response)? (R2)  
   - How would overestimation or underestimation of noise affect fairness, particularly across unevenly distributed sensitive groups? (R3)

6. **Multi-party framework challenges**: The proposed method introduces a third party, raising issues of information leakage, computation overhead, and trust. It is not discussed how the approach compares against single-party alternatives (e.g., computing fairness on DP-transformed data). (R3)  
   - Could the algorithm be generalized if non-protected attributes (in terms of fairness) are also not readily accessible and must be obtained via a third party? (R2)

7. **Disagreement among reviewers**: While Reviewers 2 and 3 rated the contribution as good (3) and fair (2) respectively (soundness 3 and 2), Reviewer 1 found the soundness poor (1) and contribution poor (1), and expressed low confidence in the novelty and privacy framing. The higher-confidence reviewer (R1, confidence 4) was more negative than the others (R2/R3, confidence 2).
