## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the INSITE framework (Individualized Nonlinear Sparse Identification Treatment Effect) that reframes longitudinal heterogeneous treatment effect estimation as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) discovery problem. INSITE learns a population ODE and then fine-tunes patient-specific parameters, producing interpretable closed-form ODEs instead of black-box neural networks. The framework can incorporate any ODE discovery method (e.g., SINDy) and handles both continuous and categorical treatments, irregular sampling, and between-subject variability. Experiments on two synthetic datasets show competitive counterfactual prediction against neural-network-based baselines.

### Strengths
- **Novel ODE perspective:** The paper is the first to bring ODE discovery into longitudinal treatment effect estimation, opening a new solution paradigm distinct from neural-network-based methods (Reviewers 2, 3, 5). This bridges two previously separate fields (Reviewer 3).
- **Interpretability:** The discovered differential equations are human-readable and transparent, unlike black-box neural networks – a key advantage for healthcare applications (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Flexible framework:** INSITE can be applied on top of any existing ODE discovery method and can handle both continuous and piecewise categorical treatments, population- and individual-level estimation, and diverse between-subject variability (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Relaxes overlap assumption:** The ODE framework can relax the standard overlap assumption in causal effect estimation (Reviewer 2).
- **Competitive performance and robustness:** Empirical results show that INSITE is competitive with baselines and yields improved results even under model misspecification (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Well-written and reproducible:** The paper is well-organized, well-written, and provides extensive appendix material (including synthetic simulations) that ensures reproducibility (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear problem setting and presentation issues – particularly noted by minority reviewers (R3, R5).**  
  * Time is inconsistently defined: Section 2 writes “at \(t\in[T]\)” (discrete), while Assumption 2.2 uses “\(t\in[0,T]\)” (continuous). The distinction between continuous trajectories and discrete observations is not clearly stated (Reviewer 5).  
  * The role of function \(g\) in Eq. (3) is not explained; it is assumed to be the identity without justification (Reviewer 5).  
  * The connection between the proposed method and existing ODE-based treatment effect methods (e.g., Neural ODEs) is not established in the main paper; the related work paragraph in Appendix B.1 incorrectly groups RNNs/LSTMs as “ODE discovery methods” (Reviewers 3, 5).  
  * Presentation issues: “\(T\)” used in Section 2 but introduced in Section 3; typos (“Intrepetible” in Table 5, missing “to” on page 8); one paragraph exceeds the 9-page limit; overuse of the full name “ODE/ODEs” (Reviewer 3).

- **Insufficient discussion of assumptions and limitations.**  
  * The method critically depends on the choice of the function library; if the true dynamics (e.g., \(x^3\)) are not in the library, the method will fail (Reviewer 1).  
  * It assumes sparsity in the dynamics, which rarely holds in high-dimensional settings; forcing sparsity may oversimplify the model (Reviewer 1).  
  * It assumes deterministic, noise-free systems, while real-world data contain stochastic elements that can lead to inaccurate parameter estimation (Reviewer 1).  
  * The “strong assumptions” mentioned for interpretability (Section 5) are not elaborated – it is unclear what they are or how they limit applicability (Reviewer 2).  
  * The assumption that dynamics can be captured by simply varying constant parameters per subject may be insufficient for complex time series (Reviewer 5).  
  * The authors do not discuss the stability of discovered ODEs (e.g., chaotic behavior, sensitivity to initial conditions) nor the identifiability of ODE parameters – both critical for reliable long-term predictions and the interpretability claim (Reviewer 3).

- **Lack of formal identification results and theoretical grounding.**  
  * No formal identification result is provided for causal effects from the ODE perspective (Reviewer 2).  
  * The claim that Assumptions 3.1 and 3.3 relax the overlap assumption (Assumption 2.2) is not adequately justified; Discrepancy 1 is described as trivial (Reviewer 5).  
  * The paper lacks a theoretical analysis of how the framework ensures correct causal inferences under the ODE assumptions (Reviewer 3).

- **Experiments are too simplistic and limited to synthetic data.**  
  * All experiments use only synthetic data; no real-world or semi-synthetic datasets are included, which would strengthen the evaluation (Reviewer 2).  
  * The synthetic data are overly simple: \(g(x)\) is the identity, \(x\) is 1-dimensional, time points are evenly spaced (no irregular sampling tested despite the claim), and the data generation process (Eq. 5) closely follows the ODE assumption (Eq. 3), raising generalizability concerns (Reviewers 1, 2).  
  * Reviewer 1 specifically questions why INSITE should outperform ODE methods in BSV scenario A (Eqs. 5–6) and why Fig. 3(b) should be monotonically increasing with \(\gamma\) – both indicate missing analyses.  
  * No ablation studies examine how performance degrades when assumptions (e.g., library completeness, sparsity, determinism) are violated (Reviewers 1, 2).  
  * The method’s ability to handle categorical treatments is not fully described, and discrete treatment plans are not empirically tested (Reviewer 1).

- **Missing guidance for practitioners and practical utility.**  
  * The paper does not explain how to learn or choose the candidate function library – a critical component for applying the method (Reviewer 4).  
  * It is unclear how the discovered ODEs inform treatment decisions or provide mechanistic insights; practical utility beyond empirical validation is not demonstrated (Reviewer 3).  
  * No comparison of computational complexity (ODE solving vs. neural network training) is given; Reviewer 3 asks whether the method is faster than neural-network approaches.  
  * If the \(g(x)=x\) assumption is invalid, it is not discussed how to integrate a neural network or other machine learning method for outcome modeling (Reviewer 4).

- **Minor issues**  
  * The paper needs a separate paragraph for the ODE discovery description (Reviewer 5).  
  * Multiple typos and formatting errors as noted above (Reviewer 3).
