## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Per-Edge Weights (PEW), a graph neural architecture that learns distinct message functions per edge for predicting maximum link utilization (MLU) in multi-commodity network flow problems. Evaluated on 17 real Service Provider topologies and 2 routing schemes (SSP, ECMP) with over 80,000 experiments, PEW outperforms standard GNNs (GAT, GCN, GraphSAGE) and an MLP. Reviewers commend the extensive empirical study but raise concerns about limited novelty, inability to generalize across topologies, scalability to large graphs, missing specialised and non‑learning baselines, and insufficient motivation for a learning‑based approach. One reviewer (score 5) is considerably more positive than the other three (score 3), praising the clarity and thoroughness, while the majority note significant weaknesses.

### Strengths
- The observation that edges in flow networks serve distinct roles is meaningful and directly motivates per‑edge parameterisation.
- PEW achieves substantial gains over standard graph architectures (GAT, GraphSAGE, GCN) on most topologies, with large differences on some datasets.
- The experimental evaluation is extensive: 17 real networks (up to 82 nodes), 2 routing schemes (SSP, ECMP), >80,000 experiments, and realistic demand matrices generated via the gravity model.
- Use of real network topologies strengthens the reliability of the results.
- The paper provides analyses of the relationship between graph structure (e.g., size, node/edge heterogeneity) and model performance.
- One reviewer (R3, score 5) found the presentation clear and easy to read, and considered the evaluation thorough and convincing.
- The architectural modification (per‑edge weight matrices) is simple and could be adopted in similar problems.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: Using distinct message functions per edge is a variation of across‑relation relational GATs (R1). The method description is only half a page (R4). The idea of applying ML to network flow problems is not new (R4).
- **Inability to generalise across topologies**: PEW assumes a static topology; a separate model must be trained for each network (R1, R2, R3). It is unclear how PEW could handle graphs of varying size or structure, or whether it is merely overfitting a specific graph (R2). Practical applicability is limited because training a model per topology is expensive and does not allow transfer to new topologies (R1, R2).
- **Permutation invariance not addressed**: PEW does not guarantee invariance to node permutation. While the authors argue node identities are known, this raises concerns about robustness when labelling is inconsistent (R2).
- **Scalability concerns**:  
  - The number of parameters grows with the number of edges, creating a bottleneck for graphs with thousands or millions of edges (R1, R2, R3).  
  - Experiments are limited to graphs ≤82 nodes; behavior on larger networks is unexplored (R1, R2).  
  - The fixed number of demand matrices (e.g., 300,000) across all topologies may be insufficient for larger graphs and could distort results (R1).
- **Unclear motivation for learning‑based approach**: For small graphs, linear programming could compute MLU directly whenever demands or topology change; the advantage of a learned predictor is not demonstrated (R1, R2). The generation of ground‑truth labels and why learning from them is beneficial are not clearly articulated (R2). No non‑learning baseline (e.g., direct optimisation or traditional routing algorithms) is compared (R2).
- **Missing baselines and unfair comparisons**:  
  - No comparison against specialised ML methods from the related work on routing flows in computer networks (R2).  
  - GCN and GraphSAGE cannot handle edge features; edge capacities are aggregated into node features, likely hurting their performance (R3).  
  - It is unclear whether the parameter budgets across models are equal (R3).  
- **Narrow evaluation scope**: Only a single regression target (MLU) is considered. It is unknown whether PEW benefits other tasks such as classification, reinforcement learning, or predicting other performance metrics (R1, R2, R3). Predicting MLU is seen as less significant than designing the routing itself (R4).
- **Insufficient justification of architectural choice**: No demonstration is provided for why traditional message‑passing is not suited for flow routing; the argument is asserted without evidence (R4). The graph representation lacks capacity notation (K used without definition) (R4).
- **Presentation issues**:  
  - One reviewer (R4) found the paper spends too much space on experimental setup, while result presentation is unclear (scale of Figure 3, vague Section 5).  
  - Table 1 and Figure 4 are unclear: MSE is not shown for changed demand/topology, and Figure 4 only shows PEW with raw demands vs. GAT with sum demands, omitting the reverse comparisons (R1).  
  - R1 questions the necessity of including all learning curves (pages 17‑33 of the appendix).  
  - The use of CPU for training is not explained (R3).  
- **Minority positive contrast**: One reviewer (R3, score 5) was markedly more positive than the other three (scores 3), praising the clarity and thoroughness of the evaluation. Nevertheless, even this reviewer raised concerns about generalisation, scalability, and fairness of baselines.
