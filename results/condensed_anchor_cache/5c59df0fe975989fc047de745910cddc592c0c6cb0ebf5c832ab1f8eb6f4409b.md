## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Over-Smoothing adversarial Injection (OSI), a universal method to enhance graph injection attacks (GIA) by amplifying over-smoothing in GNNs. It introduces Feature Shift Rate (FSR) and Perturbation Shift Rate (PSR), links them to singular values of the normalized adjacency matrix, and uses a Smooth Injection Loss (SIL) to alter those values. Experiments on Cora, Citeseer, Wiki, and Computers show that OSI improves attack success rates for several base GIA methods. All reviewers found the over-smoothing perspective interesting and the motivation fresh. Two reviewers (scores 5, 5) rated the paper higher, emphasizing the importance of the problem and extensive experiments. Three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) raised significant concerns about the limited theoretical scope (linear/SGC only), prohibitive scalability (full SVD required), insufficient novelty (FSR/PSR similar to prior loss), missing comparisons (one-stage GIA methods, large-scale datasets), and unclear impact on clean-node accuracy and over-smoothing in shallow GNNs.

### Strengths
- The perspective of linking over-smoothing to graph injection attacks is fresh, interesting, and unique to the graph modality (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5).
- The theoretical analysis establishing the relationship between FSR, PSR, and singular values of the normalized adjacency matrix is interesting and sufficient (Reviewers 1, 2).  Reviewers 3 and 5 also find the introduction of FSR and PSR sensible.
- Extensive experiments on four benchmark datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Wiki, Computers) demonstrate that OSI effectively enhances several existing GIA methods (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The studied problem (enhancing graph injection attacks) is important for the security of GNNs (Reviewer 3).
- The method is effective in practice for the evaluated settings (Reviewer 5).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited theoretical scope and strong linearity assumption**: The theorems connecting FSR/PSR to singular values only hold for linearized GNNs (SGC) (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  The linearity assumption is strong; real-world models (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) use nonlinearities and weight matrices, and Eq. (6) may not be computable (or the analysis may not transfer) with activation functions (Reviewers 1, 3). How the theorems apply to popular backbone architectures and whether the over-smoothing amplification works for non-linear models remains unclear, limiting practical value (Reviewer 2, 4).  Moreover, the proposed method of encouraging over-smoothing may benefit little for targeted attacks (Reviewer 3).

2. **Severe scalability and complexity concerns**: Computing singular values of the normalized adjacency matrix is computationally expensive; back-propagation over the full spectrum (as implied by Eq. (13)) is prohibitively expensive for large graphs (Reviewers 2, 4, 5). The hyperparameter \(k\) (likely the number of top singular values to consider) is also expensive to tune (Reviewer 2).  Detailed complexity analysis and runtime/space overhead experiments are lacking (Reviewers 2, 3, 5). The authors claim "Computers" (≈14k nodes) as large, but it is small/medium; experiments on truly large graphs (e.g., OGB-arxiv, OGB-Products, MAG) are needed to demonstrate practical impact (Reviewers 2, 4, 5).

3. **Novelty concerns**: FSR and PSR are similar to the homophily loss and shift loss already proposed in Li et al. (2022, ICDE) [1], which limits the novelty of the motivation (Reviewer 5).

4. **Effect on clean nodes and natural accuracy**: The constraint that forces output representations of original nodes to be similar (even across classes) may severely decrease the natural accuracy of clean nodes (Reviewer 1). An effective attack should maintain performance on clean nodes while increasing attack success rates; experiments reporting clean-node accuracy are missing (Reviewer 1). Additionally, the relationship between injected nodes and target nodes (e.g., are target nodes 1-hop neighbors of injected nodes?) is not clearly explained, and the potential impact on 2-hop/3-hop clean nodes should be analyzed (Reviewer 1).

5. **Missing comparisons with existing GIA methods**: The paper lacks comprehensive discussion and experimental comparison with several one-stage GIA methods, such as hierarchical RL-based injection (WWW 2020) [1], scalable attack by injecting vicious nodes (Arxiv 2020) [2], and single node injection (CIKM 2021) [3] (Reviewer 3). It is unclear whether OSI can be applied to these methods, as the paper only considers the two-stage paradigm (first injection, then optimization) (Reviewer 3).  Reviewer 2 also notes missing discussions of existing GIA works.

6. **Unclear over-smoothing role in shallow GNNs**: Over-smoothing is typically associated with very deep GCNs (many layers); for shallow networks (e.g., 3-layer GCN used in experiments), node embeddings becoming similar is not necessarily a negative effect (Reviewer 5). A deeper discussion of how OSI degrades performance through encouraging over-smoothing in shallow models is needed (Reviewer 5).

7. **Unnoticability claims insufficient**: Figure 2 shows an insignificant difference in degree distribution with/without OSI; no numerical comparison is provided (Reviewer 5). The claim that OSI makes degree distributions more similar to a power-law distribution is not convincingly explained and may be a coincidence dependent on dataset and attack method (Reviewers 1, 3). An in-depth analysis or theoretical explanation is needed (Reviewer 3).

8. **Specific technical issues and writing problems**:
   - In the extreme case where all non-zero singular values are equal, the normalized adjacency matrix becomes proportional to the identity matrix, which seems undesirable; the authors should explain why this is not a problem (Reviewer 1).
   - Algorithm 1 line 11 has a confused use of \(d\) and \(b\) (Reviewer 3).
   - Typo: Figure reference in Section 1 appears as “Figure ??” (Reviewers 4, 5).
   - It is unclear whether every baseline attack model in Tables 1 and 2 is enhanced by the HAO defense (Reviewer 5) – clarification needed.
   - Why OSI does not always improve the basic GIA in the transductive setting (Appendix E) needs discussion (Reviewer 3).
   - Can OSI be adapted to targeted attacks (e.g., forcing target nodes to a specific class)? (Reviewer 1).
   - Why is FSR defined on the feature dimension instead of node pairs in Eq. (6)? (Reviewer 3).
