## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LLM4Hypergraph, a 21,500-problem benchmark spanning eight low-order, five high-order, and two isomorphism tasks, using both synthetic hypergraphs (pyramid, grid, wheel) and real-world hypergraphs from citation networks and protein structures. Six LLMs (including GPT-4o) are evaluated, and two prompting techniques, Hyper-BAG and Hyper-COT, are proposed to improve high-order reasoning. The paper reports an average 4% improvement on structure classification tasks. Reviewers agree the benchmark is comprehensive and the study thorough, but there is strong disagreement on contribution significance and dataset rigor. The minority reviewer (score 3) views the work as incremental, while majority (scores 8, 8) consider it valuable but with notable weaknesses in dataset comprehensiveness, motivation, and analysis.

### Strengths
- First comprehensive benchmark specifically for evaluating LLMs on hypergraphs, covering a wide range of tasks and hypergraph types (low-order, high-order, isomorphism).
- Thorough experimental design: evaluates six prominent LLMs, uses both synthetic and real-world hypergraphs from citation networks and protein structures.
- Well-organized and clearly written; problems are well-structured and categorized by objective (low-order, high-order, isomorphism).
- Code released for reproducibility.
- Novel prompting framework incorporating seven hypergraph languages and two techniques (Hyper-BAG and Hyper-COT); shows that suitable hypergraph representation can aid LLM comprehension.
- Identifies model strengths and weaknesses across tasks; offers insights into how prompting adaptations (CoT, BAG) improve performance on hypergraph tasks, especially for Vertex Set Connection Checks and Vertex-Set–in-Hypergraph Checks on synthetic data.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation insufficient**: *Why* is it important for LLMs to understand hypergraphs beyond the fact that similar research has been done on graphs? Practical use cases are not discussed. Simply saying “underexplored” does not justify the direction. One reviewer (score 3) asks: why is prompting a promising direction vs. other techniques like function calling? (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Incremental contribution** (minority view, Reviewer 2): The benchmark and prompting methods are naive adaptations of existing graph benchmarks and prompting (CoT, BAG) to hypergraphs. Results largely mirror those for simple graphs (CoT/BAG help), with the only novel point being that language choice matters—this is considered trivial. The paper thus “fails to find new insights specific to hypergraphs” and the contribution is insignificant.
- **Unsupported performance claims**:
  - Abstract states “average 4% (up to 9%) performance improvement on structure classification tasks”. However, Hyper-BAG gains only 2% for low-order and 2.8% for high-order hypergraphs (Table 4/5). The claim is misleading. (Reviewer 2)
  - Claim of “first instance that includes isomorphism checks” is imprecise: isomorphism is a special case of Maximum Common Subgraph (MCS) already included in GraphArena (Tang et al., 2024). Scope (e.g., “in hypergraphs”) must be specified. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of statistical rigor**: Given the slim performance gains of Hyper-BAG and Hyper-COT, the paper should report confidence intervals or standard deviations (e.g., across multiple runs and graph instances) to show that improvements are not due to stochastic variation. (Reviewer 2)
- **Dataset comprehensiveness and transparency**:
  - “Hypergraph size” undefined: does it refer to number of nodes, number of hyperedges, or sum of hyperedge sizes? Not clarified in main text. (Reviewer 3)
  - Specific sizes of synthetic and real-world hypergraphs not provided in main text. Even the “large-scale” hypergraphs in Appendix B.5 have only 15–20 vertices, far too small to capture realistic high-order structures. Analysis should consider density (exponential growth of potential hyperedges) and prompt length impact. (Reviewer 3)
  - Synthetic hypergraphs limited to pyramid, grid, wheel structures; other models (e.g., configuration models) are neglected, reducing representativeness. (Reviewer 3)
  - Real-world hypergraph descriptions and selection criteria are missing. (Reviewer 2) It is unclear how random walk sampling from real hypergraphs (Appendix A.2) ensures sampled subhypergraphs “retain intricate and authentic correlations”. (Reviewer 3)
- **Task difficulty classification unclear**: The basis for labeling tasks as low-order vs. high-order is not fully justified. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing discussion**: The paper should cite and discuss “When LLM Meets Hypergraph: A Sociological Analysis on Personality via Online Social Networks” (CIKM 2024) in related work. (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of generalizability analysis**: How do the prompting techniques/extend to other complex data structures? And how does performance depend on hypergraph domain (e.g., emails vs. coauthorship)? (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3)
- **Accuracy computation unclear**: For tasks with numeric answers (e.g., counting), how is accuracy defined? Exact match, or threshold tolerance? (Reviewer 2)
- **Minority reviewer (score 3) is significantly more negative** on contribution and claim precision; note that two other reviewers (scores 8) found the benchmark valuable despite these weaknesses.
