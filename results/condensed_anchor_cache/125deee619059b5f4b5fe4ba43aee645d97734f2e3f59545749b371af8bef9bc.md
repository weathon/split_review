## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a knowledge transfer method for curriculum learning in reinforcement learning. It decomposes a target task into simpler sub-tasks by modifying its degrees of freedom (e.g., dynamics, reward sparsity, state/action space), trains on easier source tasks first, and transfers knowledge to harder tasks by blending actions from source and target Q-functions via a softmax-based mixing of normalized action-values. The approach avoids reusing ANN weights or explicit state/action mappings. Experiments are conducted on several classic Gym environments (e.g., Mountain Car, Taxi Cab) and one real-world pump scheduling control task. Reviewers disagree on clarity and completeness: one reviewer (score 5) finds the paper well-written and original, while four others (scores 3) find it hard to follow, technically unclear, weakly benchmarked, and lacking comparison to baselines.

### Strengths

- The research direction of curriculum transfer learning is important and may inspire the community (R1, R2).
- The paper addresses a real-world control task (pump scheduling) (R1).
- The approach combines previously known methods (value function transfer, curriculum learning) in a novel way by using degrees of freedom to define task complexity (R3). One reviewer notes this as a key originality point (R3).
- The methodology section is introduced thoroughly and is presented in a clear and easily understandable manner (R5). A different reviewer states the paper is well-written, with clear terms and a well-described related work section (R3).
- The paper addresses practical concerns such as self-correction and reward normalization across sub-tasks (R3).
- The idea of mixing Q-function estimates as a form of knowledge reuse looks reasonable (R4).
- The method targets a general strategy that may handle different categories of task complexity shift (R4).
- Extensive empirical evaluations are conducted across a range of classic RL environments and control tasks (R5).

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and technical details are major issues**: The paper is hard to follow and requires significant improvements in clarity (R1, R2, R4, R5). Specific missing or unclear details include: (a) what the “sampled policy” is when source tasks are sampled based on complexity (R1); (b) the definition and learning of the mapping function \( h(\cdot) \) for different state spaces and the meaning of \(\phi\) (R1); (c) whether the equation in Section 4.2 updates the source Q-functions and why (R1); (d) the formula “\(\alpha+\beta=1\) and \(\alpha>\beta=1\)” does not make sense (R1); (e) how multiple Q-functions are combined into one source Q-function is not clear (R1); (f) the way of mixing Q-networks resembles Dueling DQN but no theoretical motivation or association is provided (R4); (g) details on generation of source tasks and hyperparameter settings are insufficient (R5); (h) it is unclear how the “Transfer+CL” in Figure 1 uses the source Q-function—specifically, whether the converged Q-function from the previous curriculum step serves as the source Q-function for the next step (R5).
- **Inconsistent terminology and typos**: Terms like “self-correction” and “self-correcting” are mixed (R1); several typos make the paper harder to understand (R1).
- **Figures are hard to read and results difficult to interpret**: Figures (e.g., Fig. 3, Fig. 4) are hard to read, show extremely large variance, and it is difficult to tell competing methods apart due to overlap (R1, R2, R4). The text claims learning is significantly enhanced but it is not clear what indicates this (R2). A table reporting statistics at selected episode steps would be more readable (R4).
- **Lack of baselines and weak comparisons**: No comparisons to standard curriculum training where policies are simply trained to convergence in a sequence of tasks (R2). The baselines such as \(\pi_1\) look weak (R4). There are too few ablation and comparative experiments, raising doubts about scalability (R5), specifically the difficulty of adjusting \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) across different tasks (R5). The validation assumes a strategy to generate a curriculum, which is central to curriculum learning, but does not benchmark other competing transfer strategies (R4).
- **Motivation and contribution are unclear**: The main contribution is stated as “demonstrating the effectiveness” in the abstract, which is too vague (R5). The paper does not clearly identify what specific problem within curriculum learning it addresses (R2). The comparison to existing work could be more detailed in the introduction (R5).
- **Limited scope of experiments**: Empirical results focus on proof-of-concept rather than systematically varying the degree of freedom to show impact on algorithm performance (R3). No theoretical proofs strengthen the claim about degrees of freedom (R3). Validation tasks are similar in scale and underlying goal; more complex and diverse environments are needed (R4). The method uses \(\max(Q)\), which prevents extension to continuous action spaces (R1). The paper demonstrates only some task variations (e.g., dynamics, reward sparsity) but does not show transfer between different state/action spaces (R4).
- **No discussion of limitations**: The paper does not discuss limitations, such as the curriculum being defined by humans, which is infeasible for complex domains and requires expert domain knowledge (R1). Automatic curriculum generation works exist (e.g., Dennis et al. 2020, Parker-Holder et al. 2022, ICML 2023) that do the same thing automatically, which the paper ignores (R1).
- **Methodological concerns**: Early in training in the target task, the target Q-function will not have converged and will have poor estimates, while using the source Q-function would likely be out-of-distribution in the target task (R2). The assumption that the source Q-value is a good proxy for a good action in the target task when tasks differ is not justified; there is no analysis of conditions under which this is expected to work (R2). The paper does not explain why blending is needed rather than simply starting with the source policy and performing standard RL training in the target task (R2). If state-action spaces are the same, simpler sequential training suffices; if different, other methods (bootstrapping, action advising, distillation) would still be needed (R2).
- **Minority positive reviewer notes that despite the paper’s completeness in methodology, the empirical results are incomplete** (R3). This reviewer (score 5) finds the paper well-written and original but agrees that more complex examples and either theoretical or more thorough empirical evidence are needed (R3). The paper feels incomplete without supporting evidence for the degree-of-freedom concept (R3).
