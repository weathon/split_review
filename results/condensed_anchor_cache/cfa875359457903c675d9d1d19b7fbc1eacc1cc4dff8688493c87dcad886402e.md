- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 6, 5, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an incentive-aware framework for federated learning that uses Wasserstein distance to quantify data heterogeneity and designs reward functions based on peer prediction and a two-stage Stackelberg game to incentivize clients to collect diverse data. The goal is to accelerate convergence by encouraging effort that reduces non-IIDness. The paper provides theoretical convergence bounds and experiments on real datasets.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a relevant and important problem: incentivizing heterogeneous data collection in FL. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- The presentation of concepts, base terminology, examples, and how the work differs from existing approaches is well thought out and clear. (Reviewer 2)
- The result for pure Nash equilibrium (Theorem 5.2) provides a useful characterization of client utility properties. (Reviewer 4)
- The paper emphasizes the cost clients incur for collecting non-IID data, a novel perspective. (Reviewer 3)
- The investigation of constants (e.g., Lipschitz constants) in the results and assumptions is appreciated. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear/Unrealistic Model of Effort and Non-IIDness**: The notion of “effort” is not justified – it is unclear how an agent can reduce the non-IID degree of its own data simply by incurring more effort. The model of effort and non-IID level is not reasonable or realistic. (Reviewer 1) The meaning of “decreasing the degree of non-iid” in Section 3.2 and the associated “cost” are not precise. (Reviewer 4)
- **Unrealistic Assumptions**: The paper assumes agents know the true central distribution (complete information) – this is not reasonable or justified. (Reviewer 1) The learner is assumed to have access to \(\mathcal{D}_c\) (the reference IID distribution); it is not explained how this assumption can be satisfied in practice or what its properties are. (Reviewer 4) The statement “decentralized agents” in the abstract contradicts the central-learner setting. (Reviewer 4)
- **Limited and Unconvincing Experimental Validation**: The experiments are very limited: the first two plots merely show that training on a limited set of labels is harmful, and the other two show utility stable across rounds – it is not clear why stability indicates equilibrium; the variation across rounds seems irrelevant. The key is Figure 4, which shows the cost-reward tradeoff. (Reviewer 3) The FL setup resamples data from the same shared pool, which is unrealistic – real clients have distinct examples. (Reviewer 3) The paper lacks comparison with existing methods or simple baselines. (Reviewer 4) The learning rate is set to \(\eta = 0.01\) for all experiments without justification. (Reviewer 4)
- **Time-Varying Dynamics Unclear**: It is not clear whether clients adjust their dataset over the course of training or whether the framework is time-varying. (Reviewer 3)
- **Figure 3 Does Not Clearly Demonstrate Equilibrium**: It is not clear why Figure 3 indicates equilibrium – the authors claim utilities of two randomly selected agents remain stable, but fluctuations (attributed to random selection of agents) make it difficult to see equilibrium. Which two agents are shown? (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Theoretical Contribution Unclear**: Lemma 4.2 is an application of an existing result and provides a looser upper bound, making its theoretical contribution unclear. The claim that Wasserstein distance is an *essential* factor is not justified – the existing bound already formalized data heterogeneity. (Reviewer 4) The lemma’s Lipschitz condition (\(L_{x|y=i}\)) is ambiguous (Lipschitz w.r.t. what?). (Reviewer 4) Variables in Lemma 4.1 (\(g_t, \bar{g}_t, f^*\)) are not defined. (Reviewer 4) The purpose of \(\text{Bound}(e)\) is unclear; the “Ninety-ninety rule” is not explained. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing Discussion of Modern FL Practices**: Pretrained weights (e.g., ImageNet, foundation models) can suppress underlying data heterogeneity – how does this affect the proposed framework? (Reviewer 2) The paper does not discuss how to assess the non-IID degree \(\delta_k\) of an agent’s data without it being provided. (Reviewer 2)
- **Key Assumptions About Equilibrium**: The condition that “equilibrium prevails when unilateral deviations affect only the deviating agent’s utility” is a strong assumption; it is not discussed whether adversarial behavior falls under this category or how the system can protect itself. (Reviewer 2)
- **Definition and Use of Wasserstein Distance**: The definition of \(\delta_k\) is not formally recalled – is it the formal definition of Wasserstein distance? Does it work for regression problems? What is the support of \(p^{(k)}\) and \(p^{(c)}\)? How can \(\delta_k\) be obtained in implementation? (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing Citations and Similarities to Prior Work**: Definition 5.1 is quite similar to Definition 6 in Blum et al., 2021, but that work is not cited when the definition is introduced. (Reviewer 1)
- **Theoretical Gaps**: Theorem 5.1 takes a partial derivative of a function \(f\) – does this require differentiability? If so, what are the practical motivations? (Reviewer 4) In Definition 5.1, how are constants \(d_k^1, d_k^2\) set or determined and how are they related for a fixed \(k\)? (Reviewer 4) Why only Nash equilibrium in a Stackelberg game? Can something be said about the leader’s behavior (Stackelberg equilibrium)? (Reviewer 4)
- **Writing and Presentation**: Writing can be improved; many statements lack precision (e.g., “encourage agents to upload models that capture data heterogeneity” is not precise; the necessity of the incentive mechanism is not demonstrated). (Reviewer 4) The paper received a presentation score of “poor” from one reviewer. (Reviewer 4)
- **Disagreement Among Reviewers**: While one reviewer (Reviewer 2, score 6) found the paper a good starting point that bridges theoretical insights with FL, another reviewer (Reviewer 4, score 3) identified major gaps in assumptions, theory, experiments, and presentation. The more positive view still noted missing discussions (e.g., pretrained weights, non-IID assessment) and defended the score after author response. The more negative view considered the theoretical contribution weak and the experiments insufficient. (All reviewers)
