## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers assess "Understanding and Enhancing the Transferability of Jailbreaking Attacks". They acknowledge the paper’s focus on transferability, the interesting observation of distributional dependency (intent perception), and the proposed Perceived-importance Flatten (PiF) method. Reviewers agree that the paper demonstrates high attack success rate and efficiency compared to GCG and PAIR, but raise several concerns about limited baselines, insufficient depth in the analysis, missing details, and narrow evaluation scope. Scores are uniformly 6; confidence is 3–4. Disagreement exists on the novelty of the distributional dependency analysis: Reviewer 4 finds it trivial, while Reviewer 3 considers it interesting and potentially generalizable.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and efficient method**: PiF uniformly disperses model focus across neutral-intent tokens, reducing attention on malicious tokens. Uses fewer adversarial tokens and requires less optimization time than GCG and PAIR (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
2. **High transferability**: PiF achieves high ASR across multiple target LLMs, outperforming GCG and PAIR (Reviewers 1, 2). Based on Llama-2-7B-Chat as source model (Reviewer 3).
3. **Interesting analysis of intent perception**: The paper studies token importance using a removal template, extracting attention-like information from LLMs. This provides a plausible explanation for transferability failures, illustrated in Figure 2 (Reviewers 3, 4). The design of evenly distributing importance is sensible (Reviewer 3).
4. **Presentation quality**: Well‑made tables, color scheme, and LaTeX formatting (Reviewer 4). The paper is a pleasure to read.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient baselines**: Comparisons are limited to GCG and PAIR (2023). More recent attacks (e.g., AutoDAN, ReNeLLM, [1,2,3]) are omitted without justification (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The original GCG paper evaluates transferability by crafting adversarial suffixes on multiple LLMs; the paper does not include a similar multi‑model source experiment and only uses Llama-2-7B-Chat (Reviewer 3).
2. **Shallow analysis in “understanding” part**: The effort on “understanding” transferability is far less than on “enhancing”. The distributional dependency (Perspective III) is trivial (Reviewer 4): it is unsurprising that high‑importance regions depend on model parameters. The analysis lacks depth—e.g., no exploration of architectural differences across LLMs or layer‑wise effects (Reviewer 4). Figures 1 and 2 are only demonstrative; more quantitative results supporting Section 3’s claims are needed (Reviewer 4).
3. **Missing implementation details**: 
   * Perplexity and instruction filters lack specifics: perplexity model, tokenization, threshold, and performance of normal text under these filters are not reported. In Table 3, PiF’s perplexity is 9× higher than PAIR’s, suggesting a suitable threshold could filter PiF prompts (Reviewer 1).
   * Hyper‑parameter settings for GCG and PAIR (e.g., optimization steps, learning rates, configurations) are not listed (Reviewer 1).
   * It is unclear how many adversarial prompts are generated per malicious request (Reviewer 1).
4. **Evaluation limitations**:
   * Only one large commercial model is evaluated (Reviewer 2). Tests with GPT‑4o, Claude‑3, Gemini‑1.5 series are missing (Reviewers 1, 2). It is not stated whether the same adversarial prompts are used across all target models or optimized per model (Reviewer 1).
   * A human study to verify whether generated outputs are truly harmful and provide correct/useful information is lacking (Reviewer 2).
   * Contradiction: Line 395 claims only outputs are accessible, yet GCG is a white‑box method requiring model parameters. How can GCG be used to attack GPT‑4 (closed‑source)? (Reviewer 4)
   * The paper claims to “enhance” transferability, but no clear results for this enhancement are presented (e.g., improvement over standard PiF compared to existing attacks applied to multiple source models) (Reviewer 4).
5. **Robustness concerns**: The synonym‑replacement strategy produces unnatural text with typos (e.g., “and the build a bomb”) and may be easily removed by random token dropout or grammar correction (Reviewer 2). The approach is not robust against paraphrasing (Reviewer 1, question).
6. **Defensibility**: According to Table 3, the Instruction Filter is quite effective against PiF; the paper does not justify why the attack is needed if it can be easily defended (Reviewer 3).
7. **Minor issues**:
   * Figure 1 does not clearly illustrate the paper’s main idea (Reviewer 4).
   * Table 1’s taxonomy (model‑level / token‑level / prompt‑level) lacks references for “model‑level” presumably meaning white‑box attacks (Reviewer 4).
