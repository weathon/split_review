## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper empirically tests whether common assumptions used in optimization convergence analysis (convexity, smoothness) hold along the actual training trajectories of modern deep learning models (e.g., ResNet, GPT-2, ViT, MLP-Mixer) with optimizers like SGD, SGDm, and AdamW. It introduces metrics such as instantaneous convexity, convexity ratio, instantaneous smoothness, and update correlation. The authors find that convexity is often violated, smoothness is often large and adapts to the learning rate, and successive gradient updates are often positively correlated rather than independent. They conclude that standard convergence analyses are unable to explain practical optimization behavior.

### Strengths
- The primary goal—verifying the specific inequalities used in optimization theory rather than just high-level assumptions—is clear and well motivated.
- The paper is well written with smooth organization; it connects high-level assumptions to specific inequalities and describes how each inequality is used in standard analyses and how it will be tested.
- The tracked quantities are well designed due to good abstraction of existing proof techniques.
- The findings in Section 3.2 (negative convexity ratio for many deep learning settings) imply that a popular convexity-based analysis technique cannot apply.
- The smoothness measure in Section 4 (relative change in gradients after each step) is computationally cheaper than the Hessian spectral norm and is shown to be large, implying that smoothness-based analyses that rely on the descent lemma do not apply.
- While some findings have been reported in prior works, this paper effectively connects the dots to provide a clearer overall picture and covers modern architectures (e.g., GPT-2) not considered in earlier studies.
- The paper illustrates the necessity for new theoretical frameworks for deep learning optimization.
- The overall results strongly support the claim that existing analyses are unable to explain convergence in realistic settings.

### Weaknesses
- **Section 3.1 significance**: Several reviewers noted that the quantity measured in Section 3.1 (convexity along the line between successive iterates) does not actually enter any convergence analysis. Thus the results there are not clearly linked to the validity of such analyses. One reviewer found the claim that the loss often lies below a local linear approximation (instantaneous convexity gap positive) surprising and asked for a 1‑D plot of \(F(\alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)x_{t+1})\) to verify there is no bug; the reviewer also suggested the difference between CIFAR-10 and ImageNet may be due to batch size rather than an intrinsic property.
- **Prior work overlap on smoothness**: The findings in Section 4 (smoothness adapts to the learning rate, the descent lemma is problematic, successive updates are anti‑correlated) are essentially equivalent to earlier results in Cohen et al. 2020/2022 and Mishkin et al. 2024 (directional smoothness). The observation of anti‑correlated updates was also noted by Xing et al. 2018. One reviewer noted that the discussion in Section 4.2 (update correlation) is similar to Cohen et al. 2020 Appendix H. The claim that “the classic smooth non-convex analysis… is problematic in practice” was already stated in Cohen et al. 2021.
- **Section 3.2 insufficient detail**: The computation of the “local linear approximation” in Section 3.2 is unclear—it is not specified whether it is a first‑order Taylor expansion nor at what point the gradient is evaluated. The text mentions successive iterates but does not clarify if the convexity ratio is an average over steps within an epoch or a cumulative average up to that epoch. (Same question raised by two reviewers concerning Figure 5 and the time range for the ratio.)
- **Smoothness measure as proxy for Hessian spectral norm (Section 4.1)**: The claim that the instantaneous smoothness is a cheap proxy for the Hessian spectral norm is not sufficiently substantiated—correlation is shown, but no theoretical justification (e.g., showing it is a lower/upper bound) is provided.
- **Figure 8 (random scaling) not informative**: The loss difference plots in Figure 8 are the same with and without random scaling, calling into question whether the guideline for optimizer design (Section 497) should be considered a guideline at all.
- **Alternatives discussed not contributive**: The discussion of alternative smooth non-convex analyses (weak convexity, complicated algorithms for non‑convex non‑smooth optimization, variants without random scaling) does not seem to contribute much to the central goal.
- **Contradictory messages about GPT-2**: Sections 3.1 and 3.2 appear contradictory: Figure 2 suggests GPT-2 is locally convex while Figure 3 indicates a lack of convexity.
- **Missing clarification on interpolation regime (Equation 4, Figure 4)**: In the interpolation regime when \(x_t\) is close to \(x_*\), both numerator and denominator in (4) should converge to zero; the quantity’s explosion at the end in Figure 4 needs explanation.
- **No assessment of convexity toward the global solution**: A reviewer asked why the paper never assesses convexity by tracking the instantaneous convexity gap between the iterate \(x_t\) and the ultimate solution \(x^*\).
- **Reference point for update correlation**: One reviewer asked whether using a better reference point (e.g., decaying learning rate or averaging weights across an epoch) would cause the average update correlation to be consistently negative.
- **Missing discussion of Appendix D experiments**: Appendix D is rough; many figures lack discussion, and some findings (e.g., D.1) could be better discussed in the main paper. A reviewer requested motivation for the additional experiments in D.3, D.4, D.8.
- **Connection to Allen-Zhu et al. theory**: A reviewer asked whether the experimental results violate the semi‑smoothness theory of Allen-Zhu et al. 2019 for ReLU networks.
- **Minor novelty**: One reviewer (score 5) rated the contribution lower (2) because much of the paper replicates prior works; other reviewers (score 6) rated contribution 2–3, acknowledging that the paper connects dots but does not introduce many new insights.
