## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Reinforcement Learning from Contrastive Distillation (RLCD), a method for aligning language models (LMs) with principles expressed in natural language without human feedback. RLCD generates preference pairs by sampling from an unaligned LM using a positive prompt (encouraging adherence to the principle) and a negative prompt (encouraging violation), producing more differentiated outputs and cleaner preference labels. These pairs train a preference model, which is then used to improve the base LM via reinforcement learning (PPO). Experiments on harmlessness, helpfulness, and story outline generation at 7B and 30B scales show RLCD outperforms RLAIF (Bai et al., 2022b) and context distillation (Huang et al., 2022) baselines. The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and the method is simple yet effective.

### Strengths
- The proposed method is simple and intuitive, with a clear motivation for why using contrasting prompts yields better preference data than prior work.
- Empirical results are strong across three diverse tasks (harmlessness, helpfulness, story outlining) and two model scales (7B and 30B), with evaluations using both GPT-4 and human judgments.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- RLCD eliminates the cost and time of collecting human preference data, requiring only an unaligned LM and prompt engineering.
- The method is generalizable: the same procedure applies to different principles and tasks, as demonstrated by experiments spanning harmlessness, helpfulness, and story outline generation.
- Experiments at multiple model sizes (7B and 30B) show the method works at different scales.

### Weaknesses
- **Methodological simplicity and lack of principled analysis**: The core contribution is a prompting trick (constructing positive and negative affix prompts). There is no theoretical justification or deeper analysis explaining why the design is better than direct RLAIF (Reviewers 2, 4). The technique is described as "straightforward" and "less technically strong" (Reviewer 2).
- **Preference model accuracy near chance**: On the harmlessness attribute, the trained preference model’s accuracy is only 2.4%–5.9% above chance (Table 5). It is unclear how such a small advantage translates into a large improvement in downstream RLHF performance, raising concerns about signal reliability and potential instability (Reviewer 5).
- **Diminishing gains with larger models and inconsistent scaling**: RLCD’s advantage over RLAIF shrinks when moving from 7B to 30B models (Table 2). On the helpfulness and outlining tasks, GPT-4 evaluation shows baseline RLAIF-30B outperforming RLCD-30B (Reviewer 3). Similarly, when RLAIF uses few-shot prompting (RLAIF-Few-30B, Appendix C), it yields both a better preference model and better aligned LM than RLCD-30B on harmlessness (Reviewer 5). This suggests RLCD may not scale well and could be limited by the model’s ability to distinguish outputs (Reviewer 5).
- **Lack of comparisons with few-shot prompting in RLCD**: RLAIF-Few-30B, which uses few-shot examples, outperforms RLCD; this technique could also be integrated into RLCD for a fairer comparison. RLCD’s zero-shot approach may be a limitation (Reviewer 5).
- **Missing standard metrics and evaluation details**: No RM-score (reward model score), perplexity, or output-perplexity are reported (Reviewer 1). No diversity metrics (e.g., Distinct-1, Distinct-2, sentence length, n-gram counts) are provided, despite Table 4 implying RLCD generations are much longer than base model sentences (Reviewer 1). Details of the GPT-4 evaluation prompt, shuffling procedure, and RLCD-Rescore setup are insufficient (Reviewer 1). The process of downstream PPO fine-tuning (input/output structure, reward derivation) is not described (Reviewer 3).
- **Incomplete description of the outlining task**: The story outline generation task is poorly described and lacks references, making it hard to assess its difficulty (Reviewer 1).
- **No experiments with human feedback in the mixture**: It is unknown whether RLCD still provides a significant advantage when human preference data is also available, which is more common in practice (Reviewer 2).
- **Potential reduction in preference data diversity**: Using fixed positive/negative prompts may reduce the diversity of the generated preference pairs (Reviewer 4).
- **Robustness to existing instructions in the original prompt**: The method’s effectiveness when the original prompt already contains positive or negative instructions is unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Minority reviewer concerns**: One reviewer (Rating 5) finds the method too simple, akin to a "prompting trick," and calls for deeper analysis and stronger baselines (Reviewer 4). Additionally, although human evaluation always prefers RLCD over RLAIF, GPT-4 evaluation on the 30B model sometimes favors RLAIF (e.g., helpfulness and outlining), indicating a discrepancy between GPT-4 and human judgment that is not explained (Reviewer 3).
