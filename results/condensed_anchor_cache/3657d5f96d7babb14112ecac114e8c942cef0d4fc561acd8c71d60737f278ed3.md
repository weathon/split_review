## Merged Review

### Summary
The four reviewers supplied mixed assessments (scores 5, 3, 5, 3). Most found the idea of training multiple parameter modes in parallel and the associated analysis of parameter drift to be intuitive and interesting. However, all reviewers identified major weaknesses in clarity, incomplete comparisons with recent ensemble-based continual learning methods, unclear or missing experimental details, potential information leakage from pre‑training, and insufficient rigor in the theoretical presentation. Reviewers 1 and 3 were more positive overall, while Reviewers 2 and 4 were significantly more critical of the presentation and motivation.

### Strengths
- The multi‑mode approach – training several parameter modes and then optimizing task allocation – is a novel and intuitively reasonable perspective on continual learning. (R1, R2, R3)
- The empirical analysis of parameter drift and the associated trade‑off between mode diversity and task allocation provides a closer look at continual learning dynamics. (R1, R4)
- The theoretical result (Theorem 1) that multi‑mode parameters are closer to multi‑task learning parameters supports the core idea. (R1, R3)
- The method shows performance improvements over some baselines (e.g., EWC) on Task‑IL and Instance‑IL benchmarks (CIFAR‑100, Tiny‑ImageNet). (R1, R3)
- R2 noted that the observation about regularization methods enforcing stability at the expense of plasticity is sound. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons with recent ensemble‑based continual learning methods.**  
  Several reviewers pointed out that the paper does not compare with methods that also use multiple models / modes, such as “Continual Learning Beyond a Single Model”, “Dynamic Network Expansion”, BatchEnsemble (ICLR 2020), Efficient Continual Learning Ensembles in Neural Network Subspaces, and CoSCL (ECCV 2022). R4 stressed that CoSCL in particular trains multiple modes in parallel with diversity regularization and includes similar empirical analyses (Figure 2 trade‑off, Figure 3 loss landscape); an in‑depth comparison is strongly encouraged. (R1, R4)

- **Clarity and organization are insufficient.**  
  - “Global geometry” is used inconsistently (sometimes referring to parameter space, sometimes to the loss landscape) and is never formally defined. (R2)  
  - Section 2 mixes background, motivation, and results; Section 3 reads like documentation for code methods rather than a coherent conceptual presentation. (R2)  
  - The description of “high level of certainty/uncertainty” (Sec. 3.1) is unclear; it is not explained how this certainty is measured. (R1)  
  - The “input loss” used during mode updates is assumed to be cross‑entropy but is not explicitly stated. (R1)  
  - The difference between task‑IL and instance‑IL is not clearly described, and the paper does not cover class‑incremental or domain‑incremental settings. (R1)  
  - Two figures share the same label (Figure 1). (R2)  
  - On page 2, Table 2 is referenced when Table 3 is intended. (R2)  
  - Figures and tables are nearly illegible. (R2)  
  - `argmin` is treated as a vector rather than a set. (R2)

- **Theoretical statement (Theorem 1) is ambiguous.**  
  - The symbols \(T(i)\) and \(i\in N\) (with \(N\) a natural number, not a set) are undefined. (R2)  
  - It is unclear whether the parameters of each mode are disjoint; if they are, how is the split done? If not, the total model size required must be reported. (R3)

- **Unclear / missing details in the method.**  
  - The coefficients \(\alpha_i\) are said to be “randomly sampled”, but no justification or description of the sampling distribution is given. Why is this better than using \(\alpha_i = 1/N\)? How does the sampling affect performance? (R2)  
  - The roles (signs) of \(\beta_{\max}\) and \(\beta_{\min}\) are not discussed. In the pseudocode, if \(\beta_{\max}\) is positive, it *minimizes* the distance, contradicting the text which says it *maximizes* it. (R2)  
  - The manuscript states that the method is “online”, yet in the appendix the model is trained for 200 epochs per task. A clarification is needed. (R3)  
  - The construction of the Instance‑IL Split‑CIFAR100 is given as dividing 100 classes into 5 tasks with 20 coarse labels per task, but the text says “each new task brings new instances from known classes”. These two descriptions are inconsistent. (R3)  
  - The motivation for the model design relies on intuitive, toy‑like empirical results; a stronger theoretical or empirical grounding is missing. (R4)

- **Pre‑training leads to potential information leakage.**  
  All experiments use a network pre‑trained on the full ImageNet dataset. Many ImageNet classes overlap or are very similar to classes in CIFAR‑100 and Tiny‑ImageNet. Reviewers cited prior work that avoids this by removing overlapping classes (e.g., [1] Learnability and algorithm for continual learning, [2] Prototype Augmentation and Self‑Supervision, [3] Dynamically Expandable Representation). (R3, R4)

- **Computational complexity is not analyzed.**  
  The method involves a backtracking algorithm, per‑mode gradient computations (Alg. 1, line 7), and an additional gradient for the combined parameters (line 12). A complexity analysis and training‑time comparison with baselines are necessary to assess scalability. (R2, R3)

- **Experimental scope and rigor are limited.**  
  - The performance gain over the strongest baseline (WSN) is modest and demonstrated only on simple benchmarks (Task‑IL on CIFAR‑100). Experiments on more complex benchmarks (e.g., larger‑scale or domain‑IL) are needed. (R4)  
  - The reported Task‑IL accuracy is low compared to prior works: e.g., [4] (Hard Attention to the Task, >80%), [5] (Supermasks in Superposition), and [6] (A theoretical study on solving continual learning, >92%). (R3)  
  - It is unclear how many random seeds were used for the experimental results. (R4)  
  - The method first computes modes from the initial task; the sensitivity of the overall performance to this initial choice was not experimentally investigated. (R3)  
  - References for EWC appear twice in the bibliography. (R4)
