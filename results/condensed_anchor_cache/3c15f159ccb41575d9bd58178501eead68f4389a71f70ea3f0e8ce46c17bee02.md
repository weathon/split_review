## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes BTBS-LNS, an ML-guided Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) framework for Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP). It introduces three key techniques: (1) Binarized Tightening – binary encoding and bound tightening to handle general integer variables; (2) an attention-based tripartite graph (based on Ding et al., 2020) with the softmax operation removed; (3) a branching network trained via imitation learning (using global optimal solutions for BTBS-LNS-G and local branching for BTBS-LNS-L) that overrides potentially incorrect variable-fixing decisions from the RL-trained LNS policy, helping escape local optima. Experiments on synthetic benchmarks, ML4CO competition instances, and MIPLIB2017 show strong performance against SCIP, Gurobi, and 13 learning-based baselines.

### Strengths
- **Integer variable handling via binarized tightening** is a novel and solid contribution, extending LNS to general MIPs beyond only binary variables (R1, R2, R3). The approach avoids expanding the problem size by re-encoding and tightening bounds.
- **Corrective/branching mechanism**: The imitation-learned policy (global or local) overriding RL decisions addresses limitations of RL and helps escape local optima (R1, R3). This is a practical way to combine different training signals.
- **Strong empirical performance**: The method is competitive with SCIP and often matches or surpasses Gurobi (v9.5.0). On MIPLIB2017 it achieves up to 10% better primal gaps than Gurobi under a 300s cutoff (R1, R2, R3). Performance is validated across four problem classes, ML4CO, and MIPLIB2017.
- **Comprehensive experimental setup**: Comparison against 13 learning-based baselines, including non-ML LNS variants, with ablation studies justifying each design choice (R1, R3). The breadth of benchmarks gives confidence in the approach.
- **Combination of multiple engineering improvements**: The paper simultaneously addresses GNN architecture, integer handling, and a corrective policy – a significant engineering effort (R1).
- **Adaptive bound-tightening**: Provides an efficient exploration–exploitation balance, with flexibility for both bounded and unbounded variables (R2).
- **(Minority positive)**: R3 highlights the originality of binarized tightening and the strong validation as a major reason for adoption in practice. R4 acknowledges the method’s effectiveness despite novelty concerns.

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental novelty of certain components**:
    - The GNN architecture is essentially the same as Ding et al. (2020); removing the softmax is an incremental tuning step, not a standalone contribution (R1, R3).
    - The “global” branching network is misnamed – it uses the same state representations as the LNS policy at inference, differing only by training objective (RL vs. imitation learning). It is more accurately described as an additional LNS policy trained differently. This similarity to prior work (e.g., Sonnerat et al., 2022 for local branching; learning from global optimum in [2] referenced by R4) is not sufficiently differentiated (R3, R4).
- **Data cost for the corrective mechanism**: Training the imitation-learned policy requires near-optimal solutions (global or local) from a MILP solver. This creates a chicken-and-egg situation: if such solutions are easy to obtain, ML may be unnecessary; if hard, data collection may be prohibitive (R1).
- **Writing and clarity issues**:
    - Paper is hard to follow due to dense information; lacks clear takeaways for each experiment (R4).
    - Bound tightening (Algorithm 1) is not clearly described; a numerical example would help (R1).
    - Table 2 caption is incomplete; Figure 2 refers to Eq. 6 but the equation is not defined (R4).
    - Attribution to Ding et al. for the tripartite graph is ambiguous – the phrase “We further develop an attention-based tripartite graph (Ding et al., 2020)” can be read as claiming it as new; should clarify that it is employed and improved (R3).
    - Minor grammatical issues (e.g., line 148, 156, 307) (R1).
- **High complexity and computational demands**: Combining multiple advanced techniques may require extensive parameter tuning and be computationally intensive (R2).
- **Dependence on initialization**: The method requires an initial feasible solution from a baseline solver (SCIP/Gurobi). Poor initialization could degrade performance, as the LNS policy’s effectiveness may depend on the starting solution quality (R2).
- **Potential for local optima**: Despite the branching network, the RL + imitation learning approach can still converge to suboptimal solutions in complex MIP landscapes (R2).
- **Training/evaluation on MIPLIB2017**:
    - It is unclear how the policies are trained for MIPLIB: how training, validation, and test splits are performed given the large variance in instance hardness (R4).
    - Results on MIPLIB in the main text are only compared against Gurobi; summaries against other baselines are missing (R4).
    - Best entries should be bolded in per-instance tables in the Appendix (R4).
- **Missing comparisons** with recent state-of-the-art methods, specifically those using GNNs and contrastive learning for LNS, such as Kong et al. (2024) and Ye et al. (R4, also R2 question).
- **Substitute variable representation**: How the index \(j\) in \(a_{i,j}^t\) is reflected as a feature for integer variables is not clearly described (features in Table 7 do not seem to cover it) (R3 question).
