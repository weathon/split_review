## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies the grokking phenomenon in a controlled setting of modular arithmetic with label corruptions. By training a two-layer MLP (and Transformers) on modular addition where a fraction of training labels are replaced with random results, the authors identify several regimes: memorization, coexistence, partial inversion, full inversion, and forgetting. They propose using the inverse participation ratio (IPR) to quantify periodicity in neuron weights as a proxy for generalization versus memorization. Key findings include: (i) simultaneous memorization of corrupted labels and near-perfect generalization on uncorrupted test data is possible; (ii) memorizing and generalizing neurons form distinct sub-networks identifiable by IPR, and pruning memorizing neurons improves accuracy on uncorrupted data while lowering accuracy on corrupted data; (iii) regularization methods (weight decay, dropout, BatchNorm) force the network to ignore corrupted data and achieve 100% accuracy on uncorrupted data; (iv) the effect of each regularizer is mechanistically interpretable: weight decay and dropout push all neurons toward generalizing representations, while BatchNorm de-amplifies memorizing neurons and amplifies generalizing ones. The paper also reports a two-stage training dynamics: first grokking (high train and test accuracy), then unlearning of memorizing representations (train accuracy dropping from 100% to 100(1-ξ)%).

### Strengths
- The paper is written very clearly and the results are well discussed (Reviewer 1).
- The idea of studying grokking under label corruption is interesting and allows neuron-level analysis of memorization (Reviewer 1).
- The identification of separate generalizing/memorizing sub-networks (collections of neurons) via IPR is a valuable result (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The phase transition phenomenon and the separation into interpretable regimes (memorization, coexistence, partial/full inversion, forgetting) are clear and meaningful (Reviewer 2).
- The randomization experiments are reminiscent of Zhang et al. (2017) and help understand generalization under label noise (Reviewer 2).
- The paper provides clear measurements that quantify network behavior (Reviewer 2).
- Insights into how different regularization techniques operate—BatchNorm works differently from dropout and weight decay—are useful (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The controlled synthetic setting is valuable for studying memorization and generalization in a simple, analytically solvable framework (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns:** The main task and analytical solution are taken from prior work (Nanda et al. 2023, Gromov 2023). The only novel procedural elements appear to be the use of IPR and label corruption (Reviewer 1). The paper follows a line of grokking papers and provides refined analysis rather than more general principles (Reviewer 2). The IPR measure itself comes from previous literature (Reviewer 3). The overall contributions are considered modest (Reviewers 1, 3 gave Contribution 2 fair; Reviewer 2 gave 3 good but noted less innovation).
- **Limited scope:** The analysis is restricted to toy datasets (modular arithmetic) and toy neural networks (two-layer MLPs, quadratic activation). It is unclear whether the findings extrapolate to more general problems, non-periodic sub-networks, or practical architectures and datasets (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The appendix includes only small variations of activation function or width, lacking in-depth analysis with more realistic deep networks (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of in-depth grokking analysis:** Despite the title emphasizing grokking, the paper does not provide detailed analysis of the grokking dynamics or new insights into that phenomenon (Reviewer 3). The learning dynamics (weight updates, gradient flow over time) and how they correlate with generalization are not studied in depth (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing analysis of memorization neuron patterns:** In the coexistence regime, there is no analysis of which neurons become memorizing vs. generalizing (e.g., location in layers, magnitude and direction of weight updates, learning trajectories) (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear significance of some findings:** The impact of weight decay is noted as not particularly significant compared to other results (Reviewer 1). The main takeaway from Sections 3.1 and 3.2 is ambiguous—whether they reveal new findings or merely confirm well-known results (Reviewer 1).
- **Unanswered mechanistic questions:** Even in the full inversion case without batch normalization, low-IPR neurons do not disappear—why? (Reviewer 1) Batch normalization downweights low-IPR activations but does not zero them, meaning memorization still occurs—how is this interpreted? (Reviewer 1) The multi-modality in Figure 4 alone does not imply low/high IPR correspond to memorizing/generalizing; that link is only clarified late in Section 2 (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing citations/typos:** The claim “In trained networks, a larger population of high-IPR neurons leads to better generalization” requires citation (Reviewer 1). “Choosing quadratic activation function… makes the problem analytically solvable” also needs a citation (Reviewer 1). A typo: “It’s role” and capitalization error “Section” (Reviewer 1).
- **Unaddressed parameter/architecture dependencies:** Why is p=97 chosen? Does behavior depend on p (odd, even, prime, size)? (Reviewer 3) In Figure 6, test accuracy remains near-perfect even after 60% of neurons are pruned—how does this ratio depend on task aspects or network size? (Reviewer 3)
- **Practical relevance unclear:** Is there any practical scenario where partial or full inversion might be observed? (Reviewer 2)
- **Reviewer disagreement on overall assessment:** One reviewer (Reviewer 2, rating 8) was more positive, finding the paper very clear and interesting with strong contributions, while the other two reviewers (Reviewers 1 and 3, both rating 5) had stronger reservations about novelty and depth. This contrast is preserved.
