## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper empirically investigates whether gradients in deep policy gradient (PG) methods lie in a low-dimensional, slowly-changing subspace—a phenomenon previously shown in supervised learning. Using PPO (on-policy) and SAC (off-policy) on six benchmark tasks (Finger-spin, Ball_in_cup, Ant, HalfCheetah, Pendulum, Walker2D), the authors compute gradient subspace fraction and subspace overlap for both policy (actor) and critic networks. They report that around 1% of parameter-space directions capture nearly all of the gradient norm, and that the subspace remains relatively stable during training. The paper discusses potential applications like subspace-constrained optimization and parameter-space exploration. Code is provided.

### Strengths

* **First demonstration of gradient subspaces in RL** – The paper extends the supervised-learning finding to two popular PG algorithms (PPO, SAC), which is non-trivial given the non-stationary data distribution in RL. This fills a gap where prior work focused on parameter subspaces (e.g., Gaya et al. 2023) or supervised learning only. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
* **Clear, well-written presentation** – The motivation, methodology, and results are described in an easy-to-follow manner, with intuitive explanations and thorough references to related work. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
* **Comprehensive experimental design** – Experiments cover on- and off-policy methods, different gradient and Hessian estimation approaches, and both actor and critic models. (Reviewers 1, 3)
* **Code released** – The authors provide their implementation for reproducibility. (Reviewer 2)
* **Discussion of future directions** – The paper outlines how the observations could enable second-order optimization or improved parameter-space exploration in RL. (Reviewers 1, 3)
* **One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 8) was more positive** – This reviewer initially gave a weak accept but raised the score to 8 after the authors promised to add explanations, run additional experiments, and modify the abstract to better scope the contribution. The other three reviewers each scored 6.

### Weaknesses

* **Weak link between experimental results and claims** – Several central claims lack rigorous justification:
  * Claim of “significantly larger curvature” from Figure 1 is qualitative; no baseline comparison is provided (“larger than what?”). (Reviewer 1)
  * Claim of “sufficiently stable to be useful for training” is not quantified—e.g., Figure 3 shows a subspace overlap value of ~0.4, but what does that number mean for algorithm design? (Reviewer 1)
  * Claim that the critic subspace “retains a larger portion of its gradient” is inconclusive because Figure 2 shows similar trends for actor and critic. (Reviewer 1)
* **Metrics lack rigorous motivation** – The chosen metrics (gradient subspace fraction, Eq. 5; subspace overlap, Eq. 7) are adopted from supervised learning but their relevance in RL is not justified:
  * The gradient subspace fraction (Eq. 5) may be trivial because any random projection preserves norm (Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma). A baseline comparison to random subspaces is needed. (Reviewer 1)
  * The subspace overlap metric (Eq. 7) is complicated, and it is unclear how it demonstrates stability or usefulness for restricting training to that subspace. (Reviewer 1)
* **Missing implementation details** – Many experimental choices are not explained:
  * In the subspace overlap equation (Eq. 7), the parameter \(k\) and whether it changes with timestep \(t\) are unspecified. (Reviewer 1)
  * How the “largest eigenvector” is determined (presumably by eigenvalue magnitude) is not explicitly stated. (Reviewer 1)
  * SAC uses multiple Q-networks; it is unclear which are analyzed. (Reviewer 1)
  * The cutoffs 2%, 0.14%, 0.07% (Page 5, Eq. 6) seem arbitrary; no sensitivity analysis is provided. (Reviewer 1)
  * The distinction between “estimated” and “true” gradients/Hessians (Figure 2) is not adequately justified, nor is the motivation for using an estimated Hessian to define the projection matrix. (Reviewer 1)
* **Limited experimental campaign** – Only six environments are tested (Finger-spin, Ball_in_cup, Ant, HalfCheetah, Pendulum, Walker2D). Environments with sparse rewards or high-dimensional observations are absent, limiting generality. More tasks and configurations are needed to confirm the conclusions. (Reviewers 3, 4)
* **No experimental validation of practical benefits** – The paper speculates about using the subspace for optimization or exploration but provides no concrete implementation or experiments demonstrating improvement. The connection to existing parameter-subspace methods (e.g., Gaya et al. 2023) is not examined experimentally. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
* **Unclear significance over parameter subspace methods** – The paper does not clearly articulate why focusing on the *gradient* subspace offers unique advantages over existing *parameter* subspace approaches for improving training efficiency. (Reviewer 2)
* **Conclusions largely replicate supervised learning findings without RL-specific insights** – The analysis does not delve into how non-stationarity, delayed rewards, or on‑ vs. off‑policy differences affect the subspace properties. Further investigation (e.g., why PPO shows a much lower gradient fraction than SAC in Figure 2) is missing. (Reviewer 3)
* **Lack of formal definitions** – Key terms like “subspace” and “high-curvature” are used informally; a precise, quantitative definition would aid interpretation and future work. (Reviewer 4)
* **Abstract initially overclaimed** – Reviewer 1 noted that the original abstract overstated the scope (e.g., implying existence for all PG methods and hinting at new methods). The authors promised to modify it (and, post-rebuttal, this reviewer acknowledged the change). (Reviewer 1)
* **Appendices not properly referenced** – The appendices are not cited in the main text and appear as a dump of graphs without accompanying textual explanation (beyond captions). (Reviewer 1)
