## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes S²GS, which augments 2D Gaussian Splatting with a learnable per-Gaussian foreground probability parameter. It targets automatic 3D scanning of rotating objects on turntables, eliminating the need for explicit object masks by optimizing this parameter under transformation constraints and regularization terms. The method requires pre-computed camera and foreground object poses (obtained via SfM). Experiments on newly collected synthetic and real datasets show S²GS outperforms 2DGS with masks by 27% in novel-view synthesis and 7% in geometry reconstruction. Reviewers acknowledge the self-supervised nature and integration into the GS framework, but raise strong concerns about practicality, the heavy assumption of known foreground poses from SfM, limited generalizability beyond simple turntable settings, missing ablations, and lack of runtime comparisons. One reviewer (R4) is significantly more negative (score 3) regarding these limitations, while others (scores 5,5,6) find the idea interesting but also question real-world applicability.

### Strengths
- Self-supervised method that removes dependency on explicit object masks by learning a per-Gaussian foreground probability.
- Integrates foreground/background segmentation directly into the Gaussian Splatting pipeline, avoiding external tools like SAM (R1).
- Can start with random initialization, reducing reliance on SfM for the segmentation initialization (R1). (Note: some reviewers (R3, R4) contradict this, arguing the method still needs SfM for transformation matrices.)
- Uses multiple regularization terms (local consistency, etc.) to guide optimization (R2).
- Collects and provides both synthetic and real datasets for the turntable setting (R3, R4).
- Reports quantitative gains (27% novel-view synthesis, 7% geometry) over 2DGS with masks (abstract, supported by R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Practical utility and setup concerns:**  
  The real data collection uses a small camera vertical range and small objects; a green screen would be a simpler alternative (R1). The turntable setup is very controlled (single-axis rotation, no translation or deformation), which is far from general real-world scenarios (R4). A minority reviewer (R4) considers the method only applicable in very limited cases and notes that any object deformation or complex translation would cause segmentation failure (score 3).  

- **Reliance on pre-computed foreground object poses:**  
  The method assumes transformation matrices for the foreground object and camera poses can be obtained via SfM (e.g., COLMAP). However, obtaining these matrices is itself the key challenge in reconstructing moving objects (as seen in Fig. 1 COLMAP failure) (R3, R4). The paper does not discuss the difficulty of obtaining these matrices versus obtaining object masks, nor does it address pose estimation challenges (R3, R4). R4 adds that if tracking/segmentation methods like “Tracking Everything Everywhere All at Once” or “SAM meets Point Tracking” are introduced, no additional training would be needed and the approach could handle more realistic examples; they suggest experiments with those alternatives.  

- **Missing details and clarity on pose derivation:**  
  The term “poses” in line 236 is ambiguous; the procedure for obtaining them is not explained (R3). The mention of “two separated regions” (line 235) raises whether mask information is still needed during preprocessing (R3). R4 also asks how \(F_t\) is calculated and suspects ground truth values are used directly, while the method claims to be self-supervised but does not solve the pose estimation problem.  

- **Insufficient experiments and ablations:**  
  - No running time reported; comparison with 2DGS-with-mask runtime is missing (R1, R2).  
  - No experiments on outdoor scenes (R2).  
  - No ablation on background clutter degree; some examples have complex backgrounds but still produce perfect meshes – explanation needed (R2).  
  - No ablation on objects with significant holes (e.g., a donut). For the Dmask data in Fig. 3, the method does not recover all holes, similar to NeuS and COLMAP (R1).  
  - Learned indicating parameters are not visualized in 3D space (R2).  
  - The advantage over 2DGS with masks is not convincingly demonstrated qualitatively: the red boxes in Fig. 3 do not help – results are mostly comparable (R1).  

- **Missing related work on 3DGS-based segmentation:**  
  Several existing works on object segmentation with 3D Gaussian Splatting are not discussed (R2).  

- **Writing and presentation issues:**  
  Sentence in line 198 (“which is especially for low for a cluttered background…”) is grammatically confusing (R3). Paragraph lines 312–317 appears unorganized (R3). The claim in line 262 that “explicit representations are more suitable” is not properly justified (R3).  

- **Specific reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments:**  
  - How does the method behave with objects containing significant holes (e.g., donut)? Does the local consistency loss still make sense? (R1)  
  - Why are both the object and camera moving? (R2)  
  - How does cluttered background influence results? (R2) — partially covered above.  
  - Can you visualize the learned indicating parameters in 3D? (R2) — covered above.  
  - Is the time advantage over 2DGS with mask? (R2) — covered.  
  - In Fig. 5, interpret more on how the method recognizes holes. (R2)  
  - Explain why explicit representations are more suitable for this problem. (R3)
