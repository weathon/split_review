## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes *agent symbolic learning*, a data-centric framework enabling language agents to self-optimize their symbolic components (prompts, tools, workflows) after deployment by mimicking back-propagation and gradient descent over text-based weights, loss, and gradients. The framework treats the agent as a symbolic network and uses LLM-as-a-judge for loss computation and LLM-generated feedback for gradient updates. Proof-of-concept experiments on standard benchmarks and real-world tasks (question answering, mathematics, coding, software development, creative writing) show improvements over static baselines. Reviewers find the motivation and analogy interesting but raise significant concerns about experimental rigor, missing comparisons, reliability of language gradients, and insufficient discussion of limitations. Two reviewers rate the paper 3, two rate it 5, reflecting a split in perceived significance and execution quality.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-motivated and addresses an important problem: moving from engineering-centric to data-centric agent optimization.
- The analogy to connectionist learning (back-propagation and gradient descent) is interesting and original, offering a novel perspective on agent self-evolution.
- The framework is clearly described and easy to follow; the writing is overall well-structured.
- The paper includes a variety of tasks (QA, math, coding, software development, creative writing), demonstrating breadth.
- The framework improves over static agent baselines and other prompt/tool optimization methods (e.g., DSPy) on the selected tasks.
- Section 4.3 provides valuable insights into real-world tasks not well-represented in mainstream benchmarks.
- The paper is of good quality with seemingly high reproducibility, and the authors commit to open-sourcing the entire framework.
- The work is considered original, especially in adding autonomous modification of the computational graph and the ability to update or implement new tools (improving over contemporary work like TextGrad).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited experimental significance and narrow evaluation**: The reported performance improvements are not very significant. The paper lacks evaluation on complex sequential decision-making agent tasks (e.g., AgentBench, AgentTuning, AgentGym, τ-bench) and does not compare against these frameworks. The abstract’s claim of outperforming on real-world tasks is not fully supported; only software development and creative writing are used for complex tasks, which may not represent the full range of agentic challenges.
- **Method overclaiming and analogy flaws**: The framework is described as “self-evolving,” but it is more akin to a multi-agent cooperation framework using reflection and discussion labeled as forward/backward propagation; it does not involve training or genuine evolution. The analogy to connectionist learning is incomplete: (a) the loss function in the symbolic setting lacks ground-truth labels, making it unsupervised rather than supervised; (b) language gradients have no mathematical guarantees of optimization direction and are susceptible to LLM hallucinations and unfaithfulness; (c) the sequential node structure assumed in the paper is not a full analogy to computational graphs in neural networks, which can be arbitrary directed acyclic graphs. The claim that prior methods (e.g., GPTSwarm) are prone to local optima of isolated components is incorrect because GPTSwarm already optimizes edge connections globally.
- **Missing open-source model experiments**: Only GPT-series backbone models are used. The paper should include experiments with open-source LMs (e.g., for replicability and to assess approach generality across backbone strengths).
- **Lack of scaling and reliability analysis**: No study on how performance scales with the size of the forward pass LM and the symbolic optimizer LM. The reliance on LLMs for computing language loss and gradients raises reliability concerns – deviations may accumulate across longer node chains, making updates unstable. The paper should test on complex tasks with extended node numbers and investigate how language loss transits under such settings.
- **Insufficient discussion of limitations and engineering-centric components**: The paper does not adequately discuss where the analogy breaks or what components remain unoptimizable (e.g., task requirements, loss prompt 𝒫_loss). These manually engineered elements are critical but not discussed as limitations. Ablation studies on the quality of task descriptions, loss/gradient prompt templates, and the contribution of individual components (prompt optimizer, workflow optimizer) are missing.
- **Convergence not addressed**: The paper does not discuss when or how the “training” process stabilizes, what metrics or criteria determine convergence, or potential challenges in reaching it.
- **Unclear experimental setup and missing details**: Many experimental details are missing: learning rate, batch size, whether optimization is supervised or unsupervised, how initial agents are implemented, what optimization method of DSPy was used, whether results are on a hold-out test set or the optimization set, and the split strategy. The wording “learning rate for each prompt” is unclear when applied to prompts, tools, and workflows. The role of PromptOptimizer and ToolOptimizer in Algorithm 1 is not described, and they may not be used in experiments.
- **Inconsistencies and typos**: Variable n is used inconsistently (number of nodes vs. arbitrary index in equations and Algorithm 1). Line 13 of Algorithm 1 appends a gradient to the trajectory, which is not mentioned in the main text; the strategy for this is unclear. There is a typo (“optimizer” should be “optimize”). Several references miss publisher information.
- **Need for expanded baselines and benchmarking clarification**: For standard LLM benchmarks, it is unclear whether GPTs are evaluated zero-shot or few-shot. Since the proposed method uses few-shot examples, few-shot prompting for GPT baselines is necessary for fair comparison. Comparisons with additional baselines (e.g., CoT, ToT) and a wider range of tasks (beyond creative writing and software development) are needed.
- **Unsupervised learning treatment**: The paper claims a data-centric, unsupervised capability, but the appendix only provides a supervised learning template. The authors should provide the unsupervised template and corresponding experimental results, clarifying the exact role of data.
- **Minor reviewer concerns**: The second paragraph of the introduction lacks needed references. There is no citation for dynamic neural nets on page 4. The claim about “components of prompts” and “functions” in lines 083/085 is vague. The programming language introduced in Zhou et al. (2023b) is not explained. The authors should cite how OpenAI implements GPTs (line 423).
