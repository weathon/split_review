## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates potential pathologies when using normalizing flows for data imputation, focusing on the diffeomorphism assumption and identifying two error types: target mode error and base mode error. It proposes metrics like the integrated OOD residual and uses a 2D toy example (four squares) to demonstrate. The goal is to improve trustworthiness for imputation in small data problems.

### Strengths

- Novel application of normalizing flows to imputation. (Reviewer 1)
- Explanation of the two error types from these models makes sense and sets up the rest of the article well. (Reviewer 2)
- Analysis of the toy target distribution is nice; the diagrams are very informative for the problems highlighted. (Reviewer 2)
- Integrated OOD residual is a reasonable metric for predictive accuracy. (Reviewer 2)
- The initial idea that multi-modal distributions will be ill-approximated by diffeomorphism is interesting and potentially solves a problem of the method. (Reviewer 3)
- Toy example thoroughly explored with appealing figures. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses

- The paper lacks a theoretical analysis of its proposed approach. (Reviewer 1)
- Presentation needs significant improvements: key terms (e.g., “out-of-distribution”) not defined; introduction to normalizing flows unclear (equation (1) connection to flows not clarified, “approximately satisfy” ambiguous, purpose of learning transform \(f\) to Gaussian not explained; “target sample” undefined). (Reviewer 1)
- Misuse of “mode” – term has a specific definition in probability distributions; better to use another term, especially before equation (6) vs. definition in line 139. (Reviewer 1)
- Equations such as (8) and (9) do not contribute meaningfully; “finitely bounded” unclear; does ReLU satisfy the three conditions for diffeomorphisms? (Reviewer 1)
- Section 2 lacks mathematical rigor: mode vaguely defined; Supp (support) not defined; equation (6) should feature a tolerance epsilon for approximate modes; nothing prevents a diffeomorphic map from mapping one mode to two approximately disjoint modes if filaments are low probability; conjecture (10) is more of a best-case scenario; equation (21) not correctly mathematically defined and could be simplified with indicator functions. (Reviewer 3)
- Notation confusing: “m_{b->}” too similar to m_b; also m_t* vs m_t. (Reviewer 2)
- No applications beyond the four-squares toy example; article unconvincing without any real data or even vaguely realistic simulated data. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- Very little discussion or implementation specifically relevant to imputation as set up in the introduction; if the point is about types of error when training normalizing flows then the framing around imputation is unnecessary and confusing. (Reviewer 2)
- Questions about low-dimensional imputation difficulty: is it that difficult? What is the comparison with other imputation methods? Does this model solve an easy problem with unnecessary accuracy? (Reviewer 2)
- Does the method make a substantial difference in a larger analysis pipeline? What about predictive uncertainty of normalizing flows? Can multiple imputation be done effectively with well-calibrated uncertainties? Neural networks do not offer well-calibrated probabilistic predictions – that is a major drawback in an imputation context. (Reviewer 2)
- Computational setup not specified (laptop, server, TPUs?). Differing compute load for different sizes or dimensions? How much work did hyperparameter tuning (“trial and error”) really comprise? Could it be done systematically? What was the metric for success? (Reviewer 2)
- The diffeomorphism assumption is already violated with a uniform target distribution and square support, even accounting for modes – poor recovery with artifacts even with right number of base modes; the best OOD residual is with one base mode, implying mode mismatch is not the dominant error source. (Reviewer 3)
- No proof that the toy example captures characteristics of real-world physical science applications; title/intro/discussion misleading – high dimensionality not tackled; small data only mentioned in conclusion, none of the difficulties of working with few samples considered. (Reviewer 3)
- Experiment shows that while multiple base modes would be better, it is risky – base modes may be left unused with high OOD errors; no solution provided for that problem. (Reviewer 3)
- Presentation lacks color bars to indicate magnitude of errors, making visual evaluation difficult. (Reviewer 3)
- OOD ratio is a Monte-Carlo estimate of an integral similar to OOD residual; why not use an integral? (Reviewer 3)
- Why would a real-world example feature multiple modes? How do we know it is an important source of error for OOD classification? (Reviewer 3)
- Note: There is disagreement among reviewers on presentation quality – Reviewer 2 found the explanation clear (Presentation rating 3), while Reviewers 1 and 3 found it convoluted and lacking rigor (ratings 1). Similarly, on soundness, Reviewer 3 judged rating 1 (stronger concerns about mathematical rigor) while others gave 2.
