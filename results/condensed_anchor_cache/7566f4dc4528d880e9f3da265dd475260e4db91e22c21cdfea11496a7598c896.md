## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LADS, a hierarchical approach for learning language-conditioned discrete latent plans by decoupling high-level policy learning from low-level policy training. It uses a VQ-VAE to learn a discrete latent space from trajectories, then trains a high-level categorical policy over these codes, and adds a semantic alignment loss to align latent plans with language instructions. Experiments on LOReL and Kitchen show strong results.

### Strengths
- **Excellent presentation**: Extremely well written and clear. The motivation is clearly presented, the related work section is well organized across three aspects, and the methods are well decomposed with good use of figures (Fig. 1). Losses are explained with clear motivation, and the paper is easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Clear goal and methodology validates claims**: The paper clearly sets out to improve HRL policy learning in a latent planning space while avoiding mode collapse, and this goal is validated through experiments (R1). The authors have pinpointed a specific issue in prior algorithm LISA and methodically addressed it (R4).
- **Novel semantic alignment objective**: This objective appears to be novel (R3).
- **Strong performance on benchmarks**: The method achieves impressive results in both compositionality and generalization tasks (R4), and significant improvement over baselines (R2).
- **Comprehensive ablation studies**: Ablations (w/o VAE, w/o CP, w/o semantic regularization) indicate which parts of the method are helpful, providing insight into performance improvements (R1, R3, R4).
- **Nice multimodality experiment**: The paper provides an experiment showing the multimodal capabilities of the proposed approach (R3, R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons to highly related works**: The method is very similar to VQ-BeT and QueST, which use a two-stage discrete skill learning step followed by a high-level policy. These works must be directly compared. VQ-BeT’s proven results in Kitchen yield **3.78** goals achieved, far higher than the **2.25** reported for LADS. The paper does not explain this discrepancy (R3). Additionally, earlier important works on language-guided HRL (Jiang et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2021, Hu et al. 2019) are omitted from the related work and could serve as simpler baselines (R1).
- **Scalability concern with discrete codebook**: The trajectory encoder codebook size is only 20 (Appendix B.2). A larger dataset with more diverse atomic instructions would need a larger codebook, which increases the output dimension of the high-level policy. It is worth investigating whether **LADS without CP** (predicting a latent code directly) or **LADS with VAE** (continuous latent space) would scale better (R2).
- **Discreteness concern for continuous instruction parameters**: Tasks use mutually exclusive atomic instructions. For continuous parameters (e.g., “open drawer 5 cm”, “open drawer 10 cm”), discrete latent plans may not capture nuances, and a continuous latent space (LADS with VAE) could be more effective. The method may be overly tailored to discrete instructions (R2).
- **Need for more thorough ablation of loss components**: The method uses several loss terms (L_VQ, L_BC, L_CE, L_align). A more detailed ablation is needed, especially to understand the importance of the behavioral cloning (BC) loss and the alignment loss. The version without semantic regularization does surprisingly poorly; an explanation is needed (R1, R3).
- **Insufficient justification for stability improvement over LISA**: The paper states that end-to-end training of high-level and low-level policies in LISA causes instability, yet LADS still trains the trajectory encoder and low-level policy end-to-end. It is unclear why using states/actions as input to the trajectory encoder (with an H-step lookahead) yields greater stability than using language embeddings as input to the high-level policy in LISA. The lookahead capability itself may be the primary advantage, not decoupling (R4).
- **Interpretability analysis lacking**: Section 5.5 results are unclear. LISA demonstrated a mapping from skill codes to word clouds and behaviors; LADS should provide a similar analysis. Are skill codes interpretable and do they collapse, or are they more stable? Figures 4 and 5 also need clearer explanation (e.g., x-axis labels, how composite distribution is calculated, why probability approaches 0, what curves represent) (R4).
- **Multi-modality argument unconvincing**: The example of “open drawer” performed quickly or slowly still corresponds to the same skill code. It is unclear how a categorical distribution over skill codes addresses multimodality, especially since the low-level policy executes only one skill at a time. The observed performance improvement may stem from the difficulty of direct latent code prediction, not from multi-modality handling (R4).
- **Semantic regularization rationale insufficient**: The algorithm uses both J_p and J_q losses with stop gradients. The paper does not adequately justify why both are needed, why J_p alone would not suffice, and what the role of stop gradients is (R4).
- **Evaluation scheme inconsistency for LOReL**: The evaluation scheme (atomic seen / atomic rephrased / composite) differs from prior work by LOReL and SkillDiffuser (seen/unseen noun/unseen verb/unseen noun/human provided), making direct comparison difficult (R3). “Procedurally labeling” demonstrations (line 362) is also not explained (R1).
- **Missing pseudocode**: The framework involves several components and neural networks; providing pseudocode would give readers a clearer holistic understanding of the training procedure (R2).
- **Minor issues**: 
  - Notation: The objective in line 192 does not include parameter θ (R2).
  - Equation 10: ℒ_CL is not defined anywhere (R3).
  - Related work is sparse and skews very recent (R1).
