## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FEBP, an automated feature engineering (AutoFE) method that uses LLMs (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) to interpret dataset descriptions and iteratively generate new features via in-context learning. Features are represented compactly in a prompt and generated using pre-defined operators; the top-performing features are added to the prompt and refined until no improvement. The approach is evaluated on 7 real-world datasets (3 classification, 4 regression) against three AutoFE baselines (including CAAFE) and compared to Light-GBM, random forests, and linear models. The authors claim state-of-the-art performance, improved interpretability, and reduced manual effort.

### Strengths
- The problem of AutoFE is important and the idea of leveraging LLMs to process semantic dataset information is timely (R1, R4).
- The paper is clearly written, well-structured, and easy to follow, with concrete running examples (R1, R3, R4).
- Experiments cover multiple real-world datasets and compare against state-of-the-art baselines, showing superior performance in some cases (R1, R3, R4).
- The approach is sound and easy to replicate; generated features remain explainable compared to deep embedding methods (R2).
- The paper includes some formalism and reports significance results (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and insufficient differentiation from CAAFE.** Multiple reviewers note that the high-level idea (using LLMs with dataset descriptions to iteratively generate features) is already present in CAAFE. R1 asks what unique research challenges are addressed beyond CAAFE. R3 states the innovation is limited—it is a prompting method without deeper module design. R4 characterizes the algorithm as merely a long restatement of the simple Figure 1 and notes the self‑correction mechanism is used in many other LLM works (e.g., text2sql). R1 specifically calls for a discussion of unique challenges vs. CAAFE. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Weak experimental significance and inconsistent results.** 
  - The improvement over CAAFE is not significant (R1).
  - In Table 2, GPT-3.5 outperforms GPT-4, which is counterintuitive; the speculation in line 320 is insufficient, and an additional experiment is needed (R3).
  - The effect of the number of initial features (Table 5, means in [0.6823, 0.684]) and temperature (Table 4, means in [0.8169, 0.8197]) is minimal; significance is unclear (R2).
  - For classifiers like Light-GBM and random forests, FEBP shows hardly any difference from baselines; gains only appear for linear models, undermining claims of generality (R4).
- **Missing comparisons and ablations.** 
  - The approach is not compared against modern automatic feature interaction learning methods such as DCN V2 and AutoInt, which could provide an upper bound (R2).
  - Ablation studies are lacking to justify design choices (e.g., cRPN) (R1). The temperature analysis is considered redundant (R3).
- **Limited feature space.** FEBP can only generate new features based on the given pre‑defined operators, restricting the flexibility and novelty of the produced features (R3).
- **Unaddressed reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms.**
  - "How is the vision and high level idea different from CAAFE? What are the unique research challenges?" (R1) – essentially the same as the novelty concern above.
  - "How many responses on average are necessary to train a downstream model?" (R2) – relates to practical efficiency.
  - "Did you consider using reflection and providing previous prompts as input apart from in‑context examples?" (R2) – suggests an unexplored improvement.
  - R3 implies the temperature analysis is redundant (already listed above), and R3 questions the GPT‑3.5 vs. GPT‑4 inconsistency (already listed).

*One reviewer (R2) is more positive overall (score 6) and highlights the approach’s soundness, replicability, and explainability, while still raising the evaluation and missing‑comparison concerns above. The other three reviewers (R1, R3, R4, all score 5) are more critical, particularly about novelty and the strength of experimental evidence.*
