## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers agree the paper addresses an important and urgent problem (fairness in text-to-image generation) with a sound method: a distributional alignment loss based on optimal transport and adjusted gradient finetuning (adjusted DFT) to directly optimize losses on generated images. The paper is well-written, references prior work, and compares extensively to previous methods. Experiments on Stable Diffusion show effectiveness in reducing gender, racial, and intersectional biases for occupational prompts, and the method supports custom target distributions and multiple demographic dimensions. However, weaknesses include: evaluation limited to single-face generation (unclear for multiple faces), unclear impact on image quality/diversity for general prompts, no comparison of computational cost with baselines, reliance on self-defined bias metrics (mixing fairness/bias terminology), and potential issues with CLIP and classifier accuracy. There is a notable contrast: Reviewer 2 (score 1) gave a very positive assessment with no major weaknesses and praised the method extensively, while Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 6) identified several specific weaknesses and requested clarifications.

### Strengths
- The fairness of image generation is an important and practical problem.
- The proposed fine-tuning method is sound, derived from first principles (distribution alignment), and intuitively appealing.
- Results validate effectiveness; extensive experiments (including on intersectional biases and multiple demographic dimensions) and comparisons to previous methods convincingly demonstrate utility and superiority.
- Comprehensive analysis of which part of the model to fine-tune (soft prompt, text encoder, U‑Net) and of the challenges in fine-tuning (e.g., failure of naive autodifferentiation and the proposed gradient adjustment mechanism with theoretical explanation and empirical validation).
- The method is widely applicable beyond demographic debiasing, allows customized target distributions, and supports debiasing along multiple dimensions simultaneously.
- The paper is clearly written and appropriately references prior work (including relevant citations such as Lester et al. for soft prompt tuning, Qi et al. for scaling concerns).
- Use of different evaluation models (CLIP‑ViT‑bigG‑14 and DINOv2 vit‑g/14) to measure image quality and attribute preservation.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation to single-face generation.** The method is only tested on images with one prominent face. Unclear how it would perform with multiple faces (e.g., generating a group of people with equal representation of sensitive attributes); the current approach focusing on the most prominent face may be insufficient.
- **Unclear effect on quality and diversity for general prompts.** The paper evaluates only targeted prompts related to specific occupations and sensitive attributes. There is no analysis of whether fine-tuning with the fairness loss degrades generation quality or diversity for a broader range of prompts, leading to potential image degradation or reduced diversity.
- **Lack of computational cost comparison.** The paper does not report training time, GPU usage, or other resource requirements for the proposed method compared to baseline methods, making it difficult to assess practical feasibility.
- **Self-defined bias metric and mixing of fairness/bias terminology.** The evaluation uses a custom bias metric; the reviewer questions why well-defined fairness metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equal opportunity) were not used, which reduces credibility. The paper also uses “fairness” and “bias” interchangeably without clear distinction.
- **Potential issues with CLIP and face classifier accuracy.** CLIP has known limitations (e.g., Shtedritski et al., Wang et al., Wolfe et al., Zhang & Ré) that could affect the efficacy of the method. The accuracy of the face detector and gender/race classifiers is not reported; if they perform poorly on certain datasets, debiasing results could be affected.
- **Unclear implementation details.** 
  - How is the expectation in Equation (4) obtained in practice? What is **u** in that equation?
  - How are different λ values used for different regions (face, non-face)? The loss ℒ_img is per image, not per pixel – clarification needed.
  - It was not explicitly stated which model components (soft prompt, text encoder, U‑Net) were actually finetuned for the main experiments in Section 5.1.
- **Concerns about generalization and overfitting.** 
  - On unseen prompts (Table 2), how good is the proposed method compared to baselines? Does the finetuning overfit to the training prompts?
  - Scaling to many fairness properties simultaneously may have negative consequences (e.g., image quality or biases when generating diverse images like bears, tables, groups); discussion of such drawbacks (e.g., Qi et al.) is missing.
- **Minor notation and clarity issues.** 
  - The symbol **y** is used both for generated target labels and for images from the frozen model.
  - The meaning of “more performative” for CLIP‑ViT‑bigG‑14 and DINOv2 vit‑g/14 is unclear.
  - Background information on U‑Net is needed in Section 4.2.
- **Choice of optimal transport loss not justified.** No comparison with other alignment metrics is provided; why optimal transport was chosen over alternatives is not discussed.
