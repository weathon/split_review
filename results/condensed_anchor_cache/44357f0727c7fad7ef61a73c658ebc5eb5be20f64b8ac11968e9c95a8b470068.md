## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper frames online LLM alignment as a bilevel optimization problem, then reduces it to an efficient single-level first-order method (SAIL) using reward-policy equivalence. SAIL generates new samples and iteratively refines alignment by exploring responses and regulating preference labels, aiming to reduce dependence on static preference datasets and distribution shifts. The authors introduce three variants (SAIL-PR, SAIL-PP, SAIL-DP) and evaluate on LLM-as-a-Judge benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-bench) and a classification benchmark (MMLU), with models from 0.5B to 8B. Reviewers agree the bilevel-to-single-level reduction is novel, but disagree on the significance of empirical gains (some call results “consistent” and “substantial”, others say they are “not impressive” and lack comparisons to recent methods). There is broad concern about incomplete evaluation, insufficient baselines, and limited exploration of alternative preference models and regularization.

### Strengths
- **Novel formulation**: Online RLHF is modeled as bilevel optimization, then reduced to a tractable single-level first-order method (reward-policy equivalence), which is a significant theoretical advance (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **Effective self-improvement mechanism**: Reduces reliance on preference oracles by using model-generated responses for iterative improvement, making online alignment more feasible (Reviewer 3).
- **Comprehensive ablation of design choices**: Figure 2 explicitly compares all combinations (SAIL-PR, SAIL-PP, SAIL-DP), giving practitioners a clear map of online RLHF variants (Reviewer 1).
- **Consistent results across scales**: Performance trends hold from 0.5B to 8B parameters (Reviewer 1).
- **Extensive evaluation metrics**: Experiments cover evaluation reward, win rate, reward margin, and efficiency, with detailed comparison across variants (Reviewers 1, 3). Results show improvements over DPO and other RLHF baselines on most measures (affirmed by Reviewers 1–4, though Reviewer 2 and 4 note exceptions).
- **Practical computational gains**: Single-level optimization reduces overhead relative to full bi-level approaches (Reviewers 1, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Performance on key benchmarks is mixed**: In Table 3, SAIL variants perform worse than baseline DPO on Eval-Reward and MT-bench (Reviewer 2). On AlpacaEval 2.0 score, SAIL does not show meaningful improvement; the explanation is insufficient, and length-controlled win rate is not reported (Reviewer 4). This undermines claims of superiority and calls for more thorough analysis.
- **Insufficient baselines**: Comparisons are limited to DPO and iterative DPO. Missing state-of-the-art offline/post-training methods such as ORPO and SimPO, which are more representative of the current alignment landscape (Reviewer 4).
- **Narrow task coverage**: The only downstream task metric is MMLU. Strongly missing are common benchmarks like ARC-Challenge (as used in the related self-improvement work SPIN, ICML24) and alignment-specific evaluations (Arena-Hard, length-controlled win rate) (Reviewer 4).
- **Dependence on initial offline dataset**: SAIL requires a quality offline dataset to bootstrap; the paper does not study sensitivity to dataset size, quality, or noise, nor strategies for when initial data is minimal or biased (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Overfitting in SAIL-DP**: SAIL-DP shows signs of overfitting to in-distribution responses, and no novel regularization techniques are proposed to improve generalization (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited exploration of preference models**: The method assumes the Bradley-Terry pairwise model, which may not capture non-transitive or context-dependent preferences. No experiments with alternative utility functions are included (Reviewer 3, also implied by Reviewer 1’s question about discarding instances with subtle preference strength).
- **Scalability concerns**: Efficiency is demonstrated only up to 8B parameters. For larger models (e.g., >100B), the cost of online response generation and reward evaluation may become prohibitive; no analysis of scaling overhead is provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of a clear “unified framework” presentation**: The writing does not convincingly explain how SAIL subsumes prior online RLHF or how practitioners should adopt it in place of iterative DPO. A dedicated usage section and stronger emphasis on the provided code are needed (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing content / layout issue**: There is a large blank space below Section 6.1, suggesting potential missing content in the paper (Reviewer 2).
- **Open questions on resource allocation**: The paper emphasizes improving reward models (RewardBench), but does not clarify whether, given a fixed budget, effort should go into online RLHF or into better reward models (Reviewer 1).
- **Unjustified emphasis on reward-margin / offline-reward evaluation**: The utility of these metrics (Section 6.1) is not convincingly argued compared to pairwise win rate (Reviewer 1).

**Minority contrast**: Reviewer 3 (score 8) is strongly positive, highlighting the innovative formulation and comprehensive evaluation; Reviewer 4 (score 3) is strongly critical, finding the empirical results unimpressive and the evaluation incomplete. Reviewers 1 (score 6) and 2 (score 6) are moderately positive but raise substantial concerns about presentation, missing baselines, and inconsistent performance. The disagreement centers on whether the theoretical advances outweigh the limited and uneven empirical gains.
