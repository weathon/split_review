## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper studies dynamic feature selection, where features are queried sequentially to minimize cost while maximizing prediction accuracy. The authors propose DIME, a greedy method that selects the next feature based on its conditional mutual information (CMI) with the response, estimated discriminatively via two jointly trained networks: a value network (CMI estimation) and a predictor network. The approach is extended to handle non-uniform acquisition costs, variable per-instance budgets, and prior information. Theoretical results show that, for cross-entropy loss, learning incremental loss improvements yields an unbiased CMI estimator. Experiments on tabular and image datasets compare DIME against static, RL-based, and greedy baselines, demonstrating improved accuracy–cost trade-offs.

### Strengths

- The problem of dynamic feature selection with a minimal budget is important and timely.
- DIME provides a novel approach to estimate CMI in a discriminative fashion, using a Predictor and Value network that jointly learn the selection policy.
- The paper includes theoretical grounding for the unbiased CMI estimator under cross-entropy loss.
- Practical issues are addressed: non-uniform feature costs, variable feature budgets per instance, and incorporation of prior information.
- The experimental evaluation is comprehensive, covering several tabular and image datasets and multiple baselines.
- The paper is well-written, with clear problem setup and effort to clarify contributions.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing related work:** Several relevant lines of work are not cited or compared against:
  - POMDP-based dynamic feature selection with theoretical bounds and mutual information usage (Liyanage, Zois, Chelmis, IEEE TAI 2021, two papers).
  - Incremental permutation feature importance (Fumagalli et al., Machine Learning 2023) and leverage model-inherent variable importance (Haug et al., KDD 2020).
  - Statistical CMI-based feature selection methods (e.g., MIM, MIFS, mRMR, JMI, CMICOT from Shishkin et al., NeurIPS 2016).
- **Improvement over simple baselines is small:** DIME’s performance on tabular datasets is very close to the greedy baseline Argmax Direct, raising the question of what benefit DIME provides over that simpler alternative.
- **Cost integration appears artificial:** While the extension to non-uniform costs is straightforward, one reviewer felt it was an artificial add-on rather than a core part of the methodology.
- **Incremental contributions for extensions:** The sections on variable budgets and prior information (Sections 4.2 and 4.3) are described as straightforward extensions that a practitioner could easily derive from Section 4.1; deeper insights would be desirable.
- **Surprisingly poor performance of RL-based methods:** RL baselines (e.g., Janisch et al.) perform much worse than greedy methods like DIME. One reviewer found this surprising and questioned whether the RL methods had properly tuned cost parameters or if the results reflect a typo. An ablation study was requested to investigate this discrepancy.
- **Unclear handling of budgets and group structure:** It is not specified whether DIME enforces a hard per-instance budget or an average budget across instances. Also, the appendix groups features – does DIME exploit this group structure?
- **Need for clearer algorithmic description:** The interplay between the two networks, CMI estimation, use of prior information, and feature selection logic is not fully transparent. An exact algorithmic representation would improve clarity.
- **Greedy one-by-one selection vs. subset selection:** The greedy approach selects one feature at a time and ignores interactions among candidate features. The paper does not discuss or compare with methods that select subsets jointly (e.g., CMICOT).
- **Missing comparison with direct statistical CMI estimation:** The paper learns CMI via a neural discriminator, but it would be informative to compare with traditional plug-in estimators (like those used in MIFS, JMI, etc.) to assess whether the learning overhead is worth the additional cost.
- **Prior information integration is vague:** How prior information enters the selection policy and affects decisions is not well explained.
