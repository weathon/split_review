## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes MOTOR (Many Outcome Time Oriented Representations), the first time-to-event (TTE) foundation model for structured medical records. It is pre-trained via a self-supervised, multi-task dynamic survival objective on 55M patient records (9B clinical events, 8,192 code-based tasks) from a private EHR and claims database. Evaluations on 19 tasks across three databases (private EHR, MIMIC-IV, Merative claims) show improvements in time-dependent C-statistics (4.6% over state-of-the-art), label efficiency (up to 95%), and robustness to temporal distribution shifts. A 143M-parameter pretrained model is publicly released.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important medical task (time-to-event prediction) with a novel foundation model approach; multiple reviewers note the significance and creativity of using EHR data for survival analysis.
- Paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Thorough and convincing experiments: comparisons to many survival baselines, external validation on MIMIC-IV and Merative claims, robustness to time shifts, and attention to ethical sub-group analyses (R2, R3, R4).
- Notable performance gains (4.6% C-statistic improvement, up to 95% label efficiency) are highlighted as significant in a field where gains are usually small (R4).
- Model and code are publicly released (R1).
- Pre-training covers both code-based and text-based tasks, and downstream tasks are carefully separated from pre-training codes to avoid leakage (R2, R4).
- Pretrained model is robust and transfers across datasets (R2, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Figure 1 clarity**: Multiple reviewers (R1, R3) find Figure 1 unclear—axes are not labeled, it is hard to understand what each survival curve represents per time step in pre-training tasks, and how predictions influence these curves. More detailed explanation is needed.
- **Selection of 8,192 code-based tasks**: R1 and R3 request justification for the selection of these tasks out of the full code set, as well as the rationale for choosing the six downstream tasks from them.
- **Missing standard deviations**: R1 notes that performance tables lack standard deviations, making it hard to assess variability.
- **Missing values handling**: R2 points out that it is not explained how missing values in time-series EHR data are represented. Since missingness can be informative (e.g., a lab not ordered might imply health), the authors should clarify whether they ignore events with missing values and discuss potential bias. This is considered a critical oversight.
- **Subsampling process rationale**: R2 states the subsampling strategy (described only in the Appendix) is central to the pre-training approach but lacks sufficient justification and explanation of its relation to downstream tasks.
- **Transformer architecture unspecified in main text**: R2 notes the exact transformer base model (attention mechanism, number of layers, hidden dimensionality) is not stated in the main body, which is a critical detail for reproducibility and comparison.
- **Lack of ablation on pre-training data amount**: R3 suggests that experiments varying the amount of pre-training data (e.g., 1%, 10%) would be beneficial to understand performance versus data scale.
- **Dynamic setting not evaluated**: R4 points out that the model is pre-trained in a dynamic survival setting but only evaluated in a static setting; dynamic tasks using landmarking would strengthen the work.
- **Native American sub-group performance drop**: R4 highlights a significant performance drop (~5%) for Native American patients compared to other ethnic groups under MOTOR-Finetune, while the authors claim the drop is statistically insignificant. R4 questions that claim and requests a clear statement of this limitation.
- **Generality to other domains**: R4 notes that the pre-training task is specific to ICD code sequences (events as sequences) and may not transfer to other survival analysis domains (e.g., cancer, finance) where data are not naturally sequences of events.
- **Questions reflecting missing details** (treated as weaknesses because they overlap with criticism/omission):
  - How is the TTE task evaluated on MIMIC? Is it predicting a diagnosis code at each ICU admission? (R1)
  - What computational resources are needed for fine-tuning or inference with the pretrained model? (R1)
  - How are events that can occur multiple times handled? Does the model predict time of next event? (R4)
  - What is the source of the pre-training data? Is patient data at the admission level or concatenated across admissions? How long did pre-training take? (R3)
- **Minority reviewer (R2) is less convinced**: While the paper is well-written and results interesting, R2 (score 6) considers the missing values handling and subsampling rationale insufficiently addressed, and the architecture details omitted, raising concerns about methodological rigor. This contrast is preserved: the other three reviewers (scores 8) are highly positive.
