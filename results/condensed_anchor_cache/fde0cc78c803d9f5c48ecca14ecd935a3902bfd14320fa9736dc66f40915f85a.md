## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents UV-Attack, a physical-world adversarial attack for person detection that leverages dynamic Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and UV mapping. It generates adversarial textures on clothing by modifying UV maps rather than RGB images, enabling real-time edits across diverse human poses and viewpoints. A novel Expectation over Pose Transformation (EoPT) loss improves success on unseen poses, and a latent diffusion model produces the adversarial pattern. Experiments report 92.75% ASR against FastRCNN in dynamic video and 49.5% ASR on YOLOv8 in black-box settings.

### Strengths
- **Innovative approach:** The use of dynamic NeRF-based UV mapping for adversarial attacks is novel and directly addresses the challenge of non-rigid human movement (R1, R2, R3, R5). The application to person detection is important and technically sound (R3).
- **Real-time texture modification:** UV-Attack enables efficient rendering and real-time texture edits, making the attack feasible for practical physical-world deployment (R1, R2).
- **High attack success and outperformance:** The method achieves very high ASR (92.75% on FastRCNN, 49.5% on YOLOv8) and significantly surpasses prior work (e.g., AdvCaT with 28.50% ASR), especially in free‑pose settings (R1, R2).
- **Comprehensive experiments:** Evaluation covers multiple detectors (Faster R‑CNN, YOLOv3, YOLOv8), varied parameters (poses, viewing angles, IoU thresholds), and both white-box and black-box transfer settings (R3, R4, R5). Physical-world testing on printed clothing demonstrates real-world applicability (R5).
- **Clear writing and organization:** The paper is well-structured and easy to follow (R3, R4, R5).
- **EoPT loss contribution:** The Expectation over Pose Transformation loss improves robustness across a wide range of poses (R5).
- **Clever use of diffusion priors and UV maps:** Adversarial patches are introduced at the texture level via UV mapping, and the robust generative priors of diffusion models are leveraged for texture interpolation (R2). Removing classifier-free guidance to expand the perturbation space is an interesting angle for boosting physical transferability (R4).
- **Ability to handle diverse poses:** Sampling from the SMPL parameter space (via GMM) allows coverage of diverse and even unseen actions (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on pretrained diffusion model:** The attack relies heavily on a fixed stable diffusion model and its latent space, which may limit generalizability to detectors trained with different data distributions or architectures. The paper does not explore using different diffusion models or fine-tuning (R1).
- **Computational complexity and lack of cost analysis:** The pipeline (dynamic NeRF, UV mapping, diffusion, PSO/Adam optimization) is multi‑step and computationally intensive. No detailed analysis of per‑step computational cost or memory requirements is provided, making it difficult to assess real-time feasibility, especially on resource‑constrained devices (R1). The computational demand could also be a limitation if frequent re‑designs for different targets/environments are required (R5).
- **Limited physical-world evaluation:** Physical experiments are restricted to a small number of environments and detection models. Lighting conditions, camera angles, and background clutter are not systematically explored, raising concerns about generalizability (R1). The number of frames captured in the physical video used to compute ASR is not mentioned; too few frames would reduce credibility (R4). Only a limited set of physical-world attack examples are shown; more complete examples are needed (R4).
- **Transferability and domain shift insufficiently examined:** The paper reports good ASR on tested detectors (Faster R‑CNN, YOLOv3, YOLOv8) but does not thoroughly investigate how adversarial textures transfer to other architectures trained on different datasets. The impact of domain shift (e.g., different backgrounds subjects) is not explored (R1). Transferability beyond YOlo and FastRCNN variants is unclear (R1, R4). Model‑specific bias in success rate is not addressed (R1).
- **Training-test contamination in digital evaluation:** Test poses are generated from a GMM, which may be unrealistic, and a GMM-based model is also used in the comparison. This raises a contamination issue (R3). The authors should use videos from a different source (R3).
- **Lack of null/non‑adversarial baseline:** There is no comparison against a non‑adversarial pattern (e.g., everyday clothes or a generated pattern from a random initial point) (R3).
- **Visual inconspicuousness not addressed:** The attack does not consider stealth or natural appearance. The generated textures may draw human attention, which limits practical applicability in sensitive contexts (R5). It is recommended to introduce constraints for more natural textures and balance adversarial effectiveness with inconspicuousness (R5).
- **Inconsistent baseline comparison:** Reported success rates for state‑of‑the‑art attacks differ significantly from those in the original publications, complicating direct comparisons. Some prior works apply constraints on patch appearance for stealth, while UV-Attack does not, making comparisons potentially unfair (R5). The authors should document the reasons for any benchmarking deviations and apply similar constraints for fair comparison (R5).
- **Missing related work:** The paper overlooks “DAP: A Dynamic Adversarial Patch for Evading Person Detectors,” which also targets non‑rigid transformations. Including it would help contextualize the novelty of UV-Attack (R5).
- **Clarity issues with EoPT loss and training details:** It is unclear how the Expectation over Pose Transformation (EoPT) loss differs from standard Expectation over Transformation (EoT); it appears to shift transformations from the image level to pose, camera, and lighting changes (R4). The training detail section is confusing (R3). Details about the training dataset (which videos, how backgrounds are chosen, whether digital and physical datasets differ) are missing (R2, R3, Q).
- **Choice of target models and setup:** Why are YOLOv3 and Faster R‑CNN chosen over more recent models? (R4). The target model setup (pretrained vs. fine‑tuned) is unclear, and clean-sample performance—crucial for interpreting attack significance, especially in the physical world—is not reported (R4).
- **No defense analysis/robustness:** There is limited discussion or evaluation of how robust UV-Attack is to adversarial training or other defense mechanisms, which would strengthen the contribution (R1).
- **Subject‑specific training limitation:** The method likely requires training a UV‑volume model and optimizing the texture for each person who will wear the adversarial clothing. Transferability to unseen subjects is not demonstrated, and addressing this limitation is important (R3, Q).
- **Sensitivity to pose distribution and clothing texture:** The attack’s sensitivity to the specific poses sampled from the GMM and to the complexity of clothing textures (patterns, colors) is not statistically analyzed (R1, Q). The purpose of including the target video when modeling the GMM is unclear (R2, Q).
- **Model ensemble not used to boost transferability:** The paper notes limited transferability due to structural differences (e.g., SSD) but does not try a common model ensemble approach to improve transferability (R4).
