## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a hierarchical divide-and-conquer grouping framework (HDG-CLIP) for Generalized Zero-Shot Learning (GZSL). It uses GPT-4 to generate class-level descriptions and a diffusion model (Stable Diffusion) to synthesize visual samples for novel classes. The joint label space is first split into base and novel subspaces, then each subspace is further divided into two smaller subspaces via unsupervised clustering on CLIP text embeddings. During testing, samples are first assigned to a subspace and then classified by a model trained on that subspace’s classes. The method reframes ZSL as a retrieval-based task, leveraging generated unseen images, real seen images, and generated text descriptions.

### Strengths
1. The paper is well-structured and clearly written, making it easy to follow.
2. The approach of using an LLM (GPT-4) and a diffusion model to generate missing data for unseen classes is novel. The combination of Stable Diffusion, GPT-4, and CLIP is interesting and practical.
3. By generating images and descriptions, the method eliminates the need for additional training for ZSL, reframing it as a retrieval task.
4. The core idea of dividing the label space into base and novel subspaces and training separate models is a new divide-and-conquer classification strategy for ZSL.
5. The method for improving the generalization capability of CLIP is noteworthy.
6. Experiment results are thorough and extensive, with comparisons against various methods. The proposed approach outperforms state-of-the-art methods by a large margin on three common GZSL benchmarks (CUB, SUN, and another dataset).
7. The use of semantic-level concept selection to filter generated visual samples and the use of GPT-4 for class-level descriptions are additional practical contributions.

### Weaknesses
- **Data leakage and ZSL definition (raised by R2 and R1):**  
  The method uses pre-trained CLIP and Stable Diffusion trained on large-scale web data. It is not explicitly shown that these models have never been exposed to unseen test images, which violates the zero-shot assumption. R2 further notes that CLIP paper itself redefined zero-shot learning to dataset-level generalization, not class-level, creating a significant gap. One reviewer (R2) argues the proposed method is actually a transductive classification method, making experiments unfair to other methods that do not use such large-scale pre-training.  
- **Source of performance gains (R3, echoed by R4):**  
  It is unclear whether the strong performance comes from the divide-and-conquer strategy or the powerful generative models (diffusion + GPT-4). R3 requests testing the divide-and-conquer strategy with traditional generative methods (e.g., without diffusion and GPT-4) to isolate the contribution. R4 notes the lack of a clear baseline, e.g., simply using real training images plus generated images for unseen classes to learn a classifier in CLIP space.  
- **Fine-tuning impact (R3):**  
  Based on Table 1, performance on CUB and SUN is notably poor without model fine-tuning. An explanation is needed for why fine-tuning is critical and what it adds.  
- **Depth of hierarchical division (R3):**  
  The paper sets depth=2 for all datasets, but the optimal depth likely depends on the number of classes in base and novel categories. Larger datasets might benefit from more segmentations; applying the same depth across datasets may not be optimal.  
- **Missing details and clarifications (R1, R2, R3):**  
  - GPU utilization and time required for generation and inference are not discussed.  
  - The connection between data generation (Sec 3.1) and the divide-and-conquer grouping (Sec 3.2) appears weak. A comparison with lighter-weight generative models would strengthen the evaluation.  
  - A direct visual comparison between real unseen images and generated unseen images is not provided; such a comparison would clarify quality and fidelity.  
  - More detail is needed on the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) metric and how it is used to determine thresholds.  
  - The difference between Figure 4(b) and Figure 4(c) is hard to distinguish.  
  - Generated image and description examples for SUN are missing.  
- **Comparison fairness and limited datasets (R1, R4):**  
  Many existing ZSL methods do not use CLIP as the image encoder. Experiments with other visual backbones (e.g., ResNet, ViT) are needed to provide a fair comparison.  
  Results are shown only on three GZSL datasets. The authors are requested to evaluate on additional datasets like Flower and APY.  
- **Minor errors and inconsistencies (R2, R4):**  
  - Method types in Table 1 are incorrect: Calibration-based methods still belong to embedding methods.  
  - I2MVFormer is cited twice; the second citation is misspelled as I2VMFormer.  
  - ICCE is missing its publication year in Table 1.  
  - Typo in lines 324 and 370: “GZSC” should be “GZSL”.  
- **Why only two subspaces for seen/unseen (R2):**  
  The paper divides each space into exactly two subspaces; the rationale for this fixed number is not justified.  
- **Limited novelty of individual components (R4):**  
  While the combination is interesting, the individual ideas (using SD, GPT-4, CLIP) have been used extensively in the literature and are mildly novel.  
- **Demonstration in other spaces (R4):**  
  The main contribution is the hierarchical divide-and-conquer grouping. Its performance should be shown in other feature spaces (e.g., using ResNet/ViT as vision backbone with expert-annotated attributes) to demonstrate general efficacy. One reviewer (R4) would raise their rating if such an experiment showed significant improvement.
