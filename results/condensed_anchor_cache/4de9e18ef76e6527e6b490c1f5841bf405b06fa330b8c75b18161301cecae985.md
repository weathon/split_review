## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces ExeDec, a decomposition-based neural program synthesis strategy that interleaves subgoal prediction (execution states) with program generation to improve compositional generalization. The authors also design five compositional generalization benchmarks based on RobustFill and DeepCoder, and evaluate ExeDec with Transformers trained from scratch and with few-shot LLMs. Reviewers generally agree that ExeDec outperforms baselines and that the benchmarks are valuable, but raise concerns about scalability, limited complexity of DSLs, clarity of the benchmark description, need for additional supervision and experiments, and insufficient discussion of algorithm loop behavior and computational cost. Two reviewers are more strongly positive (scores 8) about the approach and results, while two others (scores 6) are more critical about limitations and presentation.

### Strengths
- Novel and appealing inductive bias: using compositional generalization as an explicit inductive bias in program synthesis (R1).
- Formalization of multiple distinct forms of compositional generalization and construction of actionable benchmarks for program synthesis; this is much-needed work (R2, R3).
- ExeDec outperforms from-scratch Transformer and Latent Programmer baselines by a large margin on the generalization-focused datasets (R1, R2, R4).
- Improvements over the No-Subgoal ablation are significant, e.g., 80% → 87% on the hardest tasks, particularly in “Compose New Operation” and “Compose Different Concept” categories (R2).
- The method mirrors how humans write programs by decomposing problems into subproblems; simple and intuitive (R3).
- General algorithm that can be instantiated both with Transformers trained from scratch and with few-shot LLMs, showing generality (R2, R4).
- LLM adaptation (ExeDec-style prompting) improves performance on compositionally generalizing tasks despite LLMs struggling in baseline few-shot settings (R2, R3).
- Benchmarks provide valuable insight into settings where decomposition into subprograms is important (R3).
- Subgoal prediction (even just next line’s output) is helpful, a non-obvious finding (R2).
- Well-motivated problem discussion and thorough related work review (R1).

### Weaknesses
- The two datasets (RobustFill, DeepCoder) are small and domain-specific; the DSLs are straight-line code with no recursion, loops, or conditionals, limiting evaluation of scalability to realistic programs (R1, the authors acknowledge this as a limitation).
- I/O-based specification of subgoals is not easy to construct: intermediate states may involve auxiliary temporary variables, and output states may require complex transformations; the method’s reliance on such examples is a significant limitation (R1).
- Training with decomposition requires supervision for subgoals (execution traces). The paper does not address how to handle multiple valid subgoal decompositions; supervising only one specific decomposition may bias training (R1, R3).
- The benchmark description (Sections 2 and 3) is hard to follow; clarity is low (R4). The description needs improvement to convey the exact contents and novelty of the benchmarks (R4).
- Missing discussion about the loop in Algorithm 1: what happens if it never terminates? How does the FLOPs/computation compare between ExeDec and baselines (including beam search)? The loop gives ExeDec potentially more computational power (R4).
- No quantitative analysis of how often proposed subgoals are actually achieved, or whether they are achieved more often along successful vs unsuccessful synthesis paths (R2).
- The results, while good, are not extremely strong; stronger results would further justify the approach (R2).
- Missing experiment: finetuning a pre-trained LLM (instead of only from-scratch or few-shot) would be interesting (R3).
- Missing ablation: feeding intermediate execution traces to the Transformer baseline (without explicit decomposition) to isolate the benefit of the subgoal decomposition itself (R3).
- Questions that overlap with criticisms/missing details:
    - How to process different numbers of I/O examples (with varying sizes) in the Transformer models? (R1)
    - How to decide which snippets form a proper subgoal when straight-line code can be split arbitrarily? (R1)
    - Why does the No-Subgoal ablation perform similarly to the Transformer baseline (shouldn’t it behave like the Transformer?), and why does it perform better on some generalization benchmarks? (R1, R3)
    - Why does ExeDec improve primarily on “Compose New Operation” and “Compose Different Concepts” but not on other settings? (R2)
    - The LLM used in Table 1 is anonymized (“one of the largest available through an API”); this should be specified (R2). Also, the failure modes of LLMs (invalid code vs incorrect code) need clarification (R2).
    - Clarification needed: “test on training distribution” in Fig. 2 refers to a heldout test set from the same distribution, not training data itself (R2).
    - How do models perform on generalization tasks they were not specifically trained for? (R4)
