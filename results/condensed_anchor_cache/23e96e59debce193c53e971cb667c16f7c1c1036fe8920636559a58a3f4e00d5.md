## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes minifinetuning (MFT), a domain-adaptation method for LLMs that mitigates catastrophic forgetting without requiring pre-training data. MFT uses corrective self-distillation: it linearly combines the pre-trained (teacher) model’s probability vector with a one-hot target vector, with a coefficient chosen so that the target token becomes the most probable by a margin τ, minimizing perturbation to other tokens. The student is fine-tuned to match this corrected distribution. Experiments on GPT-Neo, Phi, Gemma, and Llama 2 across medical, legal, and math domains compare against vanilla fine-tuning, LoRA, DoRA, and Replay (which uses pre-training data). MFT consistently achieves a better specialization-to-degeneralization ratio (2-10× improvement) and shows robustness down to 500 samples. Ablations confirm each component is necessary. Replay outperforms MFT but is considered to have an “unfair advantage.” The method is composable with parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

### Strengths
- **Originality and novelty:** The paper introduces the first method specifically designed for fine-tuning off-the-shelf LLMs on a different domain without access to their pre-training data. The token-wise corrected-distribution formulation (including the special case when the original model’s prediction is already correct) is non-obvious. (Reviewer 1)
- **Core idea's potential:** The approach of softly “moving” probability mass uniformly to the target token is interesting and could inspire future optimal-transport-based improvements. (Reviewer 2)
- **Comprehensive experimental setup:** Experiments span multiple model families and sizes (GPT-Neo, Phi, Gemma, Llama 2) and three domains (medical, legal, math). Additional sweeps over LoRA/DoRA capacity, the margin parameter τ, and incremental ablations demonstrate consistent trends and support the paper’s conclusions. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4)
- **Promising results with limited data:** MFT is particularly effective in low-data settings (down to 500 samples), achieving 2-10× better specialization-to-degeneralization ratios than standard fine-tuning. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 4)
- **Practical compatibility:** MFT can be combined with low-rank adapters (LoRA, DoRA) for a combined effect. (Reviewer 1)
- **Helpful ablation:** The ablation isolating the effect of adjusting only incorrect-token distributions supports the full design. (Reviewer 4)
- **Clarity of core method:** One reviewer found the paper extremely clear and well presented, with intuitive graphs and a derivation sufficient for re-implementation. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 3 also said the presentation is clear and the method straightforward). However, this point is contested – see Weaknesses.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons with continual-learning / catastrophic-forgetting baselines:** The paper does not compare with state-of-the-art regularization-based methods for catastrophic forgetting, such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and other Fisher-matrix approaches, which are directly applicable when pre-training data is unavailable. The related work section is insufficient and largely ignores the extensive literature on continual learning and the stability-plasticity tradeoff. Including metrics from that literature (e.g., forgetting measures from Wang et al.) would strengthen the evaluation. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Hyperparameter selection is unclear:** The margin τ is set to 0.25 without a clear justification or methodology for tuning it in practice. The paper presents τ as a design choice, yet it directly governs the specialization–degeneralization tradeoff (Fig. 2). The lack of guidance on how to set τ, α, and β undermines reproducibility and practical applicability. (Reviewer 4; also raised as a question by Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 also asks why τ is expressed in probability space rather than log-probability, suggesting the choice may be critical.
- **Unjustified variable-weight design:** The method uses a variable α (enforcing a fixed margin τ) instead of a fixed weight like Replay. This can produce counterintuitive behavior: when the pre-trained model is very certain (sharp distribution), MFT suppresses it more aggressively to achieve the margin, potentially discarding key knowledge; when the model is uncertain (flat distribution), MFT adapts more weakly. The design contradicts the intuition that one should preserve certain knowledge and adapt uncertain knowledge. The paper does not explain why a variable weight is necessary or provide empirical evidence that it is better than a constant weight. (Reviewer 3) (Related: Reviewer 1 asks why not apply the correction in log-prob space; Reviewer 4 asks about interpolating with the full teacher distribution here.)
- **Baseline fairness and selection issues:**
    - LoRA/DoRA rank is fixed at 8 without justification; it is unclear whether this is an optimal setting for each baseline. Without a best-vs-best comparison, the relative performance of MFT vs. LoRA/DoRA is difficult to assess. (Reviewer 4)
    - Replay is included despite its advantage of having pre-training data, but it remains unclear whether its benefit comes from the specific data points or simply from being a regularizer (training on a larger, more diverse set). A synthetic-data replay baseline (sampling from the pre-trained model) could disentangle this. (Reviewer 1)
    - Experiments are limited to mostly parameter-efficient small models; the only larger model (Llama 2 7B) is outdated. Modern state-of-the-art LLMs (Llama 3, Mistral, Mixtral) are not tested, making the results less impactful for current practice. (Reviewer 3)
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** Multiple reviewers disagree on clarity.
    - Figures (e.g., Fig. 1b,c; Fig. 3) use different y-axis scales across subplots, making visual comparison difficult. Standardizing axes or redesigning as tables would help. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4)
    - The term “degeneralization” is non-standard; using established terminology like “catastrophic forgetting” or “performance decrease” would improve accessibility and connection to prior work. (Reviewer 4)
    - Several sentences are hard to parse (e.g., “construction of a per-token individualized corrected distribution”) and italic subheadings in Section 4 (e.g., “distillation and corrective distillation on incorrect tokens”) are ambiguous. The writing needs more precision. (Reviewer 4)
    - Tables (e.g., Table 2) are large and difficult to draw conclusions from; adding takeaway messages in captions is recommended. (Reviewer 4)
    - Table 1 is not referenced or explained in the text (what do stars mean, how are ratings derived?). (Reviewer 3)
    - Minor typos: l. 222 “Basline” -> “Baseline”; citation formatting issues (e.g., “Team et al.”; missing braces in bibtex for {OpenELM}, {LoRA}, {RLHF}). (Reviewer 1)
- **Motivation for preserving pretraining performance is insufficient:** The paper should articulate concrete practical scenarios where retaining general knowledge after specialized fine-tuning is crucial, rather than merely assuming it is desirable. (Reviewer 4)
- **Unexplained and unexpected behavior in results:**
    - In Fig. 3 (top left and top right), the 1M token line collapses to a specialization of 0 around step 400, immediately followed by a degeneralization ratio of 0; this behavior is not explained and seems contradictory. (Reviewer 1)
    - MFT appears to improve specialization (not just attenuate degeneralization) in some settings (e.g., Fig. 3 left, 1M and 2M tokens). The authors provide no intuition for why this occurs. (Reviewer 4)
- **Scope of contribution novelty questioned:** Because the paper misses the rich body of catastrophic-forgetting research, adding self-distillation to avoid forgetting is seen by some reviewers as not sufficiently novel. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
