## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a family of analytic DAG constraints for differentiable DAG learning, unifying existing constraints and offering new ones via closure under differentiation, addition, and multiplication. Theoretical properties (convergence, gradient magnitude, non-convexity) are formally established. Extensive experiments on linear and nonlinear data show improved performance over prior methods like DAGMA. Reviewers acknowledge the theoretical contributions and extensive empirical evaluation, but raise concerns about reproducibility, experimental design (e.g., limited replicates, focus on accuracy rather than gradient dynamics), unclear practical guidelines (choice of order), missing analyses (convergence rates, error bounds, computational complexity), and some inaccuracies in writing/derivations.

### Strengths
- The theoretical analysis is mathematically sound and well-structured. Propositions 3 and 4 (closure properties, gradient representation) are correct and insightful. [R1, R4]
- The class of analytic constraints unifies existing DAG constraints (e.g., exponential, polynomial) and provides a principled way to generate new ones with amplified gradients via multiplication or differentiation. [R3, R4]
- The discussion on the convexity–gradient magnitude tradeoff provides useful intuition for constraint design. [R3]
- The paper demonstrates that the proposed constraints do not suffer from vanishing gradients, a key problem in differentiable DAG learning. [R4]
- Experiments are extensive, covering low-to-high-dimensional linear SEM and nonlinear real data. The proposed method outperforms prior state-of-the-art (e.g., DAGMA, NOTEARS) across many settings. [R1, R3, R2, R4]
- The approach is theory-backed and offers an “out-of-the-shelf” method for constructing DAG constraints. [R4]

### Weaknesses
- **Limited replicates and reproducibility**: Experiments use only 10 replicates; lower-dimensional settings should have more replicates for robust results. [R1] Code availability is not mentioned in the submitted paper. [R1]
- **Missing convergence and error-bound analysis**: The paper lacks formal statements on convergence rates or error bounds for the proposed constraints (e.g., effect of truncating the power series). [R2, R4]
- **Unclear practical guidance**: Performance depends on data properties and choice of order (\(n\)), making it hard to decide which order to use in practice. [R2] The mixed messages in tables (e.g., Table 5 vs 6 with identical titles but different results; Table 6 and 7 show DAGMA better for large graphs) need clarification. [R2]
- **Experimental focus misaligned with theory**: The main contribution is solving gradient vanishing, but experiments focus on accuracy rather than analyzing learning dynamics or gradient statistics over training. [R4] The emphasis on data scale (known/unknown variance) is questionable because the acyclicity constraint is not primarily affected by scale. [R4] Table 4 lacks standard deviations and results are not significant. [R4]
- **Methodology and implementation issues**:
  - The CausalDiscoveryToolbox used for PC and GES is no longer maintained; using direct R implementations would improve reproducibility. [R1]
  - The caching claim for \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{B}/s)^{-1}\) (line 223) is unclear because \(\mathbf{B}\) changes each iteration. [R4]
  - Algorithms 1 and 2 are not properly discussed; their parameters are not explained. [R4]
  - Complexity of Algorithm 1 and standard computation is not given. [R2]
- **Missing comparisons and ablations**:
  - Computation time comparisons for constraints vs. baselines are needed to justify efficiency claims. [R2]
  - For nonlinear data, evaluation should also include synthetic data with equal noise variance (as in Bello et al., 2022). [R3]
  - A comparison of gradient magnitude (choice of \(s\)) between the proposed method and DAGMA would visualize the effectiveness of Algorithm 1. [R3]
  - What other analytic function classes (beyond exponential and polynomial) could be used, and what are their pros/cons? [R2]
- **Inaccuracies and lack of clarity**:
  - Line 106–107: incorrectly attributes performance degradation to the acyclicity constraint rather than the MSE loss (which is inconsistent for normalized data). [R4]
  - Line 127–129: convergence condition (spectral radius < \(r\)) needs more intuition/proof in the main text. [R4]
  - Line 136: is \(f(\mathbf{B})\) in Eq. 3 or Eq. 4? [R2]
  - Line 154–156: explanation of computational efficiency beyond gradient evaluation (truncation error, cost of infinite sum) is insufficient. [R4]
  - Eq. 10 derivation should be shown step-by-step by applying Prop. 1 and 2 to functions in Eq. 9. [R4]
  - Eq. 11: derivative appears incorrect: \(\frac{\partial f^s_{\text{log}}(x)}{\partial x} = -s (s-x)^{-1} = -s \cdot f^s_{inv}\)? [R4]
  - Line 224: how much speed-up is actually achieved in practice? [R2]
  - Lines 305–307: incomplete sentences? Discussion of optimization objective (17) is confusing: why is statement restricted to Gaussian SEM? Why is convexity blamed rather than the score function? [R2, R3]
  - Line 362–367: same issue: MSE is inconsistent for unequal variance, so results may not be due to convexity. [R3, R4]
  - For linear SEM with unknown variance: which score function is used? Is SHD computed on the Markov equivalence class? [R3]
  - Table 7 caption seems incorrect (describes runtime but maybe not). [R2, R3]
  - Table 3: GES value seems one order of magnitude larger than expected. [R1]
  - Minor typos: missing spaces after “nodes” (line end), before “Ng et al.”, after “DAGMA” in Table 1 caption. [R1]
- **Limited scope**: Theory currently restricted to linear SEM with equal noise variance. [R1]
