- Scores: 6, 6, 6, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
GEVRM integrates the internal model control principle into a video generation-based goal-conditioned behavioral cloning framework for robust visual manipulation under perturbations. It uses a text-conditioned video diffusion model to generate future goal images, aligns state representations via prototypical contrastive learning, and employs a diffusion policy for action prediction. Evaluated on CALVIN and Bridge datasets, it shows improved robustness to perturbations.

### Strengths
- Well-motivated and clearly written; the problem of robust VLA under perturbations is important (Reviewers 1–4).
- Video generation for goal-conditioned behavioral cloning is a sensible design (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- GEVRM outperforms previous methods (AVDC, GR-1) on the Bridge dataset for video generation quality (Reviewer 1).
- On CALVIN: GEVRM outperforms HiP, UniPi, and GR-1 on generalization (ABC→D with RGB inputs) and outperforms SuSIE on generalization with visual perturbations (Tables 2, 3; Reviewer 1).
- The generalization experiments on perturbed CALVIN environments (train on A,B,C → test on D with perturbations) are a useful contribution for evaluating robustness (Reviewer 2).
- The method innovatively combines random masking for consistency training and prototypical contrastive learning for state alignment; these techniques are described and shown to improve generation quality (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Extensive evaluation on both visual planning quality and closed-loop task success is provided (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Inconsistent and incomplete baseline comparisons.**  
  - The paper uses different baselines for different settings (AVDC on Bridge vs. GR-1 on CALVIN for video generation; HiP/UniPi/GR-1 on unperturbed rollout; SuSIE on perturbed rollout). A consistent set of baselines across all evaluations would strengthen the comparison (Reviewer 1).  
  - On CALVIN ABC-D (Table 2), only GR-1 among powerful baselines is included, but its reproduced performance is notably low; details of the GR-1 reproduction are needed. SuSIE’s results on this same setting should also be reported (Reviewer 2).  
  - For perturbed environments (Table 3), only SuSIE is compared. The performance of other baselines (HiP, UniPi, GR-1) in this setting is missing (Reviewer 2).  
  - Since robust manipulation is the core setting, comparisons to HiP, UniPi, GR-1, and pure behavioral cloning *with data augmentation* are needed to isolate the effect of the proposed method from standard augmentation techniques (Reviewer 1). It is unknown whether baselines were trained with data augmentation (Reviewer 1, Q1).

- **Unclear novelty and insufficient ablation.**  
  - The key contribution of GEVRM over prior work is not clearly articulated. The general framework of composing a text-conditioned video model with an action predictor has been widely explored (Reviewers 2, 4). The paper should clarify how GEVRM differs architecturally and what design choices cause the improvement (Reviewer 1).  
  - The connection to Internal Model Control (IMC) is weak: IMC uses the difference between model-predicted output and actual output to generate feedback, whereas GEVRM uses current state and desired goal to produce actions – essentially a goal-conditioned policy. The claimed IMC integration is not convincingly established (Reviewer 3).  
  - The paper should cite the concurrent work "Closed-Loop Visuomotor Control with Generative Expectation" (arXiv:2409.09016), which shares similar design philosophy and figure layout, but is not mentioned (Reviewer 2).  
  - Ablations are insufficient:  
    * The state alignment loss (λ) is only evaluated via a t-SNE plot; quantitative task success rate for λ=0 is needed (Reviewer 1).  
    * The random masking strategy for consistency training is not ablated against simpler alternatives (e.g., masking out the last few frames). A granular analysis of masking ratios and temporal locations is missing (Reviewer 4).  
    * The image encoder trained via prototypical contrastive learning is not compared to an off-the-shelf encoder like DINOv2 (Reviewer 3).  
    * The superiority of the diffusion policy over a simpler MLP for the goal-conditioned action head is not demonstrated (Reviewer 2, Q2).

- **No real-world robotic experiments.**  
  The method is only tested in simulation (CALVIN) and on video prediction datasets (Bridge). Real-robot validation is absent (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 notes that Bridge evaluation is only on video generation quality, not policy execution.

- **Missing analysis of computational overhead and practical efficiency.**  
  The diffusion policy for action prediction can be time-consuming, but the paper provides no analysis of inference time or computational cost compared to simpler inverse dynamics models. Practical implications for real-time control are not discussed (Reviewers 2, 4).

- **Superficial limitations discussion.**  
  The limitations section does not address potential failure cases, such as handling complex long-horizon tasks, high visual clutter, or dynamic environments (Reviewer 4).

- **Potential issues with state alignment via prototypical contrastive learning.**  
  The contrastive learning uses current and goal states from the same trajectory as positives, which may lead the encoder to focus on trivial background features that are consistent across timesteps rather than task-relevant changes (e.g., object/arm motion) (Reviewer 4, Q1). It is also unclear whether the encoder is trained on perturbed images or only clean images, and whether the same encoder is applied to perturbed inputs at test time (Reviewer 4, Q2).

- **Generalization evaluation scope.**  
  In CALVIN evaluation, it is not reported whether the language instructions for each subtask are seen during training or are novel, which is important for assessing task generalization (Reviewer 4, Q3).
