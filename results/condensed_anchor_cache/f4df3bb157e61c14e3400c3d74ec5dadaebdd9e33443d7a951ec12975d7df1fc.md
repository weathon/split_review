## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies why diffusion models generalize beyond their training set, proposing that high variance of score function estimates (especially at early times) plays a key role. Using a linear score estimator with a fixed feature map, a sample-splitting asymptotic regime (large N, small Δt, specific time sampling) and a Gaussian mixture training distribution, the authors derive the optimal closed-form solution to denoising score matching. They show that the learned distribution is equivalent to running reverse diffusion with the optimal score and then convolving with a data-dependent kernel, linking diffusion training to kernel density estimation. Applications to several machine learning tasks are discussed.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important and timely question: why diffusion models generalize rather than memorize the training set (R2, R1, R3, R4).
- It offers a novel perspective by connecting generalization to high variance in score estimation, and mathematically links diffusion models to kernel density estimation—a connection that appears insightful and original (R1, R3, R4).
- The closed-form optimal solution for the linear score estimator under the assumed regime is derived, and the covariance of the parameter estimates is analyzed, revealing the high variance phenomenon (R1, R4).
- The explicit characterization of the learned distribution as the optimal distribution convolved with a specific kernel is a concrete theoretical contribution (R1).
- The paper is generally well‑written (R1, R3), and most computations appear correct (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Core argument and motivation**  
   - The claim that high variance in score estimates drives generalization is not convincingly justified. The paper does not reconcile its analysis with standard practices that stabilize predictions at small times, e.g., the unit‑norm prediction target regularization (Karras et al., 2022) or direct‑x parameterizations, which would make targets very stable and could invalidate the variance argument (R2).  
   - The paper conflates two separate issues: the difficulty of fitting the score function at small t (due to rapidly changing targets) and the variance of the score estimates. Prior work (cited as [2]) observes that variance over $p(x_0|x_t)$ is actually small at small t and that minimizing that variance improves performance—contradicting the paper’s claim (R2).  
   - It remains unclear how high variance in the linear estimator translates to generalization in real diffusion models, and the paper does not establish a causal or even correlational link (R4, R1).

2. **Assumptions limit applicability**  
   - The analysis relies on a **linear score estimator** and a **fixed feature map**, whereas score functions are generally non‑linear. The paper does not provide examples where the true score is linear, nor does it discuss how the results might extend to non‑linear estimators or feature maps learned from data (R1, R4).  
   - The assumed asymptotic regime (sample splitting per time bin instead of reweighting, large N, small Δt) is questionable. In practice, $\lambda(t)$ reweights the loss rather than splitting samples, and the regime appears artificially constrained (R1). The paper does not justify why sample splitting is a reasonable approximation.  
   - The analysis is restricted to simple diffusion models (Eqs. 1‑2) and a **Gaussian mixture training distribution**. It is not clear whether the theoretical computations carry over to more advanced diffusion formulations (VE, VP, sub‑VP) or to non‑Gaussian mixture distributions (R3).  
   - The paper does not examine how realistic network parameterizations (e.g., those used in practice) would affect the derived results. If the model could learn the optimal score perfectly, would the analysis still hold? Without evaluating this, the connection to real diffusion models is weak (R2).

3. **Rigor and presentation**  
   - Some mathematical derivations appear heuristic rather than fully rigorous (R4).  
   - The **lack of empirical experiments** is a significant shortcoming. The paper makes testable predictions (e.g., kernel convolution, variance behavior) that could be validated on small datasets, but no experiments are provided. Several reviewers strongly suggest numerical experiments to support the claims (R2, R4).  
   - The presentation could be improved: figures would help illustrate the key ideas (e.g., kernel shapes, variance over time), and the writing in parts is unclear (R2, R4).

4. **Unaddressed questions that overlap with criticisms**  
   - How to estimate or learn the feature maps $\phi$ when they are not known a priori? (R1)  
   - How accurate can a linear estimator be for typical score functions? Would a better estimator (e.g., non‑parametric) hurt or improve generalization? (R1)  
   - How do the results change if a different time sampling distribution is used? In particular, Eq.8 (underweighting small times) is contradicted by common practice (e.g., log‑normal sampling in Karras et al. 2022 actually *overweights* small t) (R2).  
   - In the large‑sample limit ($c$ large), the derived variance becomes small—would we still expect generalization? (R1)  
   - Can the analysis be extended to other diffusion models (VE, VP, sub‑VP) or to non‑Gaussian mixture distributions? (R3)  

*Note on reviewer disagreement*: Reviewers 1 and 2 were more negative (score 3), doubting the core argument and the practical relevance of the assumptions, while Reviewers 3 and 4 were more positive (score 5), finding the connection novel but also noting the limited scope and need for more rigorous validation. All four reviewers agree on the need for experiments and clearer justification.
