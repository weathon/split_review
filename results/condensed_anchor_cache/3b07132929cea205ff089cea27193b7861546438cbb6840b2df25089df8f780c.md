## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes IB-EDL, a method that regularizes Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) with an Information Bottleneck (IB) loss to improve calibration of fine-tuned LLMs. The approach introduces a unified framework that subsumes existing EDL methods as special cases. Empirical results across Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B, and Mistral-7B on multiple tasks show improved calibration (lower ECE) and OOD detection (higher AUROC) with minimal computational overhead. The method is theoretically motivated, addressing deficiencies of previous EDL losses.

### Strengths
- Relevance: Addresses an important problem—uncertainty calibration of LLM outputs at the token level—critical for safe deployment and error estimation.
- Theoretical soundness: Principled, mathematically derived approach that identifies precise issues in applying EDL to LLMs and proposes a simple final regularization term \(\mathcal{L}_{IB-Info}\), drawing on the paragraph *Challenges when applying IB to an internal layer of an LLM*.
- Unified perspective: Frames several existing EDL methods as special cases within the IB-EDL framework.
- Experimental soundness: Extensive experiments across multiple LLMs (Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B), tasks, and evaluation modes (accuracy+ECE, OOD detection). Strong results strongly back improved calibration, especially OOD detection and noise injection experiments (Tables 3 and 4). Covers most relevant baselines; mention that LoRA-based Mixture of Experts (MixLoRA) could also serve as a baseline.
- Well-written and easy to follow.
- Promising empirical performance; addresses overconfidence problem well.

### Weaknesses
- Scope clarity: The paper is unclear whether the method applies to pre-training or only fine-tuning. Experiments clearly show it is for fine-tuning a specific task, but the abstract and introduction should state this explicitly. The method is not tested for pre-training, so claims should not imply otherwise. (Reviewer 1)
- Computational overhead: The pipeline (\(x \to f(x;\theta) \to \tilde{e} \to e \to \alpha \to \pi \to y\)) seems heavy. Lacks inference time comparison against other methods. Also lacks training time comparison with LoRA, the fine-tuning backbone. The claim of computational efficiency is not validated with time and memory costs. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- OOD evaluation incomplete: OOD experiments report only AUROC; the expected calibration error (ECE) on OOD datasets is missing, which is also an indicator of OOD performance. Calibration performance in OOD scenarios is not sufficiently analyzed. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- Novelty: Combining IB and EDL, two well-established methods, yields limited novelty; the primary contribution is implementing IB on EDL. A more detailed discussion on how the IB objective specifically improves calibration is needed, and theoretical guarantees (e.g., a bound on calibration error or a proof of convergence) are lacking. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- Applicability limited to discrete classification: The method assumes a fixed set of mutually exclusive classes and a one-hot ground truth, relying on a Dirichlet prior over a categorical distribution. It is not clear how this extends to open-ended generation where multiple semantically equivalent responses are valid. (Reviewer 3)
- Limited distribution shift scope: OOD experiments only explore small shifts within the same QA domain (OBQA → ARC, CSQA). Performance on more significant shifts (e.g., from reading comprehension to a math task) is not shown; generalization to larger semantic differences is unclear. (Reviewer 3)
- Missing analysis: No sensitivity analysis on the number of bins for ECE computation. No comparison of training flops; unclear whether the mean and covariance prediction heads require separate training. Benefit of two parameters (mean and variance) vs. one is not discussed. Effect of fine-tuning on multiple datasets simultaneously or on open-ended generation tasks is not studied. (Reviewers 3, 4)
