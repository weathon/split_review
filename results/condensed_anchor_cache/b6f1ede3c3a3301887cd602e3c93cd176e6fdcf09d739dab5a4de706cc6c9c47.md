## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a reward-conditioned data relabeling method to augment preference datasets with quality scores. The method conditions preference pairs on explicit reward values (1–10 scale) via a special prompt (e.g., "Generate a response of reward 5"), so that during DPO training only the response matching the conditioned reward is treated as preferred. This allows the model to learn from both high- and low-quality responses and to leverage quantitative reward information. Experiments on instruction-following benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT‑Bench, Arena‑Hard‑Auto) and six academic benchmarks (GSM8K, GPQA, MUSR, TruthfulQA, BBH, ARC) show improvements over DPO across several base models (Zephyr, Mistral, Qwen2, Llama3.1, Gemma2, SPPO). Ablation studies validate the approach, and state-of-the-art results are claimed on AlpacaEval 2.0 when applied to on-policy data.

### Strengths
- **Novelty in addressing overfitting/unlearning** – Reward-conditioned policies extend existing direct alignment algorithms to mitigate the unlearning of high-quality rejected responses and indiscriminate favoring of low-quality chosen responses.
- **Strong and consistent empirical results** – Significant performance gains across diverse models (Zephyr, Mistral, Qwen2, Llama3.1, Gemma2, SPPO) and benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT‑Bench, Arena‑Hard‑Auto, GSM8K, GPQA, MUSR, TruthfulQA, BBH, ARC). Trends are consistent across settings.
- **Comprehensive ablation studies** – Demonstrate that the method maximizes utility of preference data and mitigates unlearning, beyond mere dataset expansion.
- **Simple, efficient, and easy to integrate** – No architectural changes required; works with any existing direct alignment algorithm; computationally efficient; applicable to both offline and online preference data.
- **Versatile** – Can handle multi-dimensional reward attributes (e.g., helpfulness, truthfulness) and is independent of the underlying preference dataset.
- **Mitigates unlearning** – The method partially addresses the known problem of DPO unnecessarily forgetting high-quality rejected responses.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical and motivational gaps** – Section 3.1 uses the term “reward sparsity” in an unconventional way (not about trajectory-wise sparsity) and contains unclear logic about π* and assumptions. Section 3.2 is titled too strongly for a method lacking clear theoretical backing. The paper does not provide a principled explanation for why conditioning on low-reward responses during training should improve generation of high-reward responses at inference. (R2, R5)
- **Methodological ambiguity** – It is unclear how the values \( r_w^i \) and \( r_l^i \) are determined when the dataset originally provides only a binary preference indicator. The mapping from binary preferences to numeric reward scores is not explained. (R2) The categorization of responses beyond binary labels is insufficiently justified; additional quality dimensions should be considered. (R4)
- **No human evaluation** – All evaluations rely on automated metrics (LLM-as-judge) and may not reflect real-world human preferences. It is crucial to validate whether the observed improvements translate to genuine response quality as perceived by humans. (R3)
- **Incomplete comparison with related reward-augmented methods** – The paper does not compare against existing work that also learns from quantitative rewards, such as *Noise Contrastive Alignment of Language Models with Explicit Rewards* (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05369) and *ULMA: Unified Language Model Alignment with Demonstration and Point-wise Human Preference* (https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02554). (R5)
- **Sensitivity to reward label quality** – The method uses GPT‑4‑generated rewards (deterministic), but with human reward labels (which are subjective and noisy) conditioning on a specific value becomes problematic. For example, prompting for reward 9 while labeling a reward‑10 response as “dispreferred” is unrealistic and risks degenerate training. The impact of noisy reward signals is not investigated. (R5) The accuracy of the reward model matters; a useful experiment would be to flip the ArmoRM scores and observe the effect. (R2)
- **Conditioning on continuous reward values** – Prompting with a specific numeric reward (e.g., “Generate a response of reward 3.75”) is less natural than conditioning on deterministic attributes (e.g., “truthful”, “contains no mathematical mistakes”). The method appears ill‑suited for fine‑grained continuous reward scores. (R5)
- **Arbitrary scoring scale** – The choice of a 1–10 scale for quality scores is not justified. No ablation explores whether other scales (1–5, 1–100) or different granularity would affect performance. (R3)
- **Limited generalizability claims** – Experiments focus on instruction-following and academic benchmarks; real-world applications are absent. (R1)
- **Missing details on training data** – The paper uses previously unseen instructions in DPO training that match existing data, potentially limiting model adaptability. The training prompts for Appendix \( D_N^i \) are not provided. (R4)
- **Missing evidence for the unlearning problem** – The benefits of keeping rejected high-reward samples are not empirically justified. The authors should start from a model that is not DPO‑fine‑tuned and compare DPO vs. the proposed method to concretely demonstrate unlearning mitigation. (R1)
- **Typographical error** – Line 200 contains an error: \( r_{g = r_l^i}(x, y_w^i) = 0 \) is incorrectly formatted. (R4)
- **Terminology concern** – The term “unlearning” is used in a non‑standard sense, conflicting with its established meaning in ML literature. (R2)

**Contrasting opinion:** One reviewer was notably more negative (rating 3), arguing that the method lacks a strong foundation and that restructuring the dataset (creating new preference pairs) intuitively seems more effective than goal‑conditioned learning. This reviewer also considered the impact of reward labels on data quality unexplored, noted the missing training prompts, and pointed out the typographical error.
