## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides the first theoretical proof that gradient descent with greedy sparsification (TopK) and error feedback (EF21) can achieve better communication complexity than vanilla distributed gradient descent (DGD) in non-convex optimization when data features are rare and sparse. The authors introduce quantities measuring feature sparsity and rareness (c and r), and derive convergence rates that improve as features become rarer. Numerical experiments on synthetic data are provided to validate the theory.

### Strengths
- One of the few papers that attempts to theoretically prove why a heuristic algorithm (error feedback with greedy sparsification) outperforms distributed gradient descent in practice, despite prior theory suggesting otherwise (Reviewer 1).  
- Introduces the concept of feature “rareness” into optimization analysis – a good attempt to connect data structure and algorithm performance (Reviewer 3).  
- Well written, easy to follow, and provides a comprehensive review of related work (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Meaningful observations motivate the research (Reviewer 2).  
- Theoretical results are solid and rely on simple, standard assumptions (Reviewer 2). The paper is in good shape and addresses the important problem of communication complexity in distributed ML (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Strength of assumptions and practical relevance of the regime (multiple reviewers, significant disagreement)**  
  Several reviewers question whether the feature‑rareness assumption is common in practice and whether it truly explains EF’s empirical success.  
  – Reviewer 1: “assumptions are quite strong and it is not clear if this is the best scenario to explain the performance.”  
  – Reviewer 3 elaborates: “their analysis relies on some strong assumptions about the data which are uncommon in practice” and “if EF21 performs well on many types of data (without strong assumptions) … feature rareness … are NOT the true reasons.”  
  – Reviewer 2 notes that “most real‑world datasets are not sparse enough.”  
  In contrast, Reviewer 2 considered the assumptions standard and the theory solid.

- **Missing comparison: Could the same analysis improve DGD rates?**  
  Reviewer 3 points out that Lemmas 2‑5 appear general and asks whether rare/sparse features would also improve the communication complexity of DGD itself, which would weaken the claimed advantage of EF21.

- **Limited to linear models**  
  Reviewer 3: “arguments are limited to simple linear models because feature sparsity leads to model sparsity … only for linear models. For non‑linear models (e.g., DNNs) the arguments will not hold.”

- **Experiments are toy, mismatch with non‑convex theory**  
  – Reviewer 2: experiments are “rather toy and the practical applicability is limited” and suggests experiments on real‑world datasets.  
  – Reviewer 3: “the experiments also only used convex regression models but not more complicated neural networks. There is a discrepancy between the experiments with the non‑convex theoretical analysis.”

- **Lack of stochastic setting**  
  Reviewer 3: “The paper only studied deterministic setting without stochastic gradients. SGD‑type methods are more practical. What’s the situation in the stochastic setting?”

- **Novelty concerns (minority critical view)**  
  – Reviewer 2: “I have some concerns about the novelty … since it mostly builds on the previous work EF21.”  
  – Reviewer 3: “the main modification in the proof … is improving Young’s inequality and the smoothness constant L using rareness/sparsity. This is not very challenging and novel in my evaluation.”  
  (Other reviewers rated contribution as 3 = good.)

- **Presentation suggestions**  
  Reviewer 4 recommends specifying which section/part of the appendix when referring to it, and moving the discussion of limitations and future work to the main text.
