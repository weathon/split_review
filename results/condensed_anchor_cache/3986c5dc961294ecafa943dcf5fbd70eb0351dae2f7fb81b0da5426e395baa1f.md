## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper studies tabular finite-horizon MDPs and introduces EQO (Exploration via Quasi-Optimism), a variant of UCRL/UCBVI that uses a simple exploration bonus proportional to the inverse of the state-action visit count, \(b_k(s,a) = c_k / N_k(s,a)\), without any explicit variance estimation. The constant \(c_k\) is inflated by a factor of \(\sqrt{K}\) or \(\sqrt{k}\) (depending on the regret setting). The algorithm is based on the new principle of *quasi-optimism*, where value estimates are not required to be optimistic at every episode but only in a looser sense. EQO is shown to achieve the sharpest known minimax optimal regret bound for tabular RL under minimal assumptions, as well as a minimax optimal PAC guarantee for best-policy identification (though without an adaptive stopping criterion). A simple numerical experiment on one environment (with varying size/horizon) indicates that EQO outperforms existing baseline algorithms in both cumulative regret and computational efficiency.

### Strengths

1. **Novelty of the quasi-optimism principle.** The idea of relaxing strong optimism to "quasi-optimism" is novel and interesting. Most existing works require that the estimated Q-functions be optimistic at every state-action-step-episode, and showing that this can be relaxed is a valuable conceptual contribution.

2. **First bonus‑based algorithm without variance to achieve minimax optimal regret.** To the best of the reviewers’ knowledge, it is the first result where a simple bonus scheme (no variance estimates) attains the minimax optimal regret bound; previously only posterior-sampling-based algorithms (e.g., Tiapkin et al., 2022) achieved this without direct variance usage. This is a major improvement over prior variance‑dependent approaches.

3. **Simplicity and practicality.** The algorithm is substantially simpler than earlier minimax optimal algorithms (e.g., Azar et al., 2017). It does not require variance estimation and is easy to implement. This simplicity, combined with optimal theoretical guarantees, is an exciting and important step toward making provably efficient RL more practical.

4. **Tightest known bound under mild assumptions.** EQO achieves the tightest known regret bound in the time-homogeneous setting and also matches lower bounds for PAC sample complexity. The relaxation of common technical assumptions (Assumptions 1 and 2) is a relevant additional contribution.

5. **Well‑written and well‑motivated.** The paper is overall clearly written, with good intuition for the results and clean notation/proofs. The context and motivation are well presented.

### Weaknesses

1. **Inability to handle instance‑dependent or logarithmic regret bounds.** Several reviewers expressed concern that the inflated constant \(c_k\) (scaling as \(\sqrt{K}\) or \(\sqrt{k}\)) likely prevents any logarithmic (gap‑dependent) regret bound. While existing minimax‑optimal methods can often transition to logarithmic regret in favorable MDPs, EQO probably continues to suffer \(\sqrt{K}\) regret even in easy instances. This limits its practical adaptivity and is a significant gap compared to many prior algorithms that achieve instance‑dependent guarantees (e.g., [1]–[5] cited by Reviewer 1). The lack of discussion of instance‑dependent literature is a notable oversight and weakens the claim that existing methods are solely minimax‑focused.

2. **Non‑adaptivity to deterministic environments.** The bonus always has order \(H\sqrt{K}/N\), whereas variance‑aware (Bernstein) bonuses can shrink to \(H/N\) in deterministic environments. EQO is therefore far less efficient when the environment exhibits low or zero variance.

3. **Low‑order terms do not match the lower bound.** The algorithm is minimax optimal only in the dominant term; the lower-order term (e.g., \(S^2A\)) does not match the lower bound, while some prior work (e.g., [1] in Reviewer 4’s comment) achieves tight low-order bounds even for time‑homogeneous settings. It remains open whether the low-order term can be tightened to \(SA\).

4. **Insufficient experimental evaluation.**  
   - Only a single (though variable‑size) toy environment is tested. To convincingly claim superior empirical performance, additional environments (e.g., from the BRIDGE dataset) should be benchmarked.  
   - The experiments focus on the low‑\(K\) regime; it is unclear what happens in the large‑\(K\) regime where variance‑aware algorithms can transition to logarithmic regret while EQO may still suffer \(\sqrt{K}\) regret.  
   - No comparison is provided with randomized exploration methods such as PSRL and RLSVI (or the algorithm of Tiapkin et al., 2022). Such comparisons are necessary to validate the claimed practical benefit.  
   - Testing on "favorable" MDP instances (e.g., with large gaps that allow rapid elimination) would reveal whether EQO can compete with instance‑adaptive algorithms.

5. **Missing comparisons and acknowledgment of similar bonus forms.** The bonus term appears similar to expressions in earlier works (Eq. (4) in [2] and Theorem 2/3 in [3], as noted by Reviewer 4). The paper does not discuss these connections or clarify the novelty of the bonus structure beyond the variance‑handling interpretation.

6. **Presentation of the technical novelty needs improvement.** The main proof idea of quasi‑optimism—especially why the additional term scales as \(\lambda_k H\) rather than \(\lambda_k H^2\), and how the subtle use of variance/second‑moment bounds prevents error accumulation through the horizon—is not sufficiently highlighted in the main text. A more intuitive explanation of the role of Freedman’s inequality would help. (One reviewer would raise the score if this were improved.)

7. **Lack of adaptive stopping in the PAC guarantee (Theorem 4).** The algorithm requires a predefined number of episodes \(K_0\) and has no adaptive stopping rule, making the guarantee conservative compared to algorithms that stop early when the policy is already good.

8. **Motivation slightly overstated.** Tabular RL has almost no real‑world applications, so the importance of "practicality" for tabular algorithms should not be overemphasized. The theoretical contribution stands on its own.

9. **Several open questions/omissions that weaken the analysis:**  
   - Is it provably impossible to derive a \(\log K\) regret bound for EQO due to the inflated constant?  
   - Could the techniques be extended to time‑inhomogeneous MDPs? (The paper uses time‑homogeneous transitions; it is unclear if this is essential.)  
   - Can first‑order (in \(V^*\)) or second‑order (in \(\mathrm{Var}(V^*)\)) regret bounds be obtained?  
   - How does the bound change under sparse rewards (\(R_h^k \le 1\))?
