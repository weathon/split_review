## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Generalized Group Data Attribution (GGDA), a framework that attributes influence to groups of training points instead of individuals to improve computational efficiency. It subsumes existing methods like Influence Functions, TracIn, TRAK and reports 10x-50x speedups on MNIST, CIFAR-10, HELOC, TRAC. Reviewers were split: two rated 3 and two rated 5. Positive reviewers highlighted clear motivation, clean experimental presentation, and the innovative use of gradient-space K-Means clustering showing empirical advantages in downstream tasks. Critical reviewers questioned the novelty (group attribution reduces to summing individual attributions; the property function is already known) and the soundness of computational claims (Hessian construction still scales with n, clustering overhead, batch gradient equivalence). All reviewers noted the lack of large-scale validation and insufficient analysis of the K-Means grouping mechanism.

### Strengths
- **Clear writing and motivation**: The paper is well-written, defines concepts clearly, and addresses the important practical problem of computational cost in data attribution. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
- **General applicability**: GGDA is designed to work with multiple sample-based attribution methods (Influence Functions, TracIn, TRAK) and downstream tasks like dataset pruning and noisy label identification. (Reviewer 2)
- **Variety of data modalities**: Experiments span tabular (HELOC), image (MNIST, CIFAR-10), and text (TRAC) datasets. (Reviewer 2)
- **Innovative grouping strategy**: The use of K-Means clustering on gradient space as a grouping heuristic is novel and leads to improved attribution accuracy in pruning and label cleaning tasks. (Reviewer 3)
- **Clean experimental results and consistent problem formulation**. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The core idea of aggregating individual attributions into groups via summation is straightforward and follows from linearity of existing methods; calling groups “datapoints” trivially reduces sample complexity. The “property function” extension is already known as “target function” in prior work (e.g., in statistics and data attribution literature). (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Computational efficiency claims not fully justified**:  
  - The construction of the Hessian (for Influence Functions) or empirical FIM (for TRAK) still scales with the number of training points *n*, undermining the claimed speedup. The paper’s note that batched gradient computation is roughly equivalent to per-sample gradients also suggests limited efficiency gain without further optimization. (Reviewer 4)  
  - The batched approximation for the Hessian (as done for TRAK) lacks theoretical justification for its accuracy. (Reviewer 4)  
  - The overhead of clustering (e.g., K-Means) in high-dimensional gradient spaces scales with *n*, potentially negating the benefit. It is unclear if the 10x-50x speedups reported on small datasets will hold for larger sets. (Reviewers 2, 4)  
  - It is not clarified whether per-sample gradient computations for baselines are implemented in an optimized batched manner (e.g., via vmap) or naively, which is critical for a fair efficiency comparison. (Reviewer 2)
- **Absence of large-scale experiments**: All experiments are on small to medium datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, HELOC, TRAC); the method’s scalability claims are not validated on larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet). The cost and fidelity of the method for large *n* and high dimensionality remains untested. (Reviewers 2, 3)  
  - A specific request: experiments on billion-level data are needed to assess real-world applicability. (Reviewer 3)
- **Inadequate analysis of K-Means grouping**:  
  - Intuition for why gradient-space clustering works better than activation-space clustering is missing; gradients are sensitive to the loss and training process, but no justification is given. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The paper lacks theoretical analysis (sensitivity to initialization, convergence, distance metrics) and runtime details for K-Means execution within the overall pipeline. (Reviewer 3)  
  - The source of improvement in data selection fidelity when using group attribution is unclear—it may be due to the clustering heuristic rather than the group attribution framework itself. (Reviewer 2)
- **Missing baselines and experimental detail**:  
  - Tables 1 and 2 for dataset pruning do not include a “no removal” baseline, making it impossible to assess absolute performance. (Reviewer 2)  
  - The meaning of ± symbols in tables is unspecified: whether they indicate standard deviation across model initializations, data splits, or group assignments, and whether groups are recomputed each trial. (Reviewer 2)
- **Methodological issues**:  
  - The definition of TracIn in Section 4 deviates from the original paper (e.g., ignoring the scaling and only summing over batches containing the training sample, which is equivalent only for full-batch training). This should be clarified. (Reviewer 4)  
  - The group attribution methods (e.g., Gradient K-Means) require computing individual gradients, which reintroduces the computational cost that GGDA aims to avoid. (Reviewer 4)
- **Additional requests for deeper investigation**:  
  - A more thorough exploration of weighting schemes beyond uniform averaging (e.g., median, robust estimators) and the mechanism behind GGDA’s success in pruning (variance reduction vs. soft thresholding) would strengthen the analysis. (Reviewer 1)  
  - Further investigation into why group attribution improves data selection fidelity and whether this is due to variance reduction or the clustering heuristic. (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2)
