## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes XSAFETY, a multilingual safety benchmark covering 14 safety issues across 10 languages, built by translating and post-editing two existing datasets (Sun et al. 2023; Levy et al. 2022). Experiments on ChatGPT, PaLM2, LLaMA2, and Vicuna show that LLMs produce more unsafe responses for non-English queries, and the authors propose prompting methods (e.g., “think in English then answer”) that reduce unsafe responses by 42% for ChatGPT. Reviewers agree the problem is important but raise serious concerns about evaluation methodology, dataset construction, and validity. Three reviewers rated the paper 3 (fair, with major weaknesses); one reviewer rated it 5 and considered the contribution excellent but still noted significant weaknesses.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important and practical problem: LLMs are used in non-English languages but safety alignment often targets English, potentially creating safety disparities. (R2, R4)
- XSAFETY enables systematic multilingual safety evaluation across 10 languages and 14 safety issues, which is a valuable step beyond existing English/Chinese-only benchmarks. (R1, R3)
- Experimental results demonstrate that safety disparities across languages exist for all tested LLMs. (R1, R4)
- The proposed prompting strategies (e.g., explicit safety prompts, “think in English” prompting) are simple and can reduce unsafe responses for ChatGPT by 42% (as reported). (R3, R4; though note R1 finds the prompting method lacks novelty and shows only marginal improvements on models other than ChatGPT)
- The dataset was machine-translated but checked by professional translators; the automated evaluation (using ChatGPT + Google Translate) was also validated against a small set of human judgments. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- The benchmark’s language selection is limited to mostly high-resource languages; only Bengali and Hindi (both South Asian) are low-resource, which restricts representativeness for truly low-resource settings. (R1)
- The dataset is derived entirely via Google Translation from English and Chinese sources. This risks losing cultural specificity of harms in target languages. The removal of “China-specific” safety questions from Sun et al. (2023) is given as an example, but it remains unclear whether the benchmark is representative of harms in non-English cultures. (R2, R3)
- The validity of the underlying Sun et al. (2023) dataset is questionable: it is not peer-reviewed, and the paper provides examples in Chinese without gloss, making quality assessment impossible. (R3)
- It is unclear why a Chinese-derived dataset (Sun et al. 2023) is a good starting point for a multilingual benchmark, especially after culture-specific content is removed. One reviewer notes this reduces the problem to a “machine translation” issue rather than a true safety evaluation across cultures. (R3)
- Manual evaluation is extremely limited: only 50 instances from two scenarios (Crimes and Illegal Activities, Goal Hijacking), reporting 94% accuracy. The language(s) of these 50 instances are not specified. This small sample does not support claims about multilingual performance generally. (R2, R3)
- The annotation process lacks standard validation steps: no cross-validation, no inter-annotator agreement metrics are reported. (R1)
- Using ChatGPT both as the evaluated model and as the evaluator (via self-evaluation) undermines reliability. This is a serious concern given the importance of safety evaluation. (R1, R3)
- The presented prompting methods lack novelty and, per a footnote on page 8, show only “marginal improvements” on models other than ChatGPT. Those other models (LLaMA2, Vicuna) are actually less safe and would benefit more from effective prompting. (R1)
- The analysis of language proportions in pretraining data (Table 4) is irrelevant and potentially misleading: it uses GPT-2 data for ChatGPT and PaLM data for PaLM2, not the actual training distributions of the tested models. (R3, R4)
- The paragraph “The Resulting XSAFETY Benchmark” is unclear. The claim that consistent translation of Chinese phrases leads to similar data distributions across languages is unsupported; the example of “masu” and “sure” (grammatical particles, not translatable) undermines the argument. The authors could have instead augmented the source corpus with multiple translations per sample to improve robustness. (R4)
- No qualitative examples or actual model responses are provided to allow readers to verify the benchmark’s quality or the findings. (R2, R4)
- The paper does not mention whether the dataset or code will be released, nor does it discuss the licenses of the original datasets (Sun et al., Levy et al.) and whether translation-based reuse is permitted. (R2, R4)
- A key question from two reviewers: Can LLMs generate coherent responses in very low-resource languages (e.g., Hindi, Bengali) in the first place? If not, the safety evaluation may conflate safety with basic language capability. (R1, R3)
- Several questions from reviewers overlap with criticisms: 
  - Why does the XLing1 prompt *increase* the unsafe ratio for Hindi? (R4)
  - Are the proposed safety prompts provided in English or translated? (R4)
  - How is the “think in English then answer” prompt implemented? Does the model generate an English response first (like chain-of-thought) or is it expected to internally translate? (R4)
  - The paper mentions “XLingPrompt3” in the caption of Table 3 but never introduces it. (R1)
- A minority reviewer (score 5) notes the work’s contribution is strong but agrees with several of the above weaknesses, especially the unclear benchmark presentation and missing data/limitations discussion. The reviewer also finds the pretraining-data analysis unnecessary. (R4)
