## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes DetEmbedMetrics, a deterministic embedding-based metric for textual data quality via similarity and diversity measurement. It fine-tunes a language model to generate embeddings aligned with deterministic functions and outputs probability vectors over three similarity/diversity classes. Reviewers split: two gave score 3, two gave 1. There is agreement that the problem is important. However, most reviewers found significant issues in clarity, novelty, experimental validation, and practical relevance. Minority reviews (R1, R4) acknowledge the method’s clear presentation and computational tractability, while majority reviews (R2, R3) find the writing vague and the approach lacking technical depth and empirical support.

### Strengths

1. The problem of measuring data quality in language model training is important and timely, with significant technical and social impact.
2. The method presents a deterministic metric for similarity and diversity, and the overall approach is clearly presented and computationally more tractable than existing alternatives (R4).
3. The paper experiments with multiple similarity and diversity metrics, and provides a thorough overview of prior work (R2).
4. The introduction offers a helpful starting point for readers to understand the state of the field.

### Weaknesses

- **Clarity and presentation:** The paper is difficult to follow. Methodology details are missing: Section 3.2 and Figure 1 do not specify the formal function type; the neural network in Figure 1 is undescribed. Notation is confusing (lines 140–144 define D_i inconsistently; D_0, D_1, D_2 definitions are unclear). Typos include “diveristy” (line 184) and incorrect citation style (citep/citet, line 44). A poster-child example is needed to clarify the problem.
- **Novelty and technical depth:** The method essentially boils down to training a classifier for pairwise data classes; key choices (distance function, loss function) are not examined experimentally. The deterministic framing is not motivated and its empirical benefits are not shown—the shift from a score on ℝ to a probability vector on Δ³ actually adds complexity. No calibration study supports interpreting class scores as probabilities. The proposed methodology has no technical depth (R3).
- **Experiments are insufficient:** All results are on synthetic datasets; no validation on real-world benchmarks (e.g., GLUE/QQP for similarity, real diversity datasets). Only one baseline (all‑mpnet‑base‑v2) is compared on a single synthetic dataset; finetuning improvement is expected and not novel. The baseline distance metric (Manhattan for bge‑large‑en‑v1.5) is not canonical (dot product) and may alter results; robustness study is only provided for the proposed method, not the baseline. No ablation experiments isolating contributions. No experiments demonstrating whether the method actually aids data selection or improves language model training performance. Hyper‑parameter details are missing (not even in appendix). Why only 3 similarity classes instead of a continuous scale like STS-B? The accuracy of synthetic test samples is not reported.
- **Missing comparisons and context:** The paper does not compare with relevant prior work on data quality control in LLMs (e.g., Ge et al. 2024, Du et al. 2023 cited by R1). There is no comparison of public data collections to explain why some models perform better (R3).
- **Terminology and scope:** The paper uses “data valuation” but does not compute dollar values for datasets; the connection between data quality, similarity/diversity, embeddings, and valuation is loosely argued. The approach only considers similarity and diversity as definitions of dataset quality, ignoring other notions. No human evaluation of interpretability is provided.
- **Reproducibility:** The function form in Section 3.2 is unspecified, and Figure 1’s neural network details are absent, reducing reproducibility (R2).
