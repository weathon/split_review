## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GuideEdit, a face video editing framework using diffusion models. It leverages the assumed local linearity of latent variables in the bottleneck layer of a pretrained U-Net to extract a latent basis. By comparing the basis of the original video with that of a manipulated video, the method computes a manipulation degree that guides editing at each denoising step. The approach aims to enhance temporal consistency, identity preservation, and fine-grained control. The denoiser network does not require training. The paper evaluates on real-world videos and compares with StyleGAN-based methods.

### Strengths
- The method leverages the natural linearity of diffusion model bottlenecks to perform attribute-specific edits, and the extracted basis can focus on specific facial attributes.
- The approach is conceptually interesting for exploring decoupling of latent features in Stable Diffusion for editing, and the method is easy to follow.
- The paper provides multiple video editing examples across different identities and ages, and shows comparisons with StyleGAN-based methods, which have smooth latent spaces.
- GuideEdit outperforms previous baselines in quantitative evaluations (tables, ablation studies) and achieves superior visual quality in provided figures; ablation experiments demonstrate the contribution of each component.
- The paper includes analytical experiments (e.g., similarity between latent basis of original and manipulated video at different time steps).
- The denoiser network does not require training for editing, which is a practical advantage.
- The approach is based on conceptual feature engineering and has potential applicability to diffusion-based multi-modality.

### Weaknesses
- **Video quality and artifacts**: The produced videos have noticeable low quality with unnatural artifacts, particularly related to lips (e.g., Figure 1) and eyeglasses (supplementary). These artifacts affect facial expressions and identity, raising doubts about the assumption that the inherent linearity of latent variables in a pretrained U-Net is suitable for face video editing. The reviewer also questioned how to avoid such artifacts.
- **Inter-frame instability and temporal consistency**: The videos exhibit significant inter-frame instability. The method uses image-based SD inversion without any additional algorithms to address temporal consistency in face video editing. One reviewer asked what mechanisms ensure that desired edits (e.g., eyeglasses style and appearance) remain consistent across frames.
- **Limited examples and scope of edits**: The provided examples are few, both in the main paper and supplementary material. The paper only shows specific edits with minor modifications, and it is unclear how well the method works on diverse or larger-scale edits (e.g., hairstyle, gender changes). There are no pose variations among identities. One reviewer requested more video results to assess consistency across various scenarios.
- **Unclear attribute control**: The task is described as “face video editing with precise control,” but it is not specified which attributes can be controlled and to what extent. One reviewer asked for clarification on the types of features or features that can be adjusted and the method’s capabilities and limitations for detailed adjustments.
- **Theoretical foundation and novelty**: The paper’s central concept (local linearity of the latent variable) relies almost entirely on citations to other studies (e.g., “Understanding the Latent Space of Diffusion Models through the Lens of Riemannian Geometry,” NeurIPS 2023) without providing a thorough explanation, which reduces persuasive power. Moreover, the latent basis extraction method in Section 4.2 appears very similar to that described in the cited NeurIPS 2023 paper; the paper does not clearly articulate the novel aspects.
- **Inconsistency between theory and implementation**: Proximal guidance is claimed to use feature basis similarity between the manipulated and original video, but the actual implementation only uses it to calculate a binary mask. This discrepancy undermines the preceding theoretical analysis.
- **Poor presentation and mathematical clarity**: The method description is overly abstract. Mathematical notation lacks foundational clarity: e.g., in Section 4.1, what are \(X\) and \(X^c_0\)? Numerous subscripts and superscripts are not adequately explained. The ‘:’ notation mixes mappings with function definitions; mappings are incorrectly defined (e.g., \(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{H}\) are not spaces); \(T_C\) is defined as a space but inconsistently mixed with set notation.
- **Slow inference speed**: The DDIM sampler uses 1000 steps, and both the manipulated video and the original video require inversion, severely slowing inference. One reviewer asked about the runtime for a given video duration and the difference between DDIM and DDPM.
- **Questionable temporal stability metrics**: In Table 2, most values for TG-ID and TL-ID are greater than 0.99, raising questions about the validity of these metrics for assessing temporal stability.
- **Ablation results contradict claims**: Multiple indicators in the ablation experiments show that the method performs better in terms of image quality when the LBE or PG module is not included, suggesting that these components may not improve editing quality.
- **Missing comparisons with inpainting baselines**: One reviewer asked why a two-stage baseline using inpainting (e.g., generate masks with SAM then apply AVID or Videoshop) could not address the task, and requested elaboration on the differences.
- **Failure cases and extreme conditions**: The paper does not discuss failure cases. One reviewer asked for results on reconstructed videos after manipulation and the method’s performance under difficult conditions such as extreme poses and lighting variations.
