## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper reformulates knowledge editing as a safety threat called Editing Attack, focusing on Misinformation Injection and Bias Injection. It constructs the EditAttack dataset and implements attacks via ROME, fine-tuning, and in-context editing on LLMs (Llama3-8b, Mistral-v0.2-7b). Key findings: commonsense misinformation is more easily injected than long-tail misinformation; bias injection can cause a general bias increase in outputs; the attacks are stealthy (little impact on general knowledge/reasoning); and defending against them is hard. The paper highlights misuse risks of knowledge editing.

### Strengths
- The problem is important and timely as LLM reliance grows (R1, R5).
- The EditAttack dataset is a valuable resource for future research on these attacks and defenses (R1, R2, R4).
- Thorough investigation providing comprehensive analysis of misinformation and bias injection risks (R2, R4, R5).
- Experiments are well-designed with sound metrics that support the main claims (R4, R5).
- The attack is shown to be both effective and stealthy, indicating practical potential (R4). (Minority positive: R4 emphasizes stealthiness, though R3 questions the stealthiness experimental setup – see Weaknesses.)
- Implementation code is provided (R3).
- Paper is well-written with clear takeaways (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaim of novelty and insufficient differentiation from prior work.** The paper claims to “reformulate knowledge editing as a new type of threat”, but previous surveys already highlighted that knowledge editing can be misused for misinformation (R1 cites arXiv:2310.16218). Prior work tested simulated editing attacks (arXiv:2407.07791) and showed bias introduction via fine-tuning generalizes (arXiv:2312.14302, arXiv:2408.02946). Backdoors are another known editing threat (arXiv:2403.13355). Malicious fine-tuning has been reported to compromise safety (R5 cites “Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety”). The ICL finding overlaps with many-shot jailbreaking (R5). The paper does not clearly distinguish Editing Attack from existing attack vectors (jailbreaking, fine-tuning attacks, etc.) (R4, R5).
- **Limited experimental scope.** Only smaller open-source models (Llama3-8b, Mistral-v0.2-7b) are tested; larger models (e.g., Gemini, GPT-4o) may be more resistant (R2, R4). No experiments on black-box or closed-source models, limiting generalizability and practical relevance (R2, R3, R4, R5). The paper does not test whether the same attacks work on more advanced or larger LLMs (R2).
- **Practicality concerns.** The assumption that all injected edits are malicious is unrealistic; mixing benign edits would be more insightful (R2). Users typically do not deploy personally trained LLMs – widely used models are black boxes, making direct editing attacks infeasible (R3). Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) could correct erroneous outputs and render editing attacks ineffective (R3). The threat model (attacker, victim, attacker capabilities) is insufficiently discussed (R4). How attackers would use in-context editing in practice is unclear (R4 question). For closed-source models, no attack method is provided (R5).
- **Experimental design issues.** Table 1 reports final numbers and “performance increase”, but Table 2 adds original numbers inconsistently, with alignment issues (R1). There is no analysis of the distribution of commonsense vs long-tail misinformation or how different degrees of deviation from common knowledge affect attack effectiveness (R3). The reason why bias editing attacks affect overall fairness (Finding 2) beyond the targeted attribute is not explained (R3). The stealthiness experiment uses only a small number of edits; it is natural that this does not affect general knowledge; a more reasonable setup varies the proportion to cover general knowledge (R3). Only ROME is used among localized editing methods; more methods should be tested (R5). It is unclear whether all misinformation can be formulated into ROME’s structured format (R5). The scoring metric is not explained (e.g., what calculates the scores – an LLM judge or something else?) (R1 question).
- **Stealthiness evaluation.** While R4 considers stealthiness a strength, R3 argues that with a small number of edits the lack of impact on general knowledge is expected, and the experimental design should adjust the proportion of edits to better assess stealthiness. (Minority contrast: only R4 views this aspect positively; other reviewers find the setup insufficient.)
- **Writing and presentation.** Overuse of bold, italics, and other formatting makes the paper harder to read (R1). Some redundancy (e.g., lines 461.5–465 repeat earlier examples) (R1). The paper switches between “misinformation” and “disinformation” inconsistently – if malicious intent is assumed, “disinformation” may be more appropriate (R1 question).
