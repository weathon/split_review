## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces networked communication to mean-field games (MFGs), focusing on oracle-free settings where N decentralised agents learn along a single, non-episodic run. The authors prove that the proposed architecture has sample guarantees bounded between those of centralised and independent learning, characterise the difference in terms of network structure and communication rounds, and provide a policy-update stability guarantee. Since the theoretical parameters are not observed in practice, the paper also presents practical enhancements (including a replay buffer and modifications that break the theoretical guarantees) and demonstrates empirical convergence acceleration and robustness to learning failures and changes in population size on two grid-world tasks (cluster and agree on a single target). The work is positioned as the first empirical demonstration of several theoretical algorithms after removing their restrictive assumptions.

### Strengths

- Introduces a practical communication network to accelerate independent learning without relying on a central learner.  
- Using a communication network to interpolate between centralised and independent learning is an interesting concept.  
- Experiments justify the results; the networked communication method is shown to be robust and high-performing, combining advantages of centralised and decentralised frameworks.  
- Theoretical analysis is comprehensive, intuitive, and aligns with intuition: the networked paradigm lies conceptually between centralised and decentralised approaches in terms of sample efficiency and convergence speed.  
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (reviewers 3 and 4 explicitly note this).  
- Novelly fuses analysis of networked learning with the more realistic recent independent-learning concept in MFGs.  
- The theoretical results from analysing the number of independently learned policies are elegant, interesting, and significant, implying the usefulness of communication in learning for MFGs.  
- The discussion of related work and placement of contribution is extensive and good.  
- The main theoretical result (proving interpolation) is particularly interesting and a nice contribution.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited experiments and missing baselines.** Experiments are restricted to two grid-world tasks (cluster and agree on a single target); there is no direct comparison with existing MFG algorithms in the experiments, making it difficult to assess relative performance. The exact definitions of the tasks remain somewhat unclear, and no example involves mean-field interaction in the state dynamics.  
- **Theoretical contribution is not clearly shown to yield practical superiority.** The theoretical results do not convincingly demonstrate superiority over independent learning. Theorem 3 relies on restrictive assumptions (τₖ → 0), and the gap (1/d_𝒢)^C is small; the bound is not rigorous because the number of policies does not decrease uniformly in most graph structures, so an accurate estimation of Δ_{k,C} might be better. Reviewers 1 and 5 note that the theoretical contribution is not well motivated and that the practical modifications (which break the theoretical guarantees) are central to the empirical success, yet the paper does not include a figure comparing the theoretically-sound version with the “hacked” version.  
- **Unclear theoretical definitions and missing justifications.** Several quantities are not properly defined in the main text: operator Γ_q (first used line 233), σⁱₖ (only deducible from its use; line 351 says it can be chosen at random, which adds confusion), ε in Theorem 2, graph diameter, Δ_{k,C}, and “exploitability” and “policy divergence.” The restriction to stationary MFGs is substantial and not justified; the conditions for a unique MFG-NE in Theorem 1 are not given. The claim that (1/d_𝒢)^C = 0 on line 376 is not arithmetically possible; the intended term disappearance should be clarified. The meaning of the ≈ symbols in line 373 is imprecise.  
- **Ablation and empirical rigor missing.** The practical enhancements introduced on page 10 (transition buffer, modifications that break theoretical guarantees) are not evaluated with ablation studies. The number of independent trials is unclear, confidence intervals often overlap, and the statement “our networked architecture significantly outperforms…” is not statistically supported. Figures 1–3 are too small, text is unreadable, and the shaded region is not explained.  
- **Communication conceptualisation differs from typical MARL communication.** The paper treats communication as propagation of higher-estimated Q‑value policies rather than the usual learnable vectors (e.g., parameters of policies/value functions). This difference is not adequately addressed, and the setting (sharing policies in a competitive MFG) is not justified.  
- **Robustness analysis is limited.** Robustness experiments are restricted to failures in the communication network and changes in population size; in Fig. 3 all curves experience a similar relative shock, so the benefit over independent learning is unclear. Other types of robustness (e.g., against self-interested agents manipulating policies, model errors, heterogeneity) are not considered.  
- **Presentation and writing issues** (noted by reviewers 2 and 5): Algorithm 1 should be moved earlier on the page; the prose description in Section 3.2 is difficult to follow and would benefit from formal definitions with equation numbers and paragraph headers; the algorithm block lacks comments; the transition buffer usage is not described precisely in the final subsection of Section 3; limitations and future work are relegated to the appendix.  
- **Unresolved questions and missing analyses** that overlap with criticisms:  
  - Why does the gap between partial communication and independent learning become smaller when C grows (Theorem 3), contrary to intuition that more communication should stabilize learning?  
  - When d_𝒢 = 1, why is the gap between partial and centralised 1, and between partial and independent 0?  
  - How does the method handle changes in population size exactly?  
  - Can the authors provide any theoretical analysis supporting the intuition in the paragraph starting line 418, or a remark explaining why such analysis is difficult?  
  - Why does the green (networked) curve appear to perform better in Fig. 2 than in Fig. 1, even though the scenario is supposedly harder?  
  - The statement “independent learners cannot do this and hardly appear to learn at all” is confusing because subfigures are unlabeled.  
  - Further discussion is needed on the non-uniqueness issue raised in line 535 and how coordination arises mathematically as λ → 0.  
  - How are the RL states specifically configured in the experiments?  
  - The approximation of the decrease in divergence in Appendix B.7 should be justified.  
  - The claim on line 538 relies on a definition only given in the appendices.
