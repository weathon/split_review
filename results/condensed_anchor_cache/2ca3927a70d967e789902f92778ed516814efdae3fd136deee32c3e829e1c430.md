## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Sparse coVariance Neural Networks (S-VNNs), which sparsify the sample covariance matrix before feeding it into a Covariance Neural Network (VNN). The authors study hard and soft thresholding when the true covariance is assumed sparse, and stochastic sparsification (based on absolute covariance values ACV or ranked covariance values RCV) when the covariance is dense. The paper provides theoretical stability analysis and experiments on brain data and human action recognition, comparing against dense VNNs and sparse PCA. The reviewers find the idea sound and the theoretical analysis a useful justification, but they raise serious concerns about clarity, novelty, rigor of the theoretical claims, missing comparisons, and the handling of covariance estimation issues such as scale invariance and PSD guarantees. Reviewer scores are 1, 3, 3, 5, indicating strong disagreement on overall quality.

### Strengths

- The idea of sparsifying the covariance matrix before feeding it into a VNN is sound and practically motivated (spurious correlations, computational cost).
- The paper provides theoretical guarantees (closeness of hidden representations/predictions when using approximate vs. true covariance) that improve at rate \(\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})\) with sample size, based on a Lipschitz assumption.
- Experimental results validate predictive performance, stability, and efficiency on both synthetic and real datasets (brain data, human action recognition), and experiments generally agree with the theory.
- A large amount of relevant literature is cited.
- The paper is well-written and the motivation is clear (according to Reviewers 3 and 4; Reviewer 1 strongly disagrees, see Weaknesses).
- The theoretical analysis of stability is present and is a necessary justification for the random sparsification strategies.

### Weaknesses

- **Presentation clarity**: Reviewer 1 finds the paper very hard to follow. Several variables are not defined (e.g., \(V\), \(u\), \(u_g\), \(h_{klfg}\)), and concepts such as “covariance filters” and the relationship between \(u\) and \(u_g\) in Eq. (1) must be guessed. The paper needs major rewriting before publication. However, Reviewer 3 finds the presentation clear; this is a notable disagreement.
- **Novelty is limited**: Multiple reviewers note that the work incrementally builds upon covariance neural networks (VNNs) and on the existing framework by Sihag et al. (2022). The sparsification strategies resemble standard neural network techniques (dropout, pruning) and post-hoc thresholding rather than a fundamentally new approach to covariance estimation or VNN training. The core contribution appears to be computational speedup from increasing zero entries, not a novel method.
- **Theoretical concerns**:
  - The claim about hard/soft thresholding improving stability is not definitively proven. The bound may be tighter for sparse covariances but that does not guarantee improved stability in practice (Reviewer 2).
  - There is a major concern about the constant hidden inside the \(\mathcal{O}\) in Theorem 1 possibly depending on the covariance filter parameters \(\mathcal{H}\), similar to how PCA’s instability arises from small eigenvalue gaps. The paper does not clarify this, and the statement that “VNNs do not suffer from this” is not justified. This issue may also affect Theorem 2, Proposition 1, etc.
  - Theorem 1 explicitly writes a probability, but later results only say “with high probability” without defining what that means.
  - The paper does not ensure that soft-thresholded matrices (Definition 3) or dropout matrices (Definition 4) remain positive semidefinite (PSD). How is PSD guaranteed?
  - The theoretical results assume Gaussian data in Theorem 1, but it is unclear whether later theorems also require a Gaussian assumption. Heavy-tailed distributions without finite covariance are not addressed, and conditions on the distribution of \(x\) are absent.
  - The phrasing in Theorem 1 (“\(x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, C)\) such that \(\|x\| \leq 1\)”) is ambiguous: it reads as a conditional distribution, but should be clarified.
- **Scalability of thresholding**: Applying a single threshold across all covariance entries (Definition 2) is problematic because covariance magnitudes depend on feature variances. Features with large variance will dominate, possibly removing meaningful correlations or retaining spurious ones. The paper does not explain how the data is preprocessed to address this issue (Reviewer 3).
- **Sparsity interpretation**: Theorem 2 bounds the number of non-zero entries per row but does not directly address whether thresholding correctly separates true non-zero covariances from noise (zero covariances). It is unclear whether the method effectively denoises the data (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing comparisons**: The empirical evaluation should include methods that obtain a sparse covariance matrix via L1 regularization or similar techniques during covariance estimation (e.g., graphical lasso), not only post-hoc thresholding and robust PCA. This would strengthen the claim of improved estimation (Reviewer 4).
- **Minor issues**: 
  - Notation: using \(t\) for sample size is unusual (Reviewer 3).
  - Lemma 1: if not an original result, a citation is needed (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation (1): should \(F_{in}=F_{out}\)? (Reviewer 1)
  - Line 167/168 has incorrect grammar (Reviewer 2).
  - The discussion of PCA’s instability (line 39/40) should mention robust variants (exponential family PCA, kernel PCA) that may overcome these issues (Reviewer 2).
