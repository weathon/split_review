## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Hessian-Aware Training, which uses Hessian trace as a regularization term to flatten the loss landscape and improve model resilience to bitwise parameter errors for in-memory computing (IMC). Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet with BaseNet, LeNet, ResNet18, and ResNet50 report a 50% reduction in high-impact bit flips and benefits for pruning and quantization. One reviewer (R3, rating 5) finds the method novel and effective, while the other three reviewers (R1, R2, R4, all rating 3) raise serious concerns about novelty, experimental methodology, and framing.

### Strengths
- Makes the NN parameter space smooth and robust to various perturbations (bit flips, quantization, pruning). (R1)
- Complete experimental evaluation from accuracy to loss landscape visualization. (R2)
- Novel and effective method using Hessian trace as an optimization objective, unique compared to typical hardware/system-level solutions. (R3)
- Extensive evaluation across multiple models and datasets with improved resilience and versatility for compression (quantization, pruning). (R3)
- Potential to complement existing hardware/software resilience mechanisms, broadening applicability. (R3)
- Paper is well structured and easy to follow. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The use of Hessian trace for robustness and smoothness is widely explored in the AI/ML community and prior work (e.g., HERO: Hessian-Enhanced Robust Optimization); the paper does not adequately differentiate from existing robustness-aware optimization and quantization methods. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Computational cost and scalability**: Hutchinson’s method for approximating the Hessian trace is not novel and is more costly than alternatives (R1). The paper lacks analysis of how computational cost scales with model size (millions/billions of parameters) and does not justify the choice of top-p eigenvalues or its impact on the trade-off between cost and resilience. (R3)
- **Limited defense against malicious attacks**: The method only slightly reduces the number of sensitive weights and cannot defend against targeted bit-flip attacks that exploit remaining vulnerable bits. (R1)
- **Misleading IMC framing**: The paper is introduced as addressing IMC noise, but the validation methodology is adapted from adversarial attack literature (paper [1]) and applies bit-error patterns (uniform, exponent-only, MSB-only) that do not match real IMC hardware noise characteristics. No IMC-related experiments are presented beyond title/abstract/intro. (R1, R4; R4 recommends reframing as adversarial defense or adding IMC experiments.)
- **Inconsistent error injection across datasets**: MNIST uses all-bit flips, CIFAR-10 uses only exponent bits, ImageNet flips only the most significant exponent bit. This arbitrary choice is not justified and prevents fair comparison or generalization. (R2, R4)
- **Missing accuracy-under-attack results**: Results are vague; Table 3 shows baseline sometimes has better accuracy. The paper does not report model accuracy under bit-flip attacks or show the trend as error ratio increases. (R2)
- **Uneven effectiveness across layer types**: The method works better on fully connected layers than convolutional layers (e.g., ResNet with residual connections). No explanation is given for this discrepancy, nor analysis of how kernel size, stride, or padding affect results. (R3)
- **Tuning complexity**: Adding a Hessian-trace regularization term introduces hyperparameter tuning; no systematic study of the regularization coefficient’s sensitivity or guidelines for varying model scales is provided. (R3)
- **Unexplained distribution plots**: Figures 1 and 2 show a lower integral under the proposed method’s curve compared to the baseline. It is unclear whether fewer flip combinations were tested or if negative RAD (performance improvement) occurred and was excluded; the paper does not clarify. (R4)
- **Limited pruning experiments**: The pruning resilience demonstration is limited to a single model/task combination, making it impossible to assess generalizability. (R4)
- **Quantization results unimpressive**: Performance with Hessian regularization is similar to baseline down to 4-bit weights (a solved problem for small models); 2-bit still shows massive degradation, making models practically unusable. (R4)
