## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SincKAN, replacing splines in Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks with sinc interpolation, with theoretical foundations and practical modifications (multiple step sizes, coordinate transformation, exponential decay enforcement). Reviewers find the idea novel and the derivation rigorous (R2, R4), and note the smart choice of boundary layer examples to demonstrate performance on singular problems (R2). However, concerns dominate: limited contribution and inconsistent performance across benchmarks (R1, R2), small and artificial test problems (R3, R4), insufficient analysis of PINN-specific challenges (R2, R4), and questionable practical advantages over simpler baselines (R1, R3). Reviewer 3 (score 3) considers the PDE experiments unsuitable, while Reviewer 4 (score 6) values the principled approach but still questions real-world relevance.

### Strengths
- Novel application of sinc function to replace splines in KAN architectures (R3: "Applying sinc to replace splines in KAN is a novel approach"; R1, R4 similarly).
- Authors do not straightforwardly substitute but properly consider principled application: optimal step sizes via multiple values, coordinate transformation to map finite to infinite domain, and enforcing exponential decay via linear subtraction (R2: rigorous derivation of Eq14; R4: "properly consider its principled application").
- Smart selection of boundary layer problem (large epsilon, thin layer) to highlight benefits on singularities and PDEs with steep gradients (R2: "smart choice and shows good performance of sincKAN in the presence of singularities").
- Provides independent comparisons between KAN variants and MLPs, useful for the community assessing KANs' practical value (R4).
- Addresses the practical challenge of selecting step size by using multiple h values, removing the need for precise knowledge of the function (R3: addresses practical challenges).

### Weaknesses
- Performance is not consistently superior: Table 2 shows sincKAN is not the best option in several cases; it is unclear what advantage it offers over standard MLP or modified MLP for physics-informed loss when it does not outperform across all datasets (R1: "as shown in Table 2, it's unclear if sincKAN demonstrates convincingly better performance"; R2: "in Table 2, sincKAN is not the best option in some cases").
- The paper's claimed strengths in singularity handling are only demonstrated on boundary layer problems; the title and focus could be better aligned if that is the primary strength (R1). Missing tests on other singularity types, higher-dimensional problems, or larger epsilon (e.g., 10⁸) (R1: "what would happen if epsilon increased to 10⁸?"; "could other problems with singularities be tested?").
- The PDE and function approximation experiments are small-scale and artificial, and these problems can be solved using any numerical PDE solver; PINNs/PIKANs provide no practical advantage in such settings, so real-world relevance is not shown (R3: "chosen problems are extremely small and these problems can be solved using any numerical PDE solver"; R4: "manufactured solutions are arbitrary, 1D, and not relevant to real-world applications").
- Insufficient analysis of PINN-specific pathologies: the paper discusses spectral bias but does not address other known issues such as causality violation in chaotic PDEs, loss plateauing, or gradient pathologies (R2: "not much discussion on other issues for PINNs e.g. issue for causality... and many other issues like plateauing loss"). Suggestion to test on chaotic PDEs and reference works by Wang et al. (2021a, 2022a) and Rathore et al. (R2). More scrutiny of physics-informed losses is needed, including plots of solution errors and contributions from boundary conditions vs. PDE residual (R4: "Some plots of solutions and errors across the poorer performing methods might help understand why they are performing badly").
- The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1,2) have limited practical impact because sincKAN uses a range of step sizes instead of the optimal value; it is unclear what real advantage the theorems provide (R2: "the theorems provided do not really guarantee the performance (specially considering... authors use a range of step sizes instead of the optimal value)"; also R2 questions "the real advantages of theorems 1 and 2"). Moreover, coordinate transformation theory is not formally proven, and the requirement that functions be in the Hardy space (implying differentiability) is not discussed (R2: "advise authors formally add another theorem to Coordinate transformation section... If a function needs to be Hardy, does that mean the guarantees are only valid for differentiable functions f?").
- It is unclear whether sincKAN retains advantages of vanilla KAN (e.g., grid extension and update tricks) and, conversely, whether a direct Sinc interpolator could be used instead of sincKAN for all function approximation tasks, which would undermine the architecture's value (R2: "in 'vanilla' KANs paper there are arguments on the advantage of using B-splines... it's hard to say sincKAN still has all this merits"; R4: "Can't one use a Sinc interpolator directly for all the function approximation tasks?").
- The choice of MLP architectures in experiments is not justified; over-parameterization may cause higher final loss on these small problems, biasing comparisons in favor of sincKAN (R3: "For MLP architectures, how were the architectures chosen? Since the problems are small and toy-ish, over-parameterization may cause a higher final loss").
- Equation (6) is unclear: f(jh) is never defined, and the formulation implies needing precise knowledge of f at those points for interpolation, which is not practical (R1: "What exactly is f(jh) in equation (6)? Is it simply the value of f at jh? ... do we need to know those values precisely to approximate f(x) with this formulation?"). Basic explanation of KAN mechanism is missing for unfamiliar readers (R1).
