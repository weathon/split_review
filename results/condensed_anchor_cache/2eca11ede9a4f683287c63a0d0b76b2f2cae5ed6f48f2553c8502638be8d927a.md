## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies vulnerabilities in current data contamination detection methods for LLMs. It categorizes model providers and detection methods, then proposes Evasive Augmentation Learning (EAL)—a rephrasing-based contamination technique designed to inflate benchmark performance while evading detection. Experiments across several models and benchmarks show that rephrasing can boost scores and bypass existing detectors.

### Strengths
- The analysis and categorization of existing contamination detection methods is reasonable (Reviewer 1). The taxonomy covers metadata-, reference model-, semantic-preserving transformation-, and accessibility-based methods (Reviewer 4).
- The paper introduces a novel categorization of model providers based on contamination practices, emphasizing the possibility of deliberate ‘evasively malicious’ contamination (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The selection of base models and benchmarks is representative and comprehensive (Reviewer 1).
- The topic is highly relevant to current challenges in LLM evaluation (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed EAL method is simple and easy to understand (Reviewer 3; note disagreement from Reviewers 1 and 4 on clarity).
- Reviewer 2 found the paper well-organized with accessible key concepts (contrasting with Reviewer 1’s criticism of poor structure and Reviewer 4’s criticism of disorganization).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of detail on the proposed attack (EAL).** While EAL is described as rephrasing training data, the paper provides no formulation, algorithm, figure, or detailed description of how rephrased data is generated (e.g., the prompts or techniques used) and how contamination is inserted (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Section 5.1 is only a short paragraph (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear threat model and settings.** The paper does not define the attacker’s knowledge, capabilities, access to training data (direct modification vs. injection), scale of contamination (percentage of training data), or the adversarial objective (only increasing benchmark performance?). The role of the defender/detector and its possible actions is also unspecified (Reviewer 4). The distinction between “openly malicious” and “evasively malicious” is problematic because the openly malicious scenario has no realistic usage (Reviewer 3).
- **Poor presentation, structure, and clarity.** The paper lacks a related work section (Reviewer 4). Sections 4 and 5 contain repetition and unnecessary content (Reviewer 3). After experiment results, Section 7 reads like a related work discussion, breaking coherence (Reviewer 1). Many sentences appear GPT-generated and remain logically inconsistent (Reviewer 4). Terminology is unnecessarily complicated (e.g., “contamination” instead of “poisoning,” “actor” for “attacker,” “de-contamination” for “mitigation,” “verbatim” for “word-wise,” “sample-level” for “sentence/token-level”) (Reviewer 4). Definition 3 is unclear (Reviewer 3); Definition 4 repeats Definitions 1 and 2; Definition 5 should be linked to a rigorous formulation (Reviewer 4).
- **Experimental shortcomings.** 
  - Experiments focus only on evaluating existing detectors; no experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed EAL method or its evasiveness (Reviewer 1). The paper mentions several detection strategies but does not report corresponding experiments for them (Reviewer 4). No baseline attacks are compared (Reviewer 4).
  - The paper does not clarify how evasiveness is measured—presumably by lower TPR of detection methods? TPR and FPR are not introduced until Figure 1, but should be earlier (Reviewer 4).
  - There is no discussion of the trade-off between attack effectiveness (performance gain) and evasiveness (detection avoidance) (Reviewer 4).
  - The sample size of the contaminated set is limited, increasing variance; Reviewer 2 asks whether increasing that sample size is feasible to improve stability.
  - Reviewer 2 also asks for a per-benchmark breakdown of performance consistency.
- **Missing defense and applicability analysis.** The paper does not explore potential defenses against EAL, leaving the work incomplete (Reviewer 2). The proposed attack may not work in open, real-time updating environments because updating baseline data is often impractical (Reviewer 3).
- **Overclaimed contributions.** The paper claims to “define four (de-)contamination settings,” but these are merely summarized, not defined de novo (Reviewer 4). The rephrasing idea lacks strong intuition and novelty; it is a simple extrapolation (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 notes that the contribution is overstated because real-world feasibility is ignored.
- **Missing evaluation for certain detection methods.** Reviewer 3 asks whether EAL circumvents all methods listed in the taxonomy used, especially those that do not require pre-training data. This question overlaps with missing experiments for reported detection strategies.
