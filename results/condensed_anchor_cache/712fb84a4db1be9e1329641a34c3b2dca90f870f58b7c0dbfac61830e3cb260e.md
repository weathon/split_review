## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Medical Vision Generalist (MVG), a foundation model that unifies segmentation, cross-modal synthesis, denoising, and inpainting across four modalities (CT, MRI, X-ray, micro-ultrasound) via in-context generation with a hybrid masked image modeling (MIM) and autoregressive training pipeline. A new benchmark of 13 datasets is curated. Reviewers acknowledge the originality of unifying multiple tasks through in-context learning and the comprehensive benchmark, but have significant concerns about insufficient comparison with specialist models (which often outperform MVG on non-segmentation tasks), lack of 3D capability, unconvincing scalability claims, missing runtime/complexity analysis, and unclear clinical motivation. Reviewer 2 is substantially more critical (rating 3) than Reviewers 1 and 4 (rating 6), with Reviewer 3 in between (rating 5).

### Strengths
- Comprehensiveness of imaging modalities and tasks: segmentation, cross-modal synthesis, inpainting, denoising across CT, MRI, X-ray, micro-ultrasound (R1, R2, R4).
- Original use of in-context learning to unify multiple medical vision tasks; output space unification (colorization) useful for training across tasks (R3, R4).
- Hybrid MIM + autoregressive training yields strong performance, with the insight that pure masking is insufficient for segmentation – confirmed experimentally (R1, R2).
- MVG outperforms existing vision generalists (Painter, LVM) on most metrics and demonstrates adaptation to unseen datasets with minimal samples (R1).
- Introduction of a new comprehensive benchmark with 13 datasets across 4 modalities, a contribution to the community (R1, R4).
- Well-written and easy to follow (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **2D limitation**: Restricting to 2D images raises concerns about utility as a foundation model; 3D context is crucial for anatomical structures (e.g., brain cortex, lung vessels, heart) (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient comparison with specialist models**: Comparisons with U-Net, nnUNet are too brief; Table 3 shows specialist models outperform MVG on cross-modal synthesis, inpainting, and denoising (R1, R3). Reviewer 3 notes performance gains are marginal for these tasks compared to prior generalists, and all generalists are worse than specialists. A deeper analysis of trade-offs is needed (R1).
- **Unconvincing scalability claims**: Figure 6 and Table 6 only demonstrate that more data improves performance (a known property) and that unified training beats isolated training (established for ViT) (R2). Scalability experiments are limited to small datasets, not showing potential when unifying all datasets (R3).
- **Missing runtime/complexity analysis**: No GPU hours, memory usage, or inference time reported. Reviewer 1 asks about trade-offs for high-resolution MRI; Reviewer 2 requests comparison with nnU-Net resources; Reviewer 4 asks for architecture details (embedding size, layers, parameters, why 8 A5000 GPUs), training time of autoregressive modeling, and whether inference can run on CPU in acceptable time (R1, R2, R4).
- **Clinical motivation unclear**: Reviewers question practical benefits of a generalist model over specialist models (speed, deployment, cost-effectiveness) and note that pre-trained specialists often already achieve better performance (R2, R4). Reviewer 2 asks key insights for clinically/economically viable foundation models.
- **Ablation and component analysis lacking**: Reviewer 1 requests ablation of MIM vs. autoregressive individually and experiments with medical-specific data augmentation (cropping). Reviewer 4 questions why combine MIM and autoregressive when autoregressive alone appears superior – the motivation is unclear.
- **Generalization to unseen datasets not fully convincing**: Organs in “unseen” datasets still appear in training set; evaluation on truly novel semantics (e.g., cell data, pathology) is missing (R4). Reviewer 1 raises prompt variability: different prompt image-label pairs may lead to different outputs – how is this addressed?
- **Confound of colorization strategy**: Reviewer 3 suggests segmentation gains in Table 2/5 may be due to the colorization trick rather than task unification; an ablation isolating the effect is needed.
- **Missing essential medical imaging tasks**: Image registration and inverse reconstruction are omitted; Reviewer 2 suggests MVG’s features could support registration with a flow head and reconstruction with an unfolding approach using forward consistency.
- **Specific omissions/inconsistencies**: 
  - “13 tasks” (line 146) should be 13 datasets, 4 tasks (R1).
  - Figure 3 lacks sub-numeration (e.g., (a), (b)) making references like “upper right” hard to follow (R1).
  - Table 1 imbalance (segmentation comprises majority of datasets) not noted at beginning (R1).
  - Line 255 discussion on masking strategy for medical images should cite relevant work (e.g., Huang et al., SSL for medical image classification) (R1).
  - Table 4: large performance gap between Liver/Spleen and Lung datasets unexplained (R1).
  - Data scalability section (line 470) lacks reference to Fig. 6 (R1).
  - ACDC Dice scores: Table 2 reports 0.54, while UniverSeg paper appendix reports 0.70 – discrepancy needs explanation (R2).
  - Is the failure of mask modeling due to L1 loss? Alternative segmentation losses (e.g., Dice) not tested (R2).
