## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Topology-Invariant Pooling (TIP), a graph pooling layer that integrates persistent homology (PH) into graph neural networks. TIP uses 1‑dimensional persistence diagrams from a sublevel filtration on node features to resample edges after soft‑cluster assignments, scale edge weights by persistence information, and adds a topological loss that enforces similarity between the original and coarsened graph’s 1‑dim diagrams. The method is applied on top of DiffPool, MinCutPool, and DMoNPool, and evaluated on 3 synthetic and 7 TU benchmark datasets.

### Strengths
- The idea of leveraging persistent homology to guide graph pooling is novel. To the best of the reviewers’ knowledge, this is the first work to use PH for this purpose.
- The method is simple and can be easily integrated into several existing graph pooling layers.
- Empirical results on benchmark datasets show that TIP yields performance improvements over the base pooling methods, demonstrating its potential and flexibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Theory issue.** Theorem 1 claims that distinguishing graphs based on 1‑dimensional persistence diagrams is more expressive than 1‑WL. This is false: for example, a graph with one isolated node and a graph with two isolated nodes have identical (empty) 1‑diagrams under any filtration, but 1‑WL distinguishes them. Moreover, when filtration depends on node features that are the same across nodes, 1‑dim diagrams cannot capture more than Betti‑1, which is strictly less expressive than 1‑WL. The theorem is therefore either incorrect or irrelevant for practical purposes.
- **Weak motivation for preserving cycles.** The paper focuses on preserving 1‑dimensional topological features (cycles) during coarsening. Reviewers question why this is a general‑purpose desideratum for graph pooling or classification. For instance, coarsening a benzene ring (a 6‑cycle) to a single node legitimately changes the Betti‑1 number, yet this is reasonable structural simplification. The approach essentially reduces to cycle‑preserving pooling, and the motivation for that design choice is insufficiently argued.
- **Problematic choice of filtration and its relevance.** The method uses sublevel filtration based on a node‑level function (e.g., Forman‑Ricci curvature). The resulting persistence diagram describes the evolution of subcomplexes defined by the filtration function, not the graph’s own topology. Therefore, it is unclear why this diagram is relevant for measuring topological similarity between the original and the coarsened graph. The paper provides no convincing justification for choosing Forman‑Ricci curvature (which captures local geometry) as a filtration parameter for preserving *global* topological features, nor does it discuss or compare alternative filtration functions.
- **Insufficient and weakly significant experiments.** Experiments are limited to TU datasets (with high standard deviations). More popular and challenging benchmarks such as ZINC or OGB are not considered. The reported gains are often within one standard deviation of the base methods (except possibly on Enzymes). Comparisons are drawn only against basic pooling models, not against state‑of‑the‑art GNN architectures. Running times are not reported, and an ablation study across more datasets and pooling layers is missing. Dataset statistics are not provided.
- **Presentation and notation confusion.** The paper contains multiple unclear or inconsistent notations:
  - Page 3: $V=\{i, x_v\}$ should be $\{v, x_v\}$; the dimension $d$ of node features $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ is not explicitly stated.
  - Page 4: the nested subgraph sequence is indexed from 0 to $n$, implying nodes are added one by one, which is misleading.
  - Equation (5): `min($A^l$)` and `max($A^l$)` are not clearly defined (element‑wise min returns a scalar, not a matrix).
  - Equation (6): the Hadamard product between an $n\times n$ adjacency matrix $A^l$ and the vector $\mathcal{D}_1[1]-\mathcal{D}_1[0]$ (size $m\times 1$) is dimensionally ill‑defined; also “$D_1$” is used inconsistently with “ph()”.
- **Figure 1 unclear.** The filtration used in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b) is not specified; if it is node color, then nodes of the same color should appear/disappear simultaneously. The correspondence between the persistence diagram in Fig. 1(c) and the graphs is not explained. Fig. 1(b) itself suggests that the ratio of topological structures is stable across datasets, which may imply no additional topological preservation effort is needed.
- **Implementation alignment with PH.** The method uses resampling and persistence‑based edge weighting, but because the filtration is defined on node features (sublevel set), edge weights play no role in constructing the persistence diagram. This creates a conceptual mismatch between the PH computation and the subsequent edge‑weight manipulations.
- **Missing invariance analysis.** The paper does not discuss whether TIP is isomorphism‑invariant or how it handles isomorphic graphs.
- **Minor concerns.** The paper should clarify whether multiple filtration functions are used and how they are combined; whether 0‑dimensional persistence information is also incorporated (e.g., as node features like in TOGL); and the role of the Wasserstein distance in the learning curves versus vectorized diagrams. The “persistent sub‑topology” phrase is not defined.
