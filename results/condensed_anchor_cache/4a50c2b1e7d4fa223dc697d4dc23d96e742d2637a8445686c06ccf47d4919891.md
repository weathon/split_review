## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces trust-region sequential quadratic programming (trSQP) for training PINNs, formulating the PDE problem as a hard-constrained optimization. The method is described in detail with pseudo-code and mathematical derivations, and evaluated on 1D failure cases (e.g., transport equation). All three reviewers rate the paper 3 (neutral) and agree that the idea is interesting and the exposition is clear. However, they raise substantial concerns about the problem formulation, insufficient baselines, limited experimental scope, missing theoretical contributions, and several technical issues.

### Strengths
- The idea of using constrained optimization (specifically SQP) to address PINN failure modes is interesting and relevant.
- The proposed method successfully implements hard constraints within PINNs and mitigates ill-conditioning by alleviating the need for large penalty parameters.
- Numerical experiments show the proposed method outperforms penalty and augmented Lagrangian baselines (up to 1–3 orders of magnitude lower error).
- The paper provides a detailed, well-justified description of the algorithm (including quasi-Newton approximations, trust-region updates, and pretraining steps), making it easy to follow.
- Ablation studies and robustness experiments (e.g., parameter sensitivity, Figure 1) are included, demonstrating some degree of algorithmic stability.

### Weaknesses
- **Problem formulation is unconventional for forward PDE problems.**  
  The authors treat the empirical loss \(\mathcal{l}(\theta)\) as the objective and relegate initial/boundary conditions and the PDE to hard constraints (Eqs. 2, 3). In standard PINNs (Raissi et al., 2019) and the cited failure-mode paper (Krishnapriyan et al., 2021), the loss is defined solely on the PDE residual and boundary/initial conditions without additional data points.  
  *Reviewer 1 asks*: how can the same failure cases be observed when interior labeled observations are included?  
  *Reviewer 2 notes*: \(\mathcal{l}(\theta)\) is typical for inverse problems; for forward problems, initial/boundary conditions would be more appropriate as hard constraints rather than \(\mathcal{l}(\theta)\).  
  *Reviewer 3 states*: the paper misrepresents PINNs as data-driven; standard PINNs are data-free (only PDE and boundary loss, Eq. 3).

- **Insufficient baselines for a PINNs paper.**  
  Experiments compare only with penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods. Missing comparisons include:  
  - Vanilla PINN (Raissi et al., 2019)  
  - The method of Krishnapriyan et al. (2021) which first identified these failure modes  
  - Soft-constrained methods (to highlight the benefit of hard constraints)  
  - Adam+L-BFGS optimization (common in PINN practice; reviewer 1 notes Ref. [2] by Rathore et al. achieves comparable results with fewer hyperparameters)  
  - More recent hard-constrained approaches (e.g., augmented Lagrangian PINNs by Son et al., 2023, and Huang et al., 2022, neither cited)  

- **Experiments are limited in scope and lack essential comparisons.**  
  - All problems are 1D; no 2D or higher-dimensional validation is provided.  
  - No computational time or number-of-iterations-to-convergence data are reported, despite the claim of improved training efficiency.  
  - *Reviewer 3 asks*: How long are models trained? What are inference times?  
  - *Reviewer 3 asks*: Why does the method sometimes perform better for negative coefficients than positive ones in Figure 1 (asymmetry), given intuition suggests symmetric behavior?  
  - *Reviewer 1 asks* whether the failure cases can simply be fixed by reweighting loss terms rather than using the full SQP machinery.  

- **Theoretical contributions are lacking.**  
  - Penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods have known convergence guarantees (e.g., Son et al.); no analogous convergence proof is provided for trSQP-PINN.  
  - It is unclear why a linear-quadratic approximation of the constrained problem is sufficient and whether theoretical guarantees (e.g., optimality, feasibility) are preserved.  
  - *Reviewer 3 asks*: Why should one adopt this complex algorithm if simpler methods (soft constraints, weight schedules, preconditioning) may work?  

- **The pretraining step is not novel.**  
  The pretraining procedure—optimizing the PDE and boundary loss with L-BFGS—is essentially the standard PINN training proposed by Raissi et al. and analyzed by Rathore et al. (2024). Reviewer 3 notes this has been previously proposed.  
  *Reviewer 1 also points out*: if the penalty method with pretraining (which is nearly identical to standard PINN) already works well, the paper’s claimed improvements may be an artifact of pretraining, not the SQP optimizer.

- **References contain errors and omissions.**  
  - Key baselines for augmented Lagrangian PINNs (Son et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022) are not cited.  
  - Reference [5] in the paper (Nandwani et al., 2019) does not actually consider PINNs, contrary to the authors’ claim.  
  - Related work on operator preconditioning (De Ryck et al., 2023) and NTK perspectives (Wang et al., 2022) is omitted, which are relevant to ill-conditioning claims.

- **Reviewer questions that expose unresolved weaknesses** (included as they overlap with criticisms or missing analysis):  
  - How does the method scale to 2D problems?  
  - Why should one use hard constraints in the PINNs framework, given that a simple toy example (footnote) may not arise in practice?  
  - Is the performance difference between penalty method with/without pretraining explained? (The authors’ footnote suggests they should be similar, yet large differences appear.)  
  - Are there additional hyperparameters compared to Adam+L-BFGS, and why would a user prefer trSQP-PINN?
