## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces an informalization pipeline for Lean 4 that uses static analysis and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to create parallel natural-language / formal-language datasets from Mathlib4. From this data the Herald Translator is fine-tuned for autoformalization. The model substantially outperforms baselines (TheoremLlama, InternLM2-Math-Plus-7B, Llama-3 Instruct) on miniF2F, a graduate-level textbook set, and a web-scraped set, and is demonstrated on a real-world Stack project section. The dataset and code are promised for open-source release.

### Strengths
- Clear rationale and commitment to open-source the full pipeline.
- Impressive evaluation results on both standard benchmarks and a real-world formalization task (Stack project).
- Well-designed augmentation pipeline, especially the use of structural analysis to order statement translation so that logical dependencies are available in both formal and natural language.
- Detailed qualitative comparisons with prior work (Open Bootstrapped Theorems), with candid discussion of both successes and remaining shortcomings.
- Extends the boundaries of autoformalization by incorporating retrieval-augmented generation, providing an innovative and practical tool for the community.
- Method is simple and the writing is clear.
- The resulting Herald dataset has clear utility for the community, and the Herald translator demonstrates significant improvement over existing autoformalization models.

### Weaknesses
- Lack of ablation studies on the data generation/augmentation components; in particular, the effect of multilinguistic (paraphrase) augmentation and the contribution of each pipeline stage are not isolated.
- Missing comparison with DeepSeek-Prover-1.5-Instruct on the formalization task.
- Missing comparison with other autoformalization methods (e.g., methods [1,2] cited in review 3).
- Potential contamination: the data generation/curation process may have inadvertently included parts of miniF2F in the training set; this needs to be ruled out.
- The significance of the fine-tuning results is unclear because the metric (NLI after backtranslation) and two of the three test sets are custom – representative examples of test-set statements and model outputs that pass/fail each validation step are needed.
- An audit of the LM-based NLI check against human expert judgments of semantic equivalence is essential to validate the metric.
- Did the authors try rejection sampling to remove generated statements that still contain Lean-specific formal names?
- The provenance of the “College CoT” web-scraped test set should be clarified.
- The influence of different retrieval methods on translation quality has not been investigated.
- The Pass@128 metric is highly lenient, raising compute-efficiency concerns; it must be confirmed whether all baselines in Table 2 are also evaluated with Pass@128.
- It is unclear whether Table 2 reports only theorem-statement formalization or includes proof formalization – proof-translation performance should be reported separately.
- Examples of generated NL-FL pairs from the Herald dataset are not provided, making quality assessment difficult.
- To isolate the impact of the Herald dataset, a comparison of Herald Translator trained on other datasets (e.g., MMA, LeanWorkbook) on the same benchmarks is needed.
- Why was ProofNet (available in Lean 4) not included as an additional benchmark?
- The selection process for the Stack project demonstration should be explained to confirm it was not cherry-picked.
- The paper reports only theorem-statement formalization results; what was the role of the aligned proof data in training, and why are proof-translation results not reported?
