## Merged Review

### Summary
This work proposes Stable Hadamard Memory (SHM), a memory-based architecture for partially observable reinforcement learning. SHM uses a Hadamard product-based memory update mechanism—including a calibration matrix with random selection/dropout of update parameters—to dynamically retain important information and discard irrelevant data. The method is evaluated on meta-RL, long-horizon credit assignment, and POPGym against recurrent and attention-based baselines using PPO and SAC algorithms. Results show significant improvements across tasks.

### Strengths
- Well presented and method explained in detail; evaluated against a range of baselines on multiple benchmarks (meta-RL, credit assignment, POPGym) and with multiple RL algorithms (PPO, SAC).
- Results show dramatic and consistent improvements over prior work across broad sets of tasks.
- Method is mathematically well-motivated with theoretical guarantees, especially stability guarantees; the derivation of the linear matrix C is clear and simpler than comparable derivations in State Space Models.
- The credit assignment benefits are justified and empirically shown.
- Straightforward to implement; uses a relatively novel approach by designing a linear operation with well-behaved gradients, and the random sampling of θ to break input dependence is a novel idea.
- Thorough literature review and extensive experiments.
- One reviewer (R3, score 8, high confidence) was notably more positive, praising the method’s novelty and empirical breadth; another reviewer (R2, score 6) supported acceptance but with low confidence due to orthogonal expertise.

### Weaknesses
- Novelty is unclear: The name “Stable Hadamard Memory” overemphasizes the Hadamard product (already a core component of many RNNs like GRUs) while the true contribution seems to be the calibration matrix with random selection/dropout of update parameters. This component should be the focus of the paper.
- The update rule is highly similar to linear attention (which is cited but not discussed). The paper should clearly articulate mathematical differences from linear attention and provide an empirical comparison against it.
- No code provided.
- Experiments use very few repeats (3 seeds) and baseline tuning details are incomplete: it is unclear whether baselines were tuned on the same tasks, what hyperparameter ranges were explored, and what criteria were used for selection. Efforts to normalize computational and memory cost between SHM and baselines are missing. Statistical significance of results is therefore hard to assess.
- Random selection of update parameters increases memory usage. The paper should quantify this overhead (how it scales with memory size and parameter count) and compare against baselines with the same memory footprint, including impact on training time.
- Missing citation: A paper from RLC 2024 on memory aggregation in Meta-RL is not cited.
- The paper is overly complex at times; a clearer, more concise description of why the update rule works empirically would benefit the reader.
- Writing is awkward in places, hampering understanding. Specific issues: Section 3.3 is misspelled (“Stable Hadamrad Memory”); Proposition 3 is poorly worded; the initialization of C (lines 259–264) is hard to follow; line 233 has unclear notation (“m.k”).
- Several design choices are not justified: Why use \(1 + \tanh(x)\) instead of \(\text{sigmoid}(x)\)? Why is the recurrent state real-valued (compared to complex-valued state methods like FFM)? Did the authors try complex-valued states to track phase information?
- The paper lacks discussion of scalability: (a) How does performance scale with memory size? Is there an optimal size? (b) As task complexity increases, how do computational requirements and performance change? Provide metrics to measure this impact. (c) Does SHM maintain stable and efficient updates over longer episodes? Consider stability and efficiency over extended periods.
- Reviewer confidence varies: one reviewer (R2) had low confidence (2/5), noting the work is “orthogonal” to their prior experience, and two reviewers gave score 6 (R1, R4) while one gave 8 (R3), indicating mixed assessment of the work’s soundness and contribution.
