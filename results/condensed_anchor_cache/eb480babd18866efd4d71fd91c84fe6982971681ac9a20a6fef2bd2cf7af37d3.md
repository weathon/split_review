## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces higher-order calibration as a generalization of standard calibration to predictors that output mixtures over label distributions. It provides a principled method for decomposing predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components, with theoretical guarantees that a higher-order calibrated predictor yields accurate estimates of aleatoric uncertainty. The method uses \(k\)-snapshots (multiple conditional labels per point) to approximate higher-order calibration, and shows convergence as \(k \to \infty\). Experiments on image classification support the theoretical claims. The work extends prior second-order calibration (Johnson et al., 2024) to arbitrary orders and avoids modifying the output layer. Reviewers generally praise the theoretical contributions but note several clarity issues, missing comparisons, and inadequate discussion of related work and limitations.

### Strengths
- The approach is innovative, addressing the shallow-studied area of calibration for second- (higher-) order distributions. (R1)
- The theoretical results are mathematically sound, including a necessary and sufficient condition for correct aleatoric uncertainty estimates, results for finite \(k\), and convergence guarantees. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- The paper provides a principled generalization of calibration to higher-order and \(k\)-th order calibration. (R2)
- Unlike Johnson et al. (2024), the method does not require modifying the output layer of the model. (R2)
- The approximate \(k\)-snapshot calibration is practically important for applying the theory. (R3)
- The paper is well written, with a comprehensive review of related work that is useful for novice readers. (R3)
- The experiments are well motivated and convincingly demonstrate the theoretical claims. (R3)
- The work addresses an important problem in conditional predictive inference. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Notation and clarity issues (R1, R4):** Definition 1.1 uses confusing notation (\(\mathbf{x}\) in bold, \(\mathbf{x} \sim [x]\)) that is not explained. This ambiguity persists in Theorems 1.2, 2.7, and Lemma 3.2, where expectations are taken over \(\mathbf{x} \sim [x]\). The overall problem setting and objective need to be stated more formally. (R4)
- **Rediscovery of known ideas (R1):** The claim that “we cannot distinguish scenario 1 from scenario 2 if all we receive are ordinary labeled examples with a single label per image” (lines 145–146) echoes the Fisher–Laplace paradox. The authors should acknowledge this.
- **Missing discussion of pitfalls of second-order distributions (R1):** The paper is unaware of an extensive literature (e.g., last paragraph of [1] Section 5) that points out shortcomings of second-order distributions for disentangling aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. The authors should discuss whether their method overcomes these issues, compare with credal-set-based UQ (Hüllermeier, Sale, Caprio, Desterke), and discuss limitations of learning a level-2 predictor from level-0 samples.
- **Incomplete comparisons to other calibration notions (R1):** How does the proposed higher-order calibration relate to type-2 validity [2] and credal set calibration [3]? This is not discussed.
- **Missing related work (R2, R4):**
  - The paper omits Kotelevskii & Panov (2024) on risk decompositions for proper scoring rules, and Durasov et al. (2022) (Zigzag) which is the first work to predict pairs of labels. (R2)
  - A connection to Lee and Barber's work on distribution-free inference with hierarchical data (multiple label samples, \(k\)-th moment coverage) is not explored. (R4)
- **Experimental weaknesses (R2, R3):**
  - Only one of the two proposed strategies to achieve \(k\)-th order calibration (post-hoc calibration) is used in experiments; the proper-loss minimization strategy is not evaluated, and there is no comparison between them. (R2)
  - The baseline from Johnson et al. (2024) (Cheat NN) is not included; this would provide a direct comparison to the prior method, even though it requires an output-layer modification. (R2)
  - The estimation of entropy values in experiments is not explained; how empirical entropy is computed and how estimation errors affect overall uncertainty estimates is unclear. (R3)
  - The paper does not test whether higher-order calibration improves “hard-OOD” detection (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. ImageNet). (R2)
- **Missing details and sections (multiple reviewers):**
  - There is no Conclusion section, which would help summarize key takeaways and limitations. (R1, R2)
  - Figure 1 (page 2) is never referenced in the text. (R2)
  - The “Summary of main results” (lines 101–102) is too long and introduces definitions/theorems; it should be renamed to “Background” or “Preliminaries”. (R2)
  - Some appendix proofs are not referenced (e.g., Theorem 2.6 in Appendix D.4). (R2)
- **Unclear or insufficiently explained concepts (R1, R2):**
  - Line 270: Why is \(k\) in parentheses in \(g: \mathcal{X} \to \Delta \mathcal{Y}^{(k)}\)?
  - The conceptual simplification in line 273 is unclear. Clarify with an example: for \(\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,3\}\), \(k=3\), and \(g(x)=((0.9,0.05,0.05),(0.9,0.05,0.05),(0.8,0.1,0.1))\), does this correspond to an empirical second-order distribution giving probability \(2/3\) to \((0.9,0.05,0.05)\), \(1/3\) to \((0.8,0.1,0.1)\)? (R1)
  - Why is the Bayesian posterior predictive considered a higher-order predictor? It outputs an averaged (first-order) distribution over labels, not a distribution over distributions. (R2)
  - The claim that epistemic uncertainty is “precisely the true ‘variance’ in \(f^*(x)\) as \(x \sim [x]\)” needs clarification: how does this correspond to the variance of the Bayesian mixture? (R2)
  - For non-strictly convex generalized entropy (e.g., Zero-one score), do the necessary and sufficient conditions for higher-order calibration to produce calibrated aleatoric uncertainty still hold? (R2)
- **Technical notes (R1):**
  - The 1-Wasserstein metric on \(\Delta\Delta\mathcal{Y}\) is well-defined only when \(\mathcal{Y}\) is discrete; this should be mentioned.
  - Using \(G\) as any concave generalized entropy function conflicts with the standard notation for the generalized Hartley measure (see [4], eq. (27)).
- **Running example (R3):** The running example should be discussed in more detail (either in the main text or appendix) to aid understanding of definitions and notation.
