## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates zero-shot visual brain decoding across unseen subjects using fMRI from 177 subjects watching HCP movies. The authors introduce a uniform processing pipeline (upsampling voxels, CLIP encodings, cosine similarity retrieval) that avoids subject-specific layers, and report that performance improves with more training subjects, generalizes across architectures, and is affected by inter-subject similarity (e.g., gender). Reviewers find the problem setting timely and the paper clearly written, but raise major concerns about dataset validity, lack of comparisons to prior zero-shot or alignment methods, limited methodological novelty, and potential confounds in the video-derived stimulus-fMRI pairs.

### Strengths
- **Clear writing and logical rationale** (R1, R2, R3). The motivation for zero-shot cross-subject decoding is well introduced.
- **Rich experimental design** (R1, R3). Multiple validations across architectures (MLP, CNN, Transformer) and analyses of gender and train-test similarity are original and informative.
- **Timely problem** (R2). The question of aligning subjects without per-subject layers aligns with current discussions in neuroAI.
- **Dataset contribution** (R1). Consolidating a 177-subject image-fMRI set from HCP movie-watching breaks the routine of NSD-only data.
- **Promising scaling results** (R3). The finding that adding subjects improves performance motivates larger datasets and foundation models.
- **Addressing scalability and dataset scarcity** (R4). The paper explicitly targets two major challenges, though evidence is deemed insufficient.

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of quantitative comparison with prior zero-shot/alignment methods** (R1, R2, R4). No comparisons to methods using hyperalignment, RSA, anatomical alignment (e.g., fsaverage), or common representational spaces (e.g., Scotti et al. 2024b, MindEye2) are provided. The authors claim other methods are parameter-heavy but give no quantitative cost analysis (R2). R1 notes that CLIP-MUSED also uses PCA downsampling for alignment without additional training. R4 stresses that a direct comparison with MindEye2 is essential to assess relative effectiveness.
2. **Dataset validity and potential confounds** (R1, R3, R4).  
   - The fMRI signal for each frame may reflect accumulated activity from prior stimuli, not a clean one-to-one response to the presented image (R4).  
   - Movie frames within 1-second sampling are highly correlated; test frames may come from same scenes as training frames, risking overfitting to specific scenes rather than general visual features (R3).  
   - The large performance gap between the proposed dataset and NSD (e.g., Table 2 vs. Table 6) is unexplained and raises doubts about data quality (R4). R1 further notes that all subjects in the proposed dataset see identical stimuli, whereas NSD subjects see different image sets, making it impossible to attribute generalization gains to the method rather than dataset properties.
3. **Misreported or missing state-of-the-art numbers** (R4). The paper claims MindEye1 achieves 84% top-1 accuracy, but Scotti et al. (2024a) report 93.1% (image retrieval) and 90.1% (brain retrieval). The proposed approach thus does not surpass SOTA, contrary to the implication in the text.
4. **Insufficient explanation of how adding subjects solves alignment** (R2). The model may simply overfit to shared video-level noise rather than learning true neural representations. No rationale is given for why more subjects improve functional alignment when voxel-level variability remains unaddressed.
5. **Dataset curation and architecture details are lacking** (R3). Missing: uniform sampling of test images? overlap between adjacent frames in train/test? upsampling methodology (bicubic interpolation? artifacts?), number of parameters per architecture, hyperparameter selection procedure. The Transformer is surprisingly large (24 layers) without justification. The use of a 4-s averaging window after the target frame is suboptimal given that the peak BOLD response occurs at 4 s; alternative pooling (e.g., 2–6 s) should be investigated.
6. **Simple retrieval task and failure on reconstruction** (R1). The decoding task is limited to image retrieval; the method is reported to fail on reconstruction (consistent with downsampling-based approaches), reducing its generality.
7. **Contradiction regarding hand-picked voxels** (R4). Lines 51–52 claim hand-picking is not ideal, yet Table 6 shows it outperforms whole-brain data. Whole-brain data may contain redundancy/noise (Lin et al., 2022), but the paper does not address this.
8. **Poor performance in one NSDGeneral variant** (R4). Table 6 row 7 shows extremely low accuracy, casting doubt on the pipeline’s ability to generalize. R1 also questions the claim that poor NSD results are due to few subjects, given the dataset confound.
9. **Minor issues** (R3): line 39 mis-cites Benchetrit et al. (2023) as fMRI decoding when they used MEG; conclusion mistakenly says “brain encoding” instead of “decoding”. (R4) Comparison of model size (parameters) with existing architectures is missing, weakening scalability claims.
