## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes GraphEval, a lightweight graph-based LLM framework for evaluating research ideas. It decomposes complex ideas into viewpoint nodes (via small LLMs), connects them into viewpoint-graphs using LLM-based relation extraction and/or BERT similarity, and then evaluates ideas via two methods: GraphEval-LP (training-free label propagation) and GraphEval-GNN (trained GNN with additional novelty/plagiarism detection). Experiments on two datasets (ICLR Papers, AI Researcher) show F1 improvements of at least 14% over baselines with low computation and API cost. The framework also detects plagiarized ideas.

### Strengths
* Introducing graph-structured evaluation by breaking complex ideas into interconnected viewpoint nodes is novel and sound.
* The approach is lightweight, requiring only a 7B LLM and low computation/API costs; GraphEval-GNN includes a novelty detection component.
* Significant F1 improvements (≥14%) over multiple baselines, with effective plagiarism detection.
* The paper is well-written, clearly describing viewpoint extraction, graph construction, label propagation, GNN training, and the plagiarism mechanism.

### Weaknesses
* **Limited evaluation scope:** Only two datasets (ICLR Papers, AI Researcher) are used; dataset sizes are small (e.g., 160 ICLR papers). Generalizability to other domains or types of research content is unclear. One reviewer (Rating 5) considers the experimental evaluation insufficient, while the other three are more positive.
* **No ablation or hyperparameter studies:** Missing analyses of the impact of different LLMs/GNNs, varying parameters $k$ and $m$, and the effect of each component (viewpoint extraction, relation extraction, graph construction). No analysis of computational complexity, training/inference time, or scalability for larger graphs.
* **Theoretical justification and verification lacking:** No analysis of whether LLM-extracted viewpoints are accurate, whether overlap exists between viewpoints of different ideas, or how hallucinations and token limitations are mitigated. The plagiarism detection mechanism also lacks theoretical grounding.
* **Reliance solely on textual relevance:** The graph is constructed only from textual information, which may not fully capture a paper’s acceptance potential. Temporal edges are not integrated; the paper treats edges within/across documents as undirected without clarifying temporal dependencies.
* **Sparse graph edges:** LLM-based relation extraction yields only 10.73% edge density on average, potentially limiting effectiveness. Alternative relation extraction methods (unsupervised, hybrid) are not explored.
* **Missing implementation details and reproducibility:** No code or datasets released. Important details are absent: exact prompts for CoT, ToT, and viewpoint extraction; dataset composition (positive/negative sample ratio); initialization procedure for node features in GraphEval-LP (line 270-274); definition of $n$ in label prediction (line 288); informal variable names like “max_iters” and “cos sim”.
* **Underutilization of LLM reasoning:** The framework relies mainly on label propagation/GNN and does not fully exploit LLM generative or reasoning capabilities for interpretability.
* **Minor issues:** Typos (e.g., missing period after “Sec” in line 302, inconsistency between “macro F1” and “micro F1” in lines 399-422); missing related work on automated paper review generation [1-3] and on GraphRAG; unclear color coding in Figure 1 (negative/positive prompts).
* **Comparison gap:** Fine-tuning BERT (with far fewer parameters) achieves comparable performance, but fine-tuning a 7B model is not evaluated.
