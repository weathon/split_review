## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes replacing Relative Positional Bias (RPB) with Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) in vision transformers to enable seamless use with fused attention kernels (e.g., Flash Attention). It extends RoPE to 2D images, studies multiple RoPE implementations (2D RoPE, Axial RoPE, partial hidden-dimension RoPE), and develops a fast CUDA implementation for Axial RoPE. Experiments compare RoPE vs. RPB on ImageNet classification across several model sizes, reporting improved or similar accuracy with inference speedups. Reviewer 1 (score 8) found the work clear and the CUDA implementation valuable, while Reviewers 2 and 3 (scores 5 and 6) questioned novelty, result conclusiveness, and scope.

### Strengths
- The extension of RoPE to images is presented clearly and makes intuitive sense; figures effectively illustrate the ideas (R1, R2).
- The paper does a great job of motivating and describing the CUDA implementation and provides an easy-to-use Python wrapper (R1, R3).
- It explores multiple Rotatory Positional Embedding variants (2D RoPE, Axial RoPE) and reports comparisons (R1).
- Instead of applying RPB (which slows down attention in fused kernels like Flash Attention), RoPE adds positional information before attention, making it compatible with any modern fast attention implementation (R3).
- Found that applying RoPE to only a fraction of the hidden dimensions is sufficient to achieve competitive performance, offering a practical efficiency trade-off (R3, R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty:** RoPE has been applied to vision transformers before. Notably, Heo et al. (2024) and Chu et al. (2024) have explored 2D RoPE; the resemblance to Heo et al.’s work is not discussed. Additionally, P. Jeevan & A. Sethi (WACV 2022) already used RoPE in ViT and is not cited (R2, R3). The paper’s first two contributions are unconvincing (R2). Reviewer 1 acknowledged the novelty is limited both in method and results (R1).
- **Inconclusive results:** The paper lacks a definitive conclusion on whether 2D RoPE or Axial RoPE is superior. Table 4 shows 2D RoPE outperforming Axial RoPE, but no in-depth analysis of trade-offs (cost, memory, performance across architectures) is given; this hampers practical assessment (R2). Table 3 shows the best performance when no positional bias is introduced at all – the paper does not explain this result nor why RoPE is even needed if no bias is optimal (R2, R3).
- **Modest accuracy gains:** The paper claims “noteworthy gains,” but Table 2 shows improvements of only 0.1–0.2% in most cases (R1). Reviewer 1 also noted that support for the impact of small improvements introduced in Section 3.3.2 is lacking (R1).
- **Limited generalization testing:** Evaluation is only on ImageNet classification; no experiments on downstream tasks (e.g., detection, segmentation) to assess generalization ability of Axial RoPE (R2). Reviewers 1 and 2 also noted the scope is limited to cases where fused attention is used, and performance gains may not scale to larger model/resolution combinations (R3, R1).
- **Lack of novelty reasoning:** Questions remain about the actual contributions: Is Axial RoPE a novel contribution or simply an analysis of an existing method? (R3). Further justification for RoPE’s superior performance beyond intuitive explanations is needed (R1). Why did the authors choose non-shared angles for Axial RoPE when Table 4 vs. Table 5 shows shared angles consistently perform better? (R2).
