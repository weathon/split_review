## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Layer Regression (LR), a detection method that analyzes internal layer outputs of DNNs using a small MLP trained to identify deviations caused by adversarial perturbations. Reviewers praise the clear motivation, theoretical foundation, lightweight nature, and high detection AUC on several architectures and domains. However, significant concerns dominate: the theoretical proof is flawed (incorrectly assumes linear amplification, ignores nonlinear activations/skip connections), evaluations lack adaptive attacks (attacker is not given access to the detector), baselines are weak and outdated (simple input processing, missing recent SOTA detectors), and the reported AUC values are implausibly high (contradict known bounds on robust accuracy). Additional weaknesses include arbitrary layer selection, threshold sensitivity, overfitting risk, missing evaluations on minimal-perturbation attacks (e.g., C&W), and missing experiments on video/audio despite claims. One reviewer (score 5) finds the experiments convincing, but the majority argue the method is not robustly validated.

### Strengths
- Clear motivation and theoretical justification (Theorem 1) for the detection principle (R1, R2, R3).
- Lightweight: uses a small MLP, computationally efficient for real-time detection (R1).
- Empirically shows high detection AUC and efficiency on several architectures and domains (R2, R3).
- Demonstrates applicability to image, video, and audio tasks (claimed, experiments on image and video shown; R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Fatally flawed theoretical proof:** Theorem 1 bases on Goodfellow et al.’s linear amplification remark, which is an empirical observation, not a proven theorem. The proof does not apply to nonlinear activation functions (sigmoid, tanh) or architectures with skip connections/batch normalization; counter-examples exist. The analysis misses key assumptions and is mathematically not rigorous (R2, R5).
- **No adaptive attack evaluation:** The white-box scenario only gives the attacker access to the classifier, not the detector. A proper adaptive attack that simultaneously minimizes detection and classification loss is missing. All reviewers (R1, R3, R4, R5) consider this a critical omission—without adaptive attacks the claimed robustness is unsubstantiated.
- **Weak and outdated baselines:** Compared only to simple input processing methods like JPEG compression and legacy detectors. Missing modern detectors such as those based on semantic contradiction (R1), expected perturbation score (R1), and other strong baselines (R3, R4). The evaluation does not reflect current state-of-the-art in adversarial detection.
- **Implausible detection AUCs:** Reported AUC >0.99 on CIFAR-100, but the best robust defense accuracy on CIFAR-100 (from RobustBench) is <45%. Such high AUC would imply a nearly perfect classifier, which contradicts known lower bounds. This suggests the detector may exploit artifacts in the tested adversarial examples rather than truly detecting them (R5).
- **Arbitrary input selection:** The choice of which early-layer activations feed the MLP is heuristic and not systematically justified. The subset often needs to be selected per model and per dataset, reducing practicality and generalizability (R1, R3).
- **Threshold sensitivity:** Detection relies on a manually set threshold; no rigorous analysis of how threshold choice affects performance across datasets and attacks (R1).
- **Overfitting risk:** The MLP is trained on clean data only, with high-dimensional input from layer activations. The paper does not address regularization or generalization to out-of-distribution adversarial inputs (R1).
- **Missing evaluation on minimal-perturbation attacks:** Not tested against attacks like Carlini & Wagner (C&W) that produce adversarial examples with small L-p norms. Also unclear if a detector trained on L∞ attacks can detect L2 or L0 perturbations (R2, R5).
- **Missing targeted attack and cross-domain experiments:** No evaluation against targeted attacks (R3). Despite claiming applicability to audio and video, only image and video results are shown; audio experiments are absent (R5). No experiments on CIFAR-10, the most common adversarial robustness benchmark (R5).
