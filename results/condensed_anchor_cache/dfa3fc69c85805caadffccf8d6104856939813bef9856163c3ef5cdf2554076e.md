## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces CMP-CV, an automated cross-validation framework using the CTRPv2 dataset, and benchmarks four drug representations (graph, molecular descriptors, fingerprints, SMILES) for deep learning models in drug response prediction. It analyzes prediction errors across drug descriptor space, finding molecular descriptors and SMILES perform slightly better overall but rankings vary by region, highlighting non‑uniform error.

### Strengths
- The problem of studying inductive biases for drug response prediction is interesting and relevant in drug discovery (R2).
- The analysis on the effectiveness of certain representations across drug domains is interesting, e.g., descriptor and Morgan representations are more effective with highly soluble drug candidates; includes importance measures of molecular descriptors that are tightly captured by certain representations (R2).
- The paper calls attention to the fact that predictive error is typically non‑uniform (R4).
- The paper studies an important application of machine learning in drug discovery (R1, R3).
- The paper is well‑written and easy to understand (R1; note R2 disagrees – see Weaknesses).
- The authors provide the code necessary for reproducing the experiments (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear contribution**: The paper compares existing methods using an already‑proposed dataset and common metrics (AUC, prediction error); it is unclear which parts are the main contribution. The work appears to be a simple combination of existing methods, dataset, and evaluation metrics (R1, R2, R3, R4). The primary contribution is error analysis, not novel methods; this falls short of the acceptance criteria for ICLR (R3).
- **Insufficient description and justification of CMP‑CV**: The CMP‑CV workflow is left to the end of the paper, after the results. The description in Section 3.2 does not provide sufficient information to understand the framework; it appears to be a standard job orchestrator / hyperparameter sweep largely handled by the CANDLE framework, not a significant technical contribution (R1, R2, R4). More writing should be devoted to how metrics are actually computed and to explaining CANDLE and the paper’s contribution upon it (R1, R4).
- **Lack of comparison with other benchmarks**: There are several benchmarks for drug discovery (e.g., [1], [2]), but no comparison is made (R1). The main advantage of this benchmark over prior ones is not clarified (R1).
- **Insufficient analysis**: Although Figures 2 and Tables 2–4 seem interesting, the paper only presents results without hypothesis or justification (R1). The authors point out non‑uniformity of errors but provide no actionable recommendations on how to proceed with this knowledge (R4).
- **Writing and presentation issues**: The writing is very difficult to follow. Terminology is not disambiguated (feature space, representation, molecular descriptors, drug domains, drug regions) (R2). Specific problems:
  - Figure 1: duplicates in the x‑axis and the legend (R2). Figures 1 and 2 refer to AUC, but results show R², RMSE, and MAE (R2). R², RMSE, and MAE are correlated; showing all three is unnecessary (R4).
  - Page 4, Section 2.3.1, paragraph 2: the statement “ML model appears to perform better when the log S of the drug is more than −7” is phrased confusingly (R2).
  - Figure 3: the overlapped vertical bar (Morgan‑ATSC7p and ExtraTrees‑ATSC7p) is not explained (R1).
  - UMAPs in Figure 6 are uninformative and do not support the authors’ point; the point could be expressed differently, e.g., using Tanimoto similarities of clusters (R4).
  - Tables 2 and 3 could be expressed in just a few lines (R4).
- **Dataset and experimental design**: The trends for all models in Figures 3 and 4 are roughly the same, suggesting that the non‑uniformity in prediction error is due more to the dataset than to any choice of architecture or molecular representation (R4). The paper states that 10 models were trained on 10 random train/val/test splits, which is not standard practice – the test set is usually fixed across all CV splits (R4).
- **Lack of novelty in conclusions**: The conclusion that molecular representation significantly influences drug response prediction performance is straightforward and lacks novel insights for the ML‑based drug discovery community (R3).
- **Missing experimental details**:
  - What exactly does each model predict? It appears to be gene expression values, but the paper is not clear (R4). How do the authors go from these values to a single R², RMSE, and MAE in Figure 1? (R4).
  - How were the bins in Tables 2 and 3 chosen? (R4).
- **Disagreement on writing quality**: Reviewer 1 found the paper well‑written, but Reviewer 2 found it very difficult to follow, and Reviewer 4 rated presentation as 2 fair.
