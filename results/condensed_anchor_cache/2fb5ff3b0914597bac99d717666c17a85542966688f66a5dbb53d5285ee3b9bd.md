## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies a variant of causal contextual bandits where the context is stochastically dependent on the learner’s initial action (adaptive context). The learner chooses an initial action (intervention), observes a context, then picks a second action based on the context to maximize reward. The work extends prior deterministic-context results to stochastic contexts, proposes a convex-optimization-based algorithm (ConvExplore/CE) that uses an instance-dependent parameter λ, and provides both upper and lower bounds on simple regret that are tight up to log factors. Experiments validate the theoretical findings.

### Strengths
- The problem setting is new: it allows the context distribution (graph) to stochastically depend on the initial action, with real‑life applications such as online advertisement (cold‑start scenarios).
- The algorithm (ConvExplore) is clearly presented, consisting of three subroutines (estimating transition probabilities, causal parameters, and corresponding rewards).
- The use of a convex minimization problem for the parameter λ is interesting and provides an instance‑dependent approach.
- Solid proofs: the theoretical analysis includes both upper and lower bounds on simple regret, indicating that the proposed method is essentially tight for a large class of instances.
- Simple regret minimization guarantees are provided, and empirical results verify the theoretical claims.

### Weaknesses
- The paper restricts to binary interventions and binary rewards (0/1), which limits practical applicability to problems with only binary outcomes.
- The algorithm’s complexity scales with the number of contexts (columns of matrix P). With a very large or infinite context space (e.g., user population), maintaining and computing with P becomes computationally challenging.
- The algorithm is horizon‑dependent (requires knowing the total number of rounds T upfront); its performance when T is very small is unclear.
- The choice of λ significantly influences algorithm performance, yet no variation curve for larger λ values is provided. Moreover, how to adjust λ specifically when dealing with entirely new contexts in a new scenario is not addressed.
- When λ is less than nk (e.g., λ = 390), CE’s performance is inferior to Uniform Exploration (UE); no explanation for this behavior is given.
- Experimental results are too few; the source or generation rules of the experimental data are not provided, and the link between the cold‑start motivation and the experiments needs further explanation.
- The paper lacks comparison with other exploration strategies (e.g., UCB‑based or Thompson‑Sampling‑based) beyond uniform exploration. It is not clarified in which scenarios the convex exploration would outperform UCB/TS, and the computational complexity of the convex optimization approach relative to UCB/TS is not analyzed.
- No theoretical comparison of the regret bound with existing causal bandit works (e.g., Lu 2020/2021, d‑separator‑based methods) is provided, making it difficult to assess novelty. Key differences in assumptions and the specific improvements of the derived bound should be stated.
- The upper or lower bounds of λ are not given; the practical implications of different λ values on algorithm performance are not discussed.
- The causal graph assumed for each context is rudimentary (a simple chain, similar to Lattimore 2016) and ignores recent developments (e.g., exploring d‑separators, confounded graphs). No justification is given for this restrictive assumption.
- The algorithm learns transition probabilities and reward functions independently for each context, so sample complexity scales linearly with the number of contexts. The paper does not address how this would perform when contexts are numerous (e.g., user demographics).
- The terminology “context” is confusing: in contextual bandits context typically refers to side information, but here it also denotes nodes in the causal graph (see Figure 2).
- Overall writing lacks clarity in some parts and appears rushed; concepts and derivations need better motivation and explanation.
- Several open questions remain: (i) Page 1, last paragraph: what are the “multiple other variables” in the causal graph? (ii) Is there a relationship between λ and effective dimension if the problem is modeled as a sparse bandit? (iii) Can knowledge be shared between different context graphs and reward functions (e.g., by grouping contexts with similar behaviors)? (iv) How does the stochastic transition to contexts make the analysis more challenging compared to the deterministic work of Subramanian 2022?
