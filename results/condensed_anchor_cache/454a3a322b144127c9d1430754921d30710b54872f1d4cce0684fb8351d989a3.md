## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the “representation shattering” hypothesis to explain why knowledge editing (KE) methods degrade model performance: KE distorts the latent representations of entities beyond the targeted fact, disrupting the learned global structure. The authors create synthetic knowledge graphs with cyclic relation subgraphs, pre‑train a small Transformer (nanoGPT) on them, apply the ROME editing method, and analyze representation geometry via Isomap visualizations and Frobenius‑norm differences. They find that edits—both corrective and counterfactual—disrupt the learned ring structure, and that the degree of distortion correlates with accuracy drops. Preliminary experiments on pretrained GPT‑2‑XL and Mamba suggest the phenomenon extends beyond toy settings.  

One reviewer (score 3) was notably more critical, questioning the limited scope (only cyclic graphs, only ROME, only GPT‑like models) and the heavy reliance on visual inspection; the other four reviewers (score 5) praised the novelty and insight but raised similar concerns about generality, clarity, and missing baselines.

### Strengths
- **Novel mechanistic hypothesis**: First in‑depth study of how KE distorts transformer representations, going beyond black‑box performance evaluations to propose “representation shattering” as an explanation.  
- **Insightful perspective**: Tackling KE effects through representation geometry provides a unique and interpretable angle.  
- **Well‑designed synthetic task**: The cyclic knowledge‑graph setup clearly enforces relationships so that editing one fact has trickling effects; the structure is clearly illustrated (Figure 3) and enables controlled analysis.  
- **Good visualization**: Figures 4, 5, 6, and 8 effectively show representation shattering after editing.  
- **Preliminary evidence in larger models**: Demonstrated the effect in pretrained GPT‑2‑XL and Mamba, not only the small synthetic Transformer.  
- **Combination of qualitative and quantitative evidence**: Frobenius‑norm differences (Figure 5c) complement visual inspection, and Table 1 shows overall accuracy effects.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope of experiments**:
  - Only one KE method (ROME) is tested. Reviewer 4 explicitly asks for other “locate‑and‑edit” methods (e.g., PMET) and parameter‑addition methods (e.g., GRACE). Without testing multiple methods, it is unclear whether representation shattering is a general property of KE or specific to ROME.  
  - The synthetic knowledge graphs use cyclic relation subgraphs, which are rare in real‑world KGs (which often form trees/hierarchies). Reviewers question whether the phenomenon generalizes beyond rings and whether the same methodology can be applied to real KGs without cyclic orders.  
  - Experiments are limited to a small 2‑layer nanoGPT and GPT‑2; one reviewer asks for tests on encoder‑decoder models (T5), bidirectional models (BERT), and larger LLMs (Llama, Gemma, Phi 3.5). The Mamba experiment is mentioned but is preliminary.  
  - The size of the graphs is small; it is unclear whether findings scale to larger, more complex knowledge bases.

- **Reliance on visual inspection**:
  - The paper heavily depends on Isomap visualizations to demonstrate shattering, which becomes infeasible for higher‑dimensional or unvisualizable data.  
  - The Frobenius norm is used as a quantitative metric only in one experiment (Figure 5c) and is not consistently applied or justified as a reliable measure. A more rigorous, quantitative definition of representation shattering is needed, and it should be introduced earlier (currently hidden at lines 413–415).

- **Unclear data construction and naming**:
  - The construction of the synthetic KG is confusing and contains conflicting information (e.g., Line 202 says 3 relations, Line 205 refers to 24 relations).  
  - The naming of entities and relations (e.g., “I_C4”, “III_A2”) is not explained; the number of “I”s is supposed to indicate cyclic orders but is not mentioned in the main text. Figure 3 is difficult to interpret (unclear arrow colors, which edges are edited).  
  - Subsection 3.3 needs clearer description of how nodes are connected and what I/III/ABC refer to.  
  - The ground‑truth facts after editing are not clearly stated (e.g., after editing \(1.I\_C2 = 3\), does the old edge \(1.I\_C2 = 2\) still exist? Which entity is the correct answer for relation I_C3 after the edit?). The paper should specify the post‑edit logic.

- **Missing details and presentation issues**:
  - No code, prompt templates, or examples of LLM outputs are released; this hinders reproducibility and makes it hard to understand how the KGs are queried.  
  - Multiple typos: line 191 should be \((x_i, r, x_j)\); line 291 should be “changes fact \((x_i, r, x_j)\)”; line 366 caption is grammatically broken (“the mean drop in accuracy against the in the Frobenius norm…”).  
  - Figure 1 is confusing: panel (b) is unclear, labels “182” and “608” are unexplained, and the abbreviation “DGP” is undefined (presumably “Data Generating Process”).  
  - The definition for describing representation shattering should be moved earlier in the paper.  
  - The logical inference task’s hold‑out process is not clearly explained (e.g., if an inverse relation token is held out, how does the model know it is the inverse?).

- **Lack of mitigation strategies**: The paper identifies the problem but does not discuss potential ways to avoid or reduce representation shattering, which would help direct future work.

- **Reproducibility concerns**: One reviewer notes the results seem hard to reproduce without code and clear data‑generation details.
