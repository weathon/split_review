## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a geometric framework using manifold capacity (from Chung et al. 2018) to quantify feature learning beyond the lazy vs. rich dichotomy. It theoretically shows (under limited settings) that manifold capacity tracks the degree of richness in feature learning and empirically studies this on synthetic data and two-layer networks. Additional geometric measures (radius, dimension, alignment) are used to reveal learning stages. The framework is applied to neuroscience and out-of-distribution generalization tasks.

### Strengths
- Novel use of manifold capacity and geometric measures to study the lazy vs. rich dichotomy; shows better alignment with the degree of feature learning than weight changes or NTK-label alignments.
- The research problem (understanding feature learning) is important and interesting; manifold capacity as a metric for feature learning progress is new and potentially useful.
- Theoretical analysis and empirical evidence that manifold capacity effectively quantifies feature learning; comparisons with accuracy and weight changes underscore its efficacy.
- Experiments across multiple domains (synthetic, neuroscience, OOD) support the claims; manifold capacity captures different learning stages.
- Insightful empirical findings on manifold geometry and learning stages with robust experiments.
- Most derivations in the appendix appear correct.

### Weaknesses
- **Theory issues:** The main theorem (Theorem 1) statement in the main text is nearly vacuous or circular: claiming that capacity tracks richness, but richness itself is defined by capacity. The authors should state a less vacuous claim (e.g., models with high capacity converge to high accuracy). The proof is only for a single gradient step update and under strong assumptions (second layer untrained, activation function heavily constrained) – this limitation is not clearly communicated in the main text, giving a misleading impression of generality. The link between different definitions of manifold capacity (Sections 2.1, 2.2, Theorems 1,2) is unclear and needs explicit explanation. The theoretical derivation relies on a one-step gradient argument, but this fact is not mentioned.
- **Presentation and clarity:** The paper is haphazardly written; the distinction from prior work and the problem statement are not well delineated; goals are not succinctly mentioned. The structure is cluttered – too much content is packed into the main paper, diminishing clarity. The relationship between manifold capacity and manifold geometry measures is weakly explained, relying mainly on Figure 2c with minimal analysis. Figure 2c lacks a clear explanation; the operations depicted are not defined. Equation (1) (model capacity definition) is confusing: are manifolds predefined or do they change with N? How are they selected? What values does y_i take? Appendix C for computing manifold capacity introduces undefined notations T and λ. The method for computing manifold capacity should be explicitly described in the main text, at least stating the conditions under which values are computed (exact computation is not possible without certain assumptions). Section 3.2/Figure 3b: definitions of “wealthy” and “poor” regimes are unclear, as is their relationship to input dimension; why task-relevant features exist at initialization is not explained; the minimal change in the capacity (purple) line during training is hard to interpret. Section 4 is difficult to follow because key metrics (e.g., dimension, radius) are not defined in the main paper. Critical measures such as effective radius, dimensionality, center alignment, axes alignment, and center-axes alignment are introduced without definitions, and their implications are less discussed compared to radius and dimensionality (Figures 5b, 6c). Figure 2 caption is confusing: “Higher capacity means that a higher number of manifolds per neuron can be packed in the neural state space” – what does “pack manifolds into neurons” mean? The term “untangling” is not specifically defined or measured.
- **Experimental rigor and generality:** Generalizability of observations (e.g., Figure 4) regarding hyperparameters (optimization algorithms, weight initialization, batch size, learning rate, scheduling) is not discussed; these could influence implicit biases, representations, geometric metrics, and learning stages. The algorithm for computing capacity during training is not clearly presented – details needed for reproducibility are missing: how is intrinsic dimensionality estimated? what specific methods are used for geometric measures? It is unclear which layer(s) should be analyzed and whether results are consistent across layers. The simple manifold radius magnitude may not capture problem complexity when manifold means scale identically; are there standard reference lengths compared to the radius?
- **Contribution relative to prior work:** *Minority view:* One reviewer (score 5) argues the paper contributes no more (and likely less by way of confusion) than the 2018 work by Chung et al., which describes the full intuition of manifold capacity and outlines its use. Other reviewers find the introduction of manifold capacity to the ML community interesting.
- **Other specific criticisms:** Line 170-171: variable s is not properly defined. Line 173: typo – $o_N(1) \to 1$ should be $o_N(1) \to 0$. In Section 2.1, “i-th input category” is undefined; eq. (1) implies that $\mathcal{X}_i$ (the set defining $\mathcal{M}_i$) is a variable of the $\max$ operator, not pre-defined. Weight changes are used mainly as a strawman to invoke NTK connections, which is unnecessary. Questions raised: What are the implications of the 2-layer results on deeper neural networks? Can the authors provide an example of the lazy regime (how can a network be trained without modifying internal features)?
