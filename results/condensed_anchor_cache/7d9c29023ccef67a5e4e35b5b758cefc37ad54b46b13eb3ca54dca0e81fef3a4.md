## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a new causal fairness metric, post-Intervention Cumulative Ratio Disparity (ICRD), and a corresponding training framework (ICCFL) that regularizes the model toward ICRD. The authors argue that existing interventional fairness (K-Fair) has two limitations: non-unique computation and insufficient conditionality for causal fairness. ICRD measures cumulative causal effects along prediction probabilities after intervening on the sensitive attribute, and ICCFL uses a differentiable approximation of ICRD as a penalty. Theoretical and empirical results (on several datasets) are presented. Reviewer scores and opinions vary widely: one reviewer (score 6) finds the work well-motivated and sound, while two reviewers (score 3 each) criticize it as incremental and unclear; the remaining two (score 5) are positive on the idea but point to significant exposition and justification gaps.

### Strengths
- The paper clearly illustrates the limitations of existing interventional fairness metrics (K-Fair) and the related works section is comprehensive and easy to follow. (R3)
- The formulation of ICRD is sound, with theoretical analysis showing how it addresses the identified limitations of K-Fair. (R3, R4)
- The proposed notion (ICRD) is intuitive and easy to implement, and the ICCFL framework casts fairness as a regularization term, making training straightforward. (R1)
- On several empirical datasets, the proposed method performs best in terms of causal fairness compared to several benchmarks. (R1, R5)
- The overall setting is well-chosen and the contribution appears solid. (R2)
- The paper provides useful experimental details, including Section 5.3 (empirical evidence for the benefit of ICRD) and Section 5.4 (observations on hyperparameter choice in ICCFL). (R4)
- The approach is a comprehensive improvement over K-Fairness, covering both a new notion and an algorithm with relevant theoretical support. (R4)
- It is reasonable and meaningful to uncover limitations of existing fairness notions and propose a new metric. (R5)

### Weaknesses
- The method is somewhat incremental; novelty is not high. Experiments are only OK. (R2) [Minority more negative: R2, R5]
- The theoretical results are quite intuitive and the proof straightforward; the paper should better highlight why the contributions are nontrivial. (R1)
- The proposed method assumes the causal model is known. There is no discussion of sensitivity to causal graph misspecification. (R3)
- The metric assumes a binary sensitive attribute; no discussion of extension to multiple sensitive attributes. (R3)
- The method uses causal generative models to infer exogenous variable distributions. Robustness of the approach when estimating interventional distributions with different causal generative models is not explored. (R3)
- The differences between K-Fair and the new ICRD notion are subtle. The paper would benefit from much clearer explanations. For example, Example 1 (used to illustrate limitation 1) could be reapplied after introducing ICRD to show how ICRD resolves that example, including what the possible contexts C are. (R4)
- It is unclear whether the advantages of ICRD alone justify adding yet another definition to the already large set of causal fairness definitions. The authors should discuss the general benefits of ICRD as a causal fairness definition. (R4)
- ICRD centers on disparity in cumulative causal effects, which is not necessarily always desirable for understanding discrimination. The paper should discuss potential insufficiencies, e.g., when ICRD may not be identifiable, or when enforcing ICRD=0 may be too restrictive for fairness. (R4)
- The motivation behind ICRD is somewhat ambiguous. Specifically, how is a condition defined as ‘sufficient’ for evaluating causal fairness? The sufficiency and insufficiency aspects need more elaboration. (R5)
- The claim “ICRD encompasses K-Fair and represents the cumulative causal effect of K-Fair across all decision thresholds” (lines 313–314) is difficult to interpret without additional clarification. It is also unclear how Table 1 was generated or how the decision threshold impacts outcomes. (R5)
- It is not clear that the decision threshold’s impact constitutes a limitation of K-Fair: K-Fair requires two distributions to be equivalent, so it is unclear how the threshold would influence this requirement. More discussion is needed. (R5)
- The presentation and explanations of both current work and its context in the related literature are not very clear. (R2)
- Compared to benchmarks, the proposed algorithm is not very competitive if one primarily cares about conventional metrics such as K-Fair and accuracy. (R1)
- The references do not contain any ICLR paper; the fit to this venue could be better demonstrated. (R1)
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments:**
  - How exactly does ICRD address limitation 1 (non-unique computation)? (R4 question)
  - Why does interventional fairness have fewer identifiability challenges compared to counterfactual fairness and path-specific fairness? (R4 question)
  - Could ICCFL be compared with benchmark methods using other causal fairness notions, such as path-specific fairness? (R4 question)
  - How is the set of contexts C chosen, and which contexts were used in the experiments? (R2 question)
