## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper identifies a gradient back-propagation bottleneck in the ULEEN architecture, which arises from continuous relaxation of Bloom filters (non-differentiable AND/min function). The authors propose the “Soon filter,” replacing the AND operation with a summation to enable smoother gradient flow. The approach is multiplication-free, using hashing/filtering instead of binarization. Empirical results on UCI datasets, MNIST, and MLPerf Tiny datasets are presented, showing improvements over ULEEN, BNN, and DeepShift. Reviewer confidence varies: one reviewer (score 5, confidence 3) finds it clearly written and a new SOTA for multiplication-free feed-forward models; two reviewers (score 3, confidence 5 and 4) find the modification very small, the evaluation inadequate, and the benchmarks trivial, with significant concerns about complexity analysis and generality.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, and easy to follow (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The motivation for efficient multiplication-free inference at the edge is well-articulated (Reviewer 2).
- The problem exposition (gradient bottleneck in ULEEN’s Bloom filters) is clear and interesting (Reviewer 3).
- The primary goal of setting a new state-of-the-art for multiplication-free feed-forward models is commendable (Reviewer 1).
- The Soon filter directly addresses the gradient flow issue (Reviewer 1).
- Empirical improvements are consistent across the tested tasks (Reviewer 2).
- The analysis of the gradient bottleneck and the proposed solution is insightful and clearly presented (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Modification novelty and magnitude**: The proposed change (replacing AND/OR with SUM) is very small compared to ULEEN; the core contribution is limited (Reviewer 2: contribution poor; Reviewer 1: contribution fair). One reviewer questions whether the only distinction is replacing OR with SUM (Reviewer 1).
- **Benchmark triviality and evaluation scope**: The experiments use only tiny models and datasets (UCI, MNIST, CIFAR-10). On CIFAR-10 the reported accuracy is near 50%, far below state-of-the-art (Reviewer 3). The approach is not tested on larger or more relevant models (e.g., large vision/language models) or more challenging benchmarks typical of the ML community in 2023 (Reviewer 3). There is no comparison with CNNs in general; results on MLPerf Tiny datasets are for MLPs only, not CNNs (Reviewer 2). Missing comparisons with small CNN models like MCUNetV1 and MCUNetV2 on the Visual Wake Words dataset (Reviewer 1).
- **Complexity analysis is insufficient**: Only model size is reported as a complexity metric. The computational and memory overhead of the hashing/filtering implementation is not analyzed. Key questions remain: What are the costs of the hash function? How does the memory footprint of the hash table compare to model parameters? How does this approach compare to standard binarization or low-bit quantization (e.g., 4-bit, 8-bit) in terms of operations and memory? (Reviewer 3). A detailed breakdown of hardware implications is missing (Reviewer 3).
- **Generality and applicability**: It is unclear whether the approach generalizes to other architectures (CNNs, attention, larger NN sizes) or other tasks (Reviewer 2). The application is niche and not well demonstrated for practical edge deployment (Reviewer 2). Practical applicability is questioned due to the low accuracy on even a simple dataset like CIFAR-10 (Reviewer 3).
- **End-to-end deployment evaluation absent**: The paper does not report end-to-end performance on a challenging edge device, nor does it compare latency/power metrics against alternatives in a real deployment scenario (Reviewer 2).
- **Need for more visual aids and expanded exploration**: The related work section would benefit from more illustrations to clarify prior work (Reviewer 1). The authors could augment the paper with additional contributions or deeper exploration of the ideas to increase scholarly impact (Reviewer 1).
- **Minor improvements**: While consistent, the accuracy gains over baselines (e.g., 98.6% vs. 98.5% on MNIST) are very small, raising questions about practical significance (Reviewer 2).
