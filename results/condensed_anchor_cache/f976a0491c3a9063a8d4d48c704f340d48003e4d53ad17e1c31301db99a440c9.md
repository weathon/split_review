## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper formalizes Dataset Usage Cardinality Inference (DUCI), which estimates the proportion of a given dataset used to train a machine learning model, moving beyond binary membership inference. The authors show that standard membership inference attacks produce biased estimates of data usage and propose a debiased estimator that leverages global true positive rate (TPR) and false positive rate (FPR) instead of per-sample estimates. The method is computationally efficient (e.g., 300× less cost than the optimal MLE) and matches MLE performance (max error <0.1). Experiments compare against four adapted baselines across multiple datasets, including a copyright infringement setting, and the proposed method consistently outperforms. Confidence intervals are derived via an asymptotic approach. One reviewer (R5) rated lower (6) and found the technique straightforward, while the other four (all 8) praised the novelty and importance of the problem and solution.

### Strengths
- The problem is highly relevant and timely, addressing a gap in privacy/copyright protection for data owners.
- The fine-grained formulation (estimating exact proportion) is more realistic than previous binary all/none or any/none approaches.
- The proposed method is practical, scalable, and computationally efficient.
- The debiasing insight (using global TPR/FPR instead of per-sample estimates) is intuitive and effective.
- The experimental design is thorough: comparisons with multiple baselines (MIA Guess, MIA Score, MLE with joint logits, MLE with average logits), multiple datasets, and a realistic copyright infringement scenario.
- The derivation of confidence intervals and analysis of why confidence intervals are large around p=1/2 are appreciated.
- The method is general and can be applied to any membership inference algorithm with known TPR/FPR.
- The paper is well-written and easy to read.

### Weaknesses
- **Approximation in debiasing**: Using a single global TPR and FPR across all samples may introduce bias when TPR/FPR vary per data point (e.g., non-uniform sampling, varying model confidence). The paper should analyze or bound this bias.
- **Independence assumption for confidence intervals**: The heuristic confidence intervals assume independence of \(\hat{p}_i\); while correlations are shown small experimentally, rigorous asymptotic or non-asymptotic intervals (e.g., using XBern from Pillutla et al.) are missing.
- **Poor performance around p=0**: Results (Table 4) show the method is unreliable for very small fractions, making it unsuitable for answering "if" a dataset was used. A multiplicative guarantee (e.g., \(\hat{p}/p \in (1/c, c)\)) or adaptation to dataset inference would strengthen the work.
- **Requires known training set for TPR/FPR estimation**: In practice, the full training set is often private, limiting applicability (see Zhang et al. 2024).
- **Presentation clarity issues**: Several mathematical descriptions are vague or confusing (e.g., "number of population data", \(\theta(x)_y\), reference model definition in Lines 108-115; unclear sentence in Lines 285-286). These need rewriting or removal.
- **Handling of record dependence/correlation**: The paper’s treatment is inconsistent: Section 4 does not incorporate the subgroup debiasing suggested for high correlation, and Lines 489-497 seem to downplay the issue without clear justification.
- **Missing comparison with binary data usage algorithms for p=1 and p=0**: To show DUCI is competitive, experiments should compare to state-of-the-art binary methods in these extreme cases.
- **Confidence intervals vs. absolute error**: In Figure 3, the confidence intervals appear large relative to the absolute error; this merits explanation and discussion of practical utility.
- **Effect of TPR=FPR on debiasing**: The behavior of the estimator when TPR equals FPR is not analyzed, which could matter in low-signal settings.
- **Test power downgrade**: Whether the debiasing step reduces the statistical power of the estimator is not investigated.
- **Connection to differential privacy auditing**: There is potential for connecting DUCI to DP guarantees, but no analysis is provided.
- **Concrete improvements from Appendix G**: The paper mentions two possible improvements in Appendix G but does not implement or evaluate them; more concrete results would strengthen the contribution.
- **Minority reviewer (R5) concern**: One reviewer (rating 6) found the technique simple and straightforward, expressing lower confidence in the contribution level despite acknowledging the interesting problem and thorough experiments. This contrasts with the other reviewers’ strong positive assessments.
