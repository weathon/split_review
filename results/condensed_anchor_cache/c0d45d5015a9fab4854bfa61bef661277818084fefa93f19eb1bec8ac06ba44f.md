## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses out-of-distribution (OOD) detection when in-distribution (ID) data is class-imbalanced. The authors empirically identify two failure modes: tail-class ID samples misclassified as OOD, and OOD samples predicted as head-class ID. They propose a statistical framework (ImOOD) that reveals a class-aware bias term causing this performance gap. Building on this analysis, they introduce a post-hoc normalization and a training-time regularization technique to mitigate the bias. Experiments on CIFAR10-LT, CIFAR100-LT, and ImageNet-LT show consistent improvements over several existing OOD detection methods.

### Strengths
*   **Clear motivation and problem identification:** The paper clearly demonstrates (e.g., Figure 1) the limitations of existing OOD detectors under class imbalance, specifically misclassifying tail-class ID as OOD and OOD as head-class ID. This helps understand current approach limitations.
*   **Well-motivated and theoretically-grounded method:** The proposed ImOOD framework is driven by Bayesian analysis that identifies and explains the bias term. The post-hoc normalization and training-time regularization are backed by strong statistical analysis (Section 3.1) and empirical evidence.
*   **Effective and general framework:** The method is designed to work with different scoring strategies and shows consistent performance gains across multiple benchmarks (CIFAR10-LT, CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT) compared to state-of-the-art approaches.
*   **Extensive ablation study:** The paper includes thorough ablations (e.g., Table 4) demonstrating the contribution of each component, such as the estimation of the class-prior in training-time regularization.
*   **Well-written and easy to follow:** The paper is structured clearly, and the statistical analysis includes helpful intuitive explanations connecting the bias term to empirical observations. The method is described clearly enough to understand and re-implement.

### Weaknesses
*   **Inaccurate estimation of the bias term (beta):** One reviewer (R1) raises a major concern that the three components of beta are all biased.  
    *   *P(i|X):* For detectors where the scoring function g is always positive (e.g., distance-based scoring), P(i|X) will always be >0.5, causing all points to be taken as ID—a clearly biased estimate.  
    *   *P(y|x,i):* Biased due to the well-known calibration failure of deep models, which is a core reason why MSP scores are ineffective—this issue cannot be ignored in OOD detection.  
    *   *P(y):* Biased because the training-time distribution (ID + OOD) differs from the test situation; the auxiliary OOD data (n_o) used for training cannot cover the real test OOD distribution.
*   **Limited scope of the studied problem (R4):** The paper assumes class imbalance only exists on the ID dataset. It is not clear how the method extends when the auxiliary OOD training dataset and/or target OOD dataset also exhibit class imbalance. The practicality of the scenario is questioned: would it not be more natural to first refine the ID training data to be class-balanced, and then consider the more likely class-imbalanced OOD data? Studying various combinations (balanced/imbalanced ID, auxiliary OOD, target OOD) is important.
*   **Effect of auxiliary OOD training data unclear (R2, R4):** The sensitivity of OOD performance to the choice of different OOD training data is not explored. For example, what happens if CIFAR100 is used as OOD data for a model trained with CIFAR10 as ID? More generally, the empirical validation is limited to one target OOD dataset per InD dataset; validation on more challenging OOD datasets (e.g., near-OOD, data with spurious correlations) would be helpful.
*   **Marginal performance gain from post-hoc normalization (R2, R4):** The improvement from post-hoc normalization alone (without training-time regularization) appears marginal, especially on OE and BinDisc, and in terms of AUROC/AUPR. Its effectiveness on larger datasets (e.g., ImageNet-LT) is not fully verified, suggesting heavy reliance on the training-time regularization.
*   **Missing comparisons and baselines (R1, R2, R3):**  
    *   The paper should compare against a wider range of representative OOD detection methods, including OpenMAX [1], CGDL [2], OLTR [3], distance-based methods (e.g., Mahalanobis distance), and synthesized outlier methods (Tao et al. 2023).  
    *   A closely related baseline (Jiang et al. 2023) should be discussed in detail, clarifying similarities and differences.  
    *   The paper should also consider methods that handle imbalanced data for OOD detection specifically, such as [1] from the reviewer, and discuss why previous works fail on the identified cases and how ImOOD overcomes those drawbacks.
*   **Insufficient ablation and details (R1, R4):**  
    *   Ablation studies testing the respective contributions of P(i|x), P(y), and P(y|x) are missing.  
    *   More details on how the label distribution is learned during test-time regularization are needed. Additionally, it is unclear whether the same learned distribution is used for post-hoc normalization or only for training. If discarded after training, one must know the label distribution for post-hoc normalization anyway, raising the question of why a learned distribution is necessary.
    *   The definition of g (the detector built on f) should be given earlier and more clearly.
    *   Detailed discussion of hyper-parameter settings, choice of auxiliary OOD data, and evaluation datasets is needed.
*   **Relevance of handling imbalance separately (R1):** A direct question: if learning algorithms that already handle imbalanced data are used to train the base model, is additional handling for OOD detection still needed? This connection is not clarified.
