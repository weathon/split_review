## Merged Review

### Summary
This work proposes a variation of EBM maximum‑likelihood training that uses importance sampling to reduce bias from non‑converged SGLD samples. Negative samples are drawn from a tempered model \(p_\theta^{1-\beta}(\mathbf x)\) (i.e., a “higher‑temperature” / flatter energy surface) and reweighted via self‑normalized importance sampling to approximate the gradient with respect to the original model \(p_\theta\). Additionally, a positive (training) sample is sometimes included in the set of contrastive negatives to handle samples with very low estimated likelihood. The method is controlled by a hyper‑parameter \(\beta \in [0,1]\) that trades off training stability against sample quality (\(\beta=0\) recovers standard EBM training). Experiments are conducted on toy 2‑d datasets (e.g., 8 Gaussians, Rings) and on CIFAR‑10 using both unconditional JEM and a Joint Energy‑based Model (JEM) formulation.

### Strengths
- The motivation – training EBMs with accurate likelihood estimates – is relevant and timely.
- The math behind the factorization of the model into an importance distribution \(p_\theta^{1-\beta}\) and an importance ratio \(p_\theta^{\beta}\) is correct.
- The idea of using self‑normalized importance sampling to debias non‑convergent SGLD samples is interesting and could be applied to other self‑sampling‑based EBM training methods.
- The paper is clearly written, with an intuitive explanation of the approach.
- The authors honestly report that larger \(\beta\) deprecates sample quality, highlighting a clear trade‑off between training stability and generative fidelity.
- The inclusion of experiments demonstrating the method’s effects is appreciated, along with some ablation studies.
- The paper honestly discusses experimental settings where the method shows negative results (e.g., unstable training for larger \(\beta\) on CIFAR‑10).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental evaluation and missing baselines.**  
  – The only quantitative comparison is Table 1, which compares to the original JEM paper. No comparisons are made to more recent JEM variants that incorporate spectral normalization or improved sampling methods, nor to a wide range of state‑of‑the‑art unconditional EBM models on CIFAR‑10 (which report significantly stronger FID scores).  
  – On toy data, there is no baseline such as contrastive divergence with standard regularization \(f_\theta(x_+)^2 + f_\theta(x_-)^2\) (see e.g., [1]), which would help contextualize the results.  
  – Key competitors, especially diffusion models that also excel at likelihood estimation (e.g., Kingma et al. 2023, “Variational Diffusion Models”; “Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models” by Nichol & Dhariwal 2021), are not compared against.  
  – Table 1 does not include error bars, making it impossible to assess statistical significance of the reported FID and accuracy values.
- **Missing quantitative likelihood evaluation.**  
  – The paper’s core motivation for EBMs over diffusion models is their ability to estimate (unnormalized) log‑likelihoods, yet no quantitative negative log‑likelihood (NLL) estimates are provided. Only a visual representation appears in Fig. 2. NLL comparisons to diffusion models (e.g., Table 1 of Nichol & Dhariwal 2021) are needed.
- **Method only works with very small \(\beta\) on real data; improvements are marginal.**  
  – On image data, only very small values of \(\beta\) yield stable training; thus the proposed change over standard EBM training is minimal. The resulting gains in FID and accuracy for JEM are marginal relative to the base training method.
- **Lack of theoretical grounding.**  
  – The self‑normalized importance sampling estimator assumes exact sampling from the proposal distribution \(p_\theta^{1-\beta}\), but the method uses Langevin dynamics (ULA) with finite step size, which introduces discretization error and potential slow mixing (especially for multimodal distributions). The paper provides no rigorous analysis of how these biases interact with the training procedure.  
  – The empirical modification of including a positive sample in the negative set (and effectively discarding all negative samples when it is selected) is not justified theoretically. It is not clear why this yields a high‑quality gradient estimator or under which conditions it is beneficial.  
  – The paper does not solve the problem of sampling from the EBM once trained; the method only addresses gradient estimation during training.
- **Missing related‑work section and context.**  
  – There is no dedicated related‑work section. The paper fails to cite prior work on stabilizing contrastive divergence (e.g., [2]) and prior uses of including a positive sample in the set of contrastive negatives (e.g., [3], where equation 21 in the appendix closely resembles the proposed technique; also prior contrastive estimation [4] for Bayesian experimental design).
- **Unclear contribution of Sections 3.1–3.3.**  
  – The necessity of Sections 3.1–3.3 is unclear; it is not stated whether the inclusion of positive samples among negatives is used in the experiments or ablated. No ablation study validates the importance of this component. One reviewer suggested that Sec. 3.3 could be moved to the appendix.
- **Writing and presentation issues.**  
  – Some statements lack precision or justification (e.g., “Sampling from a more uncertain distribution can lead to improved mixing”, L193; “these two values do not necessarily need to sum to 1; arbitrary values can be employed instead”, L296 – no justification offered).  
  – Important arguments are moved to the appendix while less interesting implementation details remain in the main text. The JEBM experimental setting (arguably a positive result) is not described in the main text, whereas half a page is dedicated to explaining experiments that fail.  
  – Minor misprints present, and the term “SGLD” may be a misnomer (ULA is more appropriate since gradients are not stochastic).
- **Failure to reproduce mode weights on Rings dataset.**  
  – For the “Rings” example, the algorithm does not robustly capture the relative weights of modes; this shortcoming is not discussed in the paper, raising concerns about general applicability.
- **Limited technical novelty.**  
  – The reweighting scheme is straightforward; without strong experimental results and a deeper theoretical justification for the specific temperature parameterization, the contribution is limited.
- **Minority reviewer’s more positive assessment.**  
  – One reviewer (score 5) found the derivations correct, the factorization idea interesting, and appreciated the honest reporting of the sample‑quality trade‑off; that reviewer still noted the missing baselines, the marginal improvements on image data, and the need for a related‑work section. The other reviewers (score 3) were more critical, emphasizing the lack of competitive comparisons, missing theoretical analysis, and insufficient evaluation.
