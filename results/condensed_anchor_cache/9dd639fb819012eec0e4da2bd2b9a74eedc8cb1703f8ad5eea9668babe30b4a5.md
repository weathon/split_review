## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DFLMV (Decentralized Federated Learning Majority Voting), a three-stage framework for handling noisy labels in decentralized federated learning (DFL): (1) initial DFL training until model stability; (2) label correction via majority voting using predictions from each client’s neighbor models; (3) fine-tuning on the corrected dataset. The method is designed to be lightweight, communication-efficient, and privacy-preserving without assuming any local noise model or requiring a central server. Theoretical bounds on generalization error and majority voting error rate are provided. Experiments are conducted on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-10N, CIFAR-100N, Clothing1M, and ANIMAL-10N under various noise levels and IID/non-IID settings, comparing against the PENS baseline.

### Strengths
- The problem of learning with noisy labels in the DFL setting is important and the motivation is clear.
- The core idea of using peer model majority voting for label correction in DFL is novel.
- The proposed method is relatively lightweight, communication efficient, and easy to implement.
- The three-stage framework is clearly explained, and theoretical analysis (generalization bounds, error rate bounds) is provided for each component.
- Extensive experiments cover multiple datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-10N, CIFAR-100N, Clothing1M, ANIMAL-10N) with varying noise ratios and IID/non-IID data partitions. Results demonstrate effectiveness in mitigating noisy labels.
- The approach does not require a central server, additional clean datasets, or assumptions about local noise models.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical soundness concerns (high priority):** Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 4 express serious doubts about the theoretical analysis. Theorem 1 models the gap between two population error distributions, not involving empirical error over the finite noisy dataset, making it inapplicable to practical settings where only a finite sample is available. Theorem 2 inherits similar issues. Reviewer 4 states that Theorem 1 appears factually incorrect: it attempts to adapt results from (Ke et al., 2023) but overlooks Assumptions 5 and 6 from the original work, rendering the proof flawed. The proof of Theorem 3 is confusing: `θ_1` and `θ_2` are described as differences between probability distributions, but “difference” is not defined, nor is it explained what it means for `θ_1` to be larger than `θ_2`. These issues lower overall soundness; Reviewer 1 rates soundness 1, Reviewer 4 rates soundness 1.

- **Unrealistic assumption:** The assumption that the distributions of the votes are identical is criticized by both Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 3 as too strong, especially under the non-IID settings that are central to DFL. The paper does not adequately address the implications of this assumption for the theoretical analysis, making its practical relevance questionable.

- **Incomplete experimental comparisons and baselines:** Experiments omit simple but strong baselines, such as applying state-of-the-art robust learning algorithms against noisy labels locally on each client during decentralized training. Reviewer 1 notes that the non-IID partition used is not natural and does not fully reflect real-world non-IIDness. No comparison is made with defenses against stronger corruption like Byzantine attacks in decentralized learning (e.g., Farhadkhani et al., 2023); exploring whether existing Byzantine-robust methods could apply to noisy labels would be valuable.

- **Missing ablation and implementation details (Reviewer 2):** For each dataset, the number of clients is not specified, nor is how neighbors are defined or how the number of neighbors affects utility. The number of data points needed in the initial training stage to obtain a reasonable model for relabeling is not discussed. The sensitivity of the method to the choice of initial model parameters, which could significantly impact label correction, is not explored.

- **Computational and communication overhead unclear (Reviewers 2 and 3):** Stage 2 involves clients exchanging model parameters with neighbors, which contradicts the paper’s claim that “DFLMV does not introduce any extra communication overhead.” A detailed breakdown of the computational cost of the majority voting process (especially with many clients/neighbors) and the communication overhead for exchanging models is missing. The `O(n)` computational overhead claim is vague without specifying constants or how it scales with model complexity and number of clients. Reviewer 3 also asks for specific extra computational costs for at least one setting.

- **Convergence and scalability not addressed:** There is no discussion of how the method scales with increasing numbers of clients or how the decentralized nature affects convergence rate.

- **Technical writing and clarity issues (Reviewer 4, in contrast with Reviewers 2 and 3 who found the paper well-written):** Reviewer 4 finds the paper challenging to follow. Specific issues:
  - Problem presentation (Equation (1)) appears before notations are defined.
  - Unexplained concepts/notation: `d_x`, `d_y` (line 182), `f_k` (line 193), “non-colluding neighbors” (line 299).
  - Inconsistent notation: loss function as `L` (Eq. 1), then `L_k` (Eq. 3), back to `L` (Eq. 4), then `L_k` (Eq. 8); output space alternates between `R^{d_y}` and `{1,…,C}`, leading to errors such as computing one-hot encoding of a vector in `R^{d_y}` (line 191); Theorem 1’s LHS uses `R^{d_y}` while the RHS assumes discrete `Y`; datasets defined as lowercase samples in Section 3 then uppercase random variables in Section 4; `B` and `n_peers` used interchangeably.
  - Algorithm presentation is interwoven with analysis; a dedicated section for the algorithm is recommended.
- **Related work limitations (Reviewer 4):** The claim that decentralized federated learning was introduced by Lalitha et al. (2018) is misleading; the field has a rich history. The reference to Yagli et al. (2020) for the definition of generalization error (Eq. 10) does not appear relevant to noisy labels. The paper is heavily inspired by (Ke et al., 2023), with similar notation and results, yet Ke et al. is cited only once and only in the appendix; a more explicit discussion of how the submission builds upon or differs from Ke et al. is needed.

- **Unanswered questions that overlap with weaknesses:**
  - How robust is the approach to malicious clients who intentionally send manipulated model parameters? (Reviewer 2)
  - Why not consider other voting methods, e.g., weighted majority vote based on peer distance? (Reviewer 2)
  - Why not consider the aggregated model for label correction in Stage 2, which would allow adopting existing methods? (Reviewer 3)
  - Please provide specific extra computational costs for at least one setting. (Reviewer 3)
