## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Adaptive Data Optimization (ADO), an online method that adjusts data distributions across domains during pretraining using per-domain scaling laws to estimate learning potential. ADO requires no proxy models or external knowledge. Experiments on 124M and 1.3B parameter models show comparable or better performance on seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks with negligible wall-clock overhead. Reviewers agree the idea is novel and practically appealing but raise concerns about scalability, heuristic-heavy design, insufficient ablation, and limited theoretical grounding.

### Strengths

- Novel idea: Using online scaling laws for dynamic data selection, replacing the need for pre-trained proxy models or multi-stage processes (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The derivative of loss w.r.t. number of data points provides an intuitive interpretation as loss reduction per data point.
- Practical efficiency: ADO incurs less than 0.4% additional wall-clock time (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Clear readability and motivation: Paper spends reasonable space discussing requirements for data optimization in pretraining (curriculum learning, online, task-agnostic) (Reviewers 2, 4).
- Discovery of a strong "natural" baseline that depends on number of tokens per domain (Reviewer 4).
- Several interesting observations that align with intuition and literature (e.g., lines 428, 409, 431) (Reviewer 1).
- ADO is introduced clearly and is easy to understand (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **Scalability concerns and diminishing returns**: Performance gains decrease with model size. At 124M, ADO outperforms baselines on 6/7 downstream tasks; at 1.3B, only 4/7 (Reviewer 1). The gap between ADO’s average score and the second-best baseline is less pronounced at 1.3B. This raises doubt about ADO scaling to 8B+ models (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). One reviewer notes the “natural” baseline outperforms ADO on ARC-E, SciQ, and LogiQA2 at 1.3B, undermining ADO’s claimed superiority (Reviewer 1).
- **Heavy reliance on heuristics with no ablation**: The method uses multiple hand-designed components: scaling law fitting (Eq. 1), credit assignment (Eq. 2), preference distribution (Eq. 3), temporal averaging (Eq. 4-5). Only Eq. 1 is adequately explained and validated (Reviewer 4). No ablation study shows whether all these heuristics are necessary (Reviewer 4). Reviewers question the choice of smoothing parameter $s$ (e.g., $s=0.5$), the function to compute $\lambda_k$ from $h_k$, and whether simply using $\lambda_k = h_k$ would work (Reviewer 2). The paper lacks sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters ($\gamma_1, s, \gamma_2$) and does not describe how these values were tuned (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **No theoretical justification**: Unlike prior works DoReMi (NeurIPS 2024) and DoGE (which include theoretical appendices), ADO provides no theoretical analysis of why it works (Reviewer 1). This is a significant weakness given the method claims originality but remains purely empirical (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete empirical scope**: Experiments are limited to 124M and 1.3B parameter models; no results at 2.3B, 7B, or 8B scale (Reviewers 1, 2). ADO’s relevance for modern LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B) is unsubstantiated (Reviewer 1). No experiments on vision data or LVLMs/diffusion models (Reviewer 1).
- **Figure 4 shows fewer than half of datasets achieve minimal perplexity under ADO**, suggesting potential overfitting to certain domains at the expense of others. The paper does not analyze why certain datasets fail to converge to minimal perplexity or whether this correlates with dataset characteristics (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing computational complexity analysis**: Despite claiming low overhead, the paper lacks systematic complexity analysis and comparative wall-clock time measurements against other data selection methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient training dynamics analysis**: The paper shows final sampling probabilities (Figure 5) but does not show how $\lambda_k(t)$ and $\partial \hat{L}_k / \partial n$ change with training iterations, which would help understand domain sampling evolution (Reviewer 2). It also does not compare ADO’s training loss convergence to baselines (Reviewer 1).
- **Risk of under-representation**: Some datasets may receive minimal training due to low selection probabilities; no measures to ensure balanced representation across domains are discussed (Reviewer 3).
- **Disagreement among reviewers**: One reviewer is notably more critical, emphasizing that ADO’s lack of theoretical contributions and inconsistent outperformance of the “natural” baseline at 1.3B weakens the paper relative to DoReMi/DoGE. Other reviewers are more positive but still flag the heuristic-heavy design and need for ablation and scaling experiments.
