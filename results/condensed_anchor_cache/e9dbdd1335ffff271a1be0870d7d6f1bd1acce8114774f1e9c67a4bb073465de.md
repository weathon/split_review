## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes normality normalization (NN), a new parametric layer that encourages Gaussian pre-activations. It uses a Yeo–Johnson power transform with an approximate maximum-likelihood estimate computed via a single Newton iteration, plus additive Gaussian noise scaled by hyperparameter ξ. Experiments on CIFAR, Tiny ImageNet, and ImageNet100 with ResNet and ViT backbones show consistent accuracy gains over standard BatchNorm, LayerNorm, InstanceNorm, and GroupNorm. The method also improves noise robustness. While reviewers find the information‑theoretic motivation and the empirical improvements interesting and largely convincing, major concerns are raised about the small experimental scale, suspiciously low baseline accuracies, missing ablations, and incomplete comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Well‑motivated by information theory**: Several reviewers (all except R4) find the argument that Gaussian distributions maximize representational capacity and provide noise robustness to be a principled justification.
- **Novel and principled approach**: The idea of explicitly enforcing Gaussianity during training using a power transform is original; the quadratic approximation that avoids iterative optimization is a technical contribution (R1, R5).
- **Consistent empirical improvements**: Across multiple models (ResNet, ViT), datasets (CIFAR‑10/100, Tiny ImageNet, ImageNet100), and normalization baselines (BN, LN, IN, GN), NN yields higher test accuracy and better robustness in every reported case (R1–R5).
- **Comprehensive analysis of architectural factors**: The impact of model width, depth, and minibatch size is systematically studied (R5).
- **Demonstrated improvement in normality and noise robustness**: The paper shows that pre‑activations become more Gaussian after NN and that the network is more resistant to random perturbations (R5).
- **Quadratic approximation reduces cost**: The approximate MLE solution via a single Newton step makes the method practical (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Experimental scale**: Experiments are limited to small datasets (CIFAR, Tiny ImageNet, ImageNet100). The absence of a full ImageNet experiment is a major concern (R4, R5); R5 notes that many methods (e.g., weight normalization) work on small benchmarks but fail at scale. R4 suggests training at least a small GPT‑2 style model.
- **Suspiciously low baselines**:  
  – ResNet‑50 on ImageNet100 achieves 71.6%, while typical ResNet‑50 accuracy on full ImageNet is > 75% (R4).  
  – ResNet‑18 on CIFAR‑10 with BatchNorm reports 88.89%, but a standard open‑source implementation reaches 93.02% – higher than the proposed BNN’s 90.41% (R5).  
  – Improvements may vanish when compared to properly tuned baselines; data augmentations are not consistently reported (R5).
- **Missing ablation studies**: The individual contributions of the power transform and the additive Gaussian noise are not isolated. If most gains come from the noise, the method resembles Gaussian dropout (Fast Dropout, Variational Dropout) (R5). Sensitivity to the ξ hyperparameter is not shown; if careful tweaking is required, nested cross‑validation should be used (R1).
- **Insufficient comparison with other Gaussianization techniques**: The paper does not compare against other methods that convert distributions to Gaussian (e.g., quantile transformation) (R2). Such comparisons are needed to attribute improvements specifically to the power transform.
- **Limited baseline comparisons**: Only BatchNorm and LayerNorm are compared in many experiments; GroupNorm and adaptive techniques are missing (R3). Also, no comparison to existing noise‑injection methods (e.g., adding Gaussian noise to standard BN) (R1).
- **Computational cost not reported**: No percentage increase in training time is given; the hidden constants in the O(D) complexity may be substantial (R1, R3).
- **Hyperparameter selection unclear**: How ξ (noise scale) and other choices are set across datasets/models is not justified (R4). A plot of accuracy vs. ξ would help (R1).
- **Presentation and writing issues**:  
  – The dimension “N” used for normalization (batch vs. feature) is not clearly defined at first use (R1).  
  – The term “standardization” is used for the mean/std step, conflicting with the usual preprocessing meaning (R1).  
  – The title is awkward (R1).  
  – The narrative between information‑theoretic motivation and the method is disjointed; the connection feels superficial (R2).  
  – The motivation section appears at the end of the paper, which is unconventional (R5).  
  – The claim that “seldom has the question of precisely what distribution…” is inaccurate; the information‑bottleneck and quantization literatures already study this (R5).
- **Missing analysis of joint distribution**: Only individual coordinate normality is addressed; the method’s effect on the joint distribution across features and on feature independence is not studied (R2). The effect on the dimension not being normalized is also unclear (R1).
- **Architecture choice for small datasets**: ViT on CIFAR yields very low accuracy (66.56%); a more suitable architecture like ConvNeXt should be used (R5).
- **Relevance of motivation section**: While several reviewers praise the theoretical foundation (R3), one reviewer (R4) considers Section 5 (motivation) unnecessary, stating that good empirical results are sufficient justification.
