## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents OC-Flow, a training-free framework for guided generation with pre-trained diffusion and flow matching models, formulated as an optimal control problem. It extends guidance to Riemannian manifolds (SO(3)), generalizes existing backprop-through-ODE methods (D-Flow, FlowGrad), and provides convergence guarantees (Proposition 1, Theorem 2) under assumptions (affine Gaussian path, Lipschitz continuity). Experiments cover text-guided image manipulation (CelebA-HQ), conditional molecule generation (up to 9 heavy atoms), and all-atom peptide design (<50 residues). One reviewer found the approach highly original and the theoretical backing strong (score 8), while the other three (scores 6) raised concerns about experimental reproducibility, computational cost, and the gap between continuous theory and discrete implementation.

### Strengths
- **Original framing and theory.** The formulation of guided flow-matching as an optimal control problem is original. The general non-Euclidean framework (SO(3)) comes with rare theoretical convergence analysis (Proposition 1, Theorem 2) and extensive proofs in the appendix, providing a systematic theoretical grounding.
- **Unification and clarification of prior work.** OC-Flow is shown to generalize D-Flow and FlowGrad, clarifying the connections between existing gradient-based techniques. Table 1 gives a helpful comparison of the methods.
- **Training-free guidance.** The approach works with any pre-trained flow model without retraining, making it computationally efficient and practical for real-world settings (though see Weaknesses on inference-time cost).
- **Extension to SO(3).** This is the first work to formalize guidance in SO(3), which is critical for applications in structural biology (protein/peptide design). The SO(3) results validate the importance of geometric inductive bias.
- **Empirical performance on multiple domains.** OC-Flow demonstrates state-of-the-art or competitive results on text-guided image manipulation, conditional molecule generation, and all-atom peptide design. Consistent improvements over D-Flow and FlowGrad are reported across benchmarks.
- **Strong problem motivation and clear literature review.** The paper is well-motivated, with a clear positioning relative to prior work on controlled generation, optimal transport, and Riemannian flow matching.

### Weaknesses
- **Inconsistent or missing baseline comparisons.**  
  *  The results in Table 3 (molecule generation) do not match those of Table 4 in the D-Flow paper (Ben-Hamu et al. 2024). In fact, D-Flow performs better on MAE metrics when original reported values are used.  
  *  The results in Table 5 (peptide design) scale differently than those in Table 1 of the PepFlow paper (Li et al. 2024) and fail to align with that baseline.  
  *  No error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests are reported for any experiment. For instance, Table 5 shows 0.795 vs 0.793—without uncertainty it is unclear whether OC-Flow is truly better. The same holds for Tables 2, 3, and 4.  
  → The practical superiority of OC-Flow over existing methods is therefore questionable.

- **Gap between continuous theory and discrete implementation.** All theoretical analysis (convergence proofs) is performed in the continuous-time regime, but the practical algorithm operates in discrete time (as acknowledged by the authors). This disconnect means the proven guarantees do not directly apply to the reported experiments. The paper does not provide a discrete-time analysis, a discussion of how the continuous results bound or approximate the discrete behavior, nor a continuous implementation.

- **Computational cost and scalability are not assessed.** Producing a single sample requires solving an optimization problem that involves repeated evaluation of the vector field and its gradients (20+ steps per sample). This makes generation orders of magnitude slower than unguided sampling. No generation times or memory usage are reported for any experiment. Consequently, practical applicability (especially for high-dimensional datasets like large molecules) is unclear. The computational complexity (linear, quadratic, or worse in input dimension) is not analysed. A comparison against plain (non-optimizing) baselines in terms of runtime and memory is missing.

- **Lack of ablation and necessary experiments.**  
  *  The claimed benefit of SO(3) optimization is not demonstrated: no results are split into Euclidean-only vs. SO(3) versions, and no ablation study isolates the effect of the Riemannian component.  
  *  The experiments are limited to relatively small-scale problems (CelebA-HQ, molecules ≤9 heavy atoms, peptides <50 residues).  
  *  No runtime comparison of OC-Flow vs. D-Flow and FlowGrad is provided (Table 1 only reports memory).  
  *  A brief comparison with discrete flow techniques (which have shown promise in similar applications) would help contextualize OC-Flow’s advantages and trade-offs.  
  *  The paper does not discuss how OC-Flow compares to other Riemannian flow matching methods on SO(3) or SE(3), nor whether it can be extended to hybrid Euclidean+Riemannian tasks.

- **Presentation and reproducibility issues.**  
  *  The paper is difficult to read: key concepts (co-state flow, E-MSA) are introduced with insufficient intuition, and the structure mixes methodology with theoretical proof details, making it hard to separate novel contributions from existing work.  
  *  Figure 1 is unclear and does not effectively communicate the method. Few figures overall hinder understanding.  
  *  No experimental details are given: model architectures, hyperparameters, training procedures, preprocessing, computational resources, and code are all absent. This severely limits reproducibility.

- **Concerns about contribution scope.** Equation (2) may be a trivial extension of existing formulations (e.g., Fjelde et al. 2024). Without definitive experimental results (confidence intervals, ablation on SO(3)), the tangible benefit over simpler Euclidean approaches is unproven. The focus on classifier-guided generation in SO(3) is considered niche.

- **Assumptions of the theory may not hold in practice.** The convergence proofs rely on Lipschitz continuity and boundedness of the reward function and its gradients. The paper does not discuss whether these assumptions are satisfied in the actual applications (e.g., whether the Lipschitz constant of the vector field is small or large, or whether the boundedness holds for image or molecular losses).

- **Terminology “training-free” is debatable.** Although no model retraining is needed, the method requires solving an optimization problem at inference time for each sample, which involves many ODE simulations and gradient computations. This is not “free” in the usual sense.

- **Scalability to large molecules and real-time use is unclear.** The reviewers request discussion of how to scale the method (e.g., to larger molecules or complex distributions) and whether real-time guidance (requiring immediate outputs) is feasible.
