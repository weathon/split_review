## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Visual Modality Instruction (VIM), a setting that evaluates how well Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) follow textual instructions presented solely in images. The authors construct VIM-Bench from eight existing benchmarks (OKVQA, MM-Vet, MathVista, MMMU, TextVQA, ChartQA, etc.) and train V-MLLM, a model aimed at robust instruction-following in both text-modality (TEM) and visual-modality (VIM) settings. Evaluations reveal a significant performance gap for open-source models in VIM; after tuning on a VIM training corpus, open-source models improve. Two reviewers rated the paper 5 (confidence 4 each), noting interesting findings and useful benchmarking; two rated it 3 (confidence 5 and 4), questioning the motivation, technical novelty, and fairness of the evaluation.

### Strengths
- **Interesting and practical findings** (R1, R4): (1) Open-source and closed-source VLMs are robust to the position of textual instruction in the image. (2) Two-stage instruction tuning and mixed instruction tuning yield similar performance. (3) The paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of both open- and closed-source models in the VIM setting, revealing noteworthy gaps.
- **Identifies a genuine capability gap** (R3): The paper highlights that MLLMs struggle to follow text instructions embedded in visual formats, and proposes VIM as a challenging setting to assess this.
- **Constructs a new benchmark and training corpus** (R3, R4): VIM-Bench adapts eight representative benchmarks, and the VIM training corpus (built by rendering textual instructions onto images) is shown to effectively improve instruction-following in both text and visual modalities.
- **Clear presentation** (R2): The paper is easy to read, and some figures are good.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation is unclear or limited** (R2, R4): It is not obvious what real application scenarios require instructions to be provided *only* via printed images. Reviewer 2 argues that the examples in Figure 1 do not demonstrate the necessity because the predictions do not change with different image inputs, and the benchmark questions themselves are closely tied to the image content, undermining the claimed importance of the VIM task. Reviewer 4 asks for concrete scenarios where such visual-only instructions are needed.
- **VIM is essentially an OCR test** (R1, R2, R4): Several reviewers note that the VIM setting primarily probes a specific type of OCR capability (reading text in images). Reviewer 1 observes that the setting is similar to the original task of OCR, and the training data is simply an augmentation of existing datasets by rendering textual instructions onto images. Reviewer 2 states that the benchmark is “primarily designed to probe the OCR capability of MLLMs” and has limited vision. Reviewer 4 adds that text recognition is a known limitation of general-purpose MLLMs, so the VIM evaluation risks becoming an OCR test rather than a test of instruction-following. To fairly assess visual instruction-following, the impact of text-recognition weaknesses should be minimized.
- **Limited technical contribution** (R1, R2): (a) The VIM training methodology is supervised with two variants that differ only in data mixing strategies. (b) The training procedure is “a little bit trivial” and “task-specific” (R2). (c) The constructed training corpus is a straightforward augmentation (rendering text onto images) of existing data.
- **Unfair evaluation and missing baselines** (R2, R4): (1) Evaluating open-source MLLMs in the VIM setting and comparing them to proprietary models or the specially trained V-MLLM may be unfair, because open-source models are unlikely to have encountered such visual instructions before, whereas proprietary/specialized models may have. This diminishes the experimental relevance. (2) Stronger baselines are missing: integrating an OCR front-end with MLLMs would be a straightforward approach; also, since visual instruction processing resembles a two-step process and mixed instructions improve performance, chain-of-thought prompting could provide a stronger baseline. (3) Reviewer 2 recommends comparing to MLLMs that excel at OCR.
- **Issues with the instruction location experiment (Section 2.1.2)** (R3): (a) The authors omitted a comparison with the “left” position without explanation. (b) While claiming GPT-4V and LLaVA-1.5 are robust to location, GPT-4V shows nearly 10% performance difference between “bottom” and “top”. (c) The paper could also consider constructing the VIM corpus with randomly selected positions for the embedded text instructions.
- **Performance drops on text-heavy tasks after VIM tuning** (R1, R3): In Table 3 (TEM setting), V-MLLM’s performance drops on TextVQA and ChartQA compared to LLaVA-1.5. Since these tasks require understanding text in images, this drop appears to contradict the hypothesis that VIM training helps with text understanding in images. Reviewer 1 also asks why models achieve lower performance on TextVQA after VIM tuning (Table 7).
- **Unclear training details** (R3): It is not specified whether V-MLLM is initialized with pretrained LLaVA-1.5 weights and whether all components (image encoder, projector, LLM backbone) are fine-tuned.
- **Potential image resizing inconsistency** (R3): The paper states that it keeps the resolution of raw images and adds text with the same font size, but most MLLMs resize images to a standard size. This resizing could cause inconsistent text instruction resolution across images.
- **Figure 2 is overly complex** (R3): It contains excessive information and is difficult to interpret. Simplification is needed.
- **Minor formatting issue** (R2): The citation format is incorrect; the paper should use \citep{} rather than \citet{} for parenthetical citations.

*Note: Reviewers differ in their overall assessment. Two reviewers (R1, R3) find the VIM setting interesting and the contributions solid (scores 5), while the other two (R2, R4) see the motivation as weak, the technical contribution as limited, and the evaluation as unfair (scores 3).*
