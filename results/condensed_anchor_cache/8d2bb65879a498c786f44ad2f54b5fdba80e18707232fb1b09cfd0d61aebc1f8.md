## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes FAIR, a two-stage coarse-to-fine framework for long-term mesh-based physics simulation. The first stage uses a continuous graph ODE model to predict multiple future states at coarse temporal intervals. The second stage applies a refinement (interpolation) module to these coarse predictions to produce short-term states. The process alternates autoregressively to generate long trajectories. FAIR is evaluated on four benchmark datasets (CylinderFlow, Airfoil, DeformingPlate, InflatingFont) and compared against several baselines, reporting error reductions of up to 25.4%. The paper is generally well-motivated and presents a novel future-to-present learning perspective. However, significant concerns exist regarding the experimental validity, evaluation fairness, and scope of the demonstrations, with one reviewer (Reviewer 2) strongly questioning the setup and results.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: The idea of using coarse long-term predictions to refine short-term predictions is sound and novel (Reviewers 1,3). Reviewer 2 notes some novelty in applying such a prediction+refinement approach to physics graph networks, though similar ideas exist in video prediction (e.g., Harvey et al., NeurIPS 2022) and PDE prediction (e.g., Cachay et al., NeurIPS 2023; Lippe et al., NeurIPS 2023).
- **Empirical results**: The proposed method achieves lower errors than existing baselines on all four benchmarks, with clear ablation studies confirming that the refiner improves accuracy (Reviewers 1,3,4; Reviewer 2 also notes the ablation positive).
- **Clarity of paper organization**: The paper is well-written and easy to follow for most sections (Reviewers 1,3). Figure 2 helps explain the architecture (Reviewer 2).
- **Significance of the problem**: Improving long-term stability in autoregressive physics prediction is an open and important issue (Reviewer 2). The ablation studies on trajectory length \(L\) and timestep ratio \(r\) are informative.

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Section 4 (methodology) has structural problems, is difficult to follow, and contains unclear or unfounded statements (e.g., “Given these limitations…”, “they are inferior in modeling…”, “multi-task learning framework” without explanation) (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 4 also notes poor writing, duplicated expressions (e.g., “which are flexible to produce flexible outputs”), and missing labels in figures (e.g., Fig 5 a,b,c,d not annotated; unclear colors).
  - The term “interpolation” is misleading for the refinement step; the procedure seems to refine existing coarse predictions at discrete timesteps, not interpolate between them (Reviewer 2).
  - Fig. 1 is broken in many PDF viewers (Ubuntu, Firefox, Evince) (Reviewer 2). Typo: “unstructured surfaces” should be “unstructured grids” (Reviewer 2). Duplicate notation for \(L\) (prediction length vs. number of layers) (Reviewer 3). Algorithm reference in Section 4.2 should read “learning algorithm” not “inference algorithm” (Reviewer 3).

- **Evaluation scope and datasets**:
  - Only 2D experiments are presented; scalability to 3D is unclear (Reviewer 1). Missing generalization tests to different initial/boundary conditions or material properties (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 2 notes the paper does not report physical parameters (Reynolds number, Mach number) for the Navier-Stokes cases, hampering comparability.
  - The experimental scope appears limited to steady-state simulations (converging to a fixed solution); the paper’s conclusion itself mentions failure on non-steady dynamics (Reviewer 2). The figures (Figs. 3 and 4) show only steady-state cases, which undermines the claimed advantage in capturing non-linear complex dynamics; for steady-state predictions, temporal interpolation after convergence is trivial (Reviewer 2). It is unclear whether only a steady-state subset of the Pfaff et al. (2021) datasets was used (Reviewer 2). Comparison to direct steady-state solvers (e.g., Thuerey et al., AIAA Journal 2020) is missing but could be relevant given the scope (Reviewer 2).
  - Reviewer 3 requests a comparison with GMR-GMUS (Han et al., 2022), a related method for long-term mesh simulation that achieves 400-step results on CylinderFlow. Its absence makes it hard to assess the advancement over state-of-the-art.

- **Baseline implementation and evaluation fairness**:
  - Baseline results in Table 1 are taken directly from Cao et al. (2023) without clear attribution in the table heading; the paper states “consistent with” but claims to use “their implementation” (Reviewer 2). A fair comparison requires a consistent setup across all methods (Reviewer 2).
  - The reported RMSE values differ dramatically from those in the original MeshGraphNet paper (Pfaff et al., 2021) for the same datasets (e.g., CylinderFlow 50-step RMSE: 43.9e-3 here vs. 6.3e-3 in Pfaff et al.), even though the same architecture is claimed. No explanation is given for this discrepancy, raising serious concerns about the baseline implementation (Reviewer 2).
  - In Fig. 4, the MeshGraphNet visualization appears to correspond to a different test case than the FAIR visualization (Reviewer 2). In Fig. 3, the flow fields have visual artifacts even in the ground truth, and colorbars/timestep labels are missing (Reviewer 2).
  - The ablation study (Fig. 5) lacks clear subfigure labels (Reviewers 2,3,4) and does not test baselines with the same \(r\) and \(L\) values used by FAIR. The main improvement may come from training with a larger trajectory length \(L\) (matching an entire rollout) rather than the coarse-then-refine architecture, as \(L=4\) reduces error by ~12% (Reviewer 2). Related works (Sirignano et al. 2020, Kochkov et al. 2021, Um et al. 2020) show that matching longer trajectories improves stability (Reviewer 2). Testing the \(L=1\) model on unsteady cases is necessary to disentangle this (Reviewer 2).
  - Reviewers 1,3,4 did not raise these issues; they considered the experiments strong. However, Reviewer 2’s critique is severe and questions the very validity of the reported improvements.

- **Missing ablation and comparisons**:
  - In Table 1, for DeformingPlate at 50 steps, MS-GNN-Grid obtains the best RMSE (2.78); this should be highlighted and discussed (Reviewer 3). The proposal is not uniformly better.
  - Overfitting concern: the refiner is trained on future state information, which may lead to overfitting compared to purely autoregressive methods (Reviewer 1). Generalization ability vs. autoregressive baselines is not assessed (Reviewer 1).
  - Questions that overlap with criticisms: Can the coarse-refine framework be applied to baselines and benefit them as well? (Reviewer 4). Does the offset computation in observation space (Eq. 11) use all node representations or just local neighbors? (Reviewer 3). Why is the offset computed in observation space rather than latent space? (Reviewer 3). Clarify the temporal step size and the equation \(t_l = t_0 + rl - r + 1\) (Reviewer 3). For the refiner loss (Eq. 13), is it never trained to refine the \(L\) future frames? (Reviewer 2).
