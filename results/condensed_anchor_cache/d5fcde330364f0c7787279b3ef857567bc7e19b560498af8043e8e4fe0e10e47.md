## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces DIFOCON, a data-driven framework for model-free finite-time optimal control of nonlinear systems. It reformulates control as a generative task using a conditional diffusion model with a dual-Unet architecture, an inverse dynamics module, and a retraining strategy. Experiments on two nonlinear systems (ring and swing models) report reductions in target loss (≥26.9%) and control energy (≥15.8%) versus two baselines (DiffPhyCon, PID).

### Strengths
- **Generative reformulation of control**: Casting finite-time optimal control as a conditional generative task (Eq. 4, 5) is an interesting perspective (Reviewer 1).
- **Useful methodological components**: The dual-Unet architecture, learnable inverse dynamics module, and iterative retraining strategy are practical additions; the ablation study confirms each component’s benefit (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Challenging problem setting**: The paper addresses model-free optimal control of nonlinear systems with unknown dynamics, which is a difficult problem (Reviewer 3).
- **Novelty viewpoint**: Reviewer 3 finds the use of diffusion models for control “intriguing and novel,” though Reviewers 1 and 2 question novelty relative to existing work (e.g., Ajay et al. 2022, Li et al. 2024). This contrast in opinion is noted.
- **Clear organization and ablation**: The paper is well structured and includes an ablation study (Reviewers 2, 4). The framework is relatively complete (Reviewer 4).
- **Empirical improvement**: DIFOCON outperforms the two baselines (DiffPhyCon and PID) on both test systems (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical contribution**: Equation (4) is a standard log-likelihood; the connection to the original problem (Eq. 1) is not justified, and the conditioning on the optimization goal \(J\) appears ad‑hoc. No derivation explains why the generative formulation is appropriate for control (Reviewer 1).
- **Novelty concerns**:  
  – Classifier-free guidance was already used in a related approach (Li et al. 2024); the only difference claimed is how labeling works (Reviewer 1).  
  – Many prior works use conditional diffusion for control; the key difference from Ajay et al. 2022 (which also uses an inverse dynamics module) is not discussed (Reviewer 2).  
  Reviewer 3 finds the approach novel, but this is a minority view against the majority criticism.
- **Incomplete related work**: Section 2.1 omits a large body of deep‑learning‑based control and provides only two references for finite‑time control. Existing open‑loop control literature is also not discussed (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **No running costs or constraints**: The formulation only includes a terminal state cost (Eq. 1); it is unclear how running state costs or state constraints (often crucial for physical systems) could be incorporated (Reviewer 1).
- **Problematic conditioning on \(J\)**: If the chosen optimization goal \(J\) is too high, the generated policy may be forced to be worse than optimal; if too low, no feasible trajectories satisfy the condition. Predicting a good \(J\) in advance is difficult (Reviewer 1; also posed as a question).
- **Implementation clarity**: The steps integrating inpainting, inverse dynamics, and retraining are not clearly explained; an algorithm figure is missing (Reviewer 1). The denoising principle and its role in measurement noise are not described (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient experimental validation**:  
  – Only two dynamical systems are tested (ring and swing models); both have weak nonlinearity. Experiments on highly nonlinear systems (e.g., n‑DoF manipulators, quadcopters, ground vehicles) are needed (Reviewers 1, 4).  
  – No statistical measures (standard deviation, confidence intervals) are provided; differences in results may be marginal (Reviewer 2).  
  – Measurement noise is not considered, which is unrealistic for practical applications (Reviewer 4).  
  – Scalability to higher‑dimensional problems is not investigated (Reviewer 1).  
  – Power consumption and detailed network architecture are not shown (Reviewer 4).
- **Baseline selection and comparison**: The choice of DiffPhyCon is questionable because its performance is far behind other methods; PID is not designed for nonlinear systems, so its weak performance is expected. Comparisons with data‑driven nonlinear methods (e.g., Koopman operator, feedback‑linearization) are missing (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **No performance guarantees**: The method offers no formal guarantees; it is unknown whether the computed control solves the optimal control problem or what the performance gap is. Without guarantees, recommending the method for practical control is difficult (Reviewer 3). When and why the method should be used is unclear.
- **Retraining data quality**: The retraining strategy uses model‑generated trajectories; these may be feasible but suboptimal. How such data ensures training improvement is not justified (Reviewer 2; posed as a question).
- **Inverse dynamics role unclear**: The specific function of the inverse dynamics module in the experiments is not fully elucidated (Reviewer 4; despite ablation, more detail is requested).
- **Readability and notation issues**: Several linguistic mistakes and symbol confusion (τ used for both the dataset and the conditional diffusion model, Eq. 11) reduce readability (Reviewers 1, 4).
