## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes QDHF (Quality Diversity through Human Feedback), which learns diversity metrics from human judgments via contrastive learning, replacing manually crafted diversity measures used in standard QD algorithms. The method is evaluated in robotics, reinforcement learning, and computer vision (text-to-image generation using stable diffusion + CLIP). QDHF outperforms AURORA in automatic diversity discovery, matches QD with hand-crafted metrics, and improves diversity in image generation from a single prompt.

### Strengths
- The integration of quality diversity and human feedback is innovative and addresses an important open problem: learning diversity metrics automatically rather than relying on hand-designed measures.
- QDHF is novel and shows empirical efficacy across three disparate domains (robotics, RL, CV), supporting its generality.
- In the latent space illumination experiment, the use of stable diffusion with CLIP (instead of StyleGAN) is a more sophisticated and functional choice.
- The paper is generally easy to understand and clearly presents the approach.
- The method has potential benefits for exploration, personalization, and fairness in open-ended optimization tasks.

### Weaknesses
- Only one baseline (AURORA and its variants) is compared, and AURORA is unsupervised; claiming that QDHF “outperforms current QD methods” is an unfair comparison given the different settings. Comparisons with other QD algorithms, standard RLHF, and simple heuristics (e.g., varying generation hyperparameters) are missing.
- The evaluation is predominantly quantitative. For a human-feedback method, qualitative metrics such as user satisfaction or user studies are absent. In the image generation task, only a single prompt (“a photo of an astronaut riding a horse on mars”) with 16 generated images is shown; this risks cherry-picking and lacks large-scale quantitative evaluation. Some generated images contain unreasonable artifacts (e.g., a big brown helmet above the astronaut), and selecting images by highest CLIP score may itself diminish diversity.
- All experiments use automatic feedback (e.g., CLIP scores) despite “human feedback” in the title. The paper does not address how subjective biases, inconsistencies, or noisy human input affect the learned diversity metric, nor does it discuss sample complexity or strategies for reducing the amount of required human feedback.
- Scalability and robustness are insufficiently analyzed: there is no discussion of computational resources, performance in high-dimensional state/action spaces of complex RL, or the impact of increasing latent dimensionality. An ablation study on design choices is absent, and the lack of supplementary materials makes implementation choices opaque.
- The writing contains numerous typos, and the importance and use cases of diversity metrics are not clearly motivated in the introduction. The paper is complex for readers not well-versed in quality diversity.
- The claim of extending RLHF to consider diversity is not supported by experiments in typical RLHF environments (e.g., dialog generation or image captioning), where the trade-off between diversity and rationality/accuracy would need simultaneous evaluation.
