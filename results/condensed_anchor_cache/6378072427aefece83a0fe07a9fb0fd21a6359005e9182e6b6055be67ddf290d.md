## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper provides the first formalisation of the least-privilege principle (LPP) for machine learning, characterising the fundamental trade-off between a representation’s utility for a prediction task and its leakage of information about any attribute other than the task label. Under realistic assumptions (strictly positive posterior, label sharing with the service provider), the authors prove that no feature map can simultaneously satisfy LPP and high utility (measured as mutual information between representation and label). The analysis is worst-case over all possible attributes. Empirical validation is performed on tabular datasets (12 attributes, pairwise label-sensitive attribute) and on an image classification dataset, measuring leakage via a Bayesian optimal adversary. Experiments also examine censoring models and show that suppressing the most leaked attribute increases leakage about others.

### Strengths
- The paper proves an interesting inherent trade-off between model utility and information leakage due to releasing representations (compared to only releasing labels). The fundamental leakage from the label itself is used as a baseline.
- The theoretical analysis is clear about its worst-case assumptions and conveys insights effectively.
- Experiments support the proved trade-off: a positive correlation is observed between the number of attributes that incur high leakage and the model’s utility. The censoring experiment shows that blocking one attribute increases leakage about others.
- The paper reveals the fundamental limits of least-privilege learning, which is important for understanding privacy in machine learning.
- The work is well-written and easy to follow (multiple reviewers noted clarity).

### Weaknesses
- **Model splitting and practical setup**: The assumed split (clients use only representation layers, server uses only classification head) is counter-intuitive. Since classification heads are typically small and easy to train, there is little incentive for clients to share representations; they could instead download or tune the head locally. This also differs from the setting in Melis et al. 2019, where clients compute only one embedding layer.  
- **Definition of leakage is overly strong**: The paper defines information leakage as successful inference of *any* attribute, which may be too stringent. Many attributes are less sensitive, and the proved trade-off may be largely an artefact of this worst-case definition. The practical impact is unclear when only a subset of attributes are considered sensitive. The example \(Z = \arg\max_y P(Y=y|X)\) (achieving Bayes-optimal classification with potentially less leakage) highlights that the current theoretical framework may not capture all relevant behaviours.  
- **Limited comparison to existing information-theoretic privacy work**: The paper does not clearly differentiate itself from prior works such as Zhao et al. 2020, Brown et al. 2022, and the Privacy Funnel / Privacy-Utility Tradeoff literature (Salamatian et al.). The key technical challenge when the sensitive attribute is not fixed but a superset of the input is not explained.  
- **Empirical evaluation is narrow**: Experiments are conducted on only a single tabular dataset and a single image classification model. The generalisability to other datasets, tasks (e.g., NLP), and model architectures is not demonstrated. Additionally, only single-attribute inference is tested; the applicability to multi-attribute inference or data reconstruction attacks is unclear.  
- **No comparison with cutting-edge defence schemes**: The empirical part does not compare against advanced defence methods that mitigate attribute leakage (e.g., adversarial training, information-theoretic regularisation). If such methods can circumvent the trade-off, the paper’s value is limited.  
- **Lack of practical guidance**: The discussion on how users and service providers can manage the trade-offs is limited. More elaboration on implications for practice (e.g., differential privacy at test time, de-anonymisation, encryption) would strengthen the relevance.  
- **Presentation issues**:  
  - Transition orders in the Markov chain \(Y - X - W\) (Section 2) are unclear.  
  - In Figure 4 (bottom right), the meaning of colour is ambiguous; a blue colour is used for negative values without explanation.  
  - Negative model utility \(\tilde{I}_{\infty}(Y, Z)\) is not defined or interpreted.  
  - Assumption A requires a positive posterior but does not impose a lower bound; it is unclear whether the posterior can be arbitrarily close to a point distribution.  
  - Formulas (12), (14), and (19) in Appendix A lack punctuation.  
- **Contribution is limited** (minority view: one reviewer found the contribution limited, while another rated it 4/4; others rated 2–3). The paper does not propose practical mitigation strategies, and the theoretical result is derived under strong assumptions.
