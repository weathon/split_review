## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates why grokking (sudden generalization after overfitting) occurs and why it often requires regularization. It identifies Softmax Collapse (SC) – floating-point errors when logits become large – as a key obstacle. It argues that without regularization, gradients align with a “naïve loss minimization” (NLM) direction that scales logits without improving generalization, eventually causing SC. The paper proposes **StableMax** (a numerically stable activation to prevent SC) and **⊥Grad** (an optimizer that removes the NLM component to accelerate generalization). Methods are tested on modular addition and similar tasks with MLPs and small transformers.

### Strengths
- **Novel discovery**: Identifies the crucial role of numerical stability (Softmax Collapse) in grokking, and reveals that SC results from naïve loss minimization under cross-entropy loss (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Well-written and well-motivated**: The research problem is conceptually well-motivated, aligns with current challenges, and the paper is easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 4). The hypothesis that SC inhibits grokking is interesting (Reviewer 2).
- **Concrete proposals**: Based on theoretical insights, the authors propose two concrete contributions – StableMax and ⊥Grad – that enable grokking without regularization and lead to faster generalization (Reviewers 1, 5).
- **Comprehensive analysis**: The paper provides detailed insights into the mechanics of grokking, with empirical examples that support the claims (Reviewers 4, 5).

**Contrast**: While most reviewers find the paper engaging and well-supported (scores 8,8,8), Reviewer 2 (score 5) is notably less convinced, questioning the evidence for NLM and the justification over weight decay; Reviewer 5 (score 6) also raises concerns about practical necessity.

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient evidence for Naïve Loss Minimization (NLM)** – Reviewer 2 (p4) and Reviewer 1 (p3) flag serious concerns. Reviewer 2 argues that prior work (Lyu & Li, 2019) shows gradient descent with cross‑entropy loss performs *margin maximization* after 100% training accuracy, which contradicts the claim that optimization follows the NLM direction (Eq. 9) that does not change predictions. The paper must address this contradiction and provide more direct empirical evidence that the trajectory aligns with NLM. Reviewer 1 adds that NLM has already been proven in linear models, deep linear networks, and homogeneous networks; the paper should explicitly acknowledge and discuss these connections. Also, NLM only holds for cross‑entropy loss – the limitations for other losses (e.g., squared loss) should be discussed.

2. **Lack of comparison with established regularization techniques** – Multiple reviewers question why StableMax and ⊥Grad should be preferred over simple, established methods.  
   - **Weight decay**: Reviewer 2 (p1) and Reviewer 3 (p1) ask for a comparison of ⊥AdamW against AdamW with various non‑zero weight decay values on modular addition tasks. The paper does not demonstrate advantages in computational efficiency, hyperparameter sensitivity, or robustness.  
   - **Temperature scaling / label smoothing**: Reviewer 5 (p1) and Reviewer 1 (p2) note that softmax stability issues are often handled with temperature scaling or label smoothing – computationally cheaper and easier. The paper should clarify the specific scenarios where StableMax provides a unique benefit that cannot be achieved by these simpler alternatives.

3. **Inadequate analysis of StableMax properties** – Reviewers highlight several gaps:  
   - **Self‑attention layers**: Reviewer 1 (p2) asks whether SC occurs in self‑attention layers (not just the final layer) and how it impacts model behavior; the paper should investigate this.  
   - **Sparsity**: Reviewer 1 (p2) worries that StableMax’s slower growth rate could hinder its ability to model sparse attention patterns typical in transformers. An analysis comparing sparsity patterns of attention matrices with softmax vs. StableMax is needed.  
   - **Optimization landscape**: Reviewer 2 (p2) calls for analysis of how StableMax affects the sharpness of the loss landscape, the distribution of weights, and whether it introduces new optimization challenges.  
   - **Comparison with simpler methods**: As above (point 2), a direct comparison with temperature scaling / label smoothing would help justify StableMax’s necessity (Reviewer 5, p1).

4. **Underdeveloped exploration of ⊥Grad** – Several reviewers request more thorough analysis:  
   - **Benchmarking against standard optimizers**: Reviewer 2 (p3) and Reviewer 5 (p2) ask for comparisons with SGD, Adam, etc., on varied tasks (including non‑grokking tasks), and investigation of sensitivity to learning rate, batch size, and architecture.  
   - **Combination with Adam**: Reviewer 1 (p4) asks whether ⊥Grad+Adam (⊥Adam) would accelerate grokking; empirical results with different optimizers would be useful.  
   - **Comparison with existing techniques**: Reviewer 1 (p4) notes that the motivation of ⊥Grad (amplifying angular velocity) is similar to normalization layers and weight decay; Reviewer 5 (p2) asks for comparison with gradient‑diversity or variance‑regularization methods. The paper should clarify specific advantages.  
   - **Convergence and stability**: Reviewer 2 (p3) notes the paper lacks analysis of convergence behavior and stability beyond grokking tasks. Reviewer 5 (p3) also asks about impact on margin and generalization when logit scaling is prevented, and the computational overhead of both StableMax and ⊥Grad.

5. **Limited scope of experiments** – Reviewer 3 (p2) points out that tests are only on two‑layer NNs and one‑layer transformers; deeper transformers (e.g., GPT2‑small architecture) should be tested to verify scalability. Reviewer 2 (p1) notes the paper does not demonstrate utility at larger scales or provide comparisons with standard approaches at such scales.

6. **Unfair comparison in Section 4.1** – Reviewer 3 (p3) argues that comparing one‑hot encoding with binary/scalar representations is unfair because one‑hot vectors contain no information about integers, while binary/scalar vectors do; also, the model on binary/scalar has fewer parameters, making overfitting harder. A fair comparison would use randomly generated d‑dim vectors on a uniform sphere and adjust model width to match parameter count.

7. **Minor issues** (Reviewer 4): The phrase “grokking tasks” should be reworded as “tasks where grokking is commonly observed”. Figure placement could be improved for readability. Line 302‑304 formatting (digits vs. i) should be corrected.
