## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper applies Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) to LLM alignment, learning a reward model from demonstration data only (no explicit human preferences). The authors derive a bi-level optimization framework, propose a tractable surrogate (Eq. 6) and a practical algorithm (Eq. 11) using PPO, and show that the method outperforms SFT and SPIN on TL;DR and UltraChat. The work connects IRL to the LLM setting and reinterprets SPIN as a special case. Reviewers agree on the timeliness and clarity but raise concerns about theoretical grounding, empirical thoroughness, and comparisons to alternative methods.

### Strengths
- The application of IRL to LLM fine-tuning is timely and creative, extending IRL to new domains (summarization, dialogue). Reviewers note this extends existing IRL work and provides new insights into both fields.
- The paper is generally well-written, with a clear overview of RLHF limitations, solid related work, and a well-motivated problem statement. The formulation is intuitively understandable.
- A theoretical connection is made between the proposed method and SPIN, showing SPIN as a special case of the broader IRL framework. This could spur further theoretical insights.
- The empirical evaluation (on TL;DR and UltraChat) is described in detail, including implementations, codebases, and hyperparameters, aiding reproducibility.
- The method outperforms baselines (SFT, SPIN) on almost all reported metrics, demonstrating the practical benefit of IRL over behavioral cloning in the LLM setting.
- The paper provides mathematical formulations and convergence guarantees (Theorem 4.2), though these are adaptations of known IRL results.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical derivation gaps and flawed claim of equivalence.** The derivation from Eq. 5a to Eq. 6 is unclear, and Lemma 4.1 is considered weak (the bound may be vacuous). More importantly, Eq. 11 (log-sigmoid) is **not equivalent** to Eq. 6 (linear forcing); they differ in update magnitude per pair, leading to different optimization trajectories. This breaks the stated connection between what is implemented and what is claimed to be optimized. Reviewers request clarification or acknowledgment of the discrepancy. The paper could simply start from Eq. 6 citing IRL literature and skip the questionable derivation.
- **Limited empirical thoroughness.** Only two datasets (TL;DR, UltraChat) and one pretrained model checkpoint are used. For an algorithmic contribution, the evaluation should span diverse tasks (e.g., HHH, coding) and multiple model sizes/checkpoints. No error bars or indication of multiple seeds are provided, making it impossible to assess statistical significance.
- **Missing comparisons: iterative DPO and RLHF.** The method closely resembles iterative DPO: a model generates two responses, the ground-truth demonstration is treated as preferred, and PPO is used instead of DPO. The paper should compare against:
  * Iterative DPO without any demonstration data.
  * The same setup but using DPO rather than PPO (to isolate the impact of the optimization algorithm).
  * Standard RLHF (SFT + RLHF) on the same metrics, to see if the advantage over SFT persists after RLHF tuning, or if the proposed method can match SFT+RLHF without preference data.
- **Low reward model accuracy.** In Figure 2(a), the reward accuracy peaks at 58.8%, barely above random (50%). This raises doubts about the quality of the learned reward.
- **Instability observed in SPIN baseline.** The performance on the "Chat" task drops from 79.19% to 48.04% after one SPIN iteration, suggesting potential instability or misconfiguration. The authors should explain this.
- **Practical scalability and computational cost.** The bi-level optimization requires solving an inner RL problem (PPO) in each iteration, which is computationally expensive. The paper should report training/memory/runtime costs (e.g., how long one iteration of SPIN vs. one iteration of IRL takes) and discuss scalability compared to RLHF pipelines.
- **Assumption 4.1 (bounded rewards).** The paper assumes bounded rewards without discussing how this holds in practice or whether it limits applicability to certain reward function classes.
- **Dependence on high-quality demonstration data.** The method relies on the quality and quantity of demonstrations, but there is no discussion of the relative cost of obtaining high-quality demonstrations vs. human preference labels. Experiments with varying amounts or quality (noisy/suboptimal) of demonstration data are missing (e.g., comparison to IQLearn robustness).
- **Lack of novel algorithmic insights.** While the application is new, the algorithmic design (bi-level IRL with PPO) is largely inherited from prior IRL work; the paper does not introduce novel components beyond the adaptation.
- **Minor writing issues.** Several typos exist (e.g., line 148 "wo" → "two", line 290 "detailw" → "detail"). The paper should also cite/discuss meta-learning techniques for their relevance to the challenges of bi-level optimization.
- **Synthetic preferences vs. human preferences.** The method generates synthetic preference data; there is no analysis of how these compare to actual human preferences.
