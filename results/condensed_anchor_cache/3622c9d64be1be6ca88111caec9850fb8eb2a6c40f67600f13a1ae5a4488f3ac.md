## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses the problem of unreliable human annotations in RLHF reward modeling, particularly focusing on length bias. The authors hypothesize that annotators' preferences can be unreliable due to cognitive biases, knowledge gaps, etc., and propose to incorporate reliability scores into the preference learning objective. They construct the Length Incentivized Evaluations (LIE) dataset from TruthfulQA, with four answer types: Short Correct, Short Incorrect, Long Correct, Long Incorrect. Human annotators (20) provide preferences, confirming that annotators prefer longer over correct answers. Standard reward models trained on these preferences exhibit the same length bias. To mitigate this, the authors propose Reliability Aware Preference Learning (RAPL) with two incorporation strategies: Reward Adjustment (temperature scaling of the reward) and Probability Adjustment (interpolation with a random guessing distribution). Reliability scores are obtained via (1) annotator self-reported confidence, (2) LLM-based autograder with CoT prompting, or (3) a classifier trained on the curated TRUE dataset (Testing Reasoning and Understanding Errors, 1000 preference judgments on examples with known correct answers from MMLU, BigBench, etc.). Experiments on LIE and HelpSteer2 show that Probability Adjustment with TRUE-calibrated scores yields the best CLR scores, though with uneven trends and many combinations outperformed by normal preference learning.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important and under-explored problem: making preference learning robust to unreliable human feedback in RLHF, with solid grounding in ML research from other domains.
- The LIE dataset is a well-designed, controlled dataset for evaluating length bias in reward models, useful for future work. The analysis on LIE convincingly demonstrates that both a reward model trained on LIE and one trained on HelpSteer2 assign higher scores to longer responses regardless of correctness.
- The proposed mitigation strategies (Reward Adjustment via temperature scaling and Probability Adjustment via interpolation) are reasonable, novel in this context, and can be incorporated into existing reward model training with minimal modification. Empirically testing each combination of reliability score source and incorporation method is helpful.
- The paper connects the data collection process to model training, which is intuitive.

### Weaknesses
- **Effectiveness of the proposed solutions is unclear.** Many proposed combinations increase length bias rather than decrease it. Even the best method shows sensitivity: the authors note that "this strategy doesn't work for just any value as the model trained using the average confidence value doesn’t perform too well" (line 511-512). In Table 1, many reliability-aware combinations are outperformed by normal preference learning (e.g., LLM-based methods vs. Normal PL). The CLR score for Normal PL on HelpSteer2 is only 0.02 (Table 2), suggesting the unreliability issue may not be severe in realistic datasets.
- **Heavy reliance on the TRUE dataset for calibration.** Without TRUE-calibrated scores, the RAPL methods (e.g., LLM-based or self-reported confidence) do not consistently outperform the baseline. This raises concerns about generalizability and practicality.
- **Paper structure is incomplete.** It ends abruptly after the experiments section without a clear conclusion or discussion. The first half is clear, but the mitigation section (Sections 6.1–6.4) is difficult to follow, with verbose motivation but thin technical details and result description.
- **Lack of experimental details for reproducibility.** Missing details include: (a) the fitting of reliability scores (L.469-473) – which examples are used? (b) the sampling scheme for the TRUE dataset and how each example is verified to have a single correct answer; (c) training scheme for models trained on TRUE (architecture, hyperparameters, variance across seeds/sweeps). The presentation quality is rated low by some reviewers (Presentation 1–2).
- **Theoretical justification is weak.** The two incorporation methods lack clear intuition or theoretical grounding. For temperature scaling (Reward Adjustment), it does not change the objective because the model would increase the reward difference to compensate; it can only be applied at inference time. The interpolation method (Probability Adjustment) is not novel (Christian-O et al., 2017, also acknowledged by the authors). The second term in Eq. (6), "(1-p) * 0.5", appears to be a constant offset that does not affect training.
- **Downstream alignment impact is not verified.** The paper only evaluates reward models, not how the reward model affects actual RLHF or DPO alignment. This makes the whole study less motivated. It is unclear if the method can be applied to DPO training directly or just to reward modeling.
- **Unclear which method is recommended.** With conflicting experimental trends, it is hard to derive a clear takeaway. The use of the TRUE dataset is also unclear – could the same effect be achieved by using a validation set of LIE (which also has correctness labels) instead of a separate TRUE dataset?
- **Minor issues:** Contribution 2 should be rephrased as "confirm" rather than "find" to acknowledge prior work on length bias. Abbreviations LC, LI, SC, SI are used without introduction. The name TRUE is contrived ('Testing Reasoning and Understanding Errors' is generic and not specific to the datasets used). In the LIE dataset, correctness was checked by one author; inter-annotator reliability and number of changes made are not reported.
- **Questions about the reliability estimation model:** Estimating human error probability from TRUE seems challenging and depends on annotator skill and domain. How good is the model at this task, and how well does it generalize? A simpler baseline would be to skip examples whose annotation error probability exceeds a threshold. The assumption of generalizability of TRUE scores to other domains is questionable, as they are underperformed by constant score assignments in Table 1.
