## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a semi-supervised medical image segmentation method that leverages the frozen Segment Anything Model (SAM) to generate and refine pseudo-labels for training a lightweight U-Net++ model. U-Net++ is first supervised on a small labeled set, then its predictions on unlabeled images are used as prompts (points, bounding boxes, masks) for SAM to produce refined pseudo-labels; these are combined with ground truth to retrain U-Net++ in an iterative, continuous fashion. Experiments on Kvasir-SEG (polyp) and COVID-QU-Ex (lung X-ray) show Dice improvements of ~3% and ~1% over baseline U-Net++ when using 75% and 50% labeled data, respectively, and even surpass a fully supervised U-Net++ trained on 100% labeled data. Ablations compare continuous vs. one-time pseudo-labeling, various prompt types, and pre-training strategies (SimCLR, MedSAM). Code is provided.

### Strengths
1. **Reasonable and practical pipeline** – Using the task-specific model’s prediction as a prompt for SAM to obtain better pseudo-labels is a logical and effective approach (Reviewer 2). The integration of pseudo-labeling with iterative refinement is systematic and yields consistent improvements (Reviewer 1).
2. **Performance gains in limited-label scenarios** – The method demonstrates that models trained on 75% (or 50%) labeled data can match or exceed a fully supervised baseline (Reviewer 1, 2, 4). This is notable for clinical settings with scarce annotations (Reviewer 1, 4). On 75% labeled data, performance even surpasses the 100%-labeled U-Net++ (Reviewer 3).
3. **Ablation studies and strategy exploration** – The paper thoroughly evaluates continuous vs. one-time pseudo-label generation, different prompt types (points, bounding boxes, masks), and additional variants (SimCLR, MedSAM pre-training) (Reviewer 1, 2, 3). These analyses provide helpful insights into factors affecting segmentation quality.
4. **Reproducibility and clarity** – Code is provided in supplementary materials (Reviewer 3). Experimental settings are detailed in tables, and the report is clearly presented (Reviewer 3).  
5. **Positive contrast (Reviewer 2)** – One reviewer found the approach sound, the validation across two datasets effective, and the ablations convincing, awarding the highest score (5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Incremental technical contribution** – The core idea of using a large generalist model (SAM) to generate pseudo-labels for a smaller specialized model is straightforward and already explored in semi-supervised segmentation via pseudo-labeling and iterative refinement (Reviewer 1, 2). The use of SAM specifically does not fundamentally distinguish the method from prior large-to-small model transfer techniques (Reviewer 1).  
2. **Missing semi-supervised baselines and comparisons** – The paper lacks comparisons to other state-of-the-art semi-supervised segmentation methods (Reviewer 2, 4). A critical missing baseline is training the small model on 75% (or 50%) labeled data using its own pseudo-labels (without SAM) (Reviewer 2). No comparison with typical semi-supervised works is provided (Reviewer 4).  
3. **Pseudo-label quality not quantified** – No quantitative comparison of pseudo-labels generated by U-Net++ vs. those generated by SAM (with points/bbox prompts) against ground truth (Reviewer 3). Given that SAM was pre-trained on natural images and U‑Net++ on medical images, SAM may not inherently provide better pseudo-labels (Reviewer 3). The effect of continuous pseudo-label generation on label quality over time is unreported (Reviewer 3).  
4. **Low absolute performance relative to benchmarks** – The reported Dice on Kvasir-SEG (0.649) is far below the state-of-the-art (0.95) in recent literature (Reviewer 3). This raises concerns about model hyperparameters or validity of conclusions (Reviewer 3).  
5. **Limited evaluation scope** – Only two datasets (polyp colonoscopy and lung X-ray) are used; no testing on more complex modalities such as CT or MRI (Reviewer 3, 4). The method’s generalization to targets with small details, low contrast, or small lesions is not shown (Reviewer 1, 4).  
6. **Computational overhead of continuous pseudo-labeling** – The continuous iterative refinement introduces extra time and computational cost, making it less practical for large-scale or time-sensitive applications (Reviewer 2). Reviewer Reviewer 2 and 4 both request analysis of time cost, stopping criterion, and whether a performance upper bound exists.  
7. **Weakness in experimental design and justification** – The semi-supervised settings (75% and 50% labeled) are not optimal; the baseline already performs well, so gains appear marginal (Reviewer 4). The choice of U-Net++ as backbone is not justified – nnUNet is the standard, and newer medical SAM variants like SegVol could be considered (Reviewer 4). Figure 1 is too small to illustrate the core contribution clearly (Reviewer 4).  
8. **Lack of uncertainty/filtering for poor initial predictions** – No filtering or uncertainty assessment is applied to pseudo-labels; poor U‑Net++ predictions could lead to flawed SAM prompts and degrade learning (Reviewer 1).  
9. **Questions on prompting details** – No negative points or scribes are introduced to mark background explicitly (Reviewer 3). This might improve SAM’s foreground/background distinction.  
10. **Boundary metrics not reported** – Only Dice (and IoU) are reported; boundary-focused metrics like Hausdorff Distance would provide a more comprehensive segmentation assessment (Reviewer 3).  
11. **Contrast – Reviewer 3 is notably negative** – This reviewer rates soundness 1 and contribution 1, highlighting the low performance vs. benchmarks and lack of pseudo-label validation as serious concerns, while Reviewer 2 rates soundness 3 and contribution 2, finding the method reasonable.
