## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how isolated tokens in few-shot Chain-of-Thought demonstrations can distract LLMs, leading to reasoning errors. The authors propose Few-shot Attention Intervention (FAI), which dynamically identifies distracting tokens via attention patterns and adjusts attention weights to suppress their influence. Experiments on multiple benchmarks show consistent improvements. Three reviewers rated the paper 6/10 (confidence 3-4) and one reviewer rated it 8/10 (confidence 4), finding it more novel and well-structured. All reviewers acknowledge the interesting research question and potential, but raise significant methodological, analytical, and experimental concerns.

### Strengths
- The research question is interesting, valuable, and provides novel insight into understanding few-shot CoT dynamics (all reviewers).
- The proposed FAI method is conceptually simple, easy to implement, and introduces only constant computational overhead (Reviewer 2).
- Results are significant across various reasoning tasks with Llama 3 8B, demonstrating clear improvements over baselines (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The paper is clearly written with effective figures; the motivation is well-illustrated with examples (Reviewers 2, 3).
- The proposed method enables LLMs to focus more on global information rather than individual tokens, offering a new analytical perspective (Reviewer 3).
- The experiments are fairly extensive in scope (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Inconsistency between analysis and method:** The analysis uses saliency scores to identify distracting tokens, but the proposed method uses attention scores. No evidence is given that these two measures behave similarly. The analysis should also be presented using attention scores to ensure consistency (Reviewer 1). Similarly, more supporting evidence is needed that self-attention aggregation is a reliable indicator of information flow; e.g., can the method identify the same tokens as the saliency map in the error case of Figure 2(b)? (Reviewer 2).
- **Mismatch in intervention criterion:** Section 3.3 blocks attention for tokens with “no significant information aggregation,” yet the motivation is to block high-impact (distracting) tokens. The logic appears inconsistent and needs clarification (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited innovation and incremental contribution:** The method largely builds on single-step saliency scoring (Wang et al. 2023b) and simply uses a threshold to block attention, which has been widely discussed in prior work on pre-trained language models. The paper offers limited methodological novelty (Reviewer 3). The authors claim their multi-step dynamic adjustment is superior to single-step methods, but they do not compare against such baselines in experiments; a direct comparison and runtime analysis are required (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient model validation:** Experiments are only on 7B–13B models (Llama 3 8B). The paper should verify on larger models like Llama-3-70B, or other architectures like GPT2-XL and GPT-NEO-2.7B (Reviewers 3, 4). Additionally, zero-shot results should be added to Tables 2 and 4 to contextualize few-shot CoT gains (Reviewer 2).
- **Misleading or unclear figures and examples:** 
  - Figure 1 compares sentence-start predictions ((a) and (c)) with mid-sentence/equation-completion predictions ((b) and (d)), which may explain the score distribution differences. Examples of equation completion are needed for (a) and (c) to support the motivation (Reviewer 1).
  - Figure 2 is blurry and presents positive examples using only the previous layer to show significance, while negative examples include all previous layers. This is unfair and needs thorough explanation: e.g., would the first 30 layers in (a) produce the same effect as (b) and (d)? Would layer 14 in (b) produce the same effect as (a) and (c)? (Reviewer 3).
  - In Figure 3, the attention weight matrix excludes the query question in Layer n but includes it in Layer n+1—why? (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing case studies and detailed analysis:** 
  - The paper should provide examples highlighting tokens selected by FAI to understand its advantages and drawbacks (Reviewer 2).
  - More analysis of the saliency map is needed: Figure 2 shows failure attention patterns on specific layers/heads, but what do other heads’ and layers’ attention patterns look like? (Reviewer 2).
  - The authors select samples with accuracy >90% as GSM_bad without justifying why these errors are more likely due to distraction. Visualization of FAI attention on such cases would help (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear or missing experimental details:** 
  - Experimental settings are not fully specified (e.g., decode strategy, max_new_token), harming reproducibility (Reviewer 4).
  - Hyperparameter λ is not explained or justified; how is it identified? (Reviewer 3).
  - RAFR calculation is not defined (Reviewer 3).
  - Table 3 has no explanation (Reviewer 3).
  - The term “contrastive” in Figure 4 is undefined (Reviewer 3).
- **Questions about attention-dropping scope:** Does attention-dropping only apply to tokens between decoding and in-context examples? What about tokens between different in-context examples or among decoding tokens themselves? (Reviewer 2).
- **Citation format errors:** \citet and \citep are used incorrectly throughout the paper (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Line 73 (potential typo):** “has not experienced significant information aggregation” should be the case that α > τ, not α < τ? (Reviewer 2).
- **Contradiction in text:** Lines 273–274 contradict lines 260–263 (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing discussion of limitations:** The paper does not discuss what kinds of examples FAI tends to answer incorrectly that would be correct without it, or what examples it fails on even with FAI (Reviewer 3).
- **Contrast of reviewer sentiment:** One reviewer (score 8) finds the idea novel and values the extensive experiments, while the other three (score 6) raise strong concerns about methodology, novelty, and analysis depth. The higher-rated reviewer did not address several weaknesses noted by others.
