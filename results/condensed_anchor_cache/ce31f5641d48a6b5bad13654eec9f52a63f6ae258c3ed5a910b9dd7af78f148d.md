## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents three new theorems on KL divergence between data and learned distribution for discrete diffusion language models, leading to an improved upper bound \(J_2\) on perplexity. It introduces a novel transition-rate matrix (roulette diffusion) with an analytic matrix exponential enabling efficient training, and a training objective (CEDD) using denoising cross-entropy instead of score-entropy. Empirically, CEDD outperforms SEDD (up to 10% lower perplexity/generative-perplexity, 15% faster training steps) and \(J_2\) is consistently tighter than the previously used bound \(J_1\).

### Strengths
- Well-written and clear; addresses the important and relatively underexplored problem of discrete diffusion language modeling (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- Novel theoretical contributions: (i) new KL divergence bounds and the improved perplexity upper bound \(J_2\) that is both computationally more efficient and empirically tighter than \(J_1\); (ii) score reparameterization leading to the CEDD loss; (iii) construction of the roulette diffusion matrix (generalizing both uniform and absorb matrices) with a derived analytic matrix exponential for efficient conditional probability computation (Reviewers 1,2,4).
- Empirical results demonstrate that CEDD (using denoising cross-entropy) uniformly outperforms SEDD (using score-entropy), and that \(J_2\) is consistently tighter than \(J_1\) (Reviewers 2,4).
- Advances state-of-the-art in continuous-time discrete-state (CTDS) diffusion language models (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Practical usefulness unclear**: Table 2 shows GPT-2 still performs best over SEDDs, CEDD*, and CEDDT; perplexity improvements are marginal, and the computational overhead may not be justified (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 4 adds that GPT-2-based evaluation has known issues, perplexity bounds may not be tight, and how these models compare to auto-regressive LLMs remains an open question.
- **Evaluation reliability**: Section 4.1 uses GPT-2 for likelihood (perplexity) evaluation, which may give unreliable results with imperfect models; a better approach would be to use more modern language models and report aggregated scores (Reviewer 2; also noted by Reviewer 4).
- **Training objective sensitivity**: CEDD* relies on hand-crafted time-dependent weights \(\omega(t)\) (line 324); results vary across different transition types (absorb, uniform, roulette) as seen in Table 1, raising concerns about stability and robustness of the CEDD objective (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons**: The paper only compares against SEDD; other relevant works (e.g., [2], [3] cited by Reviewer 3) incorporate predictor-corrector methods and should be included for a fair evaluation (Reviewer 3).
- **Implementation flaw in baseline**: The authors used the official SEDD implementation, which has a known flaw in the Gumbel-max trick for low-precision sampling, leading to a temperature-like effect; the exponential used in the proposed method may further anneal temperature (Reviewer 3).
- **Scalability not discussed**: No analysis of how performance scales with model size, batch size, data size, or vocabulary size—important for assessing competitiveness with modern language models (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing task diversity**: Evaluation is limited to perplexity; Reviewer 2 suggests evaluating on tasks like spell checking or grammatical error correction (as done in [1]), and Reviewer 4 proposes supervised fine-tuning on common LLM benchmarks (e.g., NLU tasks) to better measure model quality.
- **Open questions**: Reviewer 2 asks for an intuitive explanation of why \(J_2\) is tighter than \(J_1\), contrasting formulas (13) and (16). Reviewer 1 flags a potential typo at line 147: \(\mathbf{x}^i_0 = x^i_0\).
