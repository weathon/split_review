## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ELMO (Eigenspace-Lipschitz Minmax Optimizer), a second-order optimization algorithm that performs an eigendecomposition of the Hessian at each iterate and uses per-eigendirection cubic polynomials to determine direction-specific step sizes. It assumes Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian’s eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and aims to achieve a worst-case descent rate of O(log t). The approach focuses on handling concave subspaces of the loss function and emphasizes the distribution of Lipschitz parameters along eigendirections.

### Strengths
- Some reviewers highlighted the novel decomposition of the optimization problem into d separate cubic polynomials along orthogonal eigendirections, enabling closed-form solutions for direction-specific step sizes and making the approach computationally tractable (R2). The focus on the distribution of Lipschitz parameters offers a potentially novel perspective not widely explored (R3).
- The paper explores a new theoretical direction by focusing on concave subspaces, which could inspire new optimizer designs for non-convex objectives (R1). The premise that it is difficult to compare algorithms across a broad spectrum of problem classes is correct (R4).
- One reviewer checked portions of the appendix proofs and found them generally correct, and noted that preliminary numerical experiments validate that the analyzed bounds are meaningful (R1). However, another reviewer found no strengths in the paper (R5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Computational Complexity and Practicality**  
   - The method requires eigendecomposition of the Hessian at each iteration and estimation of Hessian-Lipschitz constants along each eigendirection, which is computationally intensive. The paper does not address how the cost scales with dimensionality or dataset size, especially for high-dimensional deep learning problems where the Hessian can be massive (R1, R2, R3, R5).  
   - No comparison or demonstration of practical advantages over simpler preconditioned gradient methods such as Adam, RMSprop, or L-BFGS (R1, R2). The paper only compares to first-order baselines (SGD) but positions ELMO as a quasi-Newton optimizer; missing comparisons to methods that also leverage second-order information (R1).  
   - The algorithm description lacks self-contained implementation details. Algorithm 2 uses unexplained subroutines BASE_OPT and ASSESS_LIPSCHITZ, and the estimation of Lipschitz parameters is not sufficiently detailed; Section F.1 references methods not mentioned in the paper (R2, R5). The update rule in Algorithm 1 may not depend directly on θ_t, which is confusing (R5).  
   - The assumption of Lipschitz continuous Hessian along eigendirections is overly restrictive, particularly in regions of small eigenvalues (R2). Moreover, this assumption is likely violated for neural networks with ReLU activations, even in the paper’s own experiments (R5). The claim that Lipschitz parameters for relevant subspaces are order-of-magnitude smaller than others is not demonstrated; no reference or discussion found (R5).  
   - The introduction heavily motivates hyperparameter optimization, but the computational cost and assumptions make the method impractical for that domain (R5). The method is not demonstrated to outperform simpler alternatives in experiments; the experimental section does not show ELMO’s performance (R4).

2. **Theoretical Issues**  
   - Theoretical novelty is incremental, largely built upon cubic regularized Newton methods and existing Lipschitz assumptions on eigenvalues/eigenvectors (R2).  
   - The connection to Cauchy’s method (Traub, 1982) is noted as “nearly identical” (Appendix C), yet the differences are not discussed in the main text. The paper needs to clearly delineate when ELMO offers a significant advantage over Cauchy’s method in multi-dimensional settings (R3, R4).  
   - Theorem 3.2 provides only a negative lower bound for f(θ_t)−f(θ_0), which seems weak. It is unclear how Theorem 3.3 contributes to addressing this negative lower bound (R4). Theorem 3.2 also hides the dependence on L_H, which should have been a major point of the paper (R5). Theorem 3.3 is claimed to demonstrate matching lower bound, but no relation is shown (R5).  
   - The convergence analysis does not address how the rate might be affected by practical approximations of Lipschitz parameters (R2).  
   - The paper focuses on concave subspaces but overlooks other complexities like saddle points and flat regions; insufficient justification is given for why concavity-only focus is sufficient (R1). Handling mixed convex/concave/flat regions is not explained (R1 questions).  
   - Several statements are confusing or incorrect: Lemma 3.1’s “Exists (L_t^i): i=1,…,n” is unclear; the reformulation of M_t^i(x) on page 5 is wrong (maximum infinite if nonzero); the arg min in equation (7) returns a vector but left and right sides are scalars; the update for θ_{t+1} does not seem to depend on θ_t; Theorem 3.2’s dependence on L_H is not discussed; Theorem 3.3 is not related to any performance lower bound (R5). Notation issues in line 324 and 355 cause confusion (R3). Potential typos: “concave” should be “convex” in lines 377-379 (R4). Figure 1 is labeled “Logarithmic scale” but does not appear logarithmic (R5). The phrase “step’s distance from ELMO’s step relative to ELMO’s step” is unclear (R5).

3. **Motivation and Coherence**  
   - The difficulty described in the introduction (Lines 50-65) does not serve as motivation for ELMO; the design and analysis do not address the problem of selecting the best optimizer among many (R4). The connection between the introduction, method design, experiments, and conclusion is weak (R4). The topic is very broad, making it challenging to provide a unified framework that is both broadly applicable and practically useful; the paper lacks actionable insights for specific sub-domains (R3).  
   - The meta-optimizer concept mentioned at the end lacks detail; it is unclear how it would work, what metrics would govern switches, and whether it would be computationally feasible (R1 question).

4. **Experimental Evaluation**  
   - The paper does not demonstrate the performance of ELMO in experiments (R4). Comparisons to modern optimizers (L-BFGS, RMSprop) are missing (R1). The experimental setup may violate the assumptions (ReLU activations, Appendix F) (R5).
