## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes to train instruction-conditional embedding models to also take few-shot examples as input, extending prior contrastive-learning-based embedding training that used only instructions. By adding a few positive examples to the prompt during contrastive fine-tuning, the model (bge-en-icl) achieves state-of-the-art results on MTEB and AIR-Bench. The approach preserves the original decoder-only LLM architecture and leverages in-context learning (ICL) capabilities. The work is simple, straightforward, and presents compelling (though not jaw-dropping) empirical gains.

### Strengths
- Simple, intuitive, and easy-to-read method: adding in-context examples to embedding training without architectural modifications.
- First work to explore ICL for text embedding at the 7B scale; novelty is reasonable.
- Strong and extensive empirical results on MTEB and AIR-Bench, demonstrating clear improvements over zero-shot baselines.
- Comprehensive ablation studies (Section 4.4) show that keeping the original architecture is crucial for ICL capabilities, and that few-shot outperforms zero-shot.
- Thorough empirical analysis of attention mechanisms, pooling methods, etc., with the finding that simplicity works best.
- The ablation comparing ICL to other architectural choices is especially useful for disentangling gains from data vs. architecture.
- All four reviewers agree the experiments are compelling, and two reviewers (scores 8) are highly enthusiastic.

### Weaknesses
- The performance gains are marginal (MTEB 71.2 → 71.7) and the added computational cost (slower inference from including ICL tokens) is not quantified; practitioners need a clear trade-off analysis.
- Missing ablation: it is unclear how much of the gain comes from simply increasing token count (and thus FLOPs) vs. actually incorporating new semantic information. A control experiment (e.g., substituting ICL tokens with equivalent-length random tokens, as in “let’s think dot-by-dot”) would help.
- Missing analysis on the number of few-shot examples: no experiment shows how performance scales with shot count. Both reviewers 1 and 2 explicitly request this.
- Sensitivity to demonstration selection and order is well-known in ICL, but the paper only mentions “a consistent set of in-context examples” without discussing selection strategies, whether examples are searched/randomly sampled, or whether results are averaged across different demonstrations.
- It is unclear whether the method helps more in out-of-domain scenarios (where ICL should be most useful). Reviewers ask for such an analysis.
- Key implementation details are missing:
  - How exactly are query and passage representations obtained? Section 3.1 suggests both use <EOS> embeddings; is there only one <EOS> token?
  - Why are passages/documents truncated to 256 tokens when Mistral supports 32K and at most 5 documents? Are long-doc gains consistent with other gains?
  - In evaluation, what is the “consistent set” of ICL examples? How many examples? Are they fixed per dataset or per query?
  - Are all ablations in Section 4.4 trained similarly to the baseline?
  - LoRA is used during training; is this due to budget or performance? Has different LoRA rank been compared? Could full training further improve results?
- The naming “full data” vs. “public data” is misleading because “full data” also consists of public datasets. Two reviewers (1 and 3) raise this.
- Missing comparison with NV-Embed2, which is relevant given recent results.
- Typos and formatting issues: line numbers missing (not following ICLR template), incorrect citations (`\citet{}` not used), broken quote in “task_definition”, “align” → “aligned” in §4.4, awkward phrasing “conduct the process over a single epoch” instead of “trained for a single epoch”.
- Figure 2’s right two subfigures are redundant and could be moved to appendix.
