## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Representational Similarity via Visual Concepts (RSVC), a method to compare two neural networks by extracting visual concepts from their activations using dictionary learning (building on Fel et al. 2023a,b). Two similarity measures are proposed: a correlational metric (MMCS) and a regression-based metric (CMCS) that predicts concept coefficients from one model to the other. Concept importance is also estimated and combined with similarity to study shared and unique concepts. Experiments compare ResNet-18, ResNet-50, ViT-S, ViT-L, MAE, and DINO on ImageNet (and NABirds for self-supervised models). The paper finds that (1) middle layers exhibit greatest differences, (2) some high-importance concepts have low cross-model similarity, and (3) concept similarity correlates with output agreement. The goal is to provide an interpretable tool for understanding *what* makes models similar or dissimilar, not just a single similarity score.

### Strengths
- Motivation is strong and timely: going beyond a single similarity number to answer *what* differs between representations (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). This addresses an overlooked question (R1).
- The dictionary-based concept decomposition for model comparison is novel to the best of the reviewers’ knowledge (R2). The method is intuitive and provides a way to visualize similar/dissimilar concepts (R4, R1, R3).
- The paper is well-written and clearly structured; figures and captions can be read standalone, enhancing readability (R2). The explanation of the pipeline is easy to follow (R3, R1).
- Several findings are interesting, e.g., that low-similarity, high-importance concepts exist, and that middle layers differ most between architectures (R1). The experimental breadth across architectures and training paradigms is appreciated (R2).
- The study of the relationship between concept importance and similarity comprehensively addresses a potential limitation (R3). The method goes beyond measuring similarity to also interpret dissimilar concepts (R4).
- Figure 2 provides a convincing example of what shared and unique concepts look like (R4). (However, other reviewers found it less convincing – see Weaknesses.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of validation and sanity checks** (Reviewer 2: major, confidence 4; also Reviewer 1, 5).  
   - No baseline experiments are performed, e.g., comparing a model against itself with small initialization changes or different data orderings. Without such baselines, the significance of the computed similarity values is unclear (R2).  
   - Structural variations away from a baseline (e.g., different augmentations, data subsets) should be studied to understand how the metric changes (R2).  
   - The visual concepts in Figure 2 are not clearly interpretable: both the orange and light-blue grids consistently show grid textures and hands/arms near a volleyball net, making the claimed differences unconvincing (R2, R5; also R1 notes that the semantic meaning of concepts is unclear).  
   - The paper claims to provide a tool to discover the cause of model failures (abstract), but this discussion is absent from the main text (R5).  

2. **Sensitivity to concept extraction hyperparameters and missing ablations** (Reviewer 3: confidence 4; also R1, R2, R5).  
   - The choice of dictionary size \(k\) and L1 sparsity regularization strongly affects the extracted concepts (granularity, interpretability). A thorough ablation is missing (R3).  
   - Patch extraction details are insufficient: how are patches sampled? How many? How large? Are they resized independently to input resolution? (R5, R4). Resizing patches and passing them through the model introduces resolution dependence that may distort concept meaning (R2: “Learned features are generally resolution-dependent”).  
   - The impact of the number of images used for concept extraction and for training the linear regression (Eq. 4) is not explored. The computational cost trade-offs are not discussed (R3).  
   - The value of \(\lambda\) (L1 regularization in Eq. 4) is not described as tuned; the paper should state it is tuned on a separate validation set (R5, R4).  

3. **Methodological concerns** (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).  
   - Confounding: MMCS does not account for confounders; it is also unclear how CMCS avoids them (R2).  
   - Replacement tests (Section 3.4) operate without guarantees that the basis of \(U_{2\to1}\) matches that of \(U_1\) – the learned linear map does not ensure overlapping bases (R2).  
   - RSVC uses a learned projection matrix, yet claims not to use surrogate models – this seems contradictory (R2).  
   - The approach operates per-class; this may miss shared concepts across classes, and it is unclear how to extend to non-classifier models (e.g., segmenters, representation learners) (R3).  
   - The split of \(\mathcal{I}^c\) into train/test for the regression task is not specified (R4).  
   - For self-supervised models (DINO, MAE), the paper fine-tunes on NABirds – why is this necessary? (R5).  

4. **Limited actionable insights and summary-statistic nature** (Reviewers 1, 5; also R2).  
   - The paper falls short of delivering on its central promise: only Figure 2 directly addresses *what* concepts differ; the rest (Figs. 3, 4, 5) revert to summary statistics that do not provide a detailed understanding of specific differing concepts (R1).  
   - The conclusion in Figure 3 that “model differences are largely driven by medium similarity, medium importance concepts” is not well supported: the y-axis center is near 0.0, and the x-axis is indeed centered at medium importance but the heatmap trends are unclear (R5).  
   - Figure 4: the claim that \(\Delta\)Pearson closer to zero implies lower L2/KL is not readily deducible from the plot; separate plots for high/low importance would help (R5).  
   - No head-to-head comparison against prior interpretable model comparison methods (e.g., Rosetta Neurons – Dravid et al. ICCV 2023; Bau et al. PNAS 2020; Activation Atlases – Carter et al. 2019) is provided to demonstrate advantages (R1, R3).  
   - Missing related work on disentangled representation learning (Locatello et al. 2018–2023; Eastwood et al. 2018,2022; Roth et al. 2023) could inform ablation studies (R2).  
   - Brainscore is mischaracterized: it compares activations to macaque brain recordings, not between two networks (R4).  

5. **Presentation and clarity issues** (Reviewers 5, 4, 2).  
   - Definitions of low, medium, high concept importance and concept similarity are missing; precise percentiles (e.g., top 5%) should be used (R5).  
   - Figure 3 lacks color bars, making it harder to interpret (R4). Figure 4 would be clearer with separate plots for high and low importance groups (R5).  
   - L201–L211: confusion between \(R^\rho\) and \(R^S\) – which one does \(R\) employ? (R5)  
   - Section 3.3 is redundant or needs more detail (R2).  
   - The introduction is too brief; it fails to motivate why concepts are more useful than saliency maps or LIME superpixels (R4).  
   - Typo at L250: “it *to* compute” (R3).  

6. **Minority positive contrast**: While Reviewers 2 and 4 gave scores of 8 and found the method novel and the experimental breadth strong, they also raised major weaknesses (lack of sanity checks, missing implementation details) that prevent acceptance at the current stage. The majority reviewers (1,3,5) gave 6, citing insufficient insights and lack of validation as critical shortcomings.
