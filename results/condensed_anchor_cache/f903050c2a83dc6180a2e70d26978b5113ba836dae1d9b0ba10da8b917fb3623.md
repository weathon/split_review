## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Grond, a clean-label backdoor attack that aims for stealth in input, feature, and parameter spaces. It uses targeted universal adversarial perturbations (TUAP) as the trigger and introduces Adversarial Backdoor Injection (ABI) to restrict parameter changes by pruning weights that deviate from the layer mean. Evaluations on CIFAR-10, GTSRB, and a 200-class ImageNet subset claim Grond outperforms existing attacks against nine backdoor removal and seven backdoor detection methods.

### Strengths
- **Convincing experimental results** across multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, GTSRB, ImageNet200) and architectures, showing high attack success rate and ability to bypass existing defenses (Reviewer 1, 4).  
- **Extensive ablation studies** covering various settings (Reviewer 1, 3).  
- **Clear presentation** making methodology and results easy to follow (Reviewer 1, 2).  
- **Important topic** – backdoor attacks pose a serious threat to model integrity (Reviewer 2).  
- Grond aims for **comprehensive stealth across input, feature, and parameter spaces** by dispersing backdoor effects across neurons (Reviewer 3).  
- **Adversarial Backdoor Injection** effectively limits parameter-space changes (Reviewer 3).  
- The approach is **simple and easy to understand** (Reviewer 4).  

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and reliance on existing techniques**:
  - Trigger generation (TUAP) and adversarial backdoor injection are direct adaptations of prior works [1, 2]; weight pruning for stealth is not original (Reviewer 1, 2, 4).  
  - The attack is essentially a collection of known components with limited new insights (Reviewer 1, 2).  
- **Unrealistic threat model**: assumes white-box control over the entire training process (supply chain attack setting). This setting is extremely difficult to defend (Reviewer 1). The use of a clean-label setting within a white-box scenario is contradictory – clean-label attacks are designed for scenarios with no control over labeling/training, yet here the attacker has full control (Reviewer 2).  
- **Lack of theoretical justification** for why Grond performs better than other attacks – no explanation of the underlying reasons (Reviewer 1).  
- **No evaluation against adaptive defenders**: an adversary aware of Grond’s strategy could potentially detect or remove it. For example:
  - The mask loss for Grond is much lower than benign cases, which could be used as a detection signal (Reviewer 2).  
  - Randomly perturbing weight parameters may break the backdoor, as it reduces backdoor-related weights (Reviewer 2).  
  - Proactive defenses (e.g., analyzing activation patterns, statistical weight analysis) are not considered (Reviewer 3).  
- **Missing comparisons with other stealthy clean-label attacks** (e.g., Hidden Trigger Backdoor Attacks), making it unclear if Grond offers a significant stealth advantage (Reviewer 3).  
- **Limited defense methods evaluated**: the paper focuses mainly on pruning and fine-tuning defenses, lacking sample-level detection methods that target the input space directly (Reviewer 3). The attack’s robustness to sample-based detection remains unaddressed (Reviewer 3, and Reviewer 3’s question).  
- **Input-space stealthiness is questionable**: backdoor samples from ImageNet200 show perceptible perturbations; no rigorous quality evaluation (e.g., compared to imperceptible attacks like Lira [3]) is provided (Reviewer 4).  
- **Missing experimental details**: the number of clean samples used in pruning-based and fine-tuning-based defense experiments is not clearly specified (Reviewer 4).  
- **Visualization requests**: the paper would benefit from Grad-CAM images of backdoor samples and feature-space distribution visualizations to further demonstrate stealth (Reviewer 4).
