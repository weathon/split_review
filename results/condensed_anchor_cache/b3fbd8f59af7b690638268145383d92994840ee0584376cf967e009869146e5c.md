## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes BioNAS, a neural architecture search framework built on DARTS/EG-NAS and BioTorch that searches over network cell architectures and supports different bio-inspired learning rules (FA variants). The goal is to improve bio-inspired neural network performance. Experiments claim state-of-the-art bio-inspired results on CIFAR-10 (94.86%), CIFAR-100 (76.48%), and ImageNet16‑120 (43.42%). The paper also reports that mixing learning rules reduces gradient variance and improves white-box adversarial robustness. Scores: 5, 3, 5, 6. Reviewer 4 (score 6) was more positive; Reviewer 2 (score 3) was more critical and found the work preliminary and overclaimed.

### Strengths
- Fresh perspective: integrating bio-inspired learning rules with NAS (R1, R4).
- Using a bio-plausible learning rule instead of backpropagation is interesting (R3).
- First to introduce different learning rules into a NAS search space (R4).
- Results show that mixed learning rules can improve performance and white-box adversarial robustness (R4).
- Adversarial attacks on generated bio-inspired networks illustrate an advantage of the framework (R2).
- Strong empirical support with extensive experiments across multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet16‑120) and varied settings (R3).
- Well-organized and clearly written (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Overclaimed scope and limited bio-inspired support** (R2 major, R3). Only FA variants are implemented; prominent biologically plausible algorithms (Hebbian learning, predictive coding, target propagation) are absent. The framework is effectively a specialized NAS for FA-based networks, not a general BioNAS. The claim “bio-inspired network” should be toned down. (R2 conf 5, R3 conf 5)

2. **Insufficient comparison with prior bio-inspired works** (R2 major). On CIFAR-10 only two previous Hebbian-based results are compared; on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet16‑120 no previous bio-inspired results are shown, making the SOTA claim unsupported. The applicability to larger datasets is also not demonstrated (R1). (R2 conf 5)

3. **Limited novelty** (R2 major, R3 major). BioNAS is built by adding BioTorch’s FA operations to existing NAS frameworks (DARTS, EG‑NAS) without introducing new architectural or algorithmic concepts. The paper lacks analysis of whether the searched architectures are fundamentally different from those found with standard backpropagation, and how they relate to the chosen learning rules. (R2 conf 5, R3 conf 5)

4. **Unclear motivation and missing trade-off analysis** (R3 major). NAS is computationally expensive; its goal contrasts with the efficiency of bio-inspired learning rules. The paper does not explain why an expensive search is needed or explore the computational‑cost/efficiency trade-off. Missing analysis of energy consumption (R1) and the fact that search time increases ~3× (R4) reinforce this concern. (R3 conf 5)

5. **Potential gradient obfuscation in robustness evaluation** (R3 major). The observation that multi‑step PGD attack performs worse than single‑step FGSM may indicate gradient masking or unsuccessful attacks (Athalye et al., ICML 2018). The paper does not verify this issue. Also, it is unclear whether white‑box attack gradients are computed via backpropagation or the searched learning algorithm (R4 Q1). A thorough robustness analysis with varied attack methods is missing.

6. **Missing theoretical analysis** (R4 weakness 3). Why mixing learning rules enhances white‑box adversarial robustness is not theoretically justified. Also, why FA (which still uses end‑to‑end learning with a replacement matrix) is considered bio‑inspired is not clarified (R4 Q2).

7. **Methodology issues in comparisons** (R4 weakness 2). Different rows of Table 2 (different training methods) use different network architectures, preventing fair comparisons. The paper does not detail how cell architecture and learning rules are searched simultaneously (R4 weakness 1), and it is not examined whether DARTS/EG‑NAS would improve if given the same increased search time (R4 weakness 1).

8. **Missing ablation and post‑search details** (R4 Q3). It is not reported whether the final subnet is retrained from scratch or inherits supernet weights for fine‑tuning.

9. **Questions about integration and patterns** (R2 Q1, Q2). How other bio‑inspired algorithms (Hebbian, predictive coding, target propagation) could be integrated into BioNAS is not addressed. The distribution of different learning rules in searched networks is not analyzed (e.g., interesting patterns or explainable rationale).
