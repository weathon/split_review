## Merged Review

### Summary

This work formalizes *domain certification* – a guarantee characterizing the out-of-domain (OOD) behavior of language models (LMs) deployed in constrained tasks – and proposes a test-time algorithm, VALID, that uses rejection sampling based on the log-odds ratio between a large LM and a small guide model G to bound the probability of an OOD response. The method is evaluated on TinyShakespeare, 20 Newsgroups, and MedQA / MMLU@Med. Reviewers agree the problem is well-motivated, the definitions are clear, and the algorithm is simple. However, concerns about the assumptions underlying G, the reliance on a fixed set of bad strings F, missing baselines, and limited experimental comparisons are raised, with some reviewers finding the certificate’s practical applicability currently limited.

### Strengths

- Strong theoretical foundations for classifying and certifying out-of-domain behavior (R1, R2, R4). The progression from atomic to domain certificates is clearly explained (R1).
- VALID is straightforward and uses rejection sampling in an elegant way to achieve certificates (R1, R3).
- The method is novel and addresses a needed area for safe deployment of LMs, motivated by legislation such as the EU AI Act (R1, R4).
- Empirical evaluation across diverse datasets (TinyShakespeare, 20NG, MedQA) shows generalizability and some meaningful certificates (R1, R2, R4). Results include D_T / D_F ratios (Table 1) and generative evaluation (R3).
- The algorithm cleverly harnesses the limited abilities of a very small, cheap-to-train, domain-specific LM (e.g., small GPT-2) to implement test-time rejection sampling favoring constrained responses (R3).
- Provides mathematical guarantees, which is desirable compared to existing guardrail approaches that lack formal certification (R4).
- Well written (R1).

### Weaknesses

1. **Fixed set of bad strings F is a pervasive limitation.** The method requires a predefined finite set of bad strings F (lines 132-137), leaving room for adversaries to find OOD outputs in the complement of F that are falsely permitted (R3). Reviewer 4 also questions how to ensure F is representative of all possible OOD scenarios and the impact of a poorly chosen F on certificate validity; recommendations for selecting F in practice are missing. Reviewer 1 adds that certified benchmarking was only done on MMLU@Med; results for other datasets are needed to understand how F varies across domains.

2. **Reliance on the guide model G raises multiple concerns:**
   - G is trained only on in-domain data D_T and does not consider the input (R2). If context is included in the final answer to fix this, the method cannot work when outputs must be concise and inference cost increases (R1).
   - Theorem 1 assumes G assigns exponentially small likelihood to bad strings in F because it was trained on in-domain data. However, LMs (including G if pre-trained) contain world knowledge and may assign non-negligible likelihood to some out-of-domain strings, making the theorem extremely limited (R2). Reviewer 3 asks for an analytic or empirical argument for the claim “G assigns exponentially small likelihood to samples in F,” as both benign and adversarial robustness hinge on this.
   - No ablation on G’s size or training corpus is provided (R3, R4). Stronger G (more parameters/data) may widen the set of strings it models, causing L and G to become similar and depleting the algorithm’s power (R3). Reviewer 4 also calls for exploring how G’s definition affects certificate tightness.
   - Reviewer 3 asks for more details on why small GPT-2 models were chosen and how G’s role was developed (e.g., whether the method’s effectiveness depends on G being weak). Reviewer 4 asks how to set up VALID in practice: Is G a finetuned version of L, a smaller finetuned LM, a model trained from scratch, or a simple discriminator? Since domain data is already needed, Reviewer 4 suggests using embedding-space similarity to in-domain data as a possible alternative, questioning the need for a secondary LM.

3. **Adversarial attacks on G / the composite model are not sufficiently addressed.** Reviewer 1 notes that white-box access to G is not necessary for successful attacks because some attacks generalize across models; this should be further investigated. The paper’s argument (lines 448, 453) that attacks require white-box access may be insufficient.

4. **Experimental evaluation lacks comparisons to baselines or existing guardrails.** Reviewer 4 states this is the main weakness: no comparison to existing techniques (e.g., LLM guardrails, out-of-domain detection methods) is made, even those without formal guarantees. Without this, assessing practical value is difficult. Reviewer 4 also points to a lack of discussion on related guardrail methods and how VALID compares. Reviewer 1 notes missing citations to NLP certification work (arxiv:2401.01262, 2402.15929, 2403.10144v1) and Reviewer 3 suggests related odds-ratio paradigms (Contrastive Decoding Li et al. 2210.15097, DPO Rafailov et al. 2305.18290) to improve context.

5. **Generative evaluation results are weaker than fixed-sample-set results**, with lower Youden’s J for the same domain. Since generative evaluation is more realistic, the approach is not quite deployable yet (R3). The D_T to D_F ratios in Table 1 show a correlation with domain similarity, which is expected but may highlight a limitation when good and bad behaviors are not well separated (R3). Lines 145-152 (risk tolerance example with “one OOD response per year”) are more appropriate for Discussion/Future Work; Reviewer 4 also questions the boldness of that claim.

6. **Rejection sampling with T>1 incurs computational inefficiency.** Although multiple samples could be drawn in parallel, there is additional overhead (R1).

7. **Minor:** Notation inconsistency – line 257 uses “OOD” while line 269 uses “F”; prefer one notation (R3). Also, it is unclear what F′ ∩ T′ contains (only semantically incoherent sequences?) (R4).
