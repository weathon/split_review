## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Gaussian process (GP) based solver for high-frequency and multi-scale PDEs. It uses a spectral mixture kernel (with Student’s t or Gaussian mixture) to capture dominant frequencies, applies a sparsity-inducing prior to prune excessive frequencies, and places collocation points on a grid to obtain a Kronecker product structure in the covariance matrices for efficient computation. Experiments compare against PINNs and a vanilla GP baseline on several 1D/2D PDEs.

### Strengths
* The idea of using a sparsity-inducing prior over the frequencies of a spectral mixture kernel for PDEs is promising, and the paper demonstrates robustness and applicability (R1). The contributions of using a spectral mixture kernel for multi-scale PDEs and optimising a Kronecker-factorised prior for joint estimation are novel to the best of the reviewer’s knowledge (R4).
* The method is described in detail, runtime complexity is analysed, and multiple experiments are provided (R2). The paper is well-structured, the problem is clearly motivated, and the numerical experiments show that the proposed method outperforms PINNs in terms of accuracy (R3).
* The topic is of growing interest to the ICLR community, and the paper contributes valuable concepts (R4).

### Weaknesses
* **Overclaim of novelty.** Several reviewers challenge the statements that this work is “first” in key aspects. (R1) The claim that “we are the first to discover [the spectral mixture kernel’s] rationale and effectiveness for PDE solving” is contradicted by Härkönen et al. (2023), who explicitly recover the spectral mixture kernel as a special case of their construction. (R1, R3) The claim of being “first to use the Kronecker product structure to efficiently solve PDEs” is incorrect: Kronecker product methods on regular grids are standard in numerical PDEs (e.g., Gavrilyuk et al. 2019; spectral methods like Fortunato & Townsend 2019), and in GPs the efficiency of product kernels on grids is well known (Saatci, 2012). (R3) The title and abstract may mislead readers into thinking this is the first GP-based PDE solver, whereas prior work includes Chen et al. (2021), Raissi et al. (2017), and others.
* **Missing and inadequately discussed related work.** (R1, R4) Notable GP-based PDE solvers are omitted: Cockayne et al. (2017), Wang et al. (2021), Pförtner et al. (2022), Chen et al. (2023). (R4) The derivative trick in Equation (13) was previously presented by Krämer et al. The paper also misses Owhadi’s work on Bayesian numerical homogenisation for multi-scale PDEs, and does not credit traditional multi-scale solvers (e.g., numerical homogenisation methods). (R3) The differences from earlier GP approaches (Chen et al. 2021, Raissi et al. 2017) are not clearly stated; the claimed differences seem to be (1) use of Student’s t distribution and (2) Kronecker grid for speed, but this is not well distinguished.
* **Weak baseline.** (R1) The vanilla GP baseline uses a Matern kernel but does not exploit the Kronecker/Toeplitz structure that could be applied to product Matern kernels on a grid (Wilson et al. 2015). Consequently, the reported performance gap in time and memory may be significantly overestimated. (R3) A quick experiment with Chebfun solves the 1D Poisson equation to 10 digits accuracy in a fraction of a second, raising questions about the method’s advantage over traditional spectral solvers.
* **Insufficient experimental analysis.** (R3, R4) Timings are reported only in the appendix; overall training time (wall-clock) to reach the precision in Tables 1 and 2 should be included in the main text, as computational efficiency is a central claimed advantage. (R4) Appendix C shows per-iteration runtime competitive with PINNs, but Figure 9 suggests tens of thousands of iterations are needed – overall cost is unclear. (R1) The marginal uncertainty of the GP is not evaluated for calibration. (R1, R3) The impact of the number of mixture components on accuracy and optimisation difficulty is not ablated. (R3) Source terms for the experiments should be provided (at least in the appendix) for reproducibility. (R3) Equation (6) should specify the domain of integration. (R1) Could the kernel matrices also have Toeplitz structure, which could further accelerate computations (Wilson et al. 2015)?
* **Unsubstantiated claims.** (R2) The statement that existing spectral-mixture-based methods are “unstable” and “highly sensitive to the number and scales of Gaussian variances” is not supported by any concrete example, figure, or experiment showing how small hyperparameter changes affect solution quality. (R3) The advantage of using a Student’s t distribution over a Gaussian mixture is not clearly demonstrated; performance in Table 1 appears similar.
* **Limited applicability and presentation.** (R3) A key limitation—that the Kronecker product structure restricts the method to simple grid-based geometries (Cartesian products)—is relegated to the appendix; it should be discussed in the main text, and the method’s advantage over spectral methods for such domains is questionable. (R2) The abstract is difficult to follow; it should focus on high-level contributions. Figures 2 and 3 could be moved earlier to illustrate the high-frequency/multi-scale focus. (R4) The notation around Equation (14) would benefit from explicitly writing the joint probability density being maximised (e.g., \(\mathcal{L} = \log p(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{H} \mid \Theta, \tau_1, \tau_2)\)) and from contextualising the loss against MAP or MLE estimation.

*Minority reviewer note:* Reviewer 3 (score 5, confidence 3) is more critical than the others, particularly regarding the overclaim of being first and the lack of comparison to spectral methods (Chebfun example) and the limited geometry restriction. The other three reviewers score the paper 6 (confidence 4–5), but they also identify significant missing related work and suggest that claims of novelty be tempered.
