## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper analyzes the generalization of a two-layer neural network (fixed second layer, squared ReLU activation) trained with gradient descent (GD) versus label-noise GD (LNGD) on a binary classification problem where inputs consist of two patches: one signal patch (with class-dependent mean) and one noise patch (pure Gaussian noise). Under a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) regime where the noise patch norm dominates the signal and the number of training samples \(n\) is small compared to the dimension \(d\), the authors prove that standard GD overfits to noise and achieves non-vanishing test error, while LNGD (by flipping labels during training) suppresses noise memorization and allows signal learning, leading to good generalization. Synthetic experiments support the theory. Reviewers acknowledge the technical complexity and the interesting theoretical separation, but express concerns about the artificiality of the setup, lack of rigorous asymptotic notation, overlap with prior work, and sensitivity of the claimed advantage to small parameter changes.

### Strengths

- **Novel theoretical viewpoint**: The role of label noise as a regularizer in the low-SNR regime is less studied; the analysis provides a detailed proof sketch and a technically intricate treatment of signal and noise coefficients (R1).
- **Rigorous theoretical techniques**: Use of supermartingale arguments and concentration inequalities adds depth (R2); proofs involve complicated techniques and potential technical novelty (R5).
- **Clear theoretical separation**: LNGD outperforms GD under specific SNR conditions without additional computational cost (R3); the paper establishes a clear separation (R3).
- **Well-structured and clear**: Key ideas are properly introduced, results are contextualized, and synthetic experiments corroborate the theory (R3, R4).
- **Significance and originality**: Addresses the important problem of learning in low SNR; shows that negative results on noise memorization can be avoided with little overhead (R4). The label noise analysis is new and uses techniques not present in prior work (Cao et al. 2022) (R4).
- **Reproducibility**: The synthetic experiment can be reproduced (R5).

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of rigor in asymptotic notation** (R1): Assumption 3.1 states \(d = \Omega(n^2)\) and \(n = \Omega(1)\) without specifying whether the hidden constants depend on problem parameters; Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 include \(C_1, C_2\) inside \(\Theta\) notation. This makes the statements potentially vacuous. The label noise fraction is only bounded by an undefined constant \(C\). Explicit constants (or a simplified version with fixed \(\sigma_p, \mu, m, n\)) are needed (R1, R1’s question).

2. **Overlap with prior work** (R1): Theorem 3.1 proves the same result as Cao et al. for \(q=2\) while they cover \(q>2\). If their result applies to real \(q>2\), Theorem 3.1 could be derived via approximation (e.g., \(\text{ReLU}^{2.0001}\)), diminishing the contribution.

3. **Artificial setup and lack of practical verification** (R1, R2, R4, R5):
    - The data model (two patches, one pure noise) and network (two-layer MLP with squared ReLU, no convolution) are extremely simple. Reviewer 5 describes the architecture as “strange” and notes that “two patches” and the non-convolutional model are confusing.
    - All experiments are synthetic. Prior works in this line often include image classification benchmarks (e.g., concatenating random noise to images) (R4). Reviewer 2 suggests testing on more complex functions (staircase, parity) to assess robustness.
    - Reviewer 5 provides counterexamples: changing the width from \(m=20\) to \(m=3\) makes standard GD achieve 100% test accuracy. With \(m=3\) and larger noise (\(\sigma_p = 2.0\)), standard GD reaches ~90% test accuracy while label noise GD performs miserably. Thus the claim that “label noise GD improves generalization” is not robust; it can be invalidated by a simple parameter change.

4. **Time threshold discrepancy** (R3): Theorem 3.1 (GD fails) gives \(t = \Theta(m^3 n / d)\); Theorem 3.2 (LNGD succeeds) gives \(t = \Theta(m)\). It is unclear whether these thresholds are directly comparable. Questions posed: explain comparability, compare performance under equal iteration counts, and analyze how performance of both algorithms varies with the number of iterations. Also, for larger \(m\) the LNGD success threshold becomes slower (\(\Theta(m)\)); an explanation is needed.

5. **Sensitivity to network width and parameter choices** (R5, see point 3 above): The paper does not show that simple solutions (e.g., reducing width) cannot solve the problem; therefore the necessity of label noise is not established.

6. **Effectiveness under different noise forms** (R3): It is unclear whether LNGD works for noise types other than Gaussian; the concern is that it may only be effective in a specific noise regime.

7. **Choice of activation** (R4): The use of squared ReLU is not well motivated. Prior work used higher power polynomials to suppress noise; it is unclear for which parts of the analysis squared ReLU is necessary.

8. **Clarity of the theory** (R1, R4): Theorem 3.1 should be compared more directly with Theorem 4.4 of Cao et al. (2022). Intuition for the supermartingale property in Lemma D.4 is lacking; the calculations alone do not provide insight.

9. **Minority contrast** (R5 vs. others): While reviewers 2 and 4 find the work significant and sound, reviewer 5 strongly argues that the paper’s conclusion is misleading because the example is too specific and the claimed advantage collapses under minimal changes (e.g., reducing \(m\) or increasing noise). R5 insists the paper does not prove that label noise GD improves generalization in any practical regime and that the work belongs to a class of papers that make big claims from toy examples without generalizability.
