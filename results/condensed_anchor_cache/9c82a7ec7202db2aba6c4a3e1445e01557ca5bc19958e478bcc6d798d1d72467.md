## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces DSPart, a large-scale synthetic dataset for 2D object part segmentation generated by applying diffusion-based rendering (3D-DST) to 3D models with part annotations. It comprises two subsets: DSPart-Rigid (475 shape instances, 50 categories) and DSPart-Animal (3,065 SMAL fitted poses, 40 categories), totaling ~100K images. A Part-attention Regressor Filter (PRF) is proposed to filter low-quality animal images. Experiments in both fully-supervised (SegFormer) and unsupervised domain adaptation (DAFormer) settings show that training on DSPart outperforms existing synthetic datasets (UDAPart, CC-SSL) on PartImageNet and PascalPart. However, reviewers highlight limited technical novelty, insufficient ablation to explain performance gains, concerns about scale and domain gaps, and lack of clarity on the PRF’s role in the final dataset.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 1).
- The approach effectively leverages existing 3D part annotations and diffusion models (3D-DST) to generate realistic synthetic images with precise part masks, significantly reducing the cost of 2D annotation (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The scale and diversity of the dataset are impressive: ~100K images, 90 object categories, over 3.5K 3D models (Reviewer 1).
- Training on DSPart consistently yields superior results on real part segmentation benchmarks (PartImageNet, PascalPart) compared to training on UDAPart and CC-SSL, in both supervised and UDA settings (Reviewer 1).
- The Part-attention Regressor Filter (PRF) for animals substantially improves dataset quality (Table 5) (Reviewer 4).
- The use of diffusion models provides more realistic textures and physically consistent contexts (shadows, ground-plane interactions) than prior synthetic datasets (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty.** The method appears to be a direct application of the existing 3D-DST framework with added 3D part annotations and additional models (animals). The paper does not clearly articulate what algorithmic or mathematical challenges were overcome, nor why the extension is non-trivial (Reviewer 1, Major). Reviewer 2 notes a lack of details on which base diffusion model is used and why ControlNet was not employed for controllable generation.
- **Unfair and insufficiently analyzed comparison of PRF vs. KCF.** PRF trains models on reliable human-filtered data (e.g., 13K images) that KCF does not access, making its superior performance unsurprising. The paper provides no ablation of individual PRF components (two PARE models, three metrics, thresholds), no sensitivity analysis to the amount of human-filtered data, no investigation of alternative 3D pose estimation models, and no visual examples (e.g., images kept by PRF but not by KCF, or failure cases) (Reviewer 1, Major).
- **Lack of ablation to understand why DSPart is superior.** The paper does not disentangle the factors behind DSPart’s better performance (e.g., image realism, diversity of 3D models, number of viewpoints, total image count). Controlled experiments—such as using the same 3D models as UDAPart, varying viewpoint counts, or controlling texture diversity—are missing (Reviewer 1, Major).
- **Large domain gap between synthetic and real data.** The performance gap between models trained only on synthetic DSPart vs. only on real data is large, especially for animals (Tables 3 & 4). This suggests the generated data does not capture real-world complexity and raises questions about practical utility and scalability (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 adds that synthetic images always contain a single, centered, clear object, while real images often have multiple objects in cluttered scenes, leaving domain gaps.
- **Scale and scalability concerns.** With ~100K images, the dataset is relatively small for verifying generalization (Reviewer 2). Additionally, annotating 3D data is difficult and requires skilled annotators, limiting the scalability of the approach (Reviewer 3). The paper does not address potential bias introduced by the limited pose space covered by the human-filtered data used to train PRF (Reviewer 1, Minor).
- **Unclear role of PRF in the final dataset.** PRF is highlighted but does not appear to be used for producing the final DSPart-Animal dataset (Table 3 “DSPart-Animal” vs. Table 5 “Human”). This undermines the claim of scalability (Reviewer 4). The paper lacks an explanation of why PRF was not applied for final dataset generation.
- **Missing analysis for specific results.** The paper does not explain why PRF-filtered results are close to human-filtered except for the background (Table 5), nor why DSPart-Animal yields worse IoU for back legs and tails than CC-SSL in the syn-only setting (Table 4) (Reviewer 4, Questions).
- **Limited architectural evaluation.** Only one architecture is used for supervised (SegFormer) and one for UDA (DAFormer). Results on additional architectures would strengthen claims of generalizability (Reviewer 1, Minor).
- **Tool super-category never used in experiments** (Reviewer 1, Minor).
- **PRF cost not fully accounted.** The 20-minute inference time (Table 5) omits the 100+ hours of human labor needed to obtain the training data, and the paper does not discuss whether that training data covers the full animal pose space or introduces bias (Reviewer 1, Minor).
- **Difficulty in generalizing to other categories.** Defining semantic part annotations for arbitrary 3D models (e.g., Objaverse) is challenging; the paper does not discuss a more scalable part definition approach (e.g., similar to SAM) (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
