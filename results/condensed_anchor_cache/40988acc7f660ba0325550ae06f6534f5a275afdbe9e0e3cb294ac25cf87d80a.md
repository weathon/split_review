## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MLO-MAE, a multi-level optimization framework that uses downstream classification labels during pre‑training to learn a masking strategy for MAE. The method involves three stages: MAE pre‑training, linear probing on a classification task, and updating the masking network via validation loss. Reviewers agree that the problem is important and that the reported performance (e.g., 84.8% ImageNet fine‑tune accuracy with ViT‑B) is impressive. However, there is strong disagreement about the validity of the evaluation. Five reviewers rated the paper 3, 5, 5, 6, 3 – the lowest ratings (3) and two of the mid‑ratings (5, 3) stem from fundamental concerns: the method is not self‑supervised because it uses labels during pre‑training, making comparisons with SSL baselines unfair; the experimental setup is insufficiently controlled; and the paper lacks insight into why the masking strategy works. One reviewer (rating 6) was somewhat more positive but still raised concerns about missing baselines and transferability.

### Strengths
- Addresses a valuable and important problem: random masking may be suboptimal for images, and leveraging downstream feedback to guide masking is intuitive and reasonable (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- The multi‑level optimization approach to learning the mask is interesting (R1).
- The method is concise and effective, achieving strong performance across multiple benchmarks: 84.8% fine‑tune accuracy on ImageNet with ViT‑B (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- Extensive experiments are conducted on eight datasets covering classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation (R4, R5). Transfer learning ability is also validated (R4).
- The paper is well‑organized and easy to follow (R2); although one reviewer found the presentation poor (R1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unfair comparisons / not self‑supervised**  
   MLO‑MAE uses ImageNet class labels during pre‑training (stages 2 and 3), which violates the self‑supervised learning paradigm. Comparisons with SSL methods (e.g., MAE) are therefore unfair because performance gains may come from the label supervision rather than from the proposed masking strategy (R1, R2, R3, R5). The evaluation also uses downstream data during pre‑training and again during fine‑tuning, raising validity concerns (R3).  
   *Minority contrast:* Even the more positive reviewer (R4) noted that the method essentially splits data into training/validation sets and automates parameter adjustment, questioning transferability.

2. **Deviation from motivation**  
   The paper’s motivation is to use feedback from downstream tasks, but only classification feedback is used. For object detection or semantic segmentation, a classification‑driven masking strategy may be suboptimal (R5). The current implementation does not tailor Stage‑2 tasks to match different downstream tasks (R5).

3. **Lack of insight / theoretical justification**  
   There is no clear explanation of why the proposed masking strategy improves performance, why it outperforms other learnable masking methods (SemMAE, AutoMAE), or how the classification loss leads to focusing on foreground objects (R1, R3, R5). The authors claim that masks focus on foreground regions, but the provided visualizations are not convincing and appear random (R3). No analysis of learned masking patterns is provided (R2, R3).  
   Specific questions from reviewers: What is the theoretical background behind using linear‑probing loss to train the mask? (R1) What minimal change makes learnable masks beneficial? (R1) How does the mask manage to focus on foreground objects, and is masking all foreground patches optimal? (R5)

4. **Limited model‑size experiments**  
   All ImageNet experiments use only ViT‑B; validation on other model sizes (e.g., ViT‑S, ViT‑L) is missing (R1). It is unclear whether the method works for large models or small models (R1).

5. **Incomplete ablation studies**  
   - Ablation on the number of unrolling steps is conducted only on CIFAR‑10, not on ImageNet (R5).  
   - Ablation on patch size is restricted to 32×32 images on CIFAR‑10; studies on larger images are needed (R5).  
   - The role of BLO‑MAE (bilevel optimization variant) is puzzling: it shows no positive effect, but no analysis of why it fails is given (R1).  
   - Ablation on the linear probing loss design is missing (R1).

6. **Transfer learning concerns**  
   In the transfer learning experiments, the model undergoes additional supervised fine‑tuning on ImageNet before being transferred, which is not standard and may contaminate conclusions (R2). Questions remain about whether MLO‑MAE can be transferred without this additional fine‑tuning and whether using different labeled data during pre‑training affects transfer (R2). Also, how can a model pre‑trained on ImageNet with its patch size be effectively applied to downstream tasks like CIFAR? (R3)

7. **Computational overhead / memory issues**  
   The multi‑level optimization is cumbersome because each step must wait for the previous step to complete, leading to potential cache and memory bottlenecks beyond the Jacobian computation (R3). The overall memory footprint may limit batch size and training stability; the authors should clarify memory usage and scaling with model size and dataset (R3).

8. **Data‑split sensitivity**  
   The random 8:2 split of the ImageNet training set (into \(D_{tr}\) and \(D_{val}\)) could cause performance variations, especially given mislabeled samples in the original dataset (R3). The authors should investigate the impact of different random seeds on the split and provide a plan for addressing this limitation (R3).

9. **Missing baseline comparisons**  
   Comparisons with ColorMAE and other recent methods are missing (R4). Table 5 omits \(AP_{mask}\) for Mask R‑CNN on COCO; it should be reported (R1).

10. **Writing and presentation issues**  
    The main algorithm is difficult to understand; the mathematical formulation in Section 3.2 needs revision (R1). The description of Stage 3 – how the masking network \(T\) is updated using the validation loss – is vague, and the term “unrolling steps” is not explained (R5). A clearer explanation of gradient computation and the optimization process is needed (R5).  
    *Note:* One reviewer (R2) found the paper well‑organized, but two reviewers (R1, R5) raised significant presentation concerns.
