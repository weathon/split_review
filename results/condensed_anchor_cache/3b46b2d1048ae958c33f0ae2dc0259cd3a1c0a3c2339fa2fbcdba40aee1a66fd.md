## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces TACO, a reinforcement learning (RL) method for designing cis-regulatory elements (CREs). TACO fine-tunes a pretrained autoregressive DNA model (HyenaDNA) via RL, incorporating biologically guided reward shaping from transcription factor binding site (TFBS) motifs extracted using SHAP analysis on a LightGBM oracle. The method formulates an MDP with nucleotide-level actions and uses the oracle’s predicted activity as a final reward with intermediate TFBS-based rewards. Evaluation is performed on enhancer design for three human cell types and promoter design in two yeast media conditions. Compared to standard optimization baselines (genetic algorithms, hill climbing, etc.), TACO generates sequences with moderately high predicted activity and significantly higher sequence diversity. The paper is generally well-written and addresses an important problem, but reviewers raise significant concerns about evaluation realism, baseline selection, and the strength of contributions.

### Strengths
- The problem of generating diverse, high-fitness CREs is important and timely.
- The use of RL fine-tuning of a pretrained autoregressive model for CRE design is novel in this domain and shows promise.
- Meticulous design of the oracle (LightGBM trained with feature extraction and SHAP) for each benchmark.
- Automatic extraction of TF motifs via SHAP and their integration into reward shaping is an interesting and biologically motivated approach.
- The paper is clearly written, well-structured, and includes a comprehensive literature review.
- The method is tested across multiple cell types and conditions (human enhancers, yeast promoters), demonstrating flexibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic Evaluation Setting (offline MBO problem):** The oracle is trained on the complete dataset, then treated as a ground truth fitness function during optimization and for final evaluation. This does not reflect real biological sequence design, where only low-fitness data is available and intermediate measurements are impractical due to cost and parallelism. Controlling for out-of-distribution (OOD) sequences is critical but not addressed. This concern is raised strongly by Reviewer 3; the other reviewers also note that tasks may be “too easy” because all methods achieve high predicted activity, suggesting a flat fitness landscape or non-discriminative oracle.
- **Insufficient Baselines and Missing Comparisons:** Baselines rely on older optimization techniques (e.g., genetic algorithms) that have been consistently outperformed by modern generative methods (autoregressive models, diffusion models). Essential missing comparisons include: D3, DNADiffusion, regLM (as mentioned in the paper’s own review), and the COMs method by Reddy et al. (2024) for designing cell-type-specific promoters. Without these, the novelty and effectiveness of TACO cannot be properly assessed. (Reviewers 1 and 3)
- **Diversity Metrics are Insufficient:** The diversity metric only avoids mode collapse. Additional metrics like mean pairwise cosine similarity in embedding space, or the evaluation pipeline from D3, should be provided, especially since predicted activity appears non-discriminative. (Reviewer 1)
- **TFBS Reward Shaping Not Clearly Beneficial:** Figure 5 shows that TACO with alpha=0 (no TFBS reward) achieves comparable diversity while maintaining high activity. Including TACO(alpha=0) in Tables 2 and 3 would likely show it as a top performer, raising questions about the practical utility of the TFBS reward. A broader ablation of alpha values and a more detailed analysis are needed. (Reviewer 1)
- **Missing Ablations on Key Contributions:** The paper claims novelty from (a) using an autoregressive model as policy and (b) adding TFBS reward. These should be ablated on all tasks: e.g., a different policy model with the TFBS reward, and the autoregressive policy without the TFBS reward. RL for biological sequence design has been applied before (Angermueller et al., 2019) and that related work is underemphasized. (Reviewer 3)
- **Limitation to Known Motifs:** The TFBS reward only rewards known motifs, potentially preventing the method from designing novel TFBS motifs—a task of interest to the community. Whether the method can discover new motifs is unclear. (Reviewer 3)
- **Figure Quality and Presentation Issues:** Figures 3A and 5A are poor quality (appear as screenshots, unreadable labels). Reference formatting is inconsistent and contains errors (e.g., many arXiv versions, missing URLs, incorrect capitalization). Minor errors: abstract grammar, Equation 1 typo (p_theta→π_theta), Line 368 incomplete reference. (Reviewer 3)
- **Additional Technical and Methodological Questions Requiring Clarification (from Reviewers 1 and 2):**
    - Why retrain HyenaDNA from scratch instead of using a pretrained version? The model’s receptive field (1 million) seems excessive for CREs of length 80–200.
    - Details on MDP: How is maximum steps T determined? Is one optimization iteration one episode? How does “256 oracle calls per iteration” relate to T?
    - Training details for yeast Enformer oracle (sequences, target variable).
    - Why fitness percentile range for D is 20–80% rather than a wider range like 10–90%?
    - Could cosine similarity be used as a distance metric in LightGBM? Could TFBS pairwise interactions or distance constraints be added?
    - What threshold or adaptive strategy defines “low likelihood” for entropy regularization? How does this affect exploration-exploitation?
    - Why generate 128 sequences and report top 6? Why not report mean ± std of fitness over all generated sequences, including low-fitness ones?
    - Would continuing optimization beyond 100 iterations yield further gains? How do initial conditions affect diversity and fitness?
    - Authors claim a lack of strong encoder backbones like ESM in DNA, but many exist (Nucleotide Transformer, DNABert, HyenaDNA, Evo, Enformer, etc.). This needs justification.

- **Missing Discussion and Placement:** The paper lacks a substantial Discussion section to contextualize results and limitations. Several parts (equations, Section 4.3 on regularization, Algorithm 1 placement) could be moved to appendices to improve readability and allow for a larger discussion/conclusion. (Reviewers 1 and 2)
- **Contrast between Reviewers:** A minority reviewer (Reviewer 3, score 5) considers the evaluation tasks fundamentally unrealistic and the novelty overstated, while the other reviewers (scores 8) are more positive but agree on important weaknesses in baselines, ablation, and evaluation rigor. This split underscores the need for the authors to address the offline MBO framing and to compare against modern generative and conservative optimization methods.
