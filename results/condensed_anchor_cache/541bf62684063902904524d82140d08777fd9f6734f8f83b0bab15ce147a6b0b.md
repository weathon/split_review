## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a two-stage data valuation framework, JST, that leverages the outlier-sensitivity of methods like optimal transport to first select low-quality outliers, then re-uses those outliers as a validation set to identify high-quality data (which become outliers relative to the new set). Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR, SVHN, and Food-101 show improved data selection over pure data valuation baselines. The framework is also tested with influence functions and reinforcement-learning-based valuation. One reviewer (score 8) found the solution novel and clearly illustrated; the other three (scores 6, 3, 3) were critical of the theoretical grounding, experimental scope, and clarity.

### Strengths
- The studied problem (adapting data valuation to select high-quality data rather than just filter low-quality data) is relevant and important (R3, R4).
- The narrative is clear and easy to follow; the solution is novel and well-illustrated (R1, R2). Figure 1 effectively shows noise sensitivity for different methods (R2).
- The framework demonstrates experimental effectiveness on tested tasks (R1), with experiments across multiple image datasets: MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, SVHN, Food-101 (R2).
- The authors assess JST with semi-value approaches such as Data Banzhaf and acknowledge where JST may not perform well (R2).
- The figures in both the methodology and results sections are descriptive, nicely drawn, and intuitive (R2).

**Note:** One reviewer (score 8) was significantly more positive about the novelty and illustration; the other reviewers (scores 6, 3, 3) found the theoretical and empirical support lacking.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical justification:** The paper does not explain mathematically the observed insensitivity of data valuation methods to in-distribution data, nor does it characterize when JST is provably effective. Comparison methods (Just et al. 2023; Koh et al. 2017) include theoretical analysis (R1, R3).  
- **Unclear definition of "outlier":** The term is used without precise definition – how do outliers differ from noisy data? How are they distinguished from in-distribution data? The definition appears to shift (line 79: “high-quality data become outliers”) (R4). A clear example or definition should be given at the start (R4).  
- **Inconsistency between Figure 1 and Figure 4:** In round 1 of Figure 4, outliers are mixed with in-distribution points at low data values. This contradicts the clean separation implied by Figure 1 and raises doubts about the effectiveness of using outliers as a validation set when the initial ranking is noisy (R1).  
- **Figure 1 clarity:** It is unclear how the normalized outlier-detection and in-distribution-detection rates are computed. Is there a ground truth for classifying outliers vs. in-distribution? What would an ideal figure look like for a good data valuation method? (R3, R4)  
- **Overclaimed results and marginal improvements:** In Figure 5, the three curves are very close; stating “much higher accuracy” is overstated. The strong performance of the random baseline is not adequately discussed, especially given its horizontal-line behavior in Figure 3 (R1).  
- **Limited experimental scope:** Experiments only consider corrupted images (noise types like Gaussian, impulse). The method should be tested on other corruption types (e.g., label noise, structured outliers, low representation of sensitive groups). Would JST work for tabular data or natural (non-synthetic) outliers? (R2, R3, R4)  
- **Missing comparisons with other data valuation methods:** There are many existing methods (e.g., Sim et al. 2022). Although JST examines optimal transport, influence functions, and RL-based methods, a broader comparison is needed (R3).  
- **Time cost and scalability:** Adding a second stage likely more than doubles computation time. No experiments on time cost or trade-offs are provided; the marginal gains in Figure 5 may not justify the overhead (R1, R2). How scalable is two-level data valuation? (R2)  
- **Stability and replication issues:** The stability of JST across runs is not studied. Would duplication or near-duplication “poison” the second-level validation set? (R2)  
- **Rationale for varying noise levels across experiments:** E.g., noise level 10 in Figure 8 vs. 6/9 in Figure 7 on the same dataset – reasoning is not provided (R2).  
- **Sensitivity to the choice of validation set:** The utility function from a “general clean validation” set may be significantly different from the one using “low-quality noisy validation.” It is unclear how data values change between the two stages and whether ranking is preserved (R2).  
- **Assumption that “outlier to outliers” implies high quality:** In practice, high-quality data is narrowly defined, while there are many possible types of outliers. An outlier to a different outlier distribution is not necessarily high-quality (R3).  
- **Not adaptable to marginal-contribution-based methods (e.g., Data Banzhaf) due to lack of higher sensitivity to outliers.** Given that Banzhaf is replication-robust and consistent, the incentive to use JST over such methods is unclear (R1, R2).  
- **Figure and caption clarity:** Captions are insufficiently descriptive (e.g., Figure 3 does not state the base data valuation method; Figure 6 with random valuation is unclear on selection criteria). Text font on figures should be larger (R2). Symbols confusing: “-” in Figure 1 subtitles (“Noise Level - Std. Dev”, “NDRoutlier-NDRin-distribution”) (R1).  
- **Citation format:** Should be (author, year) instead of author (year) (R4).  
- **Feature extraction for general datasets:** Instead of assuming ResNet-18, a principled general method should be proposed (R3).  
- **Class balance:** Does JST affect class balance compared to the base method? Does it make things better or worse? (R2)
