## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewer scores range from 3 to 8, indicating substantial disagreement about the method's completeness and evaluation. The paper proposes Radar, a training-free, post-hoc method for accelerating long-context Transformer decoding. It groups tokens into contiguous segments, uses random projections to estimate aggregate attention scores per segment, and then only computes exact attention for the top‑k segments. A theoretical analysis bounds the retrieval probability of the top‑k tokens. Experiments show speedups on GPUs with small accuracy loss on several long-context tasks. Reviewers generally agree that the problem is significant and the approach is novel and practical, but several raise concerns about missing comparisons, incomplete complexity analysis, insufficient evaluation of the approximation quality, and unaddressed memory overheads. One reviewer (score 8, no weaknesses) found the work convincing, while the most critical reviewer (score 3, confidence 4) cited a lack of comparisons with several prior works and no reduction in KV‑cache memory.

### Strengths
- The hierarchical search approach using averaged random projection features to identify important segments without retraining is novel (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- The method is training‑free and applicable to any pre‑trained Transformer, avoiding additional training costs (R1, R2, R3).
- Theoretical analysis with proofs of algorithmic correctness and time complexity is provided and insightful (R1, R2, R3).
- Experimental results demonstrate real GPU speedups with competitive performance across tasks; the method outperforms streaming attention and sliding window baselines (R1, R4, R5). The algorithm is simple and practical (R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons with relevant prior work**: The paper does not compare against Scatterbrain (Chen et al., 2021), Kdeformer (Zandieh et al., 2023), Hyperattention (Han et al., 2023), SubGen (Zandieh et al., 2024), or Routing Transformers (Roy et al., 2021). The reviewer (R4) considers this a significant gap, and the authors should explicitly evaluate against these methods to establish relative performance.
- **Time‑complexity analysis is incomplete**: The claimed reduction to \(O(t^{3/2})\) ignores the cost of dynamic restructuring in lines 8–11 of Algorithm 1. One reviewer (R3) calculates that this step may cost \(\sum_{c=1}^{\sqrt{t}} c^2 = O(t^{3/2})\) instead of the assumed \(O(t)\), meaning the time to compute a single segment’s importance score is not constant. The authors need to provide a more detailed complexity breakdown or clarify the implementation.
- **Lack of direct evaluation of approximation quality**: The paper does not present true vs. approximated attention maps (heatmaps or text examples) or directly measure top‑k retrieval accuracy (R1, R5). The theoretical bound in Theorem 2 is not empirically validated; without measuring whether the algorithm actually recovers the correct top‑k index set, the practical relevance of the bound is unclear (R5). Similarly, there is no analysis of the attention score distribution, which would inform a principled choice of \(k\) (R5).
- **Contiguous segment assumption and segmentation details**: The method assumes tokens are grouped into contiguous segments. The paper does not discuss adversarial cases where the most important tokens are spread across many different segments, nor does it provide a guarantee that the top‑k segments contain the top‑k tokens (R1). It is also not clarified whether segmentation is always contiguous or whether alternative orderings were considered (R4).
- **KV‑cache memory not addressed**: The method still requires storing all key‑value embeddings during generation, so it does not reduce the memory burden of the KV cache (R4).
- **Post‑training only; no exploration of joint training**: The authors do not investigate whether incorporating the hierarchical approximation during training could further improve performance (R1).
- **Lack of explanation for failure cases of baselines**: H2O and SnapKV perform surprisingly poorly on the Mistral PG19 model (Appendix C). The paper offers no intuition for this behavior, which could help clarify why Radar generalizes better across architectures (R5).
- **Minor presentation issues** (R5): A typo on line 837 (square brackets incorrectly enclose an equality); Lemma 1 could be restated in terms of concentration to the expectation to give a practical error bound; using logarithmic time scales in figures would better visualize the claimed power‑law improvement; and the color scheme in Figure 4 should distinguish that different dimensions (not algorithms) are being compared.
