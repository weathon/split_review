## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces GUIDE, an inference-time attention enhancement method to improve LLM instruction following. It works by adding a bias to attention logits before softmax for tokens enclosed in special tags (e.g., `<!-> ... <-!>`). The bias is set so that the *Influence* score — a modified attention rollout that captures the ratio between residual stream and attention output — matches the Influence obtained when the instruction tokens are uppercased. Experiments on French summarization (metric: probability output is French), needle-in-a-haystack, and JSON generation (Jaccard index of keys) show improvement over prompting baselines and uppercasing, occasionally outperforming supervised fine-tuning (up to 1M tokens). Models used are <7B. One reviewer is notably more positive (score 5 vs. 3) but still raises concerns.

### Strengths
- The method is simple, intuitive, and training-free; it is presented straightforwardly and clearly.
- It achieves substantial improvements on selected tasks without extra resources, making it an appealing plug-and-play mechanism for deployed models.
- The Influence score provides an intuitive way to guide attention bias and offers insight into how instructions propagate through layers.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient evaluation of output quality and correctness.**  
   - The metric *p(output in French)* does not measure summary quality; nonsensical outputs may occur (e.g., δ=2). Human evaluation or automated metrics for summarization are needed.  
   - For JSON generation, the Jaccard index does not verify correctness of extracted information.  
   - It is unclear how “output in French” is evaluated.  
   - Changing attention weights may degrade output accuracy; this must be measured explicitly.  
   - Reviewer 2 asks for measurement of whether the method hurts performance.

2. **Limited scope of tasks, models, and benchmarks.**  
   - Tasks are synthetic/toy; a complex reasoning dataset should be tested to see if heavy attention on instructions breaks comprehension.  
   - Only small models (<7B) are used; experiments on larger/stronger models (e.g., Llama-3) are missing (Reviewer 2).  
   - Existing instruction-following benchmarks like IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023) should be included for more rigorous evaluation.

3. **Ambiguity and lack of clarity.**  
   - The role of Influence is ambiguous: is it used per-example to set δ dynamically or is δ a fixed hyperparameter for all examples? The plots/tables show a constant δ, raising doubt about its necessity.  
   - Influence should be called a “probe” rather than a metric, because a low Influence score does not necessarily mean poor instruction following.  
   - The manuscript is difficult to read: relationships between sections are unclear; in Sec.3.1 it is not clear what is preliminary vs. proposed; the R_U and I_U functions at the first layer are not defined before the recursive formula.  
   - Figure 4’s y-axis label “Depth” is not defined in the text.

4. **Missing related work and comparisons.**  
   - The paper does not discuss prior prompt engineering work using tags (e.g., arXiv:2309.13078) or the relationship to vector norm-based attention analysis (arXiv:2004.10102).  
   - A relevant method, Zhang et al. (ICLR 2024) “Tell Your Model Where to Attend”, is not cited or compared.

5. **Missing ablations and finer-grained experiments.**  
   - A direct comparison of Influence vs. traditional attention rollout (e.g., on verb singularity classification as in the original rollout paper) is needed to justify Influence as a contribution.  
   - Only a limited set of prompt templates is used; the effect of different prompt structures and instruction positions should be tested.  
   - Does supervised fine-tuning achieve a similar attention distribution? If not, that would further demonstrate the method’s advantage.

6. **Holistic impact on comprehension and safety not assessed.**  
   - No evaluation of how attention augmentation affects the model’s understanding of non-instruction parts of the input.  
   - Potential for malicious use / jailbreaking: e.g., wrapping adversarial instructions with the special tag, or randomly tagging many tokens. An adversarial attack benchmark should be tested.

7. **Specific experimental details unclear.**  
   - In the needle-in-a-haystack experiment: is the special tag placed on the needle token or the instruction tokens? If on the needle, it does not demonstrate instruction-following.  
   - Whether experiments are zero-shot or few-shot is not stated; if zero-shot, what would change with few-shot in-context learning?

8. **Missing automatic improvement and broader applicability.**  
   - Could an algorithm automatically set the bias using the Influence metric?  
   - For tasks like summarization, the instruction is not the only important part; how does the method handle cases where important content is elsewhere in the context?  
   - Would the method still work with LLMs trained on structured prompts (e.g., Llama3-Instruct)?
