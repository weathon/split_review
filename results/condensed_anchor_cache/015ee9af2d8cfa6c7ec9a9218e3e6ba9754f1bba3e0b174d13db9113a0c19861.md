## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper systematically evaluates 9 popular Transformer-based segmentation networks on two public organ and pathology datasets, arguing that convolutional backbones dominate performance while the Transformer component itself often contributes little. The authors also question the need for long-range dependencies in medical image segmentation, show that Transformers only bridge the gap to CNNs as dataset size increases, and use error and representational similarity analyses to reveal underutilized Transformers. The study is investigative and challenges the current trend of adding Transformer layers in hybrid architectures.

### Strengths
- **Systematic ablation and analysis**: The paper clearly dissects the roles of Transformer versus convolutional components in 9 architectures, showing that removing Transformer layers often yields minimal performance drops (Reviewer 1, 2, 3). This challenges the assumption that Transformers are critical in these models (Reviewer 2).
- **Extensive experiments and categorical benchmark**: A detailed comparative study with ablation of key architectural components, including quantitative evaluations and representational similarity analysis (Reviewer 1, 3). The benchmark covers multiple popular Transformer-based models (Reviewer 3).
- **Good readability and organization**: The paper is easy to read and well-structured (Reviewer 4). Minor: clearer caption in Fig. 3 would help (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and lack of novelty**: The findings replicate well-known insights from the Vision Transformer (ViT) paper (2020) – i.e., Transformers need large data to outperform CNNs and suffer on small medical datasets (Reviewer 1). The paper is more investigative than innovative, resembling a technical report or survey rather than a research contribution (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). Reviewer 4 states there is *no contribution or novelty*, calling it a mere comparison of nine frameworks.  
- **Narrow dataset coverage**: Only two public medical segmentation datasets are used (organ and pathology), which may not be convincing enough for an investigative study (Reviewer 1, 2). The findings may not generalize to other imaging scenarios, e.g., cardiac video segmentation where temporal dynamics might benefit from Transformers (Reviewer 2). The criticism is further limited to U-Net-like models popular in medical imaging, reducing appeal to the broader segmentation community handling panoptic/instance/video tasks (Reviewer 3).  
- **Ablation design concerns**: Removing Transformer blocks but keeping convolutional up/down sampling paths may not be the fairest comparison; replacing Transformer blocks with convolutional blocks would be more meaningful (Reviewer 1). The study also lacks ablation on hyperparameter sensitivity; a common practice for selecting transformer hyperparameters would be useful (Reviewer 1).  
- **Missing qualitative and feature-level analyses**: No qualitative comparison of segmentation masks, which is essential in medical AI (Reviewer 4). No comparison of intermediate attention maps (Reviewer 4). Representation similarity could be visualized as heat maps across network layers to give readers insight into feature extraction (Reviewer 4).  
- **Methodological concerns in error/representational analysis**:  
  - The use of volumetric error overlap to infer model behavior is questionable because all models give point estimates; the observed differences could be due to underlying uncertainty rather than architecture. The authors should provide volumetric error maps across three runs of the same model as a reference for uncertainty (Reviewer 3).  
  - The thresholds (0.95, 0.85, etc.) for representational change measurement appear arbitrary and need justification (Reviewer 3).  
- **Incomplete literature review and selection bias**: The paper lacks a thorough literature review (Reviewer 4). The choice of 9 out of many Transformer networks is not justified by a valid assumption (Reviewer 4).  
- **Unfair comparison with nnU-Net**: nnU-Net, used as a benchmark, benefits from extensive data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization that the evaluated Transformer models may not apply. For a fair comparison, data augmentation and training protocols should be standardized across models (Reviewer 2).  
- **Applicability of findings**: The success of Transformers is often attributed to less inductive bias; applications that do not need that property may not benefit. Findings 2) and 3) directly translate this point, reducing their novelty (Reviewer 1). The paper offers no new ideas or solutions to mitigate the identified flaws, which is a major weakness (Reviewer 3).  
- **Pretraining not clarified**: It is unclear whether the Transformer blocks used in the evaluated models were pretrained on natural images (e.g., ImageNet) (Reviewer 2).  

*Contrast in reviewer opinions*: While most reviewers (1,2,3) rate the paper as a fair-to-good investigative study (scores 3,5,5) but lacking innovation, Reviewer 4 is highly negative (score 1), deeming it devoid of contribution, with flawed experimental design, insufficient literature review, and missing qualitative and attention-map analyses.
