## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces PowerSoftmax, a homomorphic encryption (HE)-friendly variant of self-attention that replaces the exponential softmax with polynomial scaling to enable private Transformer inference. The authors propose three modifications: Lipschitz division to prevent division by zero, Stable PowerSoftmax scaling (analogous to subtracting max in standard softmax), and a Length-Agnostic Range that uses different functions for training and inference to narrow the input range. They train polynomial variants of RoBERTa up to 32 layers and 1.4 billion parameters—the largest polynomial LLMs to date—and evaluate on multiple benchmarks (including ARC), showing reasoning and in-context learning capabilities comparable to standard transformers. A latency breakdown (Figure 3) is provided.

### Strengths
- **Important and timely research question:** Protecting user privacy in LLM inference is critical, and making self-attention HE-friendly is a key challenge.
- **Wide range of experiments:** Experiments cover BERT-based, GPT-based, and ViT-based models, many tasks, parameter sizes (70M, 135M, 1.4B), deep models (32 layers), and include comparisons with standard softmax and epsilon variations. Results are averaged over three seeds with all parameters recorded, ensuring reproducibility. Ablation studies on 8 datasets are included.
- **Proposed methods improve HE efficiency:** Replacing non-polynomial functions (exponential/softmax) with polynomial counterparts reduces the overhead of HE-based private inference; the paper claims up to 6% time reduction relative to softmax overhead from prior work.
- **Stable training and polynomial form:** Stable PowerSoftmax and Lipschitz division address numerical stability, and the Length-Agnostic variant enables pre-computation to speed inference. The approach extends the Approximation-Aware Training (AAT) paradigm to transformers with better scalability than prior direct-approximation or pointwise-attention methods.
- **Code and detailed results provided.**

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient HE experiments and missing latency comparison.** No actual HE implementation was conducted—only a latency breakdown (Figure 3) without concrete HE settings. Critical details missing: HE scheme (BFV, CKKS, or TFHE), polynomial modulus degree, ciphertext modulus size, noise variance, security level (e.g., 128-bit), threat model (semi-honest/malicious), multiplicative depth, and whether bootstrapping is applied after each operation. The paper claims efficiency but provides no latency comparison with previous HE-based neural network methods, making it impossible to assess the method’s practical advantage. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
2. **Limited novelty.** Replacing softmax with a polynomial function is a standard technique in private LLM inference. Many accompanying techniques are incremental: adding epsilon (Lipschitz division) is standard practice; scaling numerator/denominator (Stable PowerSoftmax) is conceptually identical to subtracting max in regular softmax; the Supplementary training procedure and polynomial approximations are adapted from prior work; and the \(l_\infty\)-norm regularizer (Eqn. 8) is well-known. The main original contributions reduce to PowerSoftmax and separating training/inference functions with Length-Agnostic Range. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
3. **Scalability concerns and training-from-scratch requirement.** The method requires retraining from scratch, which is often impractical for large LLMs. While a 32-layer Transformer is demonstrated, it remains questionable whether the approach can scale to much larger models (e.g., hundreds of billions of parameters) without loss of expressivity. The simplification of complex nonlinear functions may cause incomplete retention of nonlinearity and expressive power compared to standard softmax. (Reviewers 2, 3)
4. **Unclear threat model and security description.** The problem setting (Section 3) is vague regarding adversarial capabilities and the role of parties (client, model provider, server). Why model weights must be encrypted in a two-party setting is not justified. The claim about HE in untrusted environments (line 109) is inaccurate: HE also suffers from verifiability issues and side-channel attacks. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Reliability concerns with zero-shot and 5-shot evaluations.** The paper uses these few-shot results to claim ICL and reasoning capabilities (e.g., ARC), but these alone are insufficient to gauge model quality. A fully fine-tuned evaluation on downstream tasks would be more appropriate to assess the impact of the polynomial approximation. (Reviewer 2, related to Reviewer 1’s broader support for the conclusions)
6. **Presentation and writing issues.** Several notation problems: \(\epsilon'\) in Eqn. (5) and \(\odot\) in Eqn. (9) are undefined (the latter defined only for ciphertext scalars, not matrices); “x” in line 258 should be formatted as \(x\); line 219 references Eqn. (6) without prior description, disrupting readability. In Section 4.3, division invariant is discussed, but Figure 2 shows subtraction invariant (\(x-\max(|x|)\)), causing inconsistency. (Reviewers 3, 4)
7. **GELU function not addressed in latency breakdown.** Figure 3 does not include the latency of GELU. Is GELU not used? Is softmax the only bottleneck? (Reviewer 3)
8. **Missing details on parameter \(p\) (polynomial degree) and multiplicative depth.** The choice of \(p\) is pivotal for the efficiency-utility trade-off, but no guidance is given. Multiplicative depth for HE operations is not stated, nor is it clear if bootstrapping is performed after each shown operation. (Reviewer 3)
9. **Clarification needed on training stability claim.** The authors state (line 218) that Eqn. (2) and Eqn. (6) lead to training instability, but provide no detailed explanation. (Reviewer 4)

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* One reviewer (score 6) considered the method a breakthrough in scalability and efficiency, highlighting the extension to 1.4B parameters and the length-agnostic inference as novel. The other three reviewers (score 3) found the contribution limited, noting the lack of actual HE comparison, the incremental nature of most techniques, and scalability concerns. This contrast is preserved.
