## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Active In-Context Learning (AICL) for Large Multimodal Models (LMMs), which dynamically selects and annotates a small set of test samples at query time via Spectral-based Representative Sampling (SRS) and uses Similarity-enhanced TopK Prompt Construction (SimTopKPC) to leverage those samples. Experiments on 10 specialized datasets with four LMMs are reported. Two reviewers rated the paper 5, and two rated it 3, with the lower scores driven primarily by concerns about limited novelty, weak baselines, and an unclear problem setting.

### Strengths
1. The paper addresses a practical and significant problem: reducing the need for large labeled training sets in multimodal ICL, especially in specialized domains.  
2. All reviewers found the paper well-written, clear, and easy to follow, with well-organized figures and tables (R1, R2, R3).  
3. The experimental evaluation is comprehensive across multiple datasets and models, and the method shows performance improvements over the baselines tested (R1, R2, R3).  
4. The approach is technically sound, lightweight, and easy to implement (R1, R2).  
5. The idea of fully leveraging active samples through prompt construction is appreciated as a step toward maximizing annotation efficiency in active learning (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and overclaimed contributions.** The core techniques—spectral clustering for representative selection and cosine similarity for retrieval—are well-known in active learning and ICL. Several reviewers note that clustering for representative sampling was proposed by Kang et al. (PAKDD 2004) for active learning, and active ICL was previously introduced by Margatina et al. (EMNLP 2023), which also uses clustering for selection. The authors do not adequately differentiate their work from these prior methods (R1, R2, R3). The claimed novel component of explicitly informing the LMM about the most similar image’s true class (SimTopKPC) may cause the model to simply perform nearest-neighbor classification, not genuine reasoning; this potential shortcut is not experimentally verified (R3).

2. **Unclear or flawed problem setting.** The paper lacks clarity on how active learning, ICL, and training/test data relate. It is ambiguous whether test data is assumed unlabeled, and if so, how evaluation is performed on a partially labeled test set without bias. Selective sampling from the test set could artificially lower test loss if high-error instances are removed (R4). The definitions of “training set,” “unlabeled set,” and “test set” conflict with conventional usage; the paper states “active sample directly select from test batch,” which implies part of the test data is used for training—a practice that is counterintuitive and risks invalid evaluation (R4). Additionally, no details are given on which model parameters are trainable, what loss functions or optimization techniques are used, or how active sampling influences learning (R4). The equations in the paper only describe prediction functions, not a learning process (R4).

3. **Weak and incomplete baselines.** The compared baselines are mostly naive (random selection), and several important combinations (e.g., RandomAL + TopKPR, BeginAL + TopKPR) are missing (R1, R2). Recent active ICL methods for LLMs (Margatina et al., Zhang et al. 2022) are not included, and classical active learning strategies such as uncertainty sampling and diversity sampling are dismissed without justification or comparison. Because spectral clustering is itself a form of diversity sampling, the paper does not show why it outperforms other diversity-based techniques (R4). One reviewer noted a likely error in Figure 6, which appears to include a TopKPR instance in the center (R2).

4. **Assumptions not evaluated or questionable.** The method assumes the number of clusters equals the true number of classes, which is generally unknown and could strongly affect results (R3). Equation (3) proposes spreading selection over batches, but the experiments only consider a “static” setting where the budget is exhausted in the first batch; the dynamic setting is not evaluated, making the motivation for “early selection” unclear (R3). The results in Figure 2, which show a large gap between ICL and active ICL performance, may be misleading because a labeled training set more representative of the test set would likely require fewer samples (R3).

5. **Minor errors and missing details.**  
   - Equation (4): notation should be “c = 1,2,…,m” not “c ∈ 1,2,…,m” (R1).  
   - Equation (7): index i is not used; should be “x_a ∈ D_l” (R1, R3).  
   - The definition of the label budget B^i (line 297) is circular: B^i = Σ n_c · m = m · B^i? (R1, R3).  
   - Figure 11(a) caption is incorrect; Figure 10 is not referenced in the text (R1, R3).  
   - Figure 8 combines representativeness and class balance into one score, so it is unclear if the method achieves highest scores on both individually (R3).  
   - The difference between RandomAL and BeginAL is not explicitly explained (R3).  
   - The baselines leave out certain combinations (R2, R3).  
   - One reviewer questions why the authors did not use a pool-based active learning setting rather than streaming query-time selection (R2).  
   - It is unclear whether the budget B^i is equal for each batch; if not, how is it set? (R1)  
   - The datasets have small label spaces; can the method scale to larger label spaces? (R1)
