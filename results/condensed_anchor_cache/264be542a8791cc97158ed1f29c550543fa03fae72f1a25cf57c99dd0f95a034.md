## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces a clustering approach for motion vectors extracted from standard video encoders, leveraging the notion of coresets generalized to segments. The method yields an FPRAS for robust clustering and is applied to video tracking and 3D map creation from video, with implementation on low-end micro-computers (Le-Potato and Raspberry Pi) and demonstrations on synthetic and real-world videos with claimed real-time performance. The work extends coreset ideas from points to segments and adapts the results of Feldman and Schulman (2012). One reviewer rated the paper a 6, three others rated it 3, 1, and 3, representing strong disagreement.

### Strengths

- The main contribution of generalizing coreset ideas to segments and deriving a computationally efficient tracking algorithm is interesting. (R1)
- One reviewer found the paper well written and easy to follow, with theoretically sound vector clustering and no obvious errors. (R2) [Note: this is a minority view; other reviewers strongly disagree on writing quality.]
- The incorporation of traditional machine learning techniques in the age of deep learning is significantly interesting. (R2)
- The idea of directly using motion vectors from video codecs as input to computer vision tasks is original and promising. (R3)
- The paper is self-contained with detailed proofs and definitions; Figure 2 helps understand the cost function. (R3)
- The approach brings to the attention of the computer vision community a class of "probably approximately correct" algorithms. (R3)
- The proposed solution achieves real-time performance on low-end boards for popular computer vision problems like tracking and map creation. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient experimental evaluation.** (R1, R2, R3, R4)
  - Results are only demonstrated on two datasets (Big Buck Bunny, drone video) with scant quantitative analysis. (R1, R2, R3)
  - No comparison with any alternative tracking method, e.g., mean-shift based tracking (Comaniciu et al., CVPR 2000), which also achieves real-time on low-power hardware. (R1)
  - No comparison with classic clustering or robust statistical methods. (R1)
  - Claims of superiority over neural-net methods are unsubstantiated by direct comparison. (R1)
  - Only three application scenarios are validated; results are poorly demonstrated, making it impossible to judge the quality of the obtained 3D map. (R2, R4)
  - The FPS calculation is invalid: subtracting all computing times except clustering from the pipeline and dividing the number of frames by the remainder is not a valid FPS. (R3)
  - Experimental results are not extensively reported or analyzed, neither in the main text nor in the supplementary material. (R2)

- **Lack of related work and missing comparisons.** (R1, R2, R3)
  - The paper lacks a proper introduction and discussion of related work, making it difficult to assess the contribution. (R2)
  - No comparison with prior coreset work for lines (e.g., Coreset for Line-Sets Clustering, Lotan et al., 2022). (R1)
  - The relationship to Rosman et al. (2014) on coresets for segments is unclear; the claimed novelty of Algorithm 1 appears to be a simple modification. (R3)
  - No mention of how the method compares to OpenCV implementations. (R1)

- **Poor presentation, organization, and clarity.** (R1, R3, R4)
  - The paper is not well organized: introductory sections ("Video Tracking", "Motion Vectors") do not describe the problem; "Our Approach" does not describe the approach but introduces a cost function discussed later. (R3)
  - Many typos and grammatical mistakes. (R4)
  - Algorithm 1 should be replaced by a simple formula; it is unclear how the novelty generalizes previous work. (R3)
  - Algorithm 2 is not illustrated by Figure 3; a key component (Feldman and Shulman CORESET, 2012) was replaced with a different method (Bachem et al., 2018) for "easier implementation" without justification. (R3)
  - The steps of the video tracking method are unclear: no explanation for "Add for each motion vector its degree to (0, 10) and (1, 0)" and no discussion of moving beyond k=2. (R3)
  - The jump from clustering of motion vectors to 3D map creation is not explained; the empirical evaluation of map creation repeats steps almost verbatim. (R3)
  - Unusual citations (OpenCV, Python manual, Ubuntu guide, Raspberry Pi guide, etc.) are included. (R3)
  - The conclusion section cannot be moved to an appendix. (R3)
  - Confusion between "tracing" and "tracking" in Section 3. (R3, R4)
  - Figure 1: blue motion vector is hardly visible; Figure 2: side-by-side placement does not match caption indicating top/bottom. (R4)

- **Obscure theoretical presentation.** (R2, R3)
  - The proofs in Section 2 are obscure and difficult to follow; many crucial steps are relegated to the supplementary material, impacting readability. (R2, R3)

- **Missing details and unresolved questions** that overlap with criticisms or missing experiments:
  - No discussion of the tradeoff between computational complexity and (ε, δ) choices during coreset construction. (R1)
  - It is unclear why the number of samples on a segment does not depend on segment length in Algorithm 1. (R3)
  - In Algorithm 1, r is defined in Definition 2.3 but should be provided as input since the function D to which r corresponds is not given. (R3)
  - Was Algorithm 2 used at all? If Algorithm 2 already produces a clustering, what is the purpose of using Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007)? (R3)
  - How does Arthur and Vassilvitskii's k-means++ fit with the claimed implementation in Bradski (2000)? (R3)
  - How is a motion vector clustering algorithm applied to map creation? (R3)
  - Why do computations on Raspberry-Pi and utilization of a gyroscope contribute to a "fair comparison"? (R3)
  - General comment: "tracking and map creation is not computer vision" – the title should reflect that these are specific problems studied in computer vision. (R4)
