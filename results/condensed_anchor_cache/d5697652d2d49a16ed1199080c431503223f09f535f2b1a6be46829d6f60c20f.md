## Merged Review

### Summary
GFSE (Graph Foundational Structural Encoder) is a cross-domain graph structural encoder pre-trained with multiple self-supervised learning objectives (shortest path prediction, motif counting, edge-level and graph-level contrastive learning) on a Graph Transformer backbone with biased attention. It aims to produce generic, theoretically expressive positional and structural encodings (PSE) that can be integrated with downstream graph encoders (GNNs, LLMs). Evaluations on synthetic and real-world molecular, social, and citation datasets claim an average performance improvement of 20.48% across eight datasets. Reviewers note the paper is well-organized but question overclaiming, novelty, missing baselines, and insufficient ablation studies.

### Strengths
1. **Relevant problem**: The direction of graph foundation models and cross-domain structural encoding is highly relevant and challenging (R1, R2).
2. **Comprehensive PE/SE comparisons**: Extensive comparative experiments on positional and structural encodings (R1). Complexity analysis and runtime comparisons with existing structural encodings are provided (R3).
3. **Meaningful pre-training validation**: Pre-training stage validation gives deeper insights beyond downstream-only evaluation (R1). Multi-task pre-training combining different structural aspects is interesting (R4).
4. **Solid theoretical foundation**: The method is supported by theoretical expressiveness analysis (R5). Technically sound (R3).
5. **Versatility**: GFSE can be applied to basic graph reasoning, classification, and LLM-based inference (R5).
6. **Well-written**: The paper is generally easy to follow and well-structured (R3, R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Overclaiming as Foundation Model** (R1, R4, R5):
   - GFSE is a pre-trainable PE/SE module, not a graph foundation model; it is more appropriately positioned as a pre-trained structural encoding model (R1).
   - Claim of “first cross-domain graph structural encoder” ignores prior cross-domain pre-training (e.g., GCC, GraphMAE) (R4).
   - The 20.48% average improvement is selectively calculated: many results show <1% improvement (e.g., MNIST: 0.31%, PubMed: 0.38%), and best results are cherry-picked from different model combinations (R4).
   - Contribution unclear as a foundational model; would be better described as a universal graph positional encoding method (R5).

2. **Limited Technical Novelty** (R2, R3, R4):
   - The architecture is a composite of existing methods: GraphGPS, GRIT’s RRWP, Attention Bias (R2). Core architecture borrowed from GPS with minimal modifications; biased attention is a straightforward extension (R4).
   - Pre-training tasks are adapted from prior graph learning literature; multi-task learning uses standard uncertainty-based loss weighting from prior work; community detection follows [10]; motif counting based on [11] (R4).
   - Most components have been introduced in prior graph transformer models (R3).

3. **Missing Comparisons and Baselines** (R1, R2, R3, R4):
   - Downstream evaluation (Section 4.4) lacks comparisons with SOTA models; results do not meet current SOTA (R2, R3). Missing comparisons with newer graph transformers like Nodeformer and GRIT (R3).
   - Table 5 (LLM experiments) should compare with existing LLM+graph integration models (e.g., OFA) (R1).
   - No comparison with recent advances in cross-domain graph pre-training (R4).
   - Missing related work: shortest-path distance PSE [1] and graph contrastive learning [2,3] (R2).

4. **Incomplete Ablation and Missing Experiments** (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5):
   - Missing experiment: evaluation without pre-training GFSE (full pipeline from scratch on downstream). Table 4’s “train from scratch” uses a different backbone model than “fine-tuned,” making direct comparison impossible (R1).
   - Impact of number of pre-training datasets on downstream performance not shown; missing trend analysis (R1).
   - Missing ablation study comparing GFSE with GRIT’s RRWP to isolate unique contributions (R2).
   - Ablation only on pre-training sub-objectives; should also ablate pre-training domains/datasets to reveal which domains contribute to cross-domain generalization (R3, R4).
   - No analysis of task interactions, shown to be crucial in prior work (R4).
   - Lack of evidence that pre-training on one dataset (e.g., A) transfers to another (e.g., B) with strong performance (R5).
   - Would like to see performance on link prediction tasks and heterophilic graphs (R5).

5. **Motivation and Design Choices Unclear** (R1, R5):
   - Why these four pre-training tasks? Why not include graph-level reconstruction or node-level contrastive learning? Why motif counting specifically for the node-level task? (R1).
   - Why input \(P_M\) and \(P_T\) separately to the MLP rather than concatenated? (R1).
   - Why use only real-world graphs for pre-training instead of generated structures? (R1).
   - Motivation for a universal positional embedding is unclear; why not train a positional encoder per dataset? (R5). Similarity to UniAug not addressed (R5).

6. **Methodological Details Lacking** (R1, R3):
   - How are graphs from different domains (molecular, social, citation) featurized to be encoded by the same encoder? Only random walk encoding mentioned; unclear preprocessing steps for diverse graph types (R3).
   - Figure 1: How are textual features (B.2) input to GFSE? Pre-training only receives P and R, no text input; how are textual features used in downstream? (R1).
   - Equation (1): dimensions of P and R should be \(N \times (d+1)\) and \(N \times N \times (d+1)\) (R1).
   - Section 4.3 lacks detailed descriptions of hyperparameter tuning, undermining credibility (R2).

7. **Theoretical Guarantees Limitations** (R4):
   - Expressiveness analysis follows similar arguments to prior work; proofs rely on assumptions challenged in recent research (R4).

8. **Usability and Reproducibility** (R5):
   - No official codebase provided; unclear if code will be released (R5).
   - Questions on effectiveness in low-label scenarios (R5) and dataset overlap with InstructGLM (R2).

**Reviewer Disagreement**: Scores range from 3 (R4, high confidence) to 6 (R1, R2, moderate-high confidence). R4 is significantly more critical about overclaiming and novelty, while R1 and R2 are more positive but still note missing experiments and overclaiming. R3 and R5 are moderately positive but emphasize lack of SOTA comparisons and unclear foundation model positioning.
