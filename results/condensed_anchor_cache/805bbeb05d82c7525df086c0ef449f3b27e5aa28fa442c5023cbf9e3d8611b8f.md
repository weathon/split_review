## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents SPM-Diff for virtual try-on, which leverages semantic point matching as a visual correspondence prior. Semantic points extracted from the garment are warped to the target body via local flow (from GP-VTON) and augmented with 3D depth/normal maps. Experiments on VITON-HD and DressCode achieve state-of-the-art results. Reviewer ratings range from 6 to 8 (two 6’s, one 8). All reviewers acknowledge the method’s effectiveness, but there is disagreement: one reviewer (R2, confidence 5) is notably more positive about the 3D enhancement and detail preservation, while others (R1 and R3, confidence 4) emphasize the need for better technical details, evaluation of point-matching accuracy, and clearer novelty justification.

### Strengths
- The use of semantic point matching as a prior enhances garment detail preservation and reduces diffusion model stochasticity, enabling precise garment reproduction.
- The incorporation of 3D depth and normal maps improves realism and garment fit by modeling garment behavior in 3D space.
- The method achieves state-of-the-art performance on VITON-HD and DressCode benchmarks.
- Code is provided to ensure reproducibility.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- The idea of using matched semantic points as an additional supervision signal for diffusion models is interesting and reasonable.

### Weaknesses
- **Local flow warping**: The warping method (from GP-VTON) is not described in sufficient detail. Its limitations under significant pose variations, occlusions, and loose garments (e.g., dresses/skirts) are not analyzed, and it is unclear whether the point prior would still work in such challenging cases. (R1, R3)
- **Point-matching accuracy**: The accuracy of semantic point matching is not quantitatively evaluated. Missing metrics such as average correspondence error, and no comparison with other state-of-the-art point matching methods. Figure 5 would benefit from color coding (e.g., red vs. green) to distinguish correct from incorrect matches, and the method to assess correctness is unclear. (R1, R3)
- **Feature injection illustration**: The paper lacks a figure showing how point features are injected into the Main-UNet; such an illustration would help clarify the integration process. (R1)
- **Point count sensitivity**: The model’s dependence on the number of semantic points may cause instability due to interpolation/projection errors at large counts. The paper does not explore alternative point selection strategies (e.g., keypoint detectors or learned feature maps) that could mitigate this. (R2)
- **Depth/normal map reliance**: The method relies heavily on accurate depth and normal maps, which may limit generalization to datasets or real-world images without reliable 3D cues. Robustness to noisy or inaccurate depth/normal maps is not evaluated, and potential solutions (e.g., uncertainty estimates) are not discussed. (R2)
- **Novelty and differentiation**: The introduction of visual correspondence into diffusion models for virtual try-on is not clearly differentiated from existing warping-based methods (e.g., WarpDiffusion). The novelty of “semantic point matching” over traditional “warping parameters learning” is not articulated. The heavy reliance on GP-VTON for the warping step raises concerns about the technical contribution and insufficient exploration of the warping method’s failure modes. (R2, R3)
- **Lack of user studies**: Evaluations focus on image quality metrics and do not include user-centered metrics such as perceived realism, fit satisfaction, or overall experience, which are crucial for virtual try-on applications. (R2)
- **Pre-trained encoders**: The pre-trained garment and geometry feature encoders (lines 262-264) are not clearly explained regarding their training or initialization. (R3)
