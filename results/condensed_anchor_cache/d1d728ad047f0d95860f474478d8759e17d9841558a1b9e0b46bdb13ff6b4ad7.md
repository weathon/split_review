## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper critiques the “one-prompt-for-all” approach of existing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and proposes a task-specific “supervised” CoT enriched with human guidance. The authors formulate CoT reasoning into a “prompt space” and an “answer space” and argue that unsupervised CoT relies on heuristic-driven templates that are often unreliable, whereas supervision can sample more informative prompt templates. Experiments on sorting, parity checking, and other tasks compare unsupervised/supervised CoT with task-specific RNNs and transformers, claiming that supervised CoT closes the gap in context-sensitive tasks. Reviewers are divided: one sees a meaningful contribution (score 5), while three are critical (scores 3,1,1), citing weak empirical support, limited theoretical novelty, and well-known conclusions.

### Strengths

- Addresses an important shortcoming of Transformers: fixed computational depth limits sequential reasoning, and CoT provides a discrete hidden state that mitigates this limitation.
- Formulates CoT reasoning into “prompt space” and “answer space” and analyzes the complexity C(m,s) as a function of information in hidden state and CoT steps.
- Provides a comprehensive summary of prior theoretical works that understand CoT as enabling recurrent-like computation in Transformers.
- Offers an interesting perspective on the role of CoT prompts by separating prompt selection from answer generation.
- Innovatively proposes supervised CoT as a way to improve reasoning accuracy over the “one-prompt-for-all” method, highlighting real-world relevance.
- The paper is well-structured, with clear explanations of core concepts and a step-by-step breakdown of task examples.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited empirical support and overclaimed novelty**  
  - The conclusion that LLM performance improves with task-specific prompts is well-established and has driven the field of prompt engineering; the paper does not offer surprising or new empirical findings.
  - Experiments focus on straightforward tasks (sorting, parity checking), limiting generalizability to more complex multi‑step or open-ended reasoning tasks.
  - No comparisons to other advanced prompting or fine‑tuning techniques (e.g., few‑shot CoT with validation, instruction tuning, or reason‑and‑act methods) are provided, making it hard to judge the unique benefit of supervised CoT.

- **Theoretical analysis lacks rigor and practical guidance**  
  - The definitions and assumptions in Section 3 (e.g., cardinality of chess answer space, “useful” information) are imprecise and the mathematical derivation does not lead to meaningful conclusions beyond stating that both prompt space and answer space are large.
  - No systematic framework is given for discovering the correct prompt template; the paper falls back on manual prompt engineering, limiting practicality.
  - The explanation of recurrent vs. autoregressive models (Section 2) misrepresents transformer inference (the KV cache provides a hidden state over the sequence). The paper’s characterization of autoregressive models as lacking hidden state does not reflect actual LLM operation.

- **Supervision scalability and methodology**  
  - The “supervised CoT” requires case‑by‑case human feedback; the paper does not discuss the cost or feasibility of providing such supervision across diverse tasks.
  - It is unclear how the “correct step templates” are validated (e.g., on a validation set) or why a language model cannot deduce the step template from few‑shot examples.
  - The claim that unsupervised CoT prompt templates are heuristic‑driven is questionable, because CoT often includes few‑shot examples that provide grounded rather than heuristic templates.

- **Unclear objectives and missing details**  
  - The paper does not clarify whether the goal is to increase the chance of discovering the correct solution or the model’s throughput (e.g., number of output tokens). In examples like BFS vs. DFS, different output lengths can both yield correct answers, but this is not addressed.
  - Key experiment details are missing: Table 2 should clarify the average number of output tokens (steps) taken per technique.

- **Poor presentation**  
  - Every figure is extremely blurry and poorly designed; the overall presentation and readability fall far below ICLR standards.
  - The introduction is dense with technical details, making it inaccessible to readers unfamiliar with computational depth limitations and CoT theory.

- **Unresolved questions left as criticisms**  
  - Why does the answer space for chess have infinite cardinality? The number of legal game states is finite.  
  - At L306, “useful information” is undefined; e.g., knowing that only two kings remain clearly signals the game is over.  
  - Given that autoregressive models can use large context windows, what fundamentally differentiates recurrent and autoregressive models in this paradigm when both have hidden state (implicit vs. explicit)?  
  - Can the performance of supervised CoT be matched by simply fine‑tuning the LLM on the downstream dataset?  
  - Is it possible to derive an automated, generalized supervision methodology from the proposed theoretical analysis?
