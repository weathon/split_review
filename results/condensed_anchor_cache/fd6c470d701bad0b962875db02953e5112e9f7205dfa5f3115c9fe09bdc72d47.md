## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Multitask Discriminator Proximity-guided IRL (MPIRL), an inverse reinforcement learning method that learns a generalizable reward function and policy from an offline multi-task demonstration dataset, using only a few target task demonstrations. MPIRL combines a multi-task adversarial discriminator (trained on state-action pairs) with a proximity reward that penalizes states based on their temporal distance to the expert state distribution, guiding the agent back toward expert states. The method is evaluated on simulated navigation and manipulation tasks.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a relevant and important problem: few-shot IRL with multi-task data, where few demonstrations do not fully specify the task.
- The core idea (combining a multi-task adversarial discriminator with a temporal-distance-based proximity reward) is intuitive and sound. Ablation experiments show that both terms are necessary for success.
- MPIRL demonstrates substantial improvement over existing few-shot IRL and imitation learning baselines (GAIL, BC, DVD) across three simulated navigation and manipulation environments.
- The method simultaneously learns a policy and a reward function, which can be beneficial for online fine-tuning.
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow; code is provided for reproducibility.
- Reviewers varied in overall assessment (scores 6, 3, 3, 3): one reviewer was highly positive (score 6, confidence 2), while the other three (score 3, confidence 3–4) were more critical but still acknowledged problem relevance and the method’s practical potential.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical grounding for “well-shaped” reward**: The claim that MPIRL learns a “well-shaped” reward function lacks theoretical analysis or guarantee that the shaped reward preserves the same optimal policy as the true reward. Methods like CSIL (Watson et al., 2023) and OLLIE (Yue et al., 2024) provide such guarantees. Visual inspection of reward maps (Figure 6) does not clearly show a well-shaped reward; the discriminator-only and proximity-only rewards do not appear well-shaped. Visualizing combined MPIRL maps alongside these in Figure 6 would be helpful.
- **Outdated baselines**: The most recent baseline is DVD (Chen et al., 2021). Missing comparisons to more recent few-shot IRL, meta-learning, or adversarial imitation learning baselines (e.g., Orsini et al., 2021). An expert/oracle baseline (e.g., PPO trained on ground-truth reward) is also absent.
- **Relative discriminator reward**: The discriminator $D(s,a)$ is trained on the multi-task dataset but depends on the current policy, making the reward signal relative rather than absolute; the learned reward function does not independently specify the task.
- **Insufficient explanation and ablation for balancing rewards**: The choice of $\lambda_{prox}=6$ and proximity discount $\gamma=0.001$ (Appendix C.4) is not well justified. No ablation on $\lambda_{prox}$ or $c_{thresh}$ is provided, and the impact of larger $\gamma$ is unclear. The necessity of staged training (Appendix C.3) is not evaluated and is not mentioned in Algorithm 1 or the main body.
- **Missing experiments**: No evaluation with $N=1$ target demonstrations. Scalability on more challenging multi-task domains (e.g., RLBench) is not explored. No ablation using the learned reward with an online RL method (e.g., for fine-tuning).
- **Clarity issues**: The term “too few demonstrations” in the title and introduction lacks precise definition. Equation 3 is missing the inequality $> c_{thresh}$ in the first condition. Notation is confusing: $D$ is used for both the discriminator and the replay buffer. The implementation of the BC baseline (Eqn 5) is unclear: is it minimizing MSE or maximizing likelihood? The distinction between few-shot IRL and meta-learning (Section 2.2) is insufficiently explained. The derivation of the minimum value of $P(s)$ as -1 (lines 210–211) is not justified.
- **Limited contextualization of adversarial imitation learning**: The paper discusses only GAIL; a more comprehensive comparison with other adversarial imitation methods (e.g., from Orsini et al., 2021) and a discussion of how design choices influence learning would better highlight novelty.
- **Lack of intuition for discriminator loss design**: The rationale behind Eqn 1 (first term for task-specific adversarial training, second for multi-task training) is not explained.
- **Additional unresolved questions**: Can MPIRL be extended to use data of diverse quality? How robust is the method to different $\lambda_{prox}$ and $c_{thresh}$? Could alternative RL algorithms like SAC be used? Why subtract gamma for backwards re-labeling rather than multiply by a value like 0.99? What optimal policy does the combined reward function ultimately optimize?
