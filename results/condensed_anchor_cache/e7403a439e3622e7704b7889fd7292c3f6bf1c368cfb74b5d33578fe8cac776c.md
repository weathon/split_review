## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes HR-MMD, a non-parametric two-sample test for set-based membership inference, using high-level representations (e.g., last-layer embeddings) to detect whether a dataset contains training members. The method provides statistical significance guarantees and achieves high TPR at low FPR. Reviewers praise the clear motivation, generic protocol, and strong empirical results, but raise major concerns about practical assumptions, incomplete experimental details, insufficient comparisons to prior set-based MIAs (e.g., dataset inference [2104.10706], user inference [2310.09266]), lack of ablation justifying representation choices, and unclear novelty relative to the deep kernel test from Liu et al. (2020b). One reviewer (Reviewer 4) is notably more positive (score 6) and finds the approach novel and well-written, while others (scores 5) lean reject and question the technical contribution and evaluation rigor. Reviewer 3 considers the work “just an application” of prior work; Reviewer 2 after rebuttal remains leaning reject (perhaps a 4).

### Strengths
- Introduces statistical inference (two-sample test) into membership inference, providing a significance guarantee on set-level membership decisions.
- Proposes a deep kernel function useful for membership inference on deep classification models; analyzes test statistics and asymptotic behavior.
- Strong empirical results: achieves near-perfect TPR at low FPR across image datasets, a text dataset, and a tabular dataset.
- Generic protocol that can accommodate various input statistics (e.g., loss, confidence scores, high-level representations).
- Clear motivation and important problem: detecting privacy breaches when only a small proportion of a set are training members.
- Approach is novel, not relying on heuristics to achieve low FPR in the scenario where non-training members dominate a compromised dataset.
- Method is adaptable to different evaluation frameworks (loss, confidence scores).
- Results show lower false negative errors for similar FPR compared to baselines.
- Writing style is clear and pleasant to read (noted by Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- Experimental setup and evaluation criteria are insufficiently explained, making it difficult to assess the method’s superiority. Metrics (TPR, Type I, Type II error) for set-based vs per-sample attacks are unclear; the paper does not explain how per-sample MIA decisions are converted to set-level decisions for fair comparison, especially in Fig 1(a).
- The consistent use of a 10% training-to-evaluation data ratio (noted in Appendix G.6) raises concerns about generalizability; performance under varying ratios and data distributions is not explored. Reviewers request experiments with different ratios.
- No ablation study in the main body justifying the choice of high-level representations (e.g., last layer); the practical assumption of access to internal representations is unrealistic in many threat models (e.g., black-box detection). Unclear how representations are obtained and what impact different choices have.
- Almost all experiments are in the appendix, with minimal description in the main body. Key details (datasets, architectures, training procedures, baseline adaptations) are missing, making the paper difficult to follow and assess.
- The comparison to existing set-based MIAs is missing. Prior works on dataset inference [2104.10706], user inference [2310.09266], and dataset inference for LLMs [2406.06443] achieve similar improvements and must be compared.
- The necessity and impact of kernel optimization (Theorem 1) are unclear. No baseline without optimization is provided; the theoretical analysis is not linked to empirical results, so the practical benefit is not demonstrated.
- Algorithmic difference and technical contribution relative to the prior deep kernel two-sample test (Liu et al., 2020b) are not well-addressed; Reviewer 3 feels the paper is “just an application” of that work.
- Experiments are limited to small-scale datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, MNIST); scalability to larger, more complex datasets (e.g., ImageNet, LLMs) is not demonstrated.
- Standard MIA evaluation uses AUROC, but this paper only reports Type I and Type II errors, obscuring the trade-off.
- Practical assumptions are questionable: setting 1 in Appendix B assumes access to a training set, which is typically private; this makes FPR evaluation difficult in practice.
- Robustness to out-of-distribution (OOD) datapoints is not addressed. The method requires a preliminary two-sample test to remove OOD data, but how this step affects HR-MMD’s integrity is not evaluated.
- Reviewer 2 notes that confidence-based attacks can be thwarted by defenses like MemGuard, though label-only attacks may circumvent this; the paper does not discuss defense scenarios.

Questions overlapping with criticisms (retained as weaknesses):
- How is the FPR computed for per-sample MIAs when converted to set-level decisions? (Reviewer 3)
- Can the method be used for auditing differential privacy? (Reviewer 3)
- Can it estimate the fraction of training data in a test set? (Reviewer 3)
- Is the kernel optimization under H1 also appropriate under H0? (Reviewer 1, Line 443)
- Why does maximizing J improve test power? (Reviewer 1, Line 429)
- Which high-level representations work best with HR-MMD? (Reviewer 4)
- What is the feasibility of using this method for LLMs? (Reviewer 2)
- How does the method perform when S_Y contains OOD samples, and how can the integrity of the preliminary two-sample test be evaluated? (Reviewer 4)
