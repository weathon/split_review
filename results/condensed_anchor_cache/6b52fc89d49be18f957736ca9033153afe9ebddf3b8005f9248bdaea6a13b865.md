## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a perceptually inspired adversarial attack for deep image compression that applies adaptive, nonlinear log-exp perturbations to the luminance channel, concentrating noise in dark regions where the human eye is less sensitive. It also proposes a defense that learns an optimal noise pattern to correct adversarial examples. Experiments target the Cheng2020 and TCM models on the Kodak dataset (24 images) under two scenarios: degrading reconstruction quality and increasing bitrate.

### Strengths
- The log-exp perturbation model effectively leverages human visual perception (Weber’s law) in a new way—applying more noise to dark, low-entropy regions—enhancing imperceptibility (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Exploring a new perturbation space for adversarial attacks on learned image compression is an interesting and emerging direction (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The paper proposes a simple defense solution that does not require additional training constraints on the model (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments demonstrate that both the attack and the defense are effective on the tested models (Reviewer 2).
- The idea of learning an optimal noise pattern for defense, and also exploiting high-entropy regions, is noted as a good starting point (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and insufficient comparisons:**  
  - The attack is evaluated only on deep image compression (Cheng2020, TCM) and not on other tasks (classification, segmentation). The method is not specific to compression, so results should be compared against the large body of adversarial imperceptible attacks in those domains (Reviewer 1).  
  - Missing comparison with closely related, published work: MALICE: Manipulation Attacks on Learned Image Compression (Liu et al., IEEE Trans. on AI, 2022) (Reviewers 1, 3). Additional missing citations: Lei et al. (2021) on out-of-distribution robustness, Lieberman et al. (2023) on spectral biases (Reviewer 3).  
  - No comparison with existing adversarial attacks to demonstrate the proposed attack’s superiority in imperceptibility or effectiveness; visual and numerical comparisons are needed (Reviewer 2).  
  - Only one dataset (Kodak, 24 images) and only two victim models are used—insufficient for generalization (Reviewers 3, 4).

- **Defense concerns:**  
  - The defense is not customized for the proposed attack and could be applied to other attacks, but it is only tested against its own attack, leaving its evaluation incomplete (Reviewer 2).  
  - The defense is overly simplistic; in a white‑box scenario an attacker could subtract the protective noise pattern, and even in a black‑box scenario repeated queries could optimize around it. The approach does not improve the model’s inherent adversarial robustness (Reviewer 4).  
  - If the attack is fully neutralized by the proposed defense, the purpose of the attack’s imperceptibility is unclear; a comprehensive evaluation including state‑of‑the‑art attacks and defenses is needed (Reviewer 2).

- **Poor writing, presentation, and organization:**  
  - The paper is disorganized; the connection between attack and defense is unclear, and the main focus is ambiguous (Reviewer 2).  
  - Tables 2 and 3 present results per individual image (24 samples) without aggregation, making it hard to draw generalized conclusions (Reviewers 2, 3).  
  - Figure captions are unclear, equations are not numbered, and notation is inconsistent (e.g., **n** used for both adversarial noise and the optimal noise pattern) (Reviewer 3).  
  - There is no Related Works section (Reviewer 3).

- **Methodological gaps:**  
  - The attack and defense objectives are defined, but the optimization methods and hyperparameter choices (e.g., δ, λ, κ) are not explained (Reviewer 3).  
  - The attack objective minimizes `PSNR(f(x*), x)` rather than `PSNR(f(x*), x*)`. This implies the model knows the original perturbation, which is unclear and not justified (Reviewer 3).  
  - The derivation of the constant 2.3 in line 148 is unsourced (Reviewer 3).  
  - Contradictory statements about human visual sensitivity: line 15 says “the human eye is less sensitive to variations in dark areas,” but line 140 says “the human visual system is more sensitive to brightness changes in dark regions.” Additional contradictions appear at lines 18, 107, 131, 175, and 178 (Reviewer 3).  
  - Clarification on Weber’s law is needed: the paper states the ratio δI/I decreases as luminance increases, but Weber’s law holds that this ratio is constant and the Just Noticeable Difference (JND) increases with luminance (Reviewer 3).  
  - Attack is only evaluated in a narrow scenario (deep compression); generalizing to other tasks and conducting a broader evaluation would allow comparison with state‑of‑the‑art attacks (Reviewer 1).

- **Novelty concern (contrasting view):** While the idea is interesting (Reviewers 1, 2, 3), Reviewer 4 finds the novelty limited and the scenario too restricted.
