## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes VBn-KAN, which augments Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks by replacing spline activation functions with Bernstein polynomials of variable degree. The approach is grounded in the Weierstrass approximation theorem and uniform convergence properties. The authors present experiments on function approximation, multivariate time series forecasting, and image classification (MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, Fashion-MNIST). All four reviewers find the paper clearly written and the theoretical motivation solid, but they raise major concerns about marginal or inconsistent performance gains, unfair or insufficient baselines (particularly missing direct B‑spline comparisons and state‑of‑the‑art results), unsupported claims about interpretability and generalization, and problematic experimental design (e.g., misleading Figure 1, non‑standard metrics, over‑optimization). Reviewer 1 (score 1) is strongly negative, while Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 (score 3 each) are moderately critical; all agree the novelty is incremental and the practical impact is not convincingly demonstrated.

### Strengths
- The use of Bernstein polynomials with variable degree is a novel architectural modification in the context of KANs.
- The paper provides a solid theoretical foundation, connecting the Weierstrass approximation theorem and uniform convergence to the choice of Bernstein polynomials.
- The approach is evaluated across diverse tasks (function approximation, time series, image classification) and compared to several KAN variants.
- The manuscript is well‑written and clearly motivates the architectural change (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Computational results generally favor VBn‑KAN over other KAN methods (Reviewer 2), though Reviewer 3 notes improvements are marginal and sometimes absent.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation and practical importance**: The paper does not convincingly establish why the variable Bernstein basis is crucial or how it addresses significant gaps in current KAN research (Reviewer 1). Several claims (e.g., reduced data influence, improved generalization from enhanced uniformity) are not backed by dedicated experiments or citations (Reviewer 3).
- **Marginal/inconsistent improvements**: Gains over other KAN variants are very small (Table 1), and VBn‑KAN is partly outperformed by vanilla KANs (Tables 2, 3) (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 1 describes the advances as incremental and not justifying a new architecture; Reviewer 2 and 4 also find it hard to be convinced from the numbers.
- **Unfair comparison in Figure 1**: The figure compares a Bernstein polynomial (low‑degree, single‑piece) to a generic cubic spline rather than B‑splines with adjustable degree. No grid resolution is specified. A fair comparison should use B‑splines of varying degree and same grid settings (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Missing direct B‑spline baseline**: The paper does not compare Bernstein polynomials directly to B‑splines, which are the standard basis in original KANs and also achieve uniform convergence (Reviewers 1, 4). The advantages of Bernstein polynomials (e.g., robustness to Runge phenomenon) are mentioned but not empirically demonstrated (Reviewer 4).
- **Image classification experiments are weak**: The datasets (MNIST, CIFAR‑10/100) are outdated toy benchmarks. No state‑of‑the‑art non‑KAN results are reported, making the claim “superior or competitive results” misleading (Reviewers 2, 3). The best KAN results are far below simple k‑NN on MNIST (99.04%) (Reviewer 2).
- **Non‑standard evaluation metric**: Table 3 uses ADE for function approximation instead of standard MSE/RMSE, making it hard to compare with other works. The authors should justify this choice and provide results in MSE/RMSE (Reviewer 1).
- **Interpretability claim unsupported**: A key selling point of KANs is interpretability, but the paper does not discuss or demonstrate how the variable basis enhances interpretability (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Bayesian optimization overkill for single parameter n**: Equation 3.5 optimizes only the degree n (one‑dimensional). Much faster algorithms (e.g., bisection line search) exist with exponential convergence (Reviewer 2).
- **Training details omitted**: Beyond Section 3.3, the paper does not explain the optimization algorithm, learning rates, number of data points, or other hyperparameters (Reviewer 2).
- **Terminology confusion**: The use of “variety” is inappropriate; it suggests algebraic geometry rather than variability. The paper inconsistently switches between “variety”, “variable”, and “variability” (Reviewer 2).
- **Theoretical claims not validated**: Section 3.2 makes claims about enhanced uniformity and generalization that are not experimentally verified (Reviewer 3). Theorem 3.3 gives a lower bound for n, but its practical use is unclear – the authors fall back to sophisticated hyperparameter tuning (Reviewer 3).
- **Tables 4/5 unclear**: It is not specified whether n is optimized individually per weight via BO or fixed; for fixed n, n=1 often outperforms n=10, suggesting overfitting for higher degrees. Summary statistics would help (Reviewer 3).
- **Undefined symbols**: In Theorem 3.3, ξ is not defined (Reviewer 3).
- **Minor errors**: Typos “interpretabilit” (l.114), “attempte” (l.39), “exits” (l.141) (Reviewers 2, 3).
