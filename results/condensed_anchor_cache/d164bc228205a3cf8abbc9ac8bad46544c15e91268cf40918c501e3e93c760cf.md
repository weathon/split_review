## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using influence functions to quantify the impact of individual human feedback samples on reward model performance in RLHF. To enable application to LLM-scale reward models and large preference datasets, the authors introduce a compute-efficient approximation combining DataInf (for Hessian approximation) and OPORP (for gradient vector compression, reducing storage from 160 MB to 256 KB). Two applications are demonstrated: (1) detecting labeler biases (length bias, sycophancy) in preference datasets, and (2) guiding non-expert labelers to align their labeling strategy with expert feedback by adjusting linear weights of sub-scores. Experiments use models like Llama-3-8B and datasets like HelpSteer2, showing competitive AUC against GPT-4o and Gemini baselines. Reviewer scores range from 5 to 8, with disagreement on the degree of novelty and practical feasibility.

### Strengths
- **Novel application of influence functions to reward model interpretability**: Several reviewers (1,3,6) highlight this as a promising direction for understanding human feedback impact and enhancing RLHF transparency. (However, Reviewers 3 and 5 note that the technical novelty is limited, as it applies existing methods from [1] directly.)
- **Compute-efficient and practical methodology**: The use of DataInf for Hessian approximation and OPORP vector compression reduces computational cost (2.5× speedup) and storage (from 160 MB to 256 KB per gradient vector), making the approach feasible for large-scale use (Reviewers 1,4,6).
- **Clear, well-structured presentation**: The paper is easy to follow, with well-designed figures, examples, and sufficient experimental detail for reproducibility (Reviewers 1,2,5).
- **Convincing experimental results**: Bias detection (length, sycophancy) shows competitive AUC compared to GPT-4o and Gemini baselines. The method effectively identifies problematic data points (Reviewers 2,5). Ablation studies on validation set composition are provided (Reviewer 5).
- **Relevant applications**: Detecting labeler bias and guiding non-expert labelers to improve their alignment with expert labeling are timely and important for RLHF (Reviewers 2,4,5). The extension to labeler guidance shows measurable improvement with few samples (Reviewer 2).
- **Thoughtful limitation discussion**: Limitations (e.g., dependence on validation set quality, proof-of-concept for labeler guidance) are explicitly acknowledged (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Heavy dependence on manually curated validation sets**: The method requires high-quality, expert-annotated validation sets for each target bias. This reliance on domain knowledge and human effort limits generalizability and real-world applicability (Reviewers 1,2,5,6). Whether this dependence can be reduced is unclear (Reviewer 1).
- **Limited methodological novelty**: The work primarily applies existing influence function techniques (DataInf, OPORP) to reward modeling without introducing new technical contributions. Reviewers 3,5,6 note this as a weakness, though Reviewer 4 considers it acceptable if the integration works well.
- **Sycophancy bias detection remains challenging**: Unlike length bias, sycophancy is subjective and context-dependent, making robust detection harder. The method’s effectiveness for sycophancy is less convincing (Reviewer 1).
- **Unclear impact on other metrics**: Improving detection of one bias (e.g., length) might degrade performance on other dimensions or cause overfitting. Reviewer 2 asks for additional statistics comparing the base model and the Influence-based approach to verify no unintended harm. Reviewer 6 raises the risk of reducing response diversity when guiding labelers.
- **User guidance only proof-of-concept**: The labeler guidance experiment assumes labelers use a linear combination of sub-scores, which may not reflect real-world human judgment. Feasibility in actual human-labeler settings is unverified; Reviewer 2 suggests human-subject experiments, and Reviewer 6 notes that finding expert labelers for every aspect is impractical. Reviewer 6 also criticizes the use of SVM for weight adjustment (additional cost, potential inaccuracy) and suggests a more direct method.
- **Computational complexity concerns**: Although approximations reduce cost, the inverse Hessian calculation (via DataInf) still requires summing over training set samples, potentially reducing precision (Reviewer 6). Reviewer 2 asks whether the Hessian approximation leads to performance degradation.
- **Bias detection threshold is arbitrary**: Setting a threshold to flag biased samples is not principled; not all samples above the threshold are necessarily biased (Reviewer 6).
- **Experimental issues and missing evaluations**:
  - Figure 4 legend has an apparent mistake (both curves labeled AUC=0.8) (Reviewer 4).
  - Only four dimensions of HelpSteer2 are used in Section 5.2.1 without clear rationale (Reviewer 4). The weight selection in Appendix B2 is also not justified (Reviewer 2).
  - The method is tested only on length and sycophancy biases; other bias types and intentional adversarial feedback are not explored (Reviewers 3,6).
  - No results on downstream alignment tasks (e.g., direct alignment algorithms) are provided to verify generalizability (Reviewer 3).
  - No improvement on real-world data quality after filtering biased samples is demonstrated (Reviewer 4). No comparison to concurrent works exists (Reviewer 4).
- **Scalability and evaluation set design are unclear**: The impact of increasing dataset/model size on computational trade-offs is not analyzed (Reviewers 1,3). How to determine the evaluation set size and how performance scales with more samples is missing (Reviewer 6). The choice of reporting ROC curves over other metrics is questioned (Reviewer 6).
- **Assumptions may not hold**: The method assumes labelers can provide granular sub-objective scores and that rewards are linearly combinable; these assumptions may break in diverse, real-world labeling contexts (Reviewers 1,6). Dependencies between rewards are not addressed (Reviewer 6).
