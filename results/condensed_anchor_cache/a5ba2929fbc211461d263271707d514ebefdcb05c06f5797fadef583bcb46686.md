## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces ATLAS (Attention-based Targeted Layer Analysis and Scaling), a two-step method to localize and mitigate bias in large language models (LLMs) when responding to ambiguous comparative prompts. ATLAS first identifies biased layers by analyzing attention scores on entity tokens, then reduces bias by scaling down attention in those layers. Experiments on GPT-2 XL (1.5B), GPT-J (6B), LLaMA-2 (7B), and LLaMA-3 (8B) across BBQ, Crows-Pairs, and WinoGender show that bias is concentrated in later layers and that ATLAS reduces bias (average 0.28 point improvement in bias score) while maintaining fluency (0.82% average perplexity increase). However, several reviewers find the binary scenario simplistic, the reliance on first-token attention problematic, the need for prompt-specific scaling factors limiting, and the evaluation against only two baselines insufficient.

### Strengths
- Tackles the important challenge of mitigating bias and stereotypes in LLMs.
- The proposed approach is simple, lightweight, intuitive, and model-agnostic, yet effective in the evaluated setting.
- Analyzing the internal mechanisms of how bias emerges in LLMs (via attention analysis) is novel and provides insights into where bias occurs within the model.
- Experiments demonstrate effectiveness according to the introduced bias ratio metric and maintain fluency as measured by perplexity.
- The focus on model-internal mechanisms rather than data-centric methods offers a complementary perspective.

### Weaknesses
- **Simplistic binary setting limits real-world applicability.** The method only handles comparative prompts between two explicitly mentioned entities. Real-world scenarios may involve multiple entities (e.g., social groups, genders, ethnicities), implicit bias, or absent candidates. The method’s generalizability to such settings is unclear. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Attention analysis relies on first token of entity.** The method computes attention only on the first token of each entity. This is problematic when the first token is shared (e.g., “grand” in “grandson” vs. “grandfather”), leading to inaccurate bias localization. The method should account for the full entity representation or at least multiple tokens. (R2, R4)
- **Scaling attention disrupts attention distribution.** Summing attention weights after scaling makes them less than 1, which could disrupt the model’s internal calibration, alter confidence, or introduce new biases. No analysis is provided on how scaling affects the overall attention distribution and model behavior. (R2)
- **Method requires prompt-specific scaling factor λ.** Determining λ for each prompt limits practical usability. The paper does not provide an automated or generalizable way to set λ, nor guidelines for selection. (R2)
- **Insufficient baseline comparisons.** Only two baselines are considered, while many state-of-the-art bias mitigation techniques exist. This makes it difficult to assess the true effectiveness and novelty of ATLAS. (R2, R4)
- **No justification for choosing Approach 2 over Approach 1.** The paper proposes two approaches for bias localization (difference-based and most-probable-candidate) but selects Approach 2 without a comparative analysis of bias reduction, computational overhead, or impact on performance. (R3)
- **Evaluation metric may favor the method.** The newly introduced “bias ratio” metric is not compared to established metrics (e.g., BBQ dataset metrics). It may not capture the full spectrum of bias and may be sensitive to specific prompt formats. (R4)
- **Perplexity is not ideal for single-word outputs.** The setting often requires only a word as an answer, not full sentences. Perplexity may not reflect semantic appropriateness or relevance of short outputs. (R4)
- **Figure 1 does not directly link attention to bias.** The figure shows which layers have higher attention scores for entities, but does not demonstrate how that translates to a preference for one entity over another. A clear correlation between attention weights and the model’s bias is needed. (R2)
- **Averaging attention across tokens may obscure nuanced bias.** Averaging attention weights within a layer could neutralize opposite token-level biases (e.g., some tokens show positive bias, others negative), leading to inaccurate assessment of a layer’s contribution to bias. (R4)
- **Larger models not tested.** Experiments should include larger models such as Llama-70B to assess scalability. (R2)
- **No analysis of order effects.** Since comparative prompts involve two entities, the order matters. Results should be reported for both orders, and ideally averaged. (R2)
- **Questions overlapping with weaknesses:**
  - How can the method generalize when entity tokens are unknown or absent? (R1)
  - When the model’s output requires nuanced dependency on candidates, would suppressing attention harm performance? (R1)
  - For Approach 1 (line 269), should absolute values be used? (R2)
  - For Table 3, it would be better to report the ratio of each preferred entity to understand how much bias is resolved, not just the change ratio of the higher-prob entity. (R2)
  - Could the method be extended to biases involving multiple entities? How? (R3)
  - Clarify if “top/bottom-k layers” refers to specific layers or a range. (R4)
- **Presentation issues:** Typo in line 485: “Table 4” should be “Table 3”. (R3) The introduction of the attention mechanism in Section 2 contains errors that should be corrected. (R2)
