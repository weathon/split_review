## Merged Review

### Summary

MVMP employs multiple prompts at different training stages, a mixed self-augmentation framework, and text distillation to improve few-shot CLIP classification. It uses multiple prompts (e.g., 5) and averages predictions. On 1-shot settings the method shows good and consistent performance, with reported accuracy improvements of 4.6% and 2% over the state-of-the-art. Reviewers are mixed: three rated 3 (fair to good) and one rated 1 (reject), with the minority strongly questioning novelty, fair comparison, and writing quality.

### Strengths

- The paper addresses an important problem in few-shot learning: the limited availability of labeled data.  
- The method is easy to understand and is especially effective on fewer-shot CLIP adaptation tasks (1-shot setting).  
- Consistent improvements across different tests, supported by comprehensive experimentation.  
- The enhancements are multi-dimensional: adjusting text prompts at distinct training stages to capture broad and nuanced details, and diversifying the dataset to ensure a balanced training environment.  
- Good empirical results; the method demonstrates superior performance compared to its direct baselines.  
- An intuitive perspective for improving inference capabilities of CLIP-like vision-language models.  

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear method details**:  
  - What are \(P_{fixed}\) and \(P_t\), and where are they obtained? Are \(P_t\) and \(P_w^{(e)}\) the same?  
  - What is the evaluation parameter \(\epsilon_e\) and its formula?  
  - In equation (8), what is \(H(x)\)? If it refers to image features, the notation is confusing.  
  - How are prompt tokens processed in the transformers? Are prompts introduced at layer \(i\) pruned after that layer or retained until the last layer? This makes a significant difference.  

- **Lack of motivation for key components**:  
  - No explanation for why the consistency loss between original and mixed images benefits few-shot learning. The ablation in Figure 6 does not justify the necessity of the image augmentation (mixup regions of cross samples). Reviewer 2: the augmentation is not well motivated; the Gaussian weighting for high-layer prompts is a fixed heuristic with no technical novelty.  
  - Similarly, the text distillation loss lacks an explanation.  
  - The mixed self-augmentation appears as an additional trick not closely related to the prompting framework; even without it the method can achieve comparable performance, and baseline models can obtain the same level of improvements.  

- **Missing comparisons and unfair benchmarking**:  
  - Some state-of-the-art few-shot approaches are missing, e.g., “Exploiting Category Names for Few-Shot Classification with Vision-Language Models” (reported results are much better).  
  - The method uses both data augmentation and prompt learning, but baselines only have one of them. Ablations should compare the method without augmentation/prompting or baselines with both.  
  - In Figure 6, the score 73.8 is achieved by combining multi-prompt, multi-vision, and text diversity, but Table 2 compares this to CLIP combined with other mix-up methods alone. It would be more appropriate to compare against CLIP + multi-vision alone (which gives only 0.5% improvement). Given mix-up’s well-known effectiveness, it is not clear if multi-vision is indispensable.  
  - The multi-prompt component should be contrasted with a baseline where diverse prompts are introduced simultaneously across stages, not sequentially; the staged/curriculum learning is not justified.  
  - Cross-dataset benchmark results are underwhelming: average accuracy higher than MaPLe by only 0.2%.  

- **Duplicate of existing techniques**:  
  - Feeding multiple prompts into the text encoder has long been standard (original CLIP uses 80 different text prompts for ImageNet zero-shot inference, called prompt engineering). The paper lacks discussion of this.  
  - Multi-layer prompting for text encoders is confusing; the performance gain may simply come from additional parameters, and no explanation is given.  

- **Underestimated baselines**:  
  - CLIP’s test accuracy on ImageNet is reported as only 32.1%, but the original CLIP paper obtains 63.2% (ViT-B/32) and 68.6% (ViT-B/16) zero-shot accuracy.  

- **Claims about computational cost**:  
  - The paper claims no additional parameters or escalated computational costs, but employing more prompts likely increases cost. No comparison of computational costs with other methods is provided.  

- **Selection of the number of prompts**:  
  - Figure 5 concludes that using 5 prompts works best. How are these 5 prompts selected from the many possible hand‑designed prompts?  

- **Poor writing and presentation**:  
  - Many grammatical errors and imprecise expressions (e.g., “improving accuracy by 4.6% and 2%” is unclear; “our method improves overall accuracy by 2% to 4.6%” would be clearer).  
  - All arguments referencing Figure 2 are weak because they are not direct reflections of the proposed method.  
  - Figure 4 is confusing: readers cannot tell frozen from learnable parameters.  
  - In Figure 6, the table is a screenshot, not properly typeset in LaTeX.  

- **Minority reviewer strongly negative**: Reviewer 3 (score 1) judges the contribution very limited, saying the two basic components have been explicitly explored in the community and the paper yields no significant improvements with its simple design. Reviewer 2 (score 3) notes that the cross-dataset results are only 0.2% above MaPLe.
