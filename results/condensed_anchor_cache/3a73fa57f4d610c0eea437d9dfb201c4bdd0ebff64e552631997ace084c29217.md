## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Proximal Initialization Attack (PIA), a query-based membership inference attack for diffusion models. PIA uses the groundtruth trajectory obtained by initializing \(\epsilon\) at \(t=0\) and the predicted point to compute a distance metric, determining membership with only two queries. It achieves 5–10× speedup over the previous state-of-the-art (SecMI) while achieving superior AUC and TPR@1%FPR. The method is validated on multiple image (CIFAR-10, CelebA, LSUN) and audio (LJSpeech, VCTK, LibriTTS) datasets, including experiments on Stable Diffusion with unknown ground-truth text. The paper is the first to study MIA robustness in text-to-speech (TTS) tasks, finding that models outputting mel-spectrograms are vulnerable while models directly outputting audio are relatively robust.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to read/understand.
- The proposed idea (PIA) is simple and novel.
- Extensive experiments across multiple domains: three image datasets and three audio datasets, multiple model architectures (DDPM, DDIM, Score SDE, Stable Diffusion), and thorough ablation studies investigating the effect of various parameters.
- The method achieves state-of-the-art performance: 5–10× speedup over SecMI while maintaining or improving AUC and TPR@1%FPR, using only 2 queries, making it practical for large-scale models.
- The threat model is clearly analyzed; the attack is applicable in settings where the model exposes intermediate results (e.g., inpainting, classification).
- A valuable practical insight is provided: diffusion models that directly output audio are more robust to MIA than those outputting mel-spectrograms, suggesting a design choice for privacy.

### Weaknesses
- The motivation behind certain transformations in the method is insufficiently explained: (i) Eq. (14) – why the high-order infinitesimal term and \(dt\) can be ignored; (ii) Eq. (9) – the connection between the proposed loss and the original diffusion loss is not analyzed; (iii) the procedure for removing the influence of the random seed in the noise term is not clearly described.
- Several experimental details are missing or unclear: hyperparameter selection for all methods (including comparative baselines NA, SecMI) is not fully reported; in the Stable Diffusion experiment, the specific BLIP checkpoint and the prompt used are not given; the pipeline for TTS tasks is not explained; the overall procedure would benefit from being summarized in a single table.
- Figure readability is poor in places: Fig. 2 is too small to read, and some figures contain too many datasets and models in a single figure, reducing clarity.
- There are minor grammar errors and typos, e.g., “MIA for generation tasks … has also been extensively researched” in the related work section.
- The audio attack evaluation only uses methods originally designed for image attacks (NA, SecMI); dedicated audio attack methods are not considered.
- It is unclear how the number of queries for comparative methods was determined; it is possible those methods could achieve better performance with fewer queries.
- The paper lacks deeper analysis of why audio-output models are more robust: what are the potential costs or trade-offs of using audio output instead of mel-spectrograms? Are there other output types for TTS tasks (e.g., waveform, discrete tokens) that could also be robust?
- The application of the proposed method to discrete-time diffusion models is not clearly explained.
- It is not fully explained why the proposed method consistently outperforms the best competitors across Tables 1–3.
