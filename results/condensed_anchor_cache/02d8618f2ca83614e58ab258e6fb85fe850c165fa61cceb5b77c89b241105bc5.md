## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Modality Integration Rate (MIR) to evaluate LVLM pretraining quality by measuring the distribution distance (mean and covariance) between visual and textual features. It also introduces a lightweight MoCa module to improve alignment. Reviewers acknowledge the underexplored problem but have mixed opinions on contribution and rigor. One reviewer (score 8, confidence 3) finds MIR effective, well-validated, and the paper well written. A second reviewer (score 3, confidence 4) considers the adaptation of FID incremental, the motivation weak, and the experiments insufficient. The other two reviewers (scores 5) note strengths in applicability and practical guidance but raise concerns about theoretical depth, experimental scope, and methodological choices.

### Strengths
- MIR fills a gap in evaluating LVLM pretraining quality without requiring supervised fine-tuning, offering an effective indicator of cross-modal alignment.
- Extensive experiments demonstrate MIR’s robustness across different data types, resistance to overfitting, and positive correlation with downstream SFT benchmark performance.
- MIR provides practical guidance for optimizing data scale, data detail level, training strategies, and architectural design.
- The proposed MoCa module is lightweight and further improves cross-modal alignment, reducing MIR and enhancing multi-modal task performance.
- MIR shows advantage over traditional metrics (loss, perplexity, in-context evaluation) in multi-modal scenarios.
- The paper is well written, clear, and easy to read (R1).
- MIR is generalizable across different data and model configurations, including various types of images and texts (R3).
- The study includes both theoretical discussion and concrete application examples for practical model training and optimization (R4).

### Weaknesses
- Originality is limited: MIR merely adapts the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) to measure modality distribution distance, with minimal theoretical innovation. (Emphasized by R1 and R2 as a major weakness; R3 also notes limited originality.)
- FID has recently faced criticism as an evaluation metric (cite Jayasumana et al. 2024). The choice of FID over alternatives (KL-divergence, mutual information, maximum mean discrepancy) is not justified. (R1, R2, R4.)
- Experiments are conducted only on LLaVa-v1.5 with a small set of datasets (ALLaVA, ShareGPT4V-PT). Generalizability to other LVLMs and more diverse datasets is unproven; the used datasets may share similar characteristics (R1, R2; R4 notes this limitation.)
- Motivation is questioned: given existing evaluation metrics (loss, PPL, etc.), the need for an intermediate pretraining metric is not strongly argued (R1, R2).
- Visualization in Figure 2 using t-SNE lacks persuasiveness because t-SNE is highly sensitive to parameter tuning and random data choice, reducing rigor (R3).
- The MIR formula uses a fixed 1:1 weight ratio between mean distance and covariance without exploring whether alternative ratios yield better assessment. (R3; R4 also notes lack of sensitivity analysis for parameters.)
- Theoretical basis is insufficient: no deep mathematical analysis of the metric’s properties, underlying assumptions, or conditions for applicability (R4).
- No direct quantitative comparison with other existing modal fusion evaluation metrics to demonstrate MIR’s advantages (R4).
- Missing detail: how the ω function based on the “3σ” principle is implemented and whether outlier tokens were characterized (R1 question).
- Missing experiment: how per-layer MIR changes when using MoCa compared to the baseline (R1 question).
- Computational complexity and resource consumption of MIR in actual large-scale model training are not reported (R4 question).
- Parameter sensitivity analysis is absent: e.g., how variations in the number of model layers, modality pairing data scale, or regularization strategies affect MIR values (R4 question).
