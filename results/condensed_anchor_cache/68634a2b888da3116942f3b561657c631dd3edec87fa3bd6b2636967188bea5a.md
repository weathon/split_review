## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies a fundamental under-specification problem in margin-based preference optimization (e.g., DPO) for language model alignment: the loss only enforces a margin between preferred and dispreferred responses, without constraining individual log probabilities. This leads to two unintended consequences: (1) the probability of dispreferred (e.g., unsafe) responses can increase, and (2) reducing dispreferred probabilities often simultaneously reduces preferred probabilities. The authors derive a gradient inner-product condition that predicts when these synchronized shifts occur, theoretically analyze when the inner product is large, empirically validate on TL;DR and a simple sentiment task, and discuss implications for other margin-based algorithms.

### Strengths
- The theoretical analysis is enjoyable to read and the examples clearly demonstrate the proposed criterion (Reviewer 1). The gradient inner-product conditions are mathematically grounded and provide a high-quality theoretical contribution (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The paper addresses a fundamental and under-explored limitation of margin-based preference optimization in RLHF, which is important given the wide use of these methods (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- It provides a comprehensive review of existing methods (Table 2) and extends the analysis to other margin-based algorithms in Section 3.2 (Reviewer 2).
- The suggested condition is substantiated with experimental validation (Reviewer 2), and the proposal to address the problem at the token level is interesting (Reviewer 4).
- One reviewer (Reviewer 1, score 8) rated the work highly, praising the clarity of the theoretical analysis, while three others (scores 6, 5, 5) acknowledged the importance but were more critical of empirical coverage and presentation.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental validation:** Experiments are conducted on only one main dataset (TL;DR) and a simple sentiment classification task, using only Mistral 7B and Llama-3 8B. This lacks diversity across tasks (e.g., summarization, dialogue, instruction-following) and model scales, reducing confidence in generalizability (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). The empirical results in Section 3.3 mainly observe phenomena already reported in Pal et al. (2024) and do not fully investigate the conditions under which probability shifts occur (Reviewer 2).
- **Simplified theoretical assumptions and synthetic focus:** The theoretical analysis in Section 4.1 relies on a simplified setting (learnable last linear layer, learnable logits). Assumptions about token probability relationships between chosen/rejected responses may not hold in practice, and the extension from single-token to multi-token cases is not rigorously justified (Reviewer 3). The analysis in Section 4 centers on synthetic, controlled scenarios that do not capture the complexities of real-world language data (Reviewer 4). The provided examples are also highly simplified, which reduces confidence that the findings will impact real LLM practice (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of practical relevance for diverse response pairs:** The paper demonstrates the high-correlation problem for response pairs that closely match, but does not clearly show that the problem persists for very different response pairs (e.g., toxic vs. non-toxic). Practical relevance in scenarios with significant dissimilarity is not sufficiently addressed (Reviewer 1). One reviewer questions whether the problematic behavior (both probabilities going up or down) is actually important in real-world settings, noting that previous authors focused only on the preferred log-probability decreasing (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation issues:** The notation becomes dense and confusing in Section 3.2 (Reviewer 3). Some figures (e.g., Figure 4b) need more detailed explanations of their implications (Reviewer 3). The connection between theoretical results and practical implications could be more clearly articulated (Reviewer 3). Methodological details are missing: it is unclear how log probabilities are computed in Figure 1 (e.g., averaged over training samples), and standard deviations or confidence intervals should be provided (Reviewer 2). Figure 2’s grid interval should be adjusted to better show the margin trend (Reviewer 2). Having a dedicated figure for Section 3.3 empirical results would allow a clearer presentation without cluttering the introductory motivation (Reviewer 2).
- **Overemphasis on DPO:** The paper centers primarily on DPO in Sections 3.1 and 4, with only limited coverage of other margin-based algorithms. The authors should either explicitly announce their DPO focus in the introduction or devote more space to a balanced treatment of other methods (Reviewer 2).
- **Related questions that were not addressed:** 
  - How do the idealized pairwise comparisons relate to distributional frameworks that consider the full support of the preference distribution (e.g., Go et al. 2023, Korbak et al. 2022)? This would help assess implications for alignment training (Reviewer 2).
  - Does the work incorporate hard negative samples (where the dispreferred response is high-quality but slightly worse), or is it constrained to cases with explicit quality differences? (Reviewer 2).
  - The token-level gradient findings are intriguing—could the authors elaborate on potential methods to leverage token-level distinctions more effectively, e.g., via fine-grained adjustments? (Reviewer 2).
  - What actionable insights for real-world RLHF applications can be derived from the analysis? (Reviewer 4).
  - How would one evaluate the "safety-critical alignment tasks" mentioned in Section 5 that are said to be particularly ill-suited for margin-based preference optimization? (Reviewer 4).
  - Can you clarify more directly how token-level gradient inner products between chosen and rejected responses provide a reliable indicator for alignment behavior? (Reviewer 4).
