## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers agree that the paper targets an important and understudied area—evaluating LLM Theory of Mind (ToM) and social reasoning from a first-person perspective—and that the proposed EgoSocialArena framework combines static and interactive scenarios with controlled opponents. However, all reviewers express major concerns about unclear problem definitions, insufficient task and scoring descriptions, and a lack of rigorous theoretical grounding. The term “socialization” is repeatedly questioned for conflating introspection with genuine social capabilities. While some elements (e.g., new world and counterfactual tasks, use of rule-based/RL agents) are appreciated, the overall contribution is weakened by vague contribution claims, insufficient analysis of results, missing details on human baselines, and incomplete engagement with relevant cognitive science and multi-agent LLM literature. The paper needs substantially more clarity, methodological rigor, and interpretative depth to justify the benchmark’s utility.

### Strengths
- **First-person perspective to ToM benchmarking** – The paper shifts from the typical third-person observer stance to a first-person (egocentric) framing, which is novel for LLM evaluation and relevant for embodied AI (Reviewers 2, 4, 5).
- **Comprehensive evaluation across models** – Evaluates nine advanced LLMs including GPT-4, Claude-3.5, LLaMA, and o1-preview, with human performance from 10 graduate students (Reviewers 4, 5).
- **Static and interactive environments** – Including seven scenarios (Daily Life, Counterfactual, New World, Blackjack, Number Guessing, Limit Texas Hold’em) with a total of 2,195 entries, providing broad coverage of social interaction types (Reviewers 1, 5).
- **Controlled opponents in interactive scenarios** – Use of rule-based and reinforcement learning agents ensures consistent, reproducible baselines for evaluating ToM and adaptation (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
- **Interesting scenario design** – The new world and counterfactual tasks are highlighted as potentially valuable contributions that test reasoning under novel social norms (Reviewer 1) and the idea of measuring “socialization” (LLMs’ evolving mental states) introduces a creative dimension (Reviewer 3, 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear or contested definition of “socialization”** – Multiple reviewers find the term problematic. Reviewer 1 asks: given LLMs do not experience emotion, are they simply reporting what a person ought to feel (i.e., ToM in first-person clothing)? Reviewer 2 notes examples (e.g., counterfactual rock-paper-scissors) are not inherently social; they require commonsense or rule-matching, not socialization. Reviewer 4 argues socialization in humans encompasses collaboration, communication, deception, and norms, not merely first-order ToM from a first-person perspective. Reviewer 5 warns the term traditionally means learning normative behavior; alternatives like “adaptive inference” or “situational adaptation” are suggested.
- **Insufficient task and scoring descriptions** – Reviewers 1, 3, and 4 all note that the main text lacks clarity on what LLMs are expected to do in each evaluation, how outputs are scored, and why certain tasks (e.g., Blackjack) are considered ToM tests. While the appendix gives some rules, it does not explain the ToM relevance. Without this, interpreting why models pass some tasks and fail others is impossible (Reviewer 1).
- **Vague and uninformative contribution claims** – Both Reviewers 1 and 3 object to statements like “The powerful capabilities of the o1-preview model are truly surprising,” calling them vague. Specific, quantified reasoning for model differences is missing.
- **Inadequate human baseline** – Reviewer 1 notes that only 10 graduate students participated; this is a low sample size for robust comparison, and information about gender, neurodiversity, or other ToM-relevant factors is absent. Reviewer 4 also requests more details on individual differences.
- **Missing engagement with cognitive science and relevant multi-agent LLM literature** – Reviewer 1 points out the related work lists only previous benchmarks without citing the cognitive literature that justifies the chosen assessments. Reviewer 5 specifically notes the omission of “Hypothetical Minds: Scaffolding Theory of Mind for Multi-Agent Tasks with Large Language Models,” which also studies first-person social reasoning in embodied settings. Work on normative decision making in LLMs (cooperation, norms) is also ignored.
- **Single run per model and no robustness analysis** – Reviewer 1 observes that each model version was run only once; even with temperature=0, minor variations exist, making it impossible to assess robustness of performance differences.
- **Epistemological tension in applying ToM to rule-based/RL agents** – Reviewers 2 and 5 argue that agents without mental states cannot be the object of genuine ToM reasoning. Making an LLM infer the “mental states” of a rule-based or RL agent risks being a performance of ToM without cognitive grounding, echoing the Chinese Room argument or Dennett’s intentional stance (Reviewer 5). Reviewer 2 adds that the rule-based agent in number guessing does not represent different reasoning levels, making the ToM task trivial.
- **No embodiment despite claims** – Reviewer 2 notes the paper claims relevance to embodied AI, but none of the evaluations involve embodied agents; all are text-based.
- **Lack of analysis of performance inconsistencies** – Reviewer 1 notes that models pass some tasks with high scores and catastrophically fail others, but there is no review or interpretation of these inconsistencies beyond listing who is better at what task. Reviewer 3 also asks for intuition behind why certain results hold.
- **Ambiguous relationship between the three benchmark sections** – Reviewer 4 points out that the first-person ToM questions, novel social situations, and interactive environments differ greatly in complexity, information asymmetry, and measurement criteria. While diversity is a strength, the lack of a unifying problem definition makes it hard to use effectively.
- **Minor issues** – Figure 1 is uninformative and its legend insufficient (Reviewer 1). Table 3 color coding appears inverted (Reviewer 1). The use of italics for “socialization” is unexplained (Reviewer 1). The related work section is too long and should be condensed to free space for task details (Reviewer 3).
