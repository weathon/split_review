## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces SALMON, a self-alignment method that trains an instructable reward model on synthetic preference data generated by an instruction-tuned SFT model conditioned on a set of human-defined principles. This reward model is then used in an RLHF loop to fine-tune the base model (LLaMA-2-70b), producing Dromedary-2. The approach uses only 6 in-context exemplars and 31 principles, achieving a 7.4 MT-Bench score, outperforming LLaMA-2-Chat-70b (6.9) which relied on ~27K demonstrations and ~1.4M preference annotations. Three reviewers scored 6, one reviewer scored 8; the higher-scoring reviewer was more favorable about the method’s generality and ease of adaptation.

### Strengths
- The method only requires modifying synthetic data generation, leaving the RLHF pipeline general and easy to adapt to other tasks (Reviewer 1).
- The approach is helpful when domain-specific preference data (e.g., code, agents) is scarce (Reviewer 1).
- Finetuning with QLORA on a 70B model demonstrates clear performance improvements (Reviewer 1).
- The paper is well-written and well-organized (Reviewers 2, 3).
- SALMON significantly reduces human annotation costs compared to standard RLHF (Reviewer 3).
- SALMON can control reward scores by changing the principle, offering more control during alignment (Reviewer 3).
- The problem of keeping reward models in-distribution with the current RL policy is relevant and timely (Reviewer 2).
- The performance of the resulting model (Dromedary-2-70b) is impressive and the recipe for AI feedback is interesting (Reviewer 2).
- The approach is easy to understand and seems novel for aligning RL policies using LLMs (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty relative to Constitutional AI**: The paper distinguishes CA as safety-focused, but CA can also be made helpful; the relationship and advancement need clearer articulation (Reviewer 2; also Reviewer 3, Q1).
- **Missing quantitative evaluation of principle-following reward model**: The paper should measure how well the reward model adheres to specified principles (e.g., variance in scores for different principles, ability to distinguish responses aligning with different principles) (Reviewer 3, W1; Reviewer 2, question on sensitivity).
- **Insufficient ablations and experiments**: 
  - No evaluation of the SFT model’s ability to label preferences conditioned on a principle (e.g., does using a negative principle flip the preference?) (Reviewer 2).
  - The paper lacks ablations without the “hacks” (e.g., length bonus, pre-training on human preference data) and only with “hacks” to isolate the SALMON contribution (Reviewer 2).
  - How does performance change when training with just the 6 exemplars, just the 31 principles, different fractions, or excluding certain principle types? (Reviewer 4, Q1).
  - The effect of symbolic bonuses, especially the length bonus, should be ablated (Reviewer 2, question; Reviewer 2, weakness on length bonus inclusion).
  - No comparison to smaller models (e.g., 7B, 33B) to show generalizability (Reviewer 1).
  - No perplexity comparison to see if SALMON better maintains the reference LLM’s token distribution than PPO-based methods (Reviewer 3, W4).
- **Pre-training on human preference data weakens core claim**: The reward model is pre-trained on Anthropic HH-RLHF and SHP (~320k preference pairs) before the SALMON synthetic data (~9.8k prompts; unclear how many preference pairs result). The method’s minimal human supervision claim would be stronger without this human-preference pre-training (Reviewer 2).
- **Reward hacking claim not fully justified**: SALMON rewards could still be hacked to produce degenerative responses if trained long enough with RL (Reviewer 2).
- **Why train separate reward model?**: The SFT model acting as a judge could already serve as a reward model for multiple principles (maybe batched). A justification is needed (Reviewer 2).
- **Concerns about scope and completeness of principles**:
  - The approach assumes that the specified principles and exemplars completely specify aligned behavior, which may not be practical (exhaustive list difficult) (Reviewer 4).
  - It is unclear how to handle conflicting principles or whether a hierarchy of principles can be imposed during training (Reviewer 4, Q2, Q3).
  - There is no guarantee that the set of principles is general enough to lead to an aligned policy in all scenarios (Reviewer 4, Q4).
- **Gap to top-tier models**: Despite outperforming LLaMA-2-Chat, Dromedary-2 still has a large gap to GPT-4 (9.0) and ChatGPT (7.9) on MT-Bench. This gap should be acknowledged (Reviewer 3, W2).
- **Lack of comparison with improved RLHF methods**: Methods like DPO and P3O have shown better reward scores than PPO; the assessment of SALMON’s relative effectiveness is limited without such comparisons (Reviewer 3, W3).
- **Missing discussion of recent works**: The paper should discuss DPO, ReST, SliC-HF, etc. (Reviewer 2, question).
- **Specific technical questions that remain unanswered**:
  - What is the size of the final preference dataset generated by the self-aligned SFT model? Are preferences collected for every prompt-output pair and every principle? (Reviewer 2).
  - Since preference is computed from logprob differences, could the highest logprobability be misleading? Should consider semantically equivalent answers (Holtzmann et al.) (Reviewer 2).
  - Why is the preference label decided by the principle with the most pronounced difference instead of the sum of scores? (Reviewer 2).
  - When answers are indistinguishable per a principle, would it make sense to include a “no preference” option? (Reviewer 2).
- **Minor issues**: Typo on page 8 (“police” should be “policy”); unclear what “power RLHF trained model” means (page 7) (Reviewer 4, Q5, Q6).
- **Comment on broader applicability**: While the method could potentially be adapted to code generation, the authors could comment on this (Reviewer 1).
