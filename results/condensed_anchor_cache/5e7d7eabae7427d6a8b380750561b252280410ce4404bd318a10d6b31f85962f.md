## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates how example selection for in-context learning (ICL) amplifies biases in LLMs. Three key findings: (1) high-accuracy example selection does not guarantee low bias; (2) ICL example selection amplifies existing LLM biases; (3) example selection contributes to spurious correlations. The authors construct a new sentiment classification dataset EEC-paraphrase (GPT-3.5-paraphrased from the original Equity Evaluation Corpus) and propose ReBE (Remind with Bias-aware Embedding), which uses contrastive learning and prompt tuning to reduce spurious correlations. Evaluated on 8 LLMs (GPT, OPT, LLaMA-2) with 4 example selection methods (Random, Perplexity, Similarity, DPP) and multiple bias metrics (AvgGF, MaxTG, MaxFG). Results reported show ReBE reduces maximum bias without significantly compromising accuracy and is compatible with existing selection methods.

### Strengths
1. **Novelty in addressing bias in ICL example selection** (R1, R2, R3). The paper tackles an underexplored problem: how example selection methods in ICL amplify bias. Showing that different selection methods increase maximum bias while possibly reducing average bias is an important empirical finding. The work also disentangles native parametric bias from ICL-induced bias, offering a more holistic evaluation (R1). R2 notes that if the findings hold, they have important implications for ICL practice, though this hedge reflects lower confidence (R2 rating 3, confidence 4 vs. R1 rating 6, confidence 3).

2. **Comprehensive experimental setup** (R1, R2, R3). Experiments span multiple model families and sizes (GPT, OPT, LLaMA-2), 4 distinct example selection methods (Random, Perplexity, Similarity, DPP), and multiple bias metrics (AvgGF, MaxTG, MaxFG), strengthening generalizability (R1). R2 and R3 confirm the breadth strengthens reliability.

3. **Effectiveness of ReBE** (R1, R3). Figure 7 shows ReBE reduces MaxFG more than other methods (e.g., DPP) and does not significantly impact accuracy (R1). R3 praises the innovative combination of contrastive learning and prompt tuning and notes solid theoretical grounding for why the approach works. R1 qualifies that improvements over DPP are often modest (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
1. **Dataset construction using GPT-3.5-Turbo lacks validation** (R2, R3, R1 via agreement). The claim that EEC-paraphrase sentences are “more complex and natural” is unsubstantiated (R2). Using an LLM to generate sentences to study biases in other LLMs is methodologically questionable; inherited biases and quality control are not addressed (R2, R3). No human evaluation of paraphrases, comparison with real-world text, or analysis of artifacts introduced by GPT-3.5 is provided (R2, R3). This is a major weakness: R2 (soundness 2, presentation 1) and R3 (soundness 3) both flag this.

2. **Missing ablation studies and limited comparison with simpler debiasing methods** (R2, R3, R1 indirectly). No ablation isolating which components of ReBE (contrastive loss, prompt tuning) are responsible for bias reduction (R2). Statistical significance tests are absent; the claim “doesn’t significantly compromise accuracy” is not defined with significance testing (R2). Comparisons to existing debiasing methods (e.g., data augmentation, adversarial debiasing) are omitted, making it hard to assess ReBE’s advantages (R3). R1 notes that ReBE’s bias reduction over DPP is often modest (Figure 7); the paper should analyze conditions when ReBE is most vs. least effective and explore alternative loss functions.

3. **Bias amplification claim is too bold given mixed results** (R1, R2, R3). The statement “all LLMs exhibit an increase in the maximum gender or race bias value with random-based example selection” (lines 217–218) is not fully supported: Figure 2 shows that example selection often *reduces* average bias (AvgGF) for many models (R1). The authors should acknowledge that observed bias amplification varies by model, task, and metric, and provide nuanced conditions under which amplification occurs (R1). Experiments are limited to a single task (sentiment analysis); patterns may not hold for other tasks like toxicity detection or text classification (R2, R3). R1 also asks for deeper analysis of why average bias decreases while maximum bias increases.

4. **No analysis of the impact of number of ICL examples across methods** (R1, echoed by R2’s question). The paper only varies the number of shots for ReBE (Figure 8), not for other selection methods (Random, Perplexity, Similarity, DPP). A systematic investigation across all methods, including the bias–accuracy trade-off as the number of shots increases, is necessary (R1). R1 explicitly asks: “How does increasing the number of ICL examples impact bias in other example selection methods?”

5. **Presentation and writing quality need improvement** (R2, R1). Figures and tables are often not clearly explained in the text or captions; Figure 6 is confusing and under-described (R1, R2). Writing issues include questionable citations (e.g., Albaquerque et al. for spurious correlations followed by the simplistic example “He is a doctor; she is a nurse”) (R2). R2 rates presentation as 1 (poor) and recommends thorough rewriting; R1 also flags Figure 6.

6. **Additional computational cost of ReBE not quantified** (R1). R1 asks about the extra training time required to add ReBE to existing methods. This practical concern is not addressed in the paper.
