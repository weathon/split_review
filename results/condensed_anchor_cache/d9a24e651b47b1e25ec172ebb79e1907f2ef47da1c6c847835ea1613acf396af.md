- Scores: 6, 5, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes BioBGT, a brain graph Transformer that encodes small-world architecture via network entanglement-based node importance encoding and functional module-aware self-attention. It aims to improve biological plausibility for brain disorder detection. Reviewers agree on the clear motivation and strong empirical performance on three benchmark datasets, but diverge on the strength of the biological plausibility claims, novelty, experimental rigor, and completeness of ablations and baselines.

### Strengths

- **Biological motivation and clarity:** All reviewers note the authors’ effort to incorporate prior knowledge of brain structure (hub nodes, functional modules) into the framework. The writing is clear and well‑organized (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- **Strong empirical performance:** BioBGT shows clear improvements over baselines across multiple datasets (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 3 finds the results in Tables 1 and 2 “quite impressive,” and Table 6 still robust despite some variability.  
- **Experimental design:** Use of three widely‑recognized neuroimaging datasets with two different parcellation methods is a strength (Reviewer 3). The ablation study compares node importance encoding against other centrality measures (Reviewer 3).  
- **Generalizability:** Section 4.5 demonstrates that BioBGT can achieve comparable performance on non‑neuroimaging datasets with similar underlying properties (Reviewer 3).  
- **Original concept:** Transferring network entanglement from quantum computing to quantify node importance is original and tailored to fMRI graphs (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Equation and proof error:** In Eq. (6) of Theorem 1, the second term should be \( \log_2(Z_i / Z) \) not \( \log_2(Z_i Z) \); the proof in the appendix contains a calculation error leading to different expressions (Reviewer 1).  
- **Insufficient novelty:** The node importance encoding and self‑attention mechanism are very similar to prior works. The self‑attention formulation (Eq. 9) is nearly identical to that in [2]; the only claimed improvement is making the exponential kernel trainable, but the benefit of this is not adequately explained or compared against [2] (Reviewer 1).  
- **Weak biological plausibility evidence:**  
  - Using PCC as a proxy for biological plausibility in Section 4.4 is logically tenuous; the quantitative similarity between NE values and PCC is not studied (Reviewer 2).  
  - The BioBGT output heatmap (Fig. 5c) does not consistently show modular block‑like patterns (e.g., between ROIs 40–60) and deviates from expected functional connectivity patterns, undermining the “plausibility” claim (Reviewers 1, 2).  
  - The term “with their biological plausibility preserved” (line 345) is misleading because classification performance alone does not demonstrate biological plausibility (Reviewer 1).  
  - Strong assumptions about “biologically plausible” properties should be toned down; the methodology does not significantly incorporate known biological properties of the human brain (Reviewer 2).  
- **Missing ablation for community contrastive strategy:** No ablation study is conducted for the community contrastive training component (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- **No smaller parcellation experiment:** ADNI uses a smaller parcellation and shows weaker results; the impact of the number of ROIs on model performance is not investigated (Reviewer 3).  
- **Lack of traditional ML baselines:** Comparisons with random forests or SVM trained on the upper triangle of correlation matrices are missing; such models often match deep learning performance in connectomics (Reviewer 3).  
- **High standard deviations in tables:** Tables 1 and 2 report large standard deviations, undermining the credibility of the comparisons, and the baseline performance appears lower than expected (Reviewers 1, 2).  
- **Redundant ablations:** The node importance ablation comparisons with various graph metrics are redundant and could be merged with the FM‑Attn ablation results (Reviewer 2).  
- **Misleading term “scalability”:** Section 4.5 is about generalizability across datasets, not about parameter size, data, or computation scalability (Reviewer 2).  
- **No analysis of discovered modules:** Section 4.4 does not discuss whether the modules correspond to known brain structures or medical knowledge, nor how they contribute to classification (Reviewers 1, 3).  
- **Rationale for additive node importance:** In Eq. (7), addition is used to incorporate node importance without justification; multiplication or concatenation could be considered (Reviewer 2).  
- **Validation with only 50 nodes:** The biological plausibility validation for node importance encoding uses only 50 randomly selected nodes; it is unclear if results are consistent across all datasets (Reviewer 1).  
- **Comparison with recent Graph Transformers:** The baseline set lacks more recent Graph‑Transformer methods on functional connectivity (Reviewer 2).  
- **Minor issues:**  
  - “High correlation” (line 054) needs clarification (temporal changes?) (Reviewer 2).  
  - The category “Brain Graph Learning Models” should be revised (GAT is a GNN, not only a Graph Transformer) (Reviewer 2).  
  - Eq. (7) mentions “learnable embedding vectors” but they are not shown in the equation (Reviewer 3).  
  - No mention of parameter count comparisons (claimed in Conclusion) (Reviewer 3).  
  - Multiclass metric calculation for ADNI (F1/sensitivity/specificity for three classes) is unspecified (Reviewer 3).  
  - No experimentation with other contrastive loss functions beyond Eq. (8) (Reviewer 3).  
  - Figure 5 caption should be more informative (Reviewer 1).  
  - Merging Tables 1, 2, 6 and reducing the number of presented metrics (e.g., keep AUC and ACC) would improve readability (Reviewer 2).
