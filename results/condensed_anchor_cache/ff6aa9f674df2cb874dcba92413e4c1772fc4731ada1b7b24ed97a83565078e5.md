## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MuseCoco, a two-stage symbolic music generation system from text: first text-to-attribute understanding (BERT over synthetic captions), then attribute-to-music generation (Transformer, self-supervised on ~1M unlabeled symbolic pieces). The approach addresses the lack of paired text–symbolic music data by extracting musical attributes directly from music and using ChatGPT to produce text from attribute templates.

### Strengths
- **Sound two-stage approach**: The decomposition into text-to-attribute and attribute-to-music stages is a practical and intuitive way to circumvent the data sparsity problem (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Using deterministically extractable attributes from symbolic music makes the second stage self-supervised (R3).
- **Large-scale training**: The models are trained on a large amount of data (~1M samples, 1.2B parameters for the largest model), making the results competitive and enabling high-quality generation (R3).
- **Impressive generated music quality**: The output is far superior to GPT‑4 baselines in both musicality and controllability (R1). Objective evaluations (Table 4, ~20% improvement in control accuracy) and professional musician comments further support this (R1, R4).
- **Simple and intuitive design**: The overall methodology is straightforward, clear, and convincing (R4). The paper is well-organized and the key ideas are easy to grasp (R1, R4).
- **Potential for workflow acceleration**: The system can significantly speed up composition for musicians (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons to prior attribute-conditioned generation work**: The paper omits direct comparisons with existing systems that handle subsets of the same control attributes, such as MuseMorphose (rhythm/polyphony), Compose & Embellish (harmony/melody), Multitrack Music Transformer (instrument), and Compound Word Transformer (harmony/tempo). This weakens the claim of novelty (R3).
- **Unclear and non-standard evaluation metrics**:
    - ASA (Attribute Score Accuracy) is not clearly defined – it is ambiguous whether it evaluates the text-to-attribute model or the attribute-to-music model, and how attribute values are deterministically obtained from output music (R3).
    - AvgAttrCtrlAcc is never explicitly defined in the main paper, and its distinction from ASA remains unclear (R3).
    - ASA is computed over different sample sizes/batch sizes for different methods (GPT‑4 vs. BART‑base vs. proposed), and there is no justification for why BART‑base could not use the same sample size as the proposed method (R3).
- **Inflated text-to-attribute performance**: The data generation procedure always places the ground‑truth attribute value directly in the synthetic description. This reduces the task to keyword extraction rather than true text understanding; the problem becomes non‑extractive only if ChatGPT is used to obfuscate the attributes (R3).
- **Incomplete baseline comparison**: The BART‑base baseline was likely not retrained on the same data as the proposed model (implied use of pretrained checkpoint). Differences in data quality/size and 50% fewer parameters make it unclear whether advantages come from model design or simply from more/better training data (R3).
- **Reproducibility concerns**: Training is performed on private datasets; a comparison using publicly available data (e.g., the Lakh dataset) would strengthen the work (R2). The question “Why was the Lakh dataset not used?” remains unanswered (R2).
- **Lack of justification for the attribute set**: The paper does not explain how the chosen attributes were selected, nor provides evidence that this is a good base set. Expanding the set would require a complete retrain (R2). Some attributes may be harder to control than others; no analysis of control difficulty per attribute is given (R4, Q2).
- **Limited handling of abstract or vague text prompts**: The two-stage model naturally excels when the text contains explicit attribute keywords, but the paper does not evaluate cases where the prompt is abstract or ambiguous. This overclaims the generality of the solution (R4). The question “how does the model behave with vague text?” is not addressed (R4, Q3).
- **Cherry-picking impression and insufficient in‑paper examples**: Although objective evaluations and professional comments are provided, the lack of multiple input–output examples (e.g., sheets or piano rolls) in the main body leaves room for readers to suspect cherry-picking (R1). Specifically, the GPT‑4 baseline example in Figure 4 violates classical counterpoint (parallel fifths/sixths) and the key appears A minor/Aeolian rather than the intended C major; this example alone may give a poor impression of the proposed method (R1). The authors should show example outputs of MuseCoco itself (R1).
- **Overclaiming and limited scientific contribution**: The claim that music generation can be perfectly decomposed into text‑to‑attribute and attribute‑to‑music does not address the inherent ambiguity in natural language; the paper offers a practical solution rather than a new understanding of the problem (R4). The statement that the model “closely resembles human performances in musicality” is unsubstantiated because no human performance comparison was conducted (R2).
- **Writing quality issues**: The paper is wordy, often uses imprecise word choice, and contains long sentences with unnecessary logic twists (R4). Spelling errors such as “two‑stage design” (R4) and the abstract’s “three advantages” paragraph are examples (R4).
- **Missing details on attribute extraction**: No description of how musical attributes (beyond trivial ones like time signature and key) are automatically extracted from ABC or similar symbolic formats (R2). For the BERT model, it is unclear whether position encoding is used for the `[cls]` tokens representing the attribute classes; without it, the model cannot distinguish between different attribute slots (R4, Q1).
- **Limited generated music length**: The maximum length of generated music is constrained by the Transformer architecture, which restricts applicability to longer compositions (R1).
- **Unexplored extension to multi‑instrument music**: The current model focuses on single‑instrument piano‑roll style; the question “how to expand this model for multi‑instrument music?” is left open (R2).
- **Evaluation participant sampling unclear**: The subjective evaluation involved 19 participants, but the sampling method (e.g., recruitment, music expertise) is not reported (R2).
