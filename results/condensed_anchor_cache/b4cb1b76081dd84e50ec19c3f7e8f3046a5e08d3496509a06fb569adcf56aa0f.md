## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes GNeRP, a neural volume-based method for multi-view 3D reconstruction of highly specular objects. It extends scalar Signed Distance Function (SDF) fields (as in NeuS, Ref-Neus) by adding a 3D Gaussian-based representation of surface normals. These 3D Gaussians are splatted into 2D Gaussians in the image plane, which are then constrained using polarization priors (Angle of Polarization, AoP). A DoP (Degree of Polarization) reweighting strategy alleviates noise in the polarization cues. The method is evaluated on the PANDORA dataset and a newly collected PolRef dataset of reflective objects. Comparisons show improved geometry accuracy over prior state-of-the-art methods.

### Strengths

- High-quality geometry reconstruction for highly specular objects, capturing intricate details with high precision. Both quantitative and qualitative comparisons show improvements over prior methods, including those that also use polarization information or are designed for specular objects.
- The novel integration of Gaussian splatting to model polarization priors for reflective surface recovery is innovative and aligned with current research trends.
- The 3D Gaussian representation of normals is formulated via splatting to 2D Gaussians, and the paper demonstrates that the 2D Gaussian can be efficiently computed from the Angle of Polarization (AoP). Additionally, the DoP is strongly correlated with surface complexity, enabling a reweighting strategy to guide the method to use more polarization cues on areas with strong polarization and more radiance on areas with low polarization.
- The proposed method does not make strong assumptions on material properties or capturing environment, making it practical for many applications.

### Weaknesses

- The method only works for glossy objects that exhibit clear polarization information. Missing discussion of failure cases and limitations.
- The explanation of the proposed method is unclear. It is not clearly stated what the input is (e.g., is a polarization camera required? If not, how is the polarization prior obtained?).
- The proposed approach neglects the effect of self-occlusions, which can be severe for complicated objects. A qualitative discussion is needed.
- The property of polarization is closely related to materials. The approach relies on intermediate outputs of a coordinate network and seems to handle only objects with uniform material (as shown in Fig. 5).
- The method has many hyperparameters (e.g., alpha and beta varying between 0.1 and 1) that are hard to set in practice. No experiments assessed the optimal selection of these parameters, and exact values across experiments are not stated.
- The ablation study is incomplete: it does not examine the impact of alpha and beta. Additionally, only two objects are included in Table 2 while four are in Table 1; the ablation for the other two objects is missing.
- The effectiveness of the Gaussian normal representation as the major novelty is not fully verified. A baseline using standard volume ray tracing of normals with the reweighted mean loss would clarify if sampling extra points to compute a Gaussian distribution is necessary.
- The paper presents experimental results only on the PANDORA dataset and a few objects. More complex objects and less specular objects would be beneficial to understand performance limits.
- The creation of a new multi-view dataset (PolRef) is claimed as a contribution, but there is a notable lack of information and discussion about this dataset (e.g., how it was collected, more details).
- Some technical details need clarification:
    - How to compute the 2D normal distribution from a polarized image (specifically, how to get \(\hat{\Sigma}, \hat{\Lambda}\) and \(\tilde{\Sigma}, \tilde{\Lambda}\) in Equations 7–9).
    - How to decide the scale factor \(s\) in the projected normal distribution; is the mean loss scale-invariant?
    - Clarification of the relationship between \(M\) and \(M'\) (sampling points along the ray to estimate the 3D Gaussian distribution) and how the mean of the projected 2D Gaussian is computed.
    - Further justification why the 2D covariance matrices from polarization and splatting should be equal. Side-by-side visualization of both distributions would be useful, as well as discussion of noisy AoP on diffuse surfaces.
- Several typographical errors and improper terms: e.g., "alpha" in first paragraph of page 5, "common scenes" in Section 2.3, incorrect reference to Fig. 4(g) (no such subfigure), Fig. 4 caption says "blue boxes bound diffuse ones", extra parenthesis in \(L_{\text{mean}}\) and \(L_{\text{conv}}\), and "exact 3D shapes" in abstract should be replaced with "accurate".
- Missing reference to a related prior work.
- One reviewer questions if results on less glossy objects are available; DoP weighting is expected to help but not verified.
