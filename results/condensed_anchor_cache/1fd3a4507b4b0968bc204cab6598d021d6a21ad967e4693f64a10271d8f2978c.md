## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces UB-GOLD, a benchmark that unifies unsupervised graph-level anomaly detection (GLAD) and graph-level out-of-distribution detection (GLOD) under the concept of generalized graph-level OOD detection. The benchmark compares 18 (correct number) representative methods on 35 datasets spanning four practical scenarios, with multi-dimensional analyses of effectiveness, generalizability, robustness, and efficiency. The codebase is open-sourced. Reviewers generally found the paper well-written and the benchmark effort valuable, though concerns were raised about novelty, clarity of task unification, dataset domain bias, experimental details, and hyperparameter settings.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- A large number of datasets (35) are employed, spanning multiple scenarios, providing a broad evaluation. The open-source codebase facilitates future research.
- The benchmark is the first to unify GLAD and GLOD tasks under a single framework, a creative combination that simplifies evaluation and highlights their conceptual overlap.
- Beyond performance, the paper investigates generalizability, robustness, and efficiency, offering practical insights. Observations include: (1) GNN-based methods achieve best average performance but no single method is universally superior; (2) methods struggle more with near-OOD than far-OOD; (3) end-to-end methods often outperform two-step methods in both accuracy and computational cost.
- The insight that unsupervised GLAD methods can be leveraged for OOD detection is a good point.

### Weaknesses
**Task Unification and Motivation**
- The formalization of the unified task (Definition 1) needs more clarity. An anomaly could still be in-distribution (low-density region), and an OOD point might not be anomalous if many similar points exist in the test set. The assumed setting (test data unseen) needs better motivation.
- The relationship between GLAD and GLOD requires deeper discussion. Should OOD detection be considered broader than anomaly detection, or vice versa? Formal definitions (with math symbols) for each task before unification would help. Include input space, output space, objective, nature of anomaly/OOD, and whether tasks are transductive/inductive.
- One reviewer (rating 8) found the unification original and creative; others were more cautious about its novelty and clarity.

**Dataset Domain Bias**
- Of the 35 datasets, only 8 are non-molecular (mainly from finance, bioinformatics). This dominance may bias findings toward methods that perform well on molecular graphs, a point not sufficiently discussed.

**Experimental Details and Completeness**
- Generation of anomalies/OOD samples is not described in sufficient detail (e.g., distribution/density of added edges, magnitude of attribute changes, rationale). This should be added to the appendix.
- Several figures omit methods: Figure 4 shows only 15 of 19 methods; Figures 5 and 6 also have omissions. Full results for all methods should be in the appendix, with justification for omissions from main figures.
- Metrics used in Figures 4 and 5 are not specified. Complete results for all metrics (AUROC, AUPRC, etc.) should be provided, with discussion of metric suitability (e.g., AUPRC better for imbalanced data).
- On IC50-Size and similar datasets where OOD samples differ primarily in graph structure, SSL-D and GK-D methods performed poorly. An explanation of why they struggle (e.g., how they handle graph structure) is needed.
- The paper mentions "OOD Judge Score Distribution Analysis" but never references it in the main text (only in appendix). Add a citation.

**Hyperparameter Tuning and Data Leakage**
- Hyperparameters were tuned using test set performance (random search). This risks overfitting and overly optimistic results. Clarify if a validation set was used; discuss generalizability implications.
- In unsupervised GLAD, the test set may also be usable as training set (data leakage concern). Explicitly clarify that no leakage occurs and discuss potential bias.

**Clarification of ID Test Samples and Overlaps**
- The construction of in-distribution (ID) test samples needs detail: selection criteria, proportions of different labels, rationale for test_{ID} / train_{ID} ratios.
- Observed large overlaps between OOD samples in some datasets. Provide specific examples and deeper analysis of data characteristics leading to such overlaps.
- In datasets where near-OOD samples and anomalies come from the same dataset, what is the conceptual difference? Clarify whether anomalies/OOD introduced during training contamination are also retained in the test set.

**Terminology and Minor Issues**
- The term "generalisability" is used to describe near- vs. far-OOD performance, but it typically refers to performance on unseen data. Consider alternative terms like "OOD detection range" or "OOD sensitivity spectrum."
- Typo on page 4, line 084: "28" should be "18" GLAD/GLOD methods.
- Table 1 should include the anomaly ratio for each dataset.
- In Figure 6, consider superimposing CPU and GPU bars with different colors for clarity.
- On page 5, "well-trained GNNs" – clarify if this means pre-trained GNNs.
- Add concise summary sections (e.g., bullet lists) at the end of each results subsection to help digest key insights.

**Contributions to Future Research**
- Several findings are mostly expected (e.g., class imbalance impacts metrics, near-OOD harder than far-OOD). Only findings 1 and 9 (unexpectedly good performance, end-to-end efficiency) were considered novel by one reviewer. The value of what can be learned from these results is questioned.
