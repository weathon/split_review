## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes **MAD**, an end-to-end multi-alignment framework for decoding MEG signals into unseen text using a speech-decoding pipeline (Whisper) with three loss terms: low-level mel spectrogram alignment (\(L_m\)), high-level latent alignment (\(L_e\)), and text-level alignment (\(L_t\)). On the GWilliams dataset, MAD achieves a BLEU-1 score of **10.44** without teacher forcing, outperforming the reported baseline (5.49). Scores across the five reviewers are **6, 3, 3, 5, 6** – there is clear disagreement. Two reviewers (R2, R3) rate the paper low (3) citing incremental novelty, insufficient statistical testing, and single-dataset evaluation; three reviewers (R1, R4, R5) rate it higher (5–6) praising the approach, ablation studies, and no-teacher-forcing evaluation, while still noting concerns about generalizability and benchmarking fairness.

### Strengths

- **Novel multi-alignment framework**: The introduction of a multi-alignment approach (low-level, high-level, text-level) that utilizes auxiliary modalities (e.g., Mel spectrograms) to enhance MEG-to-text translation is considered novel by R1, R4, and R5. R4 calls the overall approach novel and notes the push for unseen-text evaluation is laudable. R5 highlights it as a new open-vocabulary framework that avoids teacher forcing, using a more reasonable evaluation method.
- **Strong performance on BLEU-1**: The method achieves state-of-the-art BLEU-1 (10.44) on the GWilliams dataset, significantly outperforming the baseline (R1, R5). R4 reports strong performance across metrics for both high-level accuracy and low-level semantic content.
- **Comprehensive ablation studies**: R1, R4, and R5 note the thorough ablation and experimental design that clarify the contribution of each component, especially the loss functions and alignment mechanisms. R5 says the experimental design is robust.
- **Inclusion of random baselines**: R4 appreciates the inclusion of random Gaussian baselines and argues this should be adopted as standard for future decoding studies.
- **Clarity and organization**: R1 and R5 state the paper is exceptionally well-written and easy to understand; R3 also notes the problem setting and overall approach are clearly communicated.
- **Effectiveness of high-level alignment (\(L_e\))**: R2 and R3 (minority) still acknowledge that the high-level \(L_e\) loss is proven effective.

### Weaknesses

- **Incremental originality (minority view)**: R2 and R3 consider the methodological contribution incremental. Défossez et al. (2023) introduced alignment of M/EEG signals with latent representations of a pre-trained ASR model (wav2vec2.0) using CLIP loss; this paper uses Whisper encoder instead of wav2vec2.0 and MMD loss instead of CLIP. Yang et al. (2024) combined Whisper with a convolutional adapter network (AdaLoRa); this paper uses the brain module of Défossez et al. as adapter network. [Majority reviewers (R1, R4, R5) disagree and find the approach novel.]
- **Unfair benchmarking with Défossez et al. (2023)**: R1 and R4 both criticise the comparison. The brain module within Wav2vecCTC is not trained on text, so it struggles to generate coherent words – the comparison is unfair (R1). R4 states the benchmarking appears in bad faith: the authors used only the brain model and applied a decoding head, instead of the original Défossez et al. model which returns the most likely audio segment (always real words) and can decode unseen segments. If the intention was to show that Défossez et al. needs segmented test audio, this should have been stated or the model omitted from benchmarking.
- **Insufficient statistical significance testing**: R2 and R3 argue that evaluating random effects with a single shuffling/noise baseline is insufficient. Permutation testing (e.g., Maris 2012) should be used to estimate the distribution of metrics under the null hypothesis and properly assess significance.
- **Evaluation on only one dataset**: R2, R3, and R5 all identify the limitation of testing only on the GWilliams dataset, unlike related works (NeuSpeech, Wav2vecCTC) that also test on Schoffelen et al. (2019). R5 notes this limits generalizability claims. R1 also asks why the authors did not test on alternative datasets like Armeni (2022) or perform multi-dataset validation. R3 and R5 request experiments on the Schoffelen dataset.
- **Generalization claim not supported**: R1 points out that the test set has **46% word overlap** with the training set, which fails to represent truly unseen text. The model should be compared against baselines separately on seen and unseen text. R3 and R5 also report that generalization results to unseen stories (Table 2) and subjects (Table 5) are discouraging.
- **Missing ablation details and redundancy of components**: R1 notes that ablation studies show \(L_t\) and LoRA are redundant and should be omitted. R5 asks for concrete decoding examples under different combinations of loss functions to validate the importance of high-level and low-level alignments; R1 similarly asks for performance when only using \(L_t\) and for the significance of \(L_e\).
- **Figure 1(a) mismatch and double dipping concern**: R2 and R3 observe that Figure 1(a) does not correspond to the proposed MAD model in Table 2. This discrepancy suggests possible double dipping (Kriegeskorte et al. 2009) – the authors may have tested many configurations and selected the final model based on test set results.
- **Lack of stability/variability analysis**: R2 and R3 note that results lack quantification of stability across random weight initializations; only one evaluation per setting is reported.
- **Missing comparison with and without teacher forcing**: R1 requests that baselines be compared both with and without teacher forcing.
- **Unanswered questions about sliding window inference**: R5 asks whether the inference stage aggregates outputs across 4-second sliding windows (shift 1s, random shifts ±0.5s) and what the effect of different window sizes is on decoding results. R1 also asks how the shifted 4-second window is transformed into a fixed-size sample for the Mel spectrogram.
- **Unanswered questions about cross-subject implementation**: R2 asks how cross-subject is implemented in Section A.1 and why it performs similarly to intra-subject despite a different subject index input.
- **Minor technical and presentation issues**: R3 points to inconsistent symbols, undefined \(\phi\) in (2), unclear definition of random shifts (interval vs set), and that the baseline `Wav2vec2CTC` was not proposed in Défossez et al. R1 notes a missing arrow in Figure 1(b). R4 and R3 flag citation formatting and grammatical issues.
