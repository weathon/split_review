## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper identifies that existing inductive knowledge graph completion (KGC) datasets (subsets of transductive KGs) suffer from a shortcut: the shortest path distance (SPD) between positive sample entities is significantly lower than between negative sample entities. This allows Personalized PageRank (PPR), which ignores relation types entirely, to achieve near state-of-the-art performance on most inductive benchmarks. The authors propose a new dataset construction strategy based on graph partitioning that preserves the original graph’s SPD distribution, thereby removing the PPR shortcut. They benchmark several inductive KGC methods on these new datasets and show that performance degrades relative to older datasets, confirming that the new benchmarks are more challenging.

### Strengths
- Identifies a fundamental, previously overlooked flaw in existing inductive KGC dataset construction (SPD shortcut); demonstrates that all configurations of the current data generation algorithm suffer from this problem (Reviewer 1).
- The problem and solution are significant and valuable for the community, as dataset issues can lead to misleading conclusions about model performance (Reviewer 2).
- Well-written, clear, and easy to follow (all reviewers).
- The graph partitioning remedial strategy is intuitive and effective; it maintains the original graph’s SPD distribution and provides a fairer benchmark (Reviewer 1).
- Extensive empirical evaluation covering a wide range of inductive KGC methods; results are consistent with the claims (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Detailed analysis of the relationship between PPR, SPD, and KGC performance (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- Missing key citations and related work: does not discuss Gao et al. (2023) (ISDEA+, DEq-InGram, PediaTypes, WikiTopics) and does not include these (E,R)-capable methods and datasets in experiments, which limits comprehensiveness (Reviewer 1).
- Technical contribution is considered limited: the graph partitioning strategy is straightforward and the research challenge is not significant (Reviewer 3). Note: Reviewer 3 gave a lower score (6) than Reviewers 1/2 (8), indicating disagreement on the novelty.
- The justification for why graph partitioning eliminates the SPD shortcut is not fully explained. Specifically, it is unclear how minimizing edges between partitions directly preserves the SPD characteristics relevant to link prediction; a deeper explanation of the partitioning algorithms and their properties is needed (Reviewers 2, 4).
- A reviewer suggests an alternative approach: instead of graph partitioning, directly improve the negative sampling strategy by selecting negative entities with shorter distances from the query entity, which could be independent of graph structure (Reviewer 3). This criticism is not addressed.
- The shortcut is partly attributed to the use of filtered/negative-sampled evaluation; recent methods like RED-GNN, NBFNet, and ULTRA use full evaluation (ranking over the entire entity set). The paper does not compare performance under both evaluation settings on the new benchmarks (Reviewer 3).
- Lack of discussion and evaluation of real-world inductive scenarios (e.g., new drugs, new patients, cold-start recommendations). It is unclear whether the PPR/SPD correlation exists in such practical applications (Reviewer 4).
- Transductive datasets themselves have unrealistic problems; the paper does not discuss how the new inductive datasets address or relate to these broader issues (Reviewer 4).
- Details of the graph partition method for ensuring training and inference have *different* entity/relation sets are unclear; the description in the main text and appendix is insufficient (Reviewer 4).
- Another commonly used inductive dataset, NELL, is not studied (Reviewer 4).
- Analysis of results is insufficient: the paper should explain why some methods (e.g., NBFNet) improve on new datasets while others (e.g., in the (E,R) setting) struggle; it should include variance across random seeds and discuss statistical significance of performance differences (Reviewer 2).
- Typo: line 237 reads "the different in mean SPD" (Reviewer 3).
- Specific questions remain unanswered: Why does NBFNet improve on new datasets despite being more challenging? Why does the performance gap between PPR and NBFNet vary across datasets (e.g., WN18RR vs FB237)? (Reviewer 2)
