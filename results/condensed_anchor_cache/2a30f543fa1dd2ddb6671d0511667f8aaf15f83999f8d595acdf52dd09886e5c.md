## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces LLaVA-MoD, a framework to distill large multimodal language models into efficient small-scale models using a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture and a progressive distillation strategy with mimic distillation (KL divergence) and preference distillation (PO). Experiments show LLaVA-MoD-2B outperforms larger models like Qwen-VL-Chat-7B by 8.8% on average, using only 0.3% of training data and 23% of trainable parameters, and achieves strong hallucination reduction. All reviewers find the approach novel, efficient, and validated across multiple benchmarks, but raise concerns about missing inference and memory analyses, architectural complexity, limited generality, and insufficient comparisons with existing distillation methods. The scores range from 5 to 8, with one reviewer (score 5) being notably more critical about complexity and teacher dependency.

### Strengths
- **Progressive distillation strategy**: Multiple reviewers (1, 2, 3, 4, 6) highlight the two-stage process combining mimic distillation (KL divergence) for knowledge alignment and preference distillation (PO) for hallucination reduction. Reviewer 1 calls it “well-constructed”; Reviewer 2 notes it is “interesting” and a notable strength.
- **Sparse MoE architecture**: Integration of MoE into the language model balances computational efficiency and expressiveness. Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5 praise its effectiveness; Reviewer 1 says it is “thoughtfully implemented”; Reviewer 4 calls it “flexible.”
- **Strong empirical results**: LLaVA-MoD-2B surpasses larger models like Qwen-VL-Chat-7B by 8.8% average gain with 0.3% training data and 23% trainable parameters (Reviewers 1, 3, 6). It outperforms 2B baselines (e.g., Imp-2B) and reduces hallucinations (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Reviewer 6 notes performance is “significant.”
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The paper evaluates on multiple general comprehension and hallucination benchmarks and includes extensive ablation studies validating each component (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5, 6). Reviewer 1 praises the thoroughness; Reviewer 2 says ablation “helps validate design choices.”
- **Data and parameter efficiency**: The framework achieves high performance with minimal data and parameters, making it practical for resource-constrained settings (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5, 6). Reviewer 1 emphasizes practical relevance.
- **Novel combination**: The combination of MoE with knowledge distillation in the multimodal setting is seen as a novel contribution (Reviewers 5, 6). Reviewer 5 calls it “novel.”
- **Clear writing and presentation**: The paper is well-written and clearly explains the framework (Reviewers 3, 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of inference efficiency analysis**: Multiple reviewers (1, 3, 4, 5, 6) note that the paper does not provide practical inference measurements such as FLOPs, latency, throughput, and memory usage during inference, which are critical for deployment. Reviewer 1 specifically requests a breakdown of these metrics; Reviewer 3 recommends reporting FLOPs and latency.
- **Missing comparison with existing distillation methods**: The paper does not compare with standard distillation baselines like KD or GKD to justify why the progressive distillation is needed (Reviewer 6). This limits the empirical justification of the method.
- **Insufficient MoE configuration analysis**: Several reviewers (1, 5, 6) note that the impact of different MoE designs (number of experts, routing strategies, top‑k parameter) on performance and computational trade-offs is not explored. Reviewer 1 questions why increasing experts from 4 to 8 fails to improve performance; Reviewer 5 requests analysis of routing strategies and parameter k.
- **Requirement for same vocabulary/tokenization**: The student and teacher must have identical vocabulary and token IDs, preventing cross-family transfer (Reviewer 1). This is a significant limitation.
- **Memory overhead during training**: Loading both student and teacher models simultaneously requires substantial memory, and techniques like gradient checkpointing or model parallelism are not explored (Reviewer 1). Reviewers 2 and 6 also request details on total parameters and memory/wall-clock time during training and inference.
- **Complexity and stability of progressive distillation**: The dense-to-sparse transition and multi-task training add implementation complexity and hyperparameter sensitivity, with potential training instability (Reviewer 4, score 5). Reviewer 2 also finds some architectural details unclear (e.g., differences between student and teacher models).
- **Trade-off between hallucination and comprehension**: Preference distillation primarily reduces hallucinations but may not consistently improve comprehension, potentially limiting applicability for complex instructions (Reviewer 4). This aligns with the counterintuitive finding in Table 4 where Mimic+preference underperforms Mimic alone on some metrics (Reviewer 6). Reviewer 1 asks for a deeper explanation of how preference distillation works better than RLHF.
- **Dependence on teacher model quality**: The distilled model inherits limitations of the teacher; if the teacher is biased or weak, the student will be similarly constrained (Reviewer 4).
- **Modest improvement over strong baselines**: LLaVA-MoD-2B only outperforms Imp-2B by 2.7% on average while using twice as many data samples, questioning the relative advantage (Reviewer 5).
- **Missing explanation of dense-to-sparse transition**: The dense-to-sparse loss is similar to a prior work (Sengupta et al., 2023, as noted by Reviewer 6), and the paper does not sufficiently explain why this architecture works or how it differs from prior losses (Reviewer 6).
- **Limited exploration of training stability and failure cases**: The paper lacks analysis of training stability across configurations, behavior under noisy data, and discussion of failure modes or error patterns (Reviewer 1).
- **Clarity of experimental setup**: Reviewer 2 asks for clarification on training limits for comparison methods, whether student and teacher must share the same architecture, and details on Table 1 comparisons. These points indicate insufficient clarity in the presented results.
