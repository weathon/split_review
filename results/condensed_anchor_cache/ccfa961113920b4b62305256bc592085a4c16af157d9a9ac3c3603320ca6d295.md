## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MMFRL (Multimodal Fusion with Relational Learning for Molecular Property Prediction), which enhances embedding initialization through multimodal pre-training using a continuous relational learning metric (modified contrastive/smooth similarity) and systematically explores modality fusion at early, intermediate, and late stages. The method incorporates Fingerprint, SMILES, NMR, and Image modalities via pretrained encoders. Experiments on MoleculeNet benchmarks (11 downstream tasks) claim superior performance over several baselines, and the fusion analysis provides insights into when each stage is beneficial. The paper also includes case studies for explainability.

### Strengths
- The method introduces a continuous (smooth) loss function for relational learning in multimodal molecular pre-training, moving beyond binary contrastive metrics. (R1, R4)
- Three distinct multimodal fusion strategies (early, intermediate, late) are systematically compared, with analysis of their strengths and weaknesses across tasks. (R1, R2)
- A broad set of molecular modalities is considered (Fingerprint, SMILES, NMR, Image), and pretrained encoders are used for each. (R2)
- The model is evaluated on multiple property prediction datasets from MoleculeNet and shows superior performance over several baselines. (R4)
- The writing is clear, and the visualization in Figure 2 effectively illustrates the benefits of multimodal fusion. (R3)
- The paper addresses the important topic of pretrained molecular representations and the role of multi-modality in drug discovery. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- Novelty concerns: Though Reviewer 1 viewed the continuous loss as a strength, other reviewers note that smooth contrastive learning metrics have been previously proposed in general and that the paper merely transfers this idea to the molecular domain. (R2: limited novelty of multimodal contrastive learning; R3: “smooth contrastive learning metric has been previously proposed in various studies”; R4: no clear differentiation from prior contrastive learning). Additionally, the limitation of binary metrics was already addressed by prior work such as MoleBERT (ICLR 2023), which the paper does not cite. (R2)
- Missing baseline comparisons: The paper does not compare with current state-of-the-art models such as Uni-Mol (ICLR 2023) (R2), nor with recent multimodal molecular graph-text models like MolCA (R4) and the molecule structure-text model [1] (R4). These baselines would be necessary to justify the claimed superiority. (R2, R4)
- Limited task evaluation: Experiments are confined to molecular property prediction on MoleculeNet, which generally has small sample sizes and introduces randomness (R3). The paper lacks evaluations on other important molecular tasks such as molecule-molecule interaction prediction, molecule-target affinity prediction (R2), and large-scale property prediction datasets like PCQM4Mv2, QM9 (R3) or the LBA dataset for drug design (R3, question). Additional analyses were only performed on regression tasks. (R1)
- Ambiguity in fusion stage recommendations: It remains unclear which fusion strategy (early/intermediate/late) should be used for specific task types and why. The paper claims late fusion is best when one modality dominates, but the Lipo regression case study suggests complementary modalities, creating inconsistency. (R1) A clear explanation for why different fusion stages perform differently across datasets is missing. (R4)
- Missing ablations and deeper analysis: There is no ablation removing key modalities to demonstrate their relative importance. (R1) The interpretation of lower cosine similarity between modality embeddings and fused embeddings as indicating complementarity needs further justification, including comparison with other similarity measures. (R1) The modified relational learning metric’s ability to capture both localized and global relationships is not explained. (R4, question) The paper does not evaluate out-of-distribution generalization (varying heavy atoms). (R3, question)
- Presentation issues: Figures are poorly formatted with unclear font, making them hard to read (R3). The overall paper structure, especially the experimental section, is somewhat disorganized (R3). The ablation result for the pretrained graph encoder is placed first, which seems illogical (R1). Typos: “themto” (Sect. 3.2.1), “No-pretraining” (Sect. 4.2.2). (R1, R4)
- Reproducibility: No code or checkpoints are provided. (R2)
