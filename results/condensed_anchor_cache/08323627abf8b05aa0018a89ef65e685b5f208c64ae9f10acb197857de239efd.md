## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes $\sigma$-zero, a white-box $\ell_0$-norm attack that uses a differentiable approximation of the $\ell_0$ norm to enable gradient-based optimization, combined with an adaptive projection operator. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet with both standard and robust (e.g., RobustBench C1–C8) models show that $\sigma$-zero finds minimal $\ell_0$-norm adversarial examples and outperforms competing sparse attacks in success rate, perturbation size, and computational efficiency.

### Strengths

- The problem of sparse adversarial perturbations is interesting and practically relevant (R1).
- The algorithm is well-motivated, simple, intuitive, and clearly presented (R1, R2, R3).
- Using a differentiable approximation of the $\ell_0$ norm (Osborne et al., 2000b) is a reasonable approach for gradient-based optimization (R2, R4).
- Experiments cover multiple datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet) and a wide range of robust models from RobustBench (C1–C8) (R1, R4).
- Results convincingly show that $\sigma$-zero outperforms existing sparse attacks in attack success rate, perturbation sparsity, and computational cost (R1, R2, R4).
- The authors provide the code in the supplementary materials, which will benefit the community when released publicly (R4).

### Weaknesses

1. **Missing baseline comparisons**: 
   - The white-box $\ell_0$ attacks PGD$_0$ (Croce & Hein, 2019) and Sparsefool (Modas et al., 2019) should be included as baselines (R1).
   - To make the evaluation more comprehensive, $\ell_1$-robust models (e.g., AA-$\ell_1$ from Croce & Hein, 2021; Fast-EG-$\ell_1$ from Jiang et al., 2023) should also be evaluated, since $\ell_1$ is the closest convex norm to $\ell_0$ (R1).

2. **Unfair or suboptimal configuration of baselines**:
   - Several baselines (e.g., Sparse-RS) are formulated to find perturbations with $\ell_0$ norm $\leq \epsilon$, not to minimize $\ell_0$ norm; comparing the $\ell_0$ norm vs. ASR trade-off is thus unfair to these methods (R1).
   - Sparse-RS is re-run with different sparsity levels but the results are reported for all points with the same sparsity level. Ideally, a per-point binary search should be performed; a cheaper alternative would be to run Sparse-RS on several $k$ values and select for each point the smallest $k$ that finds an adversarial example (R2).
   - The BB attack could be initialized from an image of another class or dataset, as done in prior work, to avoid the starting-point issue mentioned in Sec. 3.2 (R2).
   - It is unclear whether the default parameters for all baselines are optimal; tuning (some of them) on a small subset of test cases would strengthen the comparison (R2).
   - Sparse-RS is a black-box method and does not require a backward pass; using the same number of iterations as white-box attacks gives it a lower computational cost (e.g., 2× fewer network passes). How would the results compare when equating the number of network passes? (R2)

3. **Visual quality of adversarial images**:
   - According to Figure 3, the generated images are not visually appealing; highly intensive pixels are easily spotted (R3).
   - The goal of adversarial attacks is imperceptibility; minimizing $\ell_0$ norm alone does not guarantee better visual quality, as the approach tends to perturb a few pixels heavily while leaving others unchanged (R3).
   - The paper should report additional perceptual metrics such as SSIM, PSNR, and LPIPS to assess image quality beyond the $\ell_0$ norm (R3).

4. **Limited technical contribution and insufficient scientific discussion**:
   - The $\ell_0$-norm approximation has already been used in the context of adversarial robustness (Cinà et al., 2022). The task-specific heuristics (e.g., clipping the smallest components) are not novel (R2).
   - The paper reads more like an experimental or technical report than a comprehensive academic study; it provides sparse discussion of the scientific principles explaining why $\sigma$-zero performs better (R4).
   - The term "VRAM" is not defined; its full name should be given for clarity (R4).

5. **Missing adversarial training experiment**: 
   - The robust classifiers evaluated are mostly trained against $\ell_\infty$ and $\ell_2$ threats, so they are not designed to defend against sparse attacks. What happens if a classifier is adversarially trained with 20-step $\sigma$-zero and then evaluated with 100-step $\sigma$-zero? (R3)

6. **Loss function differentiability**: 
   - The loss function $\mathcal{L}$ defined in Equation (7) is not continuous and not differentiable everywhere, which may cause problems when calculating gradients in Algorithm 1 (line 4). Using a fully continuous and differentiable function would be better (R1).

7. **Questions about Sparse-RS results**:
   - The superscripts in Sparse-RS$^{100}$ and Sparse-RS$^{85}$ denote the maximum allowed $\ell_0$ norm $k$. Why is Sparse-RS$^{100}$ worse than Sparse-RS$^{85}$? And why is the average $|\delta|_0$ larger than the corresponding $k$ in some cases? (R1)

*Note on reviewer disagreement*: Reviewer 4 is most positive regarding the contribution and thoroughness of the experiments, while Reviewer 1 is most critical about missing baselines and fairness of comparison, and Reviewer 3 is most critical about visual quality and the need for additional metrics. Reviewer 2 also questions the novelty and baseline configuration. Despite rating the paper 5 or 6, all reviewers raise significant weaknesses.
