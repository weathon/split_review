## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FlexDock, a method for flexible molecular docking and structure relaxation based on Unbalanced Flow Matching (UFM). UFM relaxes the marginal constraints of standard Flow Matching (FM) by filtering the coupling via a RMSD cutoff (c_dock), improving transport when data from the target distribution (holo structures) is limited. The two-stage pipeline first performs “manifold docking” for approximate pose prediction, then “structure relaxation” with an energy-based loss to refine it. On the PDBBind benchmark, FlexDock raises the proportion of energetically favorable poses (PB‑Valid) from ~30% to 73% while using the same training data and architecture as DiffDock‑Pocket. Ablation studies verify each component.

### Strengths
- **Novel framework:** The introduction of Unbalanced FM for flexible molecular docking is novel and provides a principled way to trade off sample efficiency and approximation accuracy when data from one distribution is scarce (R1, R3, R4, R5).  
- **Addresses critical limitations:** The method models protein flexibility and generates physically realistic poses, overcoming rigidity assumptions and non‑physical structures common in prior docking methods (R2).  
- **Theoretical grounding:** The paper provides mathematical analysis of sample efficiency, approximation error, and theoretical derivations for the proposed objective (R1, R2, R5).  
- **Simple yet effective energy‑based loss:** The relaxation stage uses an explicit energy term that is reasonable and greatly improves the generation of energetically favorable poses (R1, R2).  
- **Two‑stage design:** Separating docking into manifold docking (approximate pose) and relaxation (refinement) effectively boosts PB‑Valid to 73% while maintaining reasonable computational costs (R4, R5).  
- **Strong empirical results:** FlexDock outperforms prior methods across multiple metrics on PDBBind. The experiments are extensive and include ablation studies that verify each component (R2, R4, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing citations and limited related‑work discussion:** The introduction lacks necessary citations (e.g., line 43 “relearn protein folding”). The related work section does not discuss the differences / advantages of the proposed method over prior work (R1, R3).  
- **Unclear methodological presentation:** The derivation of Eq.3 and its connection to the actual optimization in Eq.4 (and the lower bound) is not well explained. Also, line 182 appears to describe Eq.3 as a lower bound of Eq.2, which might be a typo. The link between theory and practical implementation is insufficiently established (R1, R3).  
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** The choice of thresholds (c_dock for coupling, c_relax for relaxation) is not analyzed in detail; sensitivity and trade‑offs are unclear. There is no principled way to select these cutoffs, and a simple RMSD‑based cutoff may limit generalization to complex conformational changes (R1, R3, R4).  
- **Coupling filtering discards large‑change examples:** The coupling q(x0,x1) = q0(x0)q1(x1)I[c(x0,x1)<c_dock] discards apo‑holo pairs where conformational change upon binding exceeds the cutoff. The model’s performance on test examples with large changes is not reported, and the high all‑atom RMSD<1Å success rate (41.7%) may be driven by proteins that barely change upon binding (R3).  
- **No significant RMSD improvement despite known pocket:** The method assumes prior knowledge of the binding pocket yet does not show meaningful improvement in RMSD (especially All‑Atom RMSD). This suggests the manifold docking module may not substantially advance approximate pose prediction (R2).  
- **Lack of detailed PB‑Valid analysis:** It is unclear whether the PB‑Valid improvement is due to intramolecular or intermolecular components of the energy function. A component‑wise analysis is missing (R2).  
- **Complexity tradeoff not quantified:** The computational cost (memory, runtime scaling with protein/ligand size) and the overhead of the coupling distribution / discriminator vs. standard flow matching are not reported (R4).  
- **Blind docking not supported:** The method is limited to pocket‑based docking; whether it can perform blind docking is not addressed (R2).  
- **Data leakage concern:** The PDBBind train/test splits used may have significant data leakage (Corso et al. 2024). It is possible that the majority of successful predictions (e.g., 41% all‑atom RMSD<1Å) overlap with training data (R3).  
- **Sampling efficiency and runtime ambiguity:** How many samples are required on average until a confidence threshold is exceeded? It is not clear whether the “Runtime” reported in Table 1 accounts for this, and the relationship between effective sample size (ESS) and the c_dock cutoff would be informative (R3).  
- **Alternative relaxation baselines:** Traditional (non‑learning) energy minimization methods (e.g., Rosetta, force‑field relaxation as in PoseBusters) are not compared. A direct comparison of PB‑Valid and runtime with these approaches, and with the proposed learned relaxation, is missing (R1, R5).  
- **Extreme-case behavior:** It is not shown whether UFM succeeds when apo and holo structures differ substantially (e.g., all apo structures for a protein exceed the RMSD cutoff and are filtered out). Consider mixing traditional and unbalanced flows with a ratio during training (R4, R5).  
- **Additional missing details:** The y‑axis of Figure 4 needs a clearer label (e.g., “% of passed predictions”). The distribution of Cα RMSD between apo and holo structures in the training and test sets is not reported. The meaning of “relative weights” in “different conformational states … their relative weights are often notably altered” is not explained (R3, R5).  
- **Lack of comparison with AlphaFold3:** A direct comparison of FlexDock with AF3 on the PB‑Valid metric is desired (R5).  
- **Trajectory physicality:** Whether the protein states along the flow trajectory are physically realistic is not addressed (R3).
