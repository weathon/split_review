## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LoRAM, a memory-efficient LoRA training scheme that trains on a pruned (small) model and then recovers and uses the low-rank matrices with the original (large) model for inference. An offline alignment step (minimal-cost continual pre-training) reduces discrepancies between pruned and original models. LoRAM is evaluated across various pruning strategies, model sizes (LLaMA-2, LLaMA-3.1 up to 70B), and tasks, showing that it can replace heavy GPU requirements (e.g., 20G HBM instead of A100-80G for LoRA training on 70B). The method is complementary with quantization (QLoRAM). Reviewers generally find the topic important and the experiments thorough, but raise concerns about novelty, fairness of comparisons, missing cost/throughput analysis, and lack of evidence for the “train small, infer large” intuition.

### Strengths
- Addresses the relevant challenge of enabling LLM fine-tuning on lower-memory devices; LoRAM enables training a 70B model on a GPU with only 20GB HBM, an impressive result (R1, R4).
- The introduction of an alignment phase to handle pruning inconsistencies is conceptually innovative (R1) and helps offset pruning drawbacks (R2). Some reviewers note the method is easy to follow and well-structured with helpful visuals (R2, R4, R5).
- Extensive experiments across various pruning algorithms (unstructured, semi-structured, structured), model sizes (7B–70B), and tasks (e.g., Alpaca, multiple downstream tasks) illustrate LoRAM’s performance broadly (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- The method is complementary with other memory reduction techniques like quantization (QLoRAM) and different pruning strategies, showing promising synergy (R4, R5).
- Reviewer 5 (score 8, high confidence) highlights the method as easy but effective, with potential for sparsity-based approaches in this field. Reviewer 3 (score 3, high confidence) is more critical, acknowledging thorough experiments but arguing the novelty is limited.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and incremental contribution**: The core idea (pruning during training, recovering for inference) is seen as an incremental step over existing methods (R1). Reviewer 3 (score 3, confidence 5) argues that LoRAM essentially combines existing pruning techniques with LoRA, and the claim of reducing trainable parameters is misleading: for unstructured pruning there is no reduction in trainable parameters; for structured pruning the reduction comes from smaller model dimensions, not from changing LoRA itself. Reviewer 1 also notes the lack of fundamental novelty.
- **Unfair or incomplete comparisons**:
  - Missing comparisons to standard LoRA without pruning on the same model, which would provide a performance upper bound and quantify the degradation due to pruning/recovery (R1, R2, R4). Several reviewers insist including baseline fine-tuning of the same original model with standard LoRA (or full fine-tuning) to show the trade-off between memory savings and accuracy (R2, R4).
  - Comparisons across model families (e.g., LLaMA-3.1-8/70B vs. LLaMA-3.2-3B) are questioned as inappropriate because different-size models have different capabilities; comparisons should be within the same model (R2).
  - No baseline using the same total training time/compute as LoRAM (including alignment phase) for full fine-tuning on the original model. This is crucial to determine if gains are from the method or simply from reduced training time (R4).
  - Only compared to LoRA and “w/o FT”; need baselines such as the pruning method alone (without LoRA) to isolate the effect of after-pruning LoRA training (R4).
  - Missing comparison of “infer small” vs “infer large”: the paper assumes benefits of inferring on the original large model over inferring on the pruned model, but no experimental evidence (accuracy, latency) supports this choice (R5). Reviewer 5 (score 8) considers this a major omission.
- **Missing cost and throughput analysis**:
  - The paper lacks a breakdown of computational cost for the offline alignment phase (FLOPs, memory, time) and for the online low-rank training phase (latency, throughput) compared to standard LoRA (R2, R4, R5). Peak memory savings are reported but not wall-clock time or tokens/second, making it hard to assess practical benefits (R4). Reviewer 5 asks specifically about training throughput given that updating pruned parameters seems time-consuming.
  - The multi-stage process (pruning, alignment, fine-tuning, recovery) may involve trade-offs in latency not addressed (R2).
- **Reproducibility**: Code is not released (R4).
- **Presentation and clarity**:
  - Frequent grammatical errors, inconsistent terminology, and formatting issues detract from readability (R1, who gave Presentation score 1). Other reviewers found presentation clear (R2, R3, R5), indicating disagreement.
  - Typo in Table 3: “0.3415” should be “34.15” (R4).
- **Evidence for underlying intuition**: The method is based on “neurons with low training utility but inference-importance.” No theoretical analysis or empirical evidence supports this phenomenon or explains the property of those unchanged weights (R5).
- **Unexplained or missing results**:
  - After fine-tuning, structured pruning outperforms semi-structured and unstructured, contradicting prior findings (e.g., SparseGPT, Wanda) where unstructured shows least accuracy degradation. The paper does not explain this (R3).
  - Instability for QLoRAM-Rand and QLoRAM-Stru on the 70B model on Alpaca (Figures 3 & 4) is not explained (R4).
  - No discussion of cases where LoRAM underperforms standard LoRA; could there be scenarios where it does not win? (R1)
  - Effect of alignment corpus size and choice is not explored (R1).
  - Consistency of improvements across different tasks is not analyzed (R1).
  - No grid search details for hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, epochs) for baselines vs. LoRAM (R2).
  - Whether the method works for domain-specific fine-tuning is not addressed (R2).
