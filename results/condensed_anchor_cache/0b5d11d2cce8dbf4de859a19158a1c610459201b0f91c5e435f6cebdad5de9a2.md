## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper formulates the novel “learning transfer problem” – transferring a source network’s training trajectory to a differently initialized target network – and proposes two algorithms (GMT and FGMT) that match gradients sequentially along the trajectory via permutation symmetry (Git Re‑basin). Experiments on Cars and CUB with CNN/MLP architectures show that the transferred parameters achieve non‑trivial accuracy before any fine‑tuning and accelerate subsequent training compared to starting from scratch. All three reviewers acknowledge the novelty of the problem and the straightforward, efficient method, but raise concerns about limited experimental scope, missing computational cost analyses, insufficient theoretical validation, and unclear motivation.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem and approach.** The learning transfer problem is original, interesting, and has broad potential in foundation‑model and ensemble training. The gradient‑matching algorithm that exploits permutation symmetry is clean and efficient (requires only tens of gradient computations and lightweight linear assignment).
- **Theoretical grounding.** The method is backed by a theoretical formulation (Theorem 3.1, Lemma 3.2) and a clear mathematical presentation.
- **Empirical demonstration of faster training.** Transferred parameters show non‑trivial accuracy before any direct training and, after fine‑tuning, converge significantly faster than training from scratch.
- **Good presentation.** The writing is clear, the mathematics well‑notated, and the problem well motivated. Experimental details are generally well documented.

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited experimental scope.**  
   - All experiments are on Cars and CUB (small‑scale datasets). There is no evaluation on larger or more complex datasets (e.g., SUN397, iNaturalist2017) to assess generalizability to real‑world scenarios (R1, R3).  
   - The architectures tested are relatively small; there is no demonstration on larger models (e.g., foundation‑scale networks) or on architectures beyond feed‑forward MLPs, such as Transformers (R1, R2).  
   - Fine‑tuning experiments assume the same number of classes as pre‑training, which does not reflect the realistic fine‑tuning scenario (different class counts, task‑specific heads) (R2).

2. **Missing computational cost analysis.**  
   - No systematic comparison of the cost of GMT/FGMT with standard training or fine‑tuning; the scaling behaviour with network size and number of time steps T is not quantified (e.g., O(N²) for backprop, O(N³) for the Hungarian algorithm) (R1, R2).  
   - The choice of T (number of trajectory samples) is not studied; a sensitivity analysis is needed because cost is proportional to T (R2).

3. **Insufficient validation of theoretical claims.**  
   - Assumption (P), Theorem 3.1, and Lemma 3.2 are presented theoretically but lack empirical verification (e.g., demonstrate that they hold under realistic training conditions) (R2).  
   - The explanation that a linearised trajectory is more stable because the actual trajectory contains noisy information is given without theoretical or empirical support (R3).

4. **Lack of motivation and justification.**  
   - The paper does not adequately justify why forcing the target network to follow the source’s trajectory is beneficial; the argument that it reduces cost is not well supported. The analysis in Section 4.3 appears post‑hoc (R3).  
   - The claim that architecture affects trajectory similarity more than the dataset needs testing on a wider variety of architectures and datasets to be conclusive (R3).

5. **Unclear experimental details.**  
   - The “Generalization ability” part in Section 4.3 is poorly explained: the figure, the number of initialization‑fine‑tuning combinations, and the meaning of the reported validation accuracies (≈ 72% for ImageNet‑Full, ≈ 50% for ImageNet‑10%) are ambiguous (R2).  
   - The performance drop in Figures 5d‑5f is acknowledged but not analysed (R2).

6. **Missing ensemble evaluation.**  
   - No experiment compares ensemble performance achieved by GMT/FGMT with that of a traditional ensemble; concerns about limited diversity of transferred networks are raised (R2, R3).

7. **Minor issues.**  
   - Typos: “strength” → “strengthen” (Introduction); “generalizes poorly than” → “generalizes poorly compared to” (Section 4.3).  
   - On page 5, make explicit where the proof of Lemma 3.2 appears in the appendix.  
   - On page 6, “such a linearly interpolated trajectory satisfies …”.

8. **Additional questions raised that overlap with weaknesses.**  
   - How does the method perform on unrelated tasks? (R1, R3)  
   - Does data augmentation violate the “same dataset” assumption? (R2)  
   - How does the choice of optimizer (e.g., Adam vs SGD) affect the gradient‑matching step? (R2)  
   - In the landscape analysis (Figure 7), would the FGMT‑only or permuted‑source+FT parameters be placed? (R2)  
   - Should the parameter‑selection step (Figure 5) be included in the pseudocode? (R3)
