## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates hallucination in large vision-language models (LVLMs) through causal mediation analysis with counterfactual edits on LLaVA-7B and MiniGPT-4 using COCO and MM-Vet. It finds that multi-head attention (MHA) modules contribute more to hallucination than MLPs, and identifies specific “hallucination heads” concentrated in middle/deeper layers that show strong attention bias toward text tokens and change slowly during instruction tuning. Two mitigation methods are proposed: a training-free adaptive deactivation of hallucination heads during decoding and a targeted fine-tuning approach. Experiments report up to 1.7× reduction in hallucination rate on COCO captioning for LLaVA-v1.5-7B, outperforming existing baselines.

### Strengths
- The analysis is systematic and step-by-step; the identification of hallucination heads is novel and well-motivated. (R1, R3, R4)
- The observation that only specific attention heads contribute to hallucination, and the additional analysis (Fig 5) showing their attention patterns are similar to the base language model, are particularly interesting. (R3)
- The training-free method does not require expensive decoding time; both approaches are simple and effective. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Results are shown on multiple datasets (COCO, Nocaps) and multiple models (LLaVA-7B, MiniGPT-4), and are promising. (R1, R3)
- The paper is the first to analyze the attention map of output text tokens in LVLMs against the underlying LLM, which is enlightening. (R4)
- The two proposed methods—decoder-level restriction and SFT—are well-grounded in the analysis and perform well. (R4)
- Some findings, though considered obvious by one reviewer (e.g., MHA more critical than FFN, language bias), were found insightful and well-justified by others. (R2 vs. R3/R4)

### Weaknesses
1. **Narrow scope of hallucination type.** The method is evaluated almost exclusively on object-level hallucination using CHAIR metrics. It does not address other common forms of hallucination such as counting errors, attribute errors, positional inaccuracies, or numerical discrepancies. (R1, R3)  
   - Equation (1) and (2) may only detect object existence, not counting; the proposed methods may not identify or mitigate heads causing counting hallucinations. (R1)

2. **Limited model scale and architecture coverage.** Experiments are conducted only on 7B-parameter models (LLaVA-7B, MiniGPT-4). It is unclear whether hallucination heads exist similarly in larger models (e.g., 70B) and whether the training-free method would transfer. (R3, R4)  
   - The training-free method was tested on too few model families; results on additional models like InstructBLIP or other architectures are needed. (R4)

3. **Insufficient evaluation of generation quality trade-offs.** The paper reports BLEU scores and CHAIR reductions but lacks human evaluations of fluency, coherence, and descriptiveness. The impact of reducing text-token reliance in hallucination heads on overall language quality is not thoroughly examined. (R3)  
   - Algorithm 1 deactivates the entire text attention weights for hallucination heads, which may be too aggressive: BLEU scores drop significantly in Figure 6(c). Yet accuracy on general tasks sometimes improves—this inconsistency is not explained. (R1)  
   - The trade-off between hallucination reduction and generation quality (e.g., descriptiveness) is missing. (R3)

4. **Missing details and clarity on methodology.**  
   - Section 4.3 does not explain the CHAIR metrics or the evaluated dataset. (R1)  
   - Tables 1 and 2 should include a column indicating which methods are training-free vs. fine-tuned. (R1)  
   - The number of samples and data split used to compute Equation (1) are not specified. (R1)  
   - The time required to compute head scores via Equation (2) is not reported. (R1)  
   - The difference between downscaling text attention weights and upscaling image attention weights is unclear because softmax normalization makes them equivalent (Figure 6(a) vs. 6(b)). (R1)  
   - Minor writing issues, e.g., “Our Method Run Fast in Generation” could be rephrased for clarity. (R3)

5. **Failure to cite and compare with related recent work.** Several relevant methods are omitted from the experiments: (R2)  
   - A. Deng et al., *Seeing is Believing: Mitigating Hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models via CLIP-Guided Decoding* (arXiv 2024).  
   - F. Liu et al., *Mitigating Hallucination in Large Multi-Modal Models via Robust Instruction Tuning* (ICLR 2024).  
   - J. Zhang et al., *Reflective Instruction Tuning: Mitigating Hallucinations in Large Vision-Language Models* (ECCV 2024).

6. **Unanswered questions about the mechanism and generalizability.**  
   - Why do certain hallucination heads exhibit slow changes during instruction tuning? What factors cause this “laziness”? (R3)  
   - For VQA tasks where the output is a single token (e.g., A/B/C/D), does the hallucination head pattern still exist? If the SFT method is used, would it be applicable to VQA? (R4)  
   - Why are hallucination heads concentrated in the middle and deep layers? The complete distribution across all 32 heads is not shown in the supplementary. (R4)  
   - The analysis only considers output tokens for attention patterns. Is it possible to distinguish hallucination heads by comparing their behavior on system tokens, image tokens, prompt tokens, and output tokens? (R4)  
   - One reviewer questions the reasonableness of observing attention heads at the decoder terminal for LLMs, since LLMs alone cannot generate image captions. (R4)  
   - The fine-tuning approach is found to be less effective than the training-free method, but the paper does not provide in-depth analysis of why. (R2)

7. **Disagreement among reviewers on novelty.** One reviewer considered some findings obvious (e.g., MHA contributes more than FFN because of larger parameter count, language bias is a known cause of hallucination), while others viewed the analysis as novel and insightful. (R2 vs. R3)
