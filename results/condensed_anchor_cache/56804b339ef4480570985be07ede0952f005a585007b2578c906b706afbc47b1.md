## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper analyzes two recent diversification methods for OOD generalization (DivDis and DBAT), which generate multiple hypotheses that agree on labeled data but disagree on unlabeled data, then pick one at test time. Through theory, toy experiments, and real-world-data experiments, the authors show that: (1) diversification is highly sensitive to the unlabeled data distribution; (2) the choice of learning algorithm (architecture, pretraining) is crucial and can cause up to a 20% absolute accuracy drop; (3) the optimal learning algorithm and unlabeled data are co-dependent; and (4) increasing the number of hypotheses K does not alleviate these pitfalls. All reviewers found the analysis original and the problem important, but opinions differed on significance, presentation clarity, and the practicality of the findings.

### Strengths
- The paper dives into the intricacies of the diverse hypothesis generation problem and illustrates how success simultaneously depends on all variables; this message should be communicated more frequently.
- Proposition #1 is illuminating: it shows that DivDis and DBAT select different diverse hypotheses depending on the agreement with the true hypothesis on the unlabeled data, and synthetic experiments (MNIST/CIFAR) support theoretical findings nicely (Reviewer 1).
- The key result—that the success of diversification depends on the inductive bias of the model architecture and that the same architecture may not work well for different test sets—is well articulated (Reviewer 2).
- The paper does a good job of articulating multiple issues such as sensitivity to unlabeled data, co-dependence, and the ineffectiveness of increasing K (Reviewer 2).
- The work is original, well written and clear (Reviewer 3); no errors or incorrect conclusions were found (Reviewer 3); valid experiments prove the rationality of the views (Reviewer 4).
- Investigating diversification theoretically from different aspects and providing key results is valuable (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and terminology**: Some reviewers found the presentation confusing and dense. The term “spurious ratio” is poorly named—it is literally the accuracy with respect to the true hypothesis \(h^*\), which is not spurious; a name like “agreement ratio” would be much clearer (Reviewer 1). The plots on the left side of Figure 2 do not seem to agree with the description of the synthetic problem in Section 4.1, making interpretation hard (Reviewer 1). In contrast, Reviewer 3 found the paper clear; this disagreement in readability should be noted.
- **Figure 2 confusion**: For the synthetic problem, \(h^*\) is defined such that instances with \(x_1>0\) are positive, but the plot shows points to the left as positive (Class 1). Moreover, hyperplanes are typically defined by their normal, so \(h^*(x) = h(x;0)\), not \(h(x;\pi/4)\) (Reviewer 1).
- **Theoretical assumptions**: Proposition 2 assumes infinite capacity of the learning algorithm, but other parts of the paper highlight inductive biases that imply less-than-infinite capacity. Can the research become more constructive by making statistical assumptions about \(D_u\) (which can be any OOD distribution)? (Reviewer 1)
- **DivDis hyperparameter scaling**: Results for DivDis in Table 1 (Section 5.2) for increasing \(K\) are not convincing because \(\alpha\) needs to increase as \(O(K^2)\) to avoid making regularization too strong and dwarfing the empirical risk. The text seems to use a fixed \(\alpha\), which would needlessly harm DivDis’ performance as \(K\) grows (Reviewer 1).
- **Practical relevance and significance**: The paper focuses on pitfalls of two ICLR 2023 papers (DivDis and DBAT); these are too recent to know how significant it is to critique them (Reviewer 3). The applications (MNIST-CIFAR concatenations, Waterbirds-CC) are contrived—constructed to have spurious correlations—rather than arising naturally. Office-Home is somewhat better, but the sensible thing would be to train on all four sources together; a real scenario (e.g., distribution shift over time) would be stronger (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 noted that the experimental setup is simple and more complicated diversification losses/approaches should be included.
- **Unsurprising findings**: The findings (e.g., importance of inductive bias) follow from No Free Lunch theorems and bias-variance tradeoff known since the 1990s; illustrating them for diversification seems more like common sense than a major advance (Reviewer 3).
- **Assumption about stage 2 (disambiguation)**: The paper assumes access to an oracle that chooses the best hypothesis in the second stage. It is not clear that stage 2 will succeed if stage 1 succeeds; more background is needed on why we can assume that stage 2 is doable (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear main takeaway**: Should we not use diversification algorithms? What is the better alternative? A more constructive message would be to identify the axes on which model selection should be done if some summary statistics of the test set (e.g., spurious ratio) can be provided in advance. Can the authors set up a model selection experiment where the spurious ratio of the unseen test set is known and the test set is simulated? Would it always lead to the correct model? (Reviewer 2)
- **Risk of model selection**: If we do model selection over multiple architectures and diversity algorithms, the cross-validation results may not generalize to the test set. What summary statistics of the test set would be needed? (Reviewer 2)
- **Need for more models**: The conclusions might need support from more models beyond DivDis and DBAT (Reviewer 4).
- **Motivation and related work**: More discussion of the unique advantages of diversification for OOD and why studying its components is important is needed (Reviewer 4).
- **Typos**: Page 5: “function. And the training distribution” should be “function, and the training distribution” (don’t start a new sentence). Page 9: “This is in consistence with” should be “This is consistent with” (Reviewer 3).
