## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates using training loss to predict out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, proposing that preserving consistent prediction variance across training and testing distributions is key and suggesting architectural adjustments to achieve that. Reviewers find the topic interesting and the presentation clear, but have substantial concerns. One reviewer (score 6) views the approach as novel and appreciates the thorough hyper-parameter investigation across corruption and domain-shift benchmarks. The other three reviewers (score 3) raise serious issues: the claimed novelty is overstated given prior work, the theoretical derivation ignores the gradient term and makes unsubstantiated assumptions, the experimental design does not truly test OOD (e.g., CIFAR-10 subsets, not genuine distribution shifts), the proposed adjustments are standard and may just mitigate overfitting rather than improve OOD generalization, and the results show performance drops that suggest capacity limitation. Several reviewers request evaluations on standard OOD benchmarks (PACS, ColoredMNIST, VLCS, WILDS), consideration of different shift types (diversity vs. correlation), practical comparisons with unmodified architectures, and integration into hyper-parameter optimization pipelines.

### Strengths
- The attempt to bridge training loss with OOD test performance is interesting and practically motivated (R1, R3, R4).
- The paper is well written and easy to follow, with clear figures (R2, R3, R4).
- The study examines conditions for a linear relationship between training and test losses and proposes variance-preserving architectures (R1).
- A thorough investigation of hyper-parameters (learning rate, weight decay, batch size, data augmentation) is conducted across corruption and domain-shift benchmarks (R1, one reviewer more positive).
- The paper focuses on model selection from the perspective of network architecture, a worthwhile direction (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and prior work:** The claim that the research question (whether training loss can predict OOD generalization) is unexplored is inaccurate. Prior work (e.g., [1],[2]) has extensively studied this. The approach from variance preservation and network architecture is also not new (e.g., [3],[4]). The paper insufficiently compares with and builds upon these works (R2).
- **Theoretical gaps:** Ignoring the gradient term \(\nabla_f \mathcal{L}\) in equations (2) and (3) is inappropriate—this term depends on \(f\) and \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and affects \(\text{Cov}(J,J')\) and \(\text{Var}(J)\), making the conclusion incomplete (R2). The derivation relies on a Taylor expansion around the mean prediction, which may be inaccurate when the loss is highly non-linear and predictions vary significantly. The assumption that prediction variance remains consistent across distributions is not justified; distribution shifts typically change model confidence and prediction patterns. Equation (4) requires near-1 correlation between predicted labels of training and testing samples, which seems unattainable (R3). More theoretical justification for the variance-preserving architecture is needed (R1).
- **Experimental design:** The CIFAR-10 experiment (random subset of training set, tested on CIFAR-10 test set) does **not** constitute an OOD scenario—the distributions are identical. The reduced training size causes overfitting, not a genuine distribution shift. This undermines the OOD claims of the paper (R3). The paper should use standard OOD benchmarks such as PACS, ColoredMNIST, VLCS (R4), and WILDS (R1). The choice of datasets over common OOD benchmarks is not justified (R4).
- **Shift types:** The paper does not consider different kinds of OOD shifts—specifically, diversity shifts and correlation shifts (including spurious correlations) [1],[2]. It should either analyze both or justify focusing only on diversity shifts (R1, R4).
- **Overfitting vs. variance preservation:** The results in Figures 2-6 show typical overfitting patterns. The proposed adjustment increases correlation primarily by preventing overfitting, which could be achieved with simpler regularization or data augmentation. The performance drop in Table 2 suggests the method limits model capacity rather than genuinely improving OOD generalization (R3).
- **Practical applicability and comparisons.** (a) It is unclear whether the proposed architecture brings benefits or drawbacks compared to a normal architecture—performance comparisons before and after modifications are needed (R4). (b) The method should be tested in a hyper-parameter optimization pipeline on larger datasets (e.g., PACS, ImageNet) and compared against other HP optimization baselines to demonstrate practical advantages (R4). (c) The architectural adjustments proposed (e.g., variance-preserving designs) are already commonly used in neural network training and do not provide new insights (R2). (d) No exploration is done on techniques to improve OOD performance prediction without modifying existing architectures (R4).
