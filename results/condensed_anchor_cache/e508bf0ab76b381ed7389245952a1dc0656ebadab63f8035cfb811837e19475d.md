## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Semantic-Aware Representation Learning (SARL) for continual/lifelong learning, drawing inspiration from biological sparse coding. SARL employs sparse activations (via k-WTA) and object prototypes to capture semantic relationships between classes across tasks. It uses semantic-aware metric learning to cluster similar objects and separate dissimilar ones, alongside prototype regularization and consistency losses. Evaluated on Seq-CIFAR10, Seq-CIFAR100, and Seq-TinyImageNet under Class-Incremental and Generalized Class-Incremental Learning, the method reports strong performance in balancing plasticity and stability.

### Strengths
- **Motivation and readability**: Multiple reviewers (R1, R2, R4) found the paper well-motivated, well-structured, and easy to follow. R1 specifically noted it is “relatively well-motivated.” The combination of ideas (sparsity, prototypes, metric learning) is considered interesting (R3).
- **Innovative inspiration**: R4 highlights the novel biological inspiration and the principled approach to incorporating semantic relationships into representation learning.
- **Effective clustering and balance**: R4 notes that SARL effectively clusters similar objects while separating dissimilar ones, achieving a valuable balance between knowledge retention and adaptation. This is validated across complex incremental learning scenarios (CIL and GCIL) with robust performance and minimized task interference (R4).
- **Strong performance**: R3 states “the performance results are very good.” R1 and R4 also report strong experimental outcomes.

### Weaknesses
- **Backbone modification and unfair advantage (R1)**: The backbone is altered (Section 3.3) by replacing ReLU with k-WTA to enforce sparse activations, which may increase similarities (Fig. 2) and potentially give the proposed method an advantage over baselines. Further investigation is needed to ensure a fair comparison.
- **Unjustified claims (R1)**: The claim (line 248) that “sparse activations further enhance the model’s ability to distinguish between similar and dissimilar objects” lacks rigorous quantitative analysis (e.g., inter/intra-class similarity metrics). The claim (line 284) that sparse coding “fosters stability … and mitigates the risk of forgetting important semantic relationships” is unsupported by direct experiments.
- **Inconsistent notation and typos (R1, R2)**: Equation (3) uses ambiguous set notation (e.g., \( S_c \) and \( C^t \) should be sets). Interval notation is inconsistent (\(0:t\) vs. \(\{0:t\}\)). Vectors are not visually distinguished from scalars (e.g., \(a_i\), \(o_b^c\)). R2 notes that below Eq. (3) “\(C^{0: t}\)” should be “\(C^{\{0: t\}}\)”. A typo in Appendix A (extra “I”) is also flagged.
- **Conflict with CIL problem setting (R2)**: Storing samples from previous tasks to update the model seems inconsistent with typical CIL assumptions (previous tasks’ data inaccessible). The authors should explain this discrepancy and potential information leakage.
- **Lack of ablation / hyperparameter sensitivity (R1, R4)**: The objective function (Eq. 7) has five loss components (regularization on prototypes, consistency regularization, semantic-aware metric loss, two cross-entropy losses), yet no ablation study is provided. R1 finds the contribution of each component unclear. R4 states the sensitivity analysis on loss weights is insufficient (Appendix not enough); a proper analysis is expected.
- **Limited recent baselines (R1, R2)**: R1 notes missing recent SOTA methods: Meta Continual Learning (ICLR 2024), Interactive Continual Learning (CVPR 2024), Bilateral Memory Consolidation (CVPR 2023). R2 adds that methods based on pre-trained models (e.g., L2P, DualPrompt) are overlooked. Authors should clarify their comparison perspective if those are deemed unfair.
- **Insufficient evaluation metrics (R2)**: Only average accuracy is reported. Metrics such as final top-1 accuracy and final forgetting should be included.
- **Computational overhead and memory (R1, R4)**: R1 asks for clarification on memory requirements (prototypes, logits from previous model state beyond the buffer). R4 notes that object prototypes and semantic-aware metric learning may increase computational overhead compared to baselines like ER; no efficiency discussion is provided.
- **Limited backbone diversity (R4)**: Experiments rely solely on ResNet-18; comparisons with other backbones (e.g., ViT) are missing, which would broaden the evaluation.
- **Novelty concerns (R3 - minority reviewer, score 3)**: One reviewer found novelty limited, arguing that deep neural networks inherently build semantic representations and that SARL appears to be a solid combination of well-established techniques. The reviewer suggests the paper may be more suitable for a different venue and requests clearer motivation for the novelty of Eqs. (1)–(6) and explicit identification of which components are novel versus standard.
- **Introduction too generic (R3)**: The introduction (lines 054–055) insufficiently characterizes limitations, and a toy problem or 2D/3D visualization (e.g., t-SNE) demonstrating the effect of semantic structure on forgetting is recommended. R3 cites Wen et al. (ECCV 2016) and Pernici et al. (CVPR 2019 workshops) as examples.
- **Unanswered reviewer questions treated as missing experiments**:
    - R1: Given \(L_2\)-normalized object representations, why use simple mean (Eq. 1) instead of a spherical manifold? Why use \(L_2\) distance in Eqs. 4 and 5 instead of similarity (sim)?
    - R1: Experimental details missing: number of steps/epochs for the warm-up stage; comparison of sparse backbone vs. regular backbone.
    - R4: In the ablation (Table 3), why is the first row performance so low (is it a traditional ResNet-18 classifier)? Why are improvements marginal on Seq-CIFAR10 compared to larger gains on Seq-CIFAR100 and Seq-TinyImageNet?
