## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers are divided: two reviewers (R2, R3) see merit in FoRA’s parameter reduction and clear presentation, but two others (R1, R4) find the contribution limited due to incremental novelty, missing comparisons, mathematical confusion, and experimental weaknesses. Reviewer confidence ranges from 3 to 5.

### Strengths
- **Provision of experiment details and code** is highly commendable (R1).
- **Trainable parameters are significantly fewer** than LoRA for comparable or better performance; the method is generally applicable across tasks (R2, minority positive view; R4 acknowledges decent performance with few parameters).
- **Paper is well‑structured and presented clearly** (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Incremental novelty**: FoRA is a straightforward combination of LoRA and FourierFT; the addition of FFT is effective but not an important advancement (R1, R3, R4). The closely related FourierFT method is not discussed, raising novelty questions (R4).
- **Missing comparisons**: AdaLoRA (which addresses rank sensitivity) is neither cited nor compared (R1). FourierFT is not compared despite strong similarity (R4).
- **Computational efficiency concerns**: Table 5 shows that memory and time costs are not better than LoRA, even though trainable parameters are reduced. This undermines the practical advantage over LoRA (R2, R3).
- **Mathematical description and notation are highly problematic**:
  - Dimensions of matrices (e.g., \(F\), \(\tilde{A}\)) conflict between the text above equation (3), equation (2), and Figure 2. The Fourier transform in (3) uses incorrect denominator coefficients (R4).
  - The construction of \(\tilde{B}\) using the “identical” procedure does not account for different dimensions (R4).
  - “Spatial auxiliary matrix” \(A’\) is never defined (R4).
  - Notation: boldface symbols like \(F_{j,k}, S_{p,q}\) denote scalars, creating confusion (R4).
  - In Figure 2, the FoRA rank \(r’\) appears much larger than the LoRA rank \(r\), yet lines 152 and 182 assign the same rank; this discrepancy is unexplained (R3).
- **Flawed or unsubstantiated claims**:
  - Claim that \(A\) and \(B\) can have “unbounded ranks” is false; the rank is at most \(r\) (R4).
  - Claim that FoRA can construct a high‑rank gradient approximation with few parameters is mathematically flawed: LoRA can learn *any* rank‑\(r\) approximation, while FoRA learns only a subset (R4). No explanation is given why that subset is good (R4).
  - Claim of “optimal” efficiency‑performance tradeoff is unsupported by any optimization problem or evidence (R4).
  - Abstract’s suggestion that LoRA’s low‑rank structure limits expressiveness is contradictory, since LoRA’s rank is adjustable (R3).
  - Citations to Candès and Baraniuk (line 180) are invalid because those works fit the sparsity pattern to data, whereas FoRA chooses it randomly (R4).
- **Experimental results are not convincing**:
  - Figure 1 does not support the claim that LoRA’s performance increases monotonically with rank; the reported performance looks almost random, undermining the paper’s motivation (R4).
  - In Table 1, FoRA wins only 3 out of 14 contests (R4).
  - No significant advantage over FourierFT in terms of parameter count or accuracy (R1).
  - Missing experimental details: dimensions \(d_1, d_2\) and FoRA rank \(r\) for the GLUE experiments are not provided (R4).
- **Lack of specific motivation and detailed explanations**: The paper uses broad claims without concrete details on why the Fourier‑basis subset is effective (R3, R4). Questions about Equations (2) and (3) and whether FoRA is a two‑layer low‑rank representation remain unanswered (R3).
