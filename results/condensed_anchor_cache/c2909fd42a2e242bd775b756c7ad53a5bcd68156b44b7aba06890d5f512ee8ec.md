## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes ProgressCounts, an LLM-driven framework that generates progress functions to estimate task progress, then discretizes progress into bins and computes count-based intrinsic rewards. The goal is to automate reward engineering for RL with fewer samples. The approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Bi-DexHands benchmark with 20× fewer reward function samples than EUREKA. One reviewer (R2, score 8) is notably more positive, praising the novelty and thorough ablation studies; the other three reviewers (scores 5) are more critical, raising concerns about motivation, comparison fairness, generalizability, and significance.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-organized, easy to follow, and the motivation for using LLMs for automated reward engineering is compelling (R1, R3).
- The core idea of reducing reward generation to estimating task progress and linking LLM-generated progress functions to count-based intrinsic rewards is original and interesting (R2, R3, R4).
- The use of domain-specific discretization within the projected subtask progression space is novel and yields promising results (R2).
- Experiments are thorough, with ablation studies providing valuable insights into each design component (R2).
- Strong empirical results: ProgressCounts outperforms manually designed dense rewards and existing automated reward generation methods on Bi-DexHands, achieving significant sample efficiency gains (20× fewer reward function samples) while requiring fewer environment interactions than EUREKA (R1, R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation for progress bins is unclear.** The rationale for mapping progress to bins rather than other representations is not articulated. A common principle in intrinsic reward design is that the representation space should effectively capture the essential aspects of the original observation space; it is not evident why progress bins are chosen over alternatives (R1). Similarly, the claim that progress-based bins are superior to simhash is not justified—simhash provides convergence guarantees by ensuring increasing intrinsic rewards correspond to increased visitation of the full state space (R1).
- **Selection method for the best progress function is missing.** The method for selecting among generated progress functions (Line 287–289) is not detailed, hindering reproducibility and comparison (R1).
- **Cumulative progress reward formulation is incorrect.** On Line 427, the authors suggest using cumulative progress as a step reward; reward shaping principles require the *difference* in progress, not the cumulative value (R1).
- **Incomplete comparisons.**  
    - In Figure 4, results for EUREKA are missing; it would be valuable to see how EUREKA’s performance improves with more samples (R2).  
    - Table 1 does not use the same number of trials for all methods. Since the best trial run is selected (Sec. 5.1), fair comparison demands identical trial counts (R3).  
    - The paper lacks comparison with other recent intrinsic reward techniques such as curiosity-driven or model-based approaches, leaving unclear whether the gains are specific to count-based rewards (R4).
- **Limited evaluation scope and generalizability concerns.**  
    - Experiments are confined to synthetic benchmarks (Bi-DexHands, MiniGrid) with simulated physics and simplified task structures; generalizability to real-world environments with sensor noise and unpredictable factors is uncertain (R4).  
    - Scalability to tasks with larger state-action spaces or more complex environments is not addressed; discretization into bins may lose information and the count-based approach could become computationally expensive (R4).
- **Dependence on LLMs introduces potential biases and practical limitations.**  
    - LLM-generated progress functions may inherit biases from pre‑training, especially in domains requiring physical reasoning or spatial relationships, and the lack of interpretability makes it hard to diagnose errors (R4).  
    - The method requires a pre-defined feature engineering library for each domain. Although claimed to be simple, this manual step demands domain expertise, limiting full automation and scalability (R3, R4).  
    - The claim that the feature library can be authored in a minute (L164) is bold, particularly for new domains without RL-expert guidance (R3).
- **Questions about MDP assumption and progress function design.**  
    - The BFS helper method for MiniGrid may require full access to the environment state, potentially breaking the MDP assumption (R3).  
    - How are the additional variables \([y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_k]\) used? (R2, R3)  
    - Did the authors try prompting the LLM to supply progress variables where progress strictly increases, thereby eliminating the need for additional variables? (R3)  
    - On which data is the range estimation of progress variables based? It seems several example rollouts (ideally successful) are needed (R3).
- **Presentation clarity issues.**  
    - The abstract’s first sentence should explicitly mention RL (R3).  
    - Provide the `Env` class: it is unclear whether the progress function has access to the same state as the policy or receives additional variables (R3).  
    - Supply hyper-parameters such as the learning rate for PPO (R3).  
    - In Figure 3 or its caption, be explicit about how many environment samples are used (R3).  
    - For the MiniGrid experiment, state the number of runs of ProgressCounts (R3).
- **Concerns about significance.**  
    The work largely follows the problem setting of EUREKA, which itself has yet to demonstrate value beyond being an interesting application of LLMs. Moreover, count-based exploration relies on training a policy over millions of timesteps, unlike earlier LLM-reward work that used model predictive control to directly translate reward specifications to actions (R3).
