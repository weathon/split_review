## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a causality-guided continual learning (CL) method for LLMs that uses a soft intervention to decouple model parameters from input data distributions, aiming to reduce catastrophic forgetting and reliance on spurious correlations under limited data. The approach combines noisy perturbation of parameters (via a KL divergence with a prior) and data augmentation, implemented on top of O-LoRA. Experiments on T5-large and Llama2 across multiple benchmarks show improvements over state-of-the-art CL baselines, especially with 100–500 samples per task. Reviewers are divided: one scores 6 (positive: novel, SOTA), two score 5 (mixed: well-motivated but missing ablations/details), and two score 3 (negative: weak causal justification, limited evaluation, and unclear novelty). All agree the method is interesting and shows promise under low-resource conditions, but significant concerns remain about clarity, completeness, and rigor.

### Strengths
- **Novel causal perspective on CL for LLMs.** The paper introduces a new way to frame continual learning through causal inference (soft intervention), which is underexplored in the LLM CL area. (R1, R4, R5)
- **Well-motivated with theoretical foundations.** The approach is grounded in variational inference and attempts to reduce dependency on spurious correlations, addressing a critical challenge. (R2, R4)
- **Outperforms several strong baselines.** The method achieves new SOTA results across multiple benchmarks (T5-large, LLaMA-2) and task orders, especially under limited data. (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5)
- **Comprehensive experimental setup.** Experiments cover multiple benchmarks, task orders, and sample sizes, with comparison to a range of existing methods. (R1, R4)
- **Innovative soft intervention balances forgetting and plasticity.** The soft intervention mechanism is praised as a way to retain past knowledge while adapting to new tasks. (R5 – minority more positive reviewer highlight)

### Weaknesses
- **Causal graph is pre-assumed and poorly explained.** The definitions of random variables (X, Y, Θ) are confusing—it is unclear whether X includes only input without labels, and how the ground-truth label Y should point to Θ in the causal graph. The criteria for applying the backdoor theorem are not justified, and other confounders (e.g., linguistic features, world knowledge) are ignored. Derivation of equation 4 and equation 6 from equation 5 (appendix) is insufficiently explained, despite being the paper’s key formulas. Step-by-step derivations or clearer explanations are needed. (R1, R3)
- **Method novelty is questionable.** The two core components—noisy perturbation of model parameters (similar to dropout/MixOut) and noisy perturbation of input data (data augmentation like back-translation)—are common regularization techniques. The paper does not adequately distinguish its approach from these standard methods or compare against them as baselines. (R4)
- **Missing ablation studies.** The paper lacks ablation of each component:
  - Performance of the contrastive method without O-LoRA (i.e., full finetuning) (R3)
  - Performance of O-LoRA + DA + CGCL (i.e., all components together vs. - each) (R3)
  - Performance with α set to zero to directly show α’s role in controlling I(Θ, X) and memory retention (R5)
  - Performance using only variational inference (without data augmentation) as a baseline (R2)
  - Results combining the proposed method with CL approaches other than O-LoRA (R2)
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and role of α unclear.** Sensitivity analysis shows the method is not highly dependent on α, even with small α. This raises doubt about whether the KL term in Eq. (4) is useful. It is unclear how to trade off more learning vs. less forgetting if the result is insensitive to α. (R4, R5)
- **Limited evaluation metrics and baseline selection.** The evaluation metrics are not clearly defined. Strong intervention baselines (e.g., “hard interventions”) are omitted, which are prominent in forgetting mitigation research. (R5) Additionally, classification tasks may not be the most appropriate for evaluating continual learning in LLMs, as instruction-tuned LLMs (e.g., Llama2-7B-instruct) can already perform many such tasks zero/few-shot; a more realistic setting would be learning newly occurred factual knowledge. (R1)
- **Insufficient analysis of task-level forgetting.** Only average performance metrics are reported, which can obscure task-specific forgetting. For long sequence CL (e.g., Task Order 6), the model’s performance on early tasks (e.g., Yelp) should be re-evaluated after each new task, using metrics like Forgetting Rate or Stability-Plasticity Trade-off. (R5)
- **Data scale is too small to be representative.** Experiments use only 100–500 samples per task. The method’s effectiveness on larger datasets (thousands or tens of thousands of samples) is untested. Comparisons with no training and large-scale training data are missing. (R1, R5)
- **Computational cost not analyzed.** The additional trainable parameters (e.g., trainable standard deviations for all LoRA weights?) and the overhead from data augmentation are not discussed. (R2)
- **Method description lacks detail.** For instance, it is unclear whether the noise added to weights uses a trainable standard deviation parameter for all weights in LoRA. (R2)
- **Claims about reducing spurious feature memorization are unsubstantiated.** The paper asserts that the method lessens memorization of spurious features, but provides no analysis (e.g., of attention weights or model predictions on training data) to support this claim. (R4)
- **Small sample sizes lack confidence intervals.** For results like Tables 3 and 4 (500 samples), standard deviations are reported, but confidence intervals or statistical significance tests should also be provided. (R3)
- **Minor issues:** Lines 698 and 700 contain a typo (“se” instead of “set”). (R2) The position of the paper as a causal ML method is not clearly stated, and prior causal ML literature is insufficiently cited. (R4)
