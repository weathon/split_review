## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically investigates the correlation between the essential Fourier frequencies required for neural network image classification (the network’s spectral bias/implicit bias) and the frequencies targeted by adversarial attacks. It uses the Fourier mask framework of Karantzas et al. to compute binary modulation masks that preserve classification, then extracts frequencies. To measure possibly non-linear correlation between the masks of original images and their adversarial counterparts, the authors propose a test based on intrinsic dimension (ID): a drop in ID of the concatenated vectors compared to when one set is shuffled indicates correlation. They apply this test to CIFAR-10 and Imagenette, reporting small but statistically significant drops in ID.

### Strengths
- **Original use of intrinsic dimension for correlation testing:** Using the collapse in ID between concatenated sets and shuffled sets as a test for non-linear correlation is a creative and novel approach that is relevant to the well-known problem of detecting correlations in high-dimensional data.
- **Compelling experimental design with shuffling:** The contrast between ID estimates for original vs. shuffled variables provides a clear signal for the presence or absence of correlation, and the idea is further exploited to demonstrate class-specific information in the masks.
- **Well-organized and clear presentation:** The paper is well-written, uses simple language, and avoids overly complicated mathematical exposition. Figures help with understanding, and the last section points to interesting future directions.
- **Important research topic:** The connection between implicit bias (spectral bias) and adversarial vulnerability is a timely and essential question for the machine learning community.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of rigorous theoretical foundation and formal definitions:** The core proposal is not formally defined, which hinders reproducibility and verification. Concepts such as “implicit bias,” “spectral bias,” and the relationship between them and the Fourier domain are only described as a “deep connection” without clear explanation. The literature review inadequately addresses these concepts and the existing work on frequency analysis of adversarial examples. No formal statement or expression of the proposed correlation test is provided.
- **Weak empirical evidence for the claimed correlation:** The reported correlation is not sharp. Cosine similarity in Table 2 is small, and the observed drops in intrinsic dimension are relatively small (e.g., 4–5) compared to shuffled baselines. Moreover, because adversarial perturbations are inherently small, some correlation between frequency masks is expected; it is unclear what beyond that can be concluded. A more comprehensive evaluation is needed, including non-linear correlation metrics, varying perturbation budgets, and analysis across different attack strengths.
- **Statistical concerns with the Z-test:** The Z-test used to assess significance of ID drops is questionable. It assumes a normal distribution and known population variance, but intrinsic dimension is non-negative, making normality unlikely. Additionally, the variance of ID estimates strongly depends on the dimensionality; applying a uniform threshold across different dimensions is inappropriate. The paper should acknowledge these limitations and consider alternatives (e.g., t-test, non-parametric tests, Poisson/Gamma distributions).
- **Method limited by reliance on known adversarial class:** The procedure for computing the Fourier mask (Equation 1) requires knowledge of the “adversarial class.” For untargeted attacks, it is unclear which class label is used; this is not stated. The paper also does not specify whether attacks are targeted or untargeted, nor does it define the adversarial class for the examples used.
- **Insufficient validation of the learned frequency masks:** The meaning and importance of the masks are not verified. For example, training a classifier solely on the masked features and comparing its accuracy to that of the original model would confirm whether the masks truly capture essential discriminative frequencies. Without such validation, it is unclear how well the masks represent the network’s spectral bias.
- **Scalability and practical limitations:** Already for the Imagenette dataset, the proposed method faces computational and reliability issues due to high dimensionality, limiting its practical applicability. The paper does not discuss how to scale the approach to larger, more realistic settings.
- **Unclear practical implications:** The paper does not articulate how the identified correlation can be leveraged to improve robustness or defense. It only reports empirical evidence without proposing new methods (e.g., adversarial training strategies, frequency-based defenses) or concrete future directions that build on the findings. The significance of the result for the adversarial robustness literature is therefore unclear.
- **Minor experimental discrepancy:** The ℓ∞ perturbation budget on CIFAR-10 is set to ε=0.01, which deviates from the standard value of 8/255 ≈ 0.03 used in the robust ML community (e.g., RobustBench). This should be justified or addressed.
- **Formatting issue:** The lack of spacing between paragraphs makes the paper harder to read and violates the conference LaTeX template instructions.

*Note: One reviewer (R1) was significantly more positive, rating the paper 5 and praising the novelty of the ID-based correlation test and the clarity of presentation. The other three reviewers rated the paper 3, citing the weaknesses above, with particular concerns about weak evidence, lack of theoretical grounding, and limited practical implications.*
