## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a variational framework for estimating the average dose-response function (ADRF) when a continuous treatment is observed with measurement error. The method treats the unobserved true treatment as a latent variable in a probabilistic model and uses variational inference (VI) to approximate its posterior. The framework can integrate existing neural-network-based ADRF estimators and is evaluated on synthetic and semi-synthetic data (IHDP dataset with ~700 samples). The problem setting (treatment measurement error) is considered interesting and under-explored by all reviewers.

### Strengths
1. **Novelty and relevance of the problem**: The paper addresses an important but rarely studied scenario—measurement error in the treatment variable—which expands the applicability of treatment effect estimation in fields like medicine and social sciences.
2. **Well-written and clearly motivated**: The paper is easy to follow, with strong motivation in the introduction. The use of the meme to illustrate the problem is appreciated. The variational autoencoder approach is well-articulated.
3. **Sound technical framework**: The VI approach is principled, technically sound, and reasonably novel for this setting. The method can leverage state-of-the-art neural ADRF estimators, offering flexibility.
4. **Significance**: Robust handling of measurement error could make treatment effect methods more practical in real-world applications. Reviewers acknowledge the potential impact.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient discussion of technical challenges unique to treatment measurement error**: Reviewer 1 noted the paper does not clearly articulate how measurement error in the *treatment* variable poses distinct challenges compared to error in covariates or outcomes. The core issues (e.g., error affecting both propensity score and outcome prediction) are not adequately separated from standard error settings. The question of technical challenges remains unanswered.
2. **Limited experimental evaluation and unconvincing results**:
   - **Lack of real-world data**: All experiments are on synthetic or semi-synthetic data (IHDP, only ~700 samples). This limits practical impact and generalizability. Without real-world validation, performance under unknown measurement error patterns is unclear.
   - **Synthetic results are not compelling**: On synthetic data, non-VI methods perform comparably unless measurement error is very high, making the improvement scenario narrow. The synthetic data generation process is not sufficiently detailed, hampering reproducibility.
   - **Semi-synthetic results show underperformance**: The proposed VI method significantly underperforms baselines on the semi-synthetic benchmark, raising doubts about its effectiveness. Reviewer 3 called the experimental results “disappointing” and questioned the method’s utility.
   - **Missing ablation**: What happens to performance if the treatment is measured *without* error? This key ablation is not provided.
3. **VAE non-identifiability not discussed**: The paper does not address the well-known non-identifiability issue of VAEs (e.g., the potential for different latent representations to yield the same likelihood). This could lead to unstable or unreliable treatment effect estimates, especially without guarantees from β-VAE. The possibility of improving identifiability via auxiliary information (e.g., Khemakhem et al., 2020) is not discussed.
4. **Presentation details and clarity issues**:
   - Fonts in plots are very small, and the proposed method is not labeled “ours,” making it hard to distinguish.
   - Figure 2 misses model parameters (θ_y, θ_t, φ) that are standard in VAE illustrations.
   - Figure 3: What does “MiM” stand for? It is not defined.
   - Metric section: The paper says “we employ the widely recognized metric” but no citation is given. The description of the metric (“quantifies proximity to true potential outcomes when considering covariates and the corresponding treatment”) is inaccurate—it measures error across all possible treatments, not just the corresponding one.
   - Table 1 shows APE = 0.0 ± 0.0 for the proposed method, while AMSE is high. This anomalous result is unexplained.
   - Appendix B.4 has a citation formatting error.
5. **DAG concerns and naming issues**:
   - Reviewer 3 questioned the DAG (Figure 1), suggesting an alternative causal structure for the motivating example (prescription non-adherence): X→S, S+U→T, X+T+ε→Y. This raises a question of whether the assumed generative model is appropriate for the motivating scenario.
   - The name “ATTNet” appears in the paper, but it seems indistinguishable from “TransTEE” (the actual baseline reference). Clarification is needed.
6. **Unclear point of the neural network approach**: Reviewer 3 noted that the main advantage of neural networks (handling high-dimensional data) is not tested. Given mediocre performance in low-dimensional synthetic settings, the added complexity is not justified.

**Note on reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Reviewer 1) was more skeptical of the technical contribution and experimental strength (rating 6), while the other two (Reviewers 2 and 3, rating 8) were more positive but still raised substantial concerns, particularly about the experiments and lack of real-world validation. Reviewer 3’s criticism of experimental underperformance is especially pointed.
