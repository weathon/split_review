## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MedResEval, a benchmark derived from MedQA designed to evaluate LLM medical reasoning by expanding difficulty across three dimensions: limited clues, larger decision space, and multi-step reasoning. It then uses this benchmark to study scaling effects of parameter size (N) and training data size (D) on performance, proposing a difficulty-dependent scaling-law formula. The main finding is that while smaller models (≤10B) can approach larger models (≈70B) on simple tasks, they underperform on complex reasoning tasks; the error reduction rate from scaling data is 1.3× greater for large models on simple tasks and 2× greater on complex tasks, emphasizing the importance of parameter scale.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and significance of MedResEval**: The benchmark expands MedQA with more complex question representations, larger decision space, and multi-step tasks. This is a meaningful extension that can also be applied to other medical datasets (e.g., MedMCQA, PubMedQA). (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Relevant problem and domain**: The paper addresses an important issue—evaluating LLM reasoning in the medical field and understanding scaling effects, which is crucial for high-stakes deployment. (Reviewers 3, 4, 5)
- **Solid experimental design**: Thorough evaluation covering 12–18 open-source models from multiple families (Llama, Qwen), with both quantitative and qualitative analyses. The scaling-law formula aligns well with empirical results. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Clear presentation**: Well-structured writing with effective diagrams, plots, tables, and examples illustrating the modifications. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Predictive value of scaling-law formula**: The formula offers a tool for future model development and provides insight into the marginal gains from increasing data vs. parameters. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- **One reviewer (score 6) is highly positive**, citing originality, quality, clarity, and significance; another reviewer (score 5) also finds the problem relevant and the benchmark useful, though the findings are not completely surprising. (Reviewers 2, 3)

### Weaknesses
- **Clinical relevance overstated**: The task definitions (Available Clues, Decision Space, Reasoning Steps) may not increase complexity as claimed. For example, the “Available Clues” example reduces the question to a binary assessment of a single provided answer, bypassing the comparative analysis among closely related distractors that characterizes real clinical scenarios. Similarly, “Reasoning Steps” adds an intermediate task but ultimately targets the same end answer, potentially simplifying rather than increasing complexity. (Reviewer 1)
- **Lack of confidence intervals / margins of error**: All evaluations report only average performance (e.g., Figure 5); no confidence intervals are provided, weakening the robustness and reliability of the claims. (Reviewers 1, 2, 5)
- **Limited model diversity**: The evaluation uses only general-purpose LLMs. The absence of medical LLMs (e.g., MedPALM, Meditron) is a major concern; specialized models might exhibit different scaling behaviors. Model architectural diversity (e.g., encoder-decoder models) is also missing. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Limited dataset diversity**: MedResEval is derived solely from MedQA. Other medical datasets (MedMCQA, PubMedQA, emrQA, DrugEHRQA) are not evaluated, raising questions about whether the findings generalize beyond MedQA. The complexity dimensions may not fully capture real-world medical reasoning. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Novelty concerns compared to existing datasets**: The benchmark still relies on MCQs, despite the existence of non-MCQ medical QA datasets (e.g., emrQA). Multi-step reasoning benchmarks also already exist (e.g., for clinical reasoning). The contribution is therefore incremental. (Reviewer 5)
- **Dependency on GPT-4 for CoT demonstrations**: The use of GPT-4-generated chain-of-thought prompts ties the benchmark to a closed-source model and diverges from realistic medical scenarios where sensitive data cannot be processed by such models due to ethics. (Reviewer 5)
- **Incomplete experimental details**: The specific prompts used are not provided, making it difficult to reproduce results and assess the impact of prompt variation on model performance. (Reviewer 5)
- **No limitations or future work section**: The paper lacks a discussion of limitations, which is needed for a complete evaluation. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **Data contamination risk**: MedQA (released 2021) may be part of the training sets for some LLMs (e.g., Llama-3, with cutoff Dec 2023), potentially turning the benchmark into a test of memorization rather than reasoning. (Reviewer 5)
- **Missing validation of difficulty dimensions**: Only the diagnosis simulation task was validated with clinicians. The other three tasks lack such validation to ensure that added options or steps are non‑trivial. (Reviewer 3)
- **Clarity issues in figures and equations**: Bar plots are hard to interpret for performance vs. N and D; scatter plots (as in Figure 6) might be better. The log‑scale axes in Figure 6 are not labeled in the caption. (Reviewer 2)  
  The variable \(K\) in Equation 2 is not clearly defined (does it refer to the 4 tasks?). The intuition behind Equation 3 and its relation to Equation 1 is insufficiently explained. (Reviewers 1, 3)  
  Minor typos: “sematic” instead of “semantic” in Figure 5. (Reviewer 3)
- **Additional questions implying missing analysis**:  
  - Will MedResEval and the case study be published? (Reviewer 2)  
  - In the “Expanding Decision Space” task, how are randomly generated answers guaranteed to be wrong? (Reviewer 2)  
  - Does the benchmark support multi-answer questions? (Reviewer 2)  
  - For the 5‑shot setting (Appendix B), what difficulty level do the examples have? (Reviewer 3)  
  - In Section 4, what is the “simple generalization effect” and how does the added baseline address it? (Reviewer 5)
- **Minority positive contrast**: Two reviewers (scores 6 and 5) are more positive, praising the benchmark’s novelty and the thoroughness of the analysis, while the other three (scores 3) raise fundamental concerns regarding clinical validity, missing confidence intervals, and limited diversity of models and data. These positive perspectives are noted but do not outweigh the main criticisms.
