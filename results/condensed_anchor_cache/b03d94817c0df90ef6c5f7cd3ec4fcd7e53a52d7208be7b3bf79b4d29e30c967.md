- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 1, 3, 1, 1

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper attempts to explain catastrophic forgetting in neural networks through a proposed “Forward Explanation,” introducing concepts such as Interleaved Representation, Task Representation Convergence Phenomena, and Forward-Interleaved Memory Encoding. Experiments are conducted on MNIST and CIFAR‑10 to track changes in layer representations. However, the work is seen as lacking novelty, rigorous experimentation, and proper scientific writing; the claims are often unsubstantiated or already well known.

### Strengths
- The topic of understanding catastrophic forgetting is highly relevant to the continual learning community (R1, R2, R3).  
- The authors conduct a thorough look at representation changes during training (R3).  
- The writing is engaging (R3) – though this is strongly outweighed by weaknesses in scientific rigor (R3, R1, R2, R4).

### Weaknesses

**Limited novelty and neglect of prior work**  
- The work ignores a large body of theoretical and empirical work on catastrophic forgetting, e.g., NTK overlap matrix, forgetting in linear regression, wide networks, information flow (R1 cites [1–11]; R2, R3, R4 agree).  
- Many claimed phenomena are trivial or already known: representations becoming class‑discriminative is a direct consequence of training a linear classifier on top (R3, R4). Using the expectation of a representation as a decoder in class‑incremental learning is standard (R4 cites iCaRL).  
- Mis‑use of terminology: “interleaved representation” actually refers to dissimilar representations (R2); split into encoder/decoder is arbitrary (R3).

**Poor structure and informal writing**  
- The manuscript appears to be borrowed from a thesis: Section 2.3 says “In the previous chapter” (R1); mentions “Conclusion One” and “Conclusion Two” without defining them (R1).  
- Informal, blog‑post style unsuitable for a conference paper: e.g., “Now, we have both good news and bad news. The good news is …” (R3).  
- Many typos and grammatical errors (R3), e.g., “After pre-trained, we trained a new decoder using” (R3).  
- Notations are confusing (R4); axes on figures are missing (R4); descriptions of experimental results insufficient (R4).

**Weak experiment section**  
- No validation set in the code – hyper‑parameters are optimized on the test set, creating biased outcomes (R1).  
- Test set is shuffled (`shuffle=True` in `DataLoader`) – unnecessary and potentially problematic for evaluation (R1).  
- Experiments are extremely simplistic, not well described, and insufficient to support the bold claims (R2).  
- No ablation study, no discussion of hyper‑parameter optimization (R1, R2).  
- Figure 4 uses accuracy on only 10 samples (R3); it is unclear whether the full test set or only 10 compressed representations are used, making the accuracy measure granular to only 11 possible values (R3).  
- Missing details to reproduce results (R1, R2, R4).  
- No measurement of forgetting ratio, backward transfer, or forward transfer as commonly done in continual learning (R1).

**Unsubstantiated claims and undefined concepts**  
- (Section 2.2) “This indirectly suggests that the model training is equivalent to training an encoder. However, we have not provided an explanation … we do not have the time to provide a comprehensive mathematical proof” – an admission of lack of rigor (R4).  
- (Section 2.4) “In the model initialization phase … they will almost all be mapped to similar representations.” – unsubstantiated (R4).  
- (Section 2.4) “If other optimization algorithms are used … it does not affect the conclusion.” – unsubstantiated (R4).  
- “The parameters of the decoder converge to the expectation of the representation” – unclear what this means (R2).  
- Training the decoder using “expected representation” is mentioned without proper definition (R2).  
- Lack of clarity about “memory trace” – is it just the collection of all layer activations? (R3).  
- (“Conclusion 2”) “the pretraining dataset is different from the target test dataset” – no details given (R2).

**Reviewer disagreement**  
One reviewer (R2, score 3) was slightly less negative than the others (scores 1), but still stresses that the paper lacks proper structure, the experiments are insufficient for ICLR, and the main intuitions are already known or misleading. The other three reviewers (R1, R3, R4) are overwhelmingly critical with scores of 1, citing lack of novelty, poor writing, weak experiments, and unsubstantiated claims.
