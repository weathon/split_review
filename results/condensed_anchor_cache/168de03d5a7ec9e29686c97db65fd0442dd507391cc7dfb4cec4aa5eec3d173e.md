## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies online convex optimization with preference feedback, motivated by RLHF for LLMs. It proposes an online mirror descent algorithm (Double-Scrible) for adversarial linear dueling bandits under the Bradley-Terry model, achieving $O(\sqrt{T})$ regret. Extensions to batched feedback (BaBle-Scrible) and ranking feedback (MNL-Scrible) preserve near-optimal guarantees. The authors claim the first efficient gradient-based algorithm with optimal regret for this problem.

### Strengths
- **Algorithmic novelty**: First gradient-based (OMD) method for adversarial preference-only feedback, using a novel gradient estimation technique without value feedback (Reviewers 1, 2, 6). The Double-Scrible algorithm explicitly handles adversarial preferences.
- **Optimal regret bounds**: Matching $O(\sqrt{T})$ upper and lower bounds (up to log factors) for pairwise, batched, and ranking settings (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). The lower bound is provided.
- **Theoretical soundness**: Analysis is rigorous, assumptions and theorems are clearly stated, proofs appear correct (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). Notation is intuitive (Reviewer 3).
- **Practical extensions**: Algorithm generalizes naturally to batched (multiple queries) and top-\(m\) ranking feedback, addressing realistic data collection scenarios (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5).
- **Potential for real-world use**: Computational tractability via gradient-based updates (eigenvalue decomposition each iteration) is noted as a strength over UCB/TS methods (Reviewers 1, 4). However, practicality is debated (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
1. **No empirical validation**: All reviewers note the complete absence of experiments or simulations. This makes it impossible to assess computational efficiency, practical performance, or scalability to high-dimensional/non-convex settings (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5). Several reviewers explicitly suggest small-scale experiments (e.g., GPT-2) to increase impact (Reviewers 1, 3).
2. **Missing related work and insufficient comparisons**: The paper lacks a dedicated related work section. Reviewers point out that existing work on dueling bandits with adversarial preferences (e.g., [1] in the paper) provides efficient algorithms and lower bounds that may overlap with claimed contributions (Reviewers 1, 5, 6). Reviewer 6 specifically argues that an efficient algorithm for contextual dueling bandits with adversarial feedback [1] already exists, and a lower bound for logistic dueling bandits was given in [2]. The advantage over these prior works is not clearly articulated.
3. **Unrealistic or restrictive assumptions**: The linear (logistic) model is a strong simplification of real-world preference dynamics. Reviewers question whether theoretical guarantees hold for non-linear, context-dependent, or high-dimensional (exponential arm space) settings typical of LLM alignment (Reviewers 2, 3, 6). No discussion of how to extend beyond linearity or avoid eigenvalue decomposition (which scales as $O(d^3)$) is provided (Reviewers 2, 4).
4. **Overclaiming of contributions**: Reviewer 6 (with high confidence) states the paper overclaims: (a) The first efficient algorithm is disputed by existence of [1]; (b) the lower bound is not novel because [2] provides a lower bound for the same problem under linear stochastic transitivity; (c) claims of computational advantage over UCB/TS are unsubstantiated. Reviewer 5 notes that the connection to LLMs is “marketing” and the problem fit to ICLR is unclear.
5. **Weak motivation for adversarial preferences**: The paper motivates adversarial human utility (§ 1) but does not provide concrete examples why preference vectors would change adversarially over time in RLHF, making the setting feel contrived (Reviewers 4, 6).
6. **Unfair comparison of regret bounds**: The regret definitions differ across pairwise, batched, and ranking settings (e.g., batched setting uses averaged regret and $B$ times more queries). Thus, claims that batched/ranking feedback improves performance are not directly comparable and may be misleading (Reviewer 4).
7. **Implementation details and scalability**: The algorithm requires eigenvalue decomposition each round, but the claimed $O(dT)$ runtime (line 347) seems to ignore this cost (Reviewer 4). No guidance on implementing in deep learning frameworks or handling non-convex objectives is given (Reviewer 6). The paper does not address how exploration is facilitated (Reviewer 2).
8. **Minor issues**: Several reviewers note a missing reference at line 458 (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Typos appear in Equation (1) and Theorem 3 superscript (Reviewer 4). The paper exceeds the 10-page limit in the current draft (Reviewer 5).
