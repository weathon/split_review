## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Clifford Group Equivariant Simplicial Message Passing Networks (CSMPNs), combining Clifford group-equivariant layers with simplicial message passing for steerable E(n)-equivariance. The method lifts point sets to simplices (e.g., Vietoris-Rips, manual lifts) and embeds simplicial data in Clifford algebra, using geometric products to compute simplex features and Clifford group equivariant networks for messages. Experiments span geometry, molecular dynamics (MD17, QM9 – see limitations), motion capture, and NBA data.

### Strengths
1. Novel combination of Clifford group-equivariant layers and simplicial message passing, providing a strong theoretical foundation. Use of Clifford algebras to represent geometric features with simplices is natural and mathematically sound.
2. The model is broadly applicable across domains (geometry, molecular dynamics, motion capture, NBA). It eliminates two main limitations of the existing EMPSN method (manual initialization, restriction of updating methods).
3. Experimental evaluation on a range of problems demonstrates versatility and that the method works. Results show CSMPN outperforms existing methods in most cases.
4. The paper is well-written, structure clear, figures of high quality.

### Weaknesses
1. The embedding of simplicial data into Clifford algebra (Sec. 3.2) is not fully and clearly described, hindering full understanding. Details are lacking on how Clifford features for higher-order simplices (beyond edges) are constructed and how permutation invariance is achieved in practice. The geometric product is mentioned, but the exact procedure for aggregating these products to ensure permutation invariance is unclear. The depiction (Figure 1) is open to different interpretations. (Reviewer 2, confidence 3)
2. The method overstates the naturality of the combination: simplices are topological, while the geometric product relates to the geometric space. Even in 3D, one can have 100-simplices, but the Clifford algebra only goes up to grade 4; higher-order simplices may not map uniquely. Different sets of vertices forming distinct simplices could, after the geometric product and subsequent projection onto the Clifford algebra basis, result in the same multivector, losing information about the specific simplex structure. This is a critical issue that needs acknowledgment and discussion. (Reviewer 3, confidence 4: more confident)
3. While CSMPN achieves best performance in most experiments, the improvement over existing methods is not significant enough to justify the added complexity of the Clifford algebra framework. A more thorough analysis of computational cost and practical trade-offs is needed. (Reviewer 2, confidence 3)
4. Detailed comparison of inference time is requested, e.g., on the MD17 atomic motion dataset. The number of simplices can grow rapidly with nodes, potentially making the method impractical for larger systems. Comparison with methods that do not use simplicial complexes (e.g., standard GNNs) would highlight computational vs. expressivity trade-offs. (Reviewer 1, confidence 3)
5. The discussion on manual lifts for molecules is too abstract. The paper would benefit from concrete examples of choosing specific simplices for molecules with multiple functional groups or complex ring systems, and directions for applying manual lifts to small/medium molecules beyond H2O. (Reviewer 1, confidence 3)
6. Contributions are somewhat thin: the paper essentially plugs together two existing ideas (simplicial message passing and Clifford group equivariant networks). (Reviewer 3, confidence 4)
7. Experimental evaluation would benefit from performance on more standard geometric deep learning benchmarks, and metrics such as parameter efficiency and training time to fully understand trade-offs against other generic equivariant methods. (Reviewer 3, confidence 4)
8. Reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms or requests for clarification:  
   – How can one “learn” the embedding through Clifford group-equivariant layers as mentioned in Sec. 3.2? (Reviewer 2)  
   – On page 2, what does “geometric products are meaningful in all such cases” mean? (Reviewer 3)  
   – On page 7, is an equal parameter count fair when comparing equivariant and non-equivariant baselines? Equivariant methods usually have far fewer parameters for the same expressivity. (Reviewer 3)
