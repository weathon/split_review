## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GEMS, a curriculum learning method using a genetic algorithm for competitive multi-agent RL. It applies population-wide crossover/mutation on scenario encodings (environment parameters, opponent policies, blind agent), uses regret to measure difficulty, and includes continuously optimized open-loop opponents for early learning. Empirical results are reported on Pong, Volley, and ACM continuous control domains against several baselines. All reviewers gave a score of 5; the overall decision was Reject.

### Strengths
- **Important problem** – Addresses instability and complexity in multi-agent training; well motivated and well organized (R1, R3).
- **Well-written and method described adequately** – The paper is clear and the approach is explained (R2, R3).
- **Novel combination** – Combining curriculum learning across both environment and opponent populations is considered novel (R2 notes PSRO only handles opponents, single-agent curricula only handle environments). One reviewer considers the combination a step forward (R2) while another considers the components (population-wide crossover from GC, regret from MAESTRO) known and the addition of the blind agent limited in novelty (R3).
- **Positive experimental results** – GEMS outperforms baselines across all three domains (R1, R2). Crossplay experiments (e.g., Table 6) show effectiveness (R3). The evaluation domains are of high complexity for the 1v1 setting (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing or limited related work** – Lacks discussion of: Evolutionary Population Curriculum (EPC, Long et al. ICLR2020) and Multi-Objective Curricula (Kang et al. CoRL2022) (R1); surveys on multi-agent curriculum and transfer (Silva & Costa 2019), agent modeling (Albrecht & Stone 2018), and random-walk pruning for curriculum size (Silva & Costa 2018) (R4). The literature review is limited (R4).
- **No theoretical analysis** – No convergence, computational complexity, or sample complexity analysis (R1). The claim that opponents “will evolve toward the Nash equilibrium” is unsupported theoretically and not empirically justified (R3).
- **Blind agent design and manual tuning** – Implementation is unclear: how is the action selected? Why this approach over applying noise to the policy or a random policy? (R2). The blind agent’s initial behavior is manually set (e.g., circling around the ego agent in ACM), requiring human tuning for each new domain; there is no automated generation method (R3, R4). A random agent would be trivial and provide little benefit (R4).
- **Limited environment customizability for curricula** – The environments used (e.g., Volley: ball velocity only) offer very simple curriculum dimensions; more complex environments (e.g., MiniGrid) would better demonstrate the algorithm’s potential (R2). It is also unclear whether the method addresses the multi-agent interaction dimension of complexity, as it focuses on state-space complexity (R1). Scaling to domains with image-based inputs or many agents is not discussed (R3).
- **Unclear modeling of opponent population** – Is the opponent population known or unknown? If unknown, how are opponent strategies generated? If known, the setting is unrealistic (R4). The problem statement (Equation 1) only considers a single ego agent, with no discussion of multiple ego agents (R3).
- **Poorly specified GA task encoding** – It is not clear what constitutes a “solution” for the GA and how crossover/mutation are applied to the scenario encoding. Concrete examples of encodings for different environments and opponent types are needed (R4). The algorithm’s u and δ parameters are not defined (R1). The term “PONG” in Section 4.3 is unexplained (R1).
- **Unclear baseline opponent usage** – Five baseline opponents are used for testing, but it is not specified whether they are seen during training or held out. To evaluate generality, held-out opponents should be used, and at least one adaptive opponent should be included to test robustness (R4).
- **Claim about “global optimum” needs clarification** – Section 3.4 mentions students exploiting instead of searching for the “global optimum”; it is unclear whether this means Pareto optimum (R1). Are agents assumed homogeneous? (R1).
- **Missing reference and typos** – “[?]” appears in Section 3.3; “a stat a state-of-the-art” is a typo (R3). Figure 2 lacks a legend (R3). Several other typos need proofreading (R1).
