## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper analyzes social bias in CLIP using a synthetic facial image dataset (CausalFace) designed to control protected attributes (race, age, gender) and confounders (pose, lighting, smile). It measures cosine similarities between image embeddings and word prompts from social psychology frameworks (ABC Model, Stereotype Content Model). Findings: non-protected attributes are powerful confounds with variance comparable to protected attributes; confounding control reveals intersectional biases not visible in real datasets like FairFace; some results challenge assumptions about age/gender dominance. Three reviewers find the analysis interesting and convincing, while one reviewer is highly critical of the novelty and causal claim.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: Deviates from traditional correlation-based bias measurement by systematically controlling confounding factors (R1, R3).
- **Well-explained motivation**: Clearly lays out limitations of current correlational bias studies (R1, R4).
- **Strong analysis along multiple axes**, e.g., intersectional biases in Figure 2 (R1).
- **Convincing experimental results** with a useful message about confounders’ impact (R2).
- **Careful handling of issues**: Demonstrates statistical similarity between synthetic and real images (R2), and makes intelligent use of controlled data design (R3).
- **Interesting empirical takeaways**: Differences between synthetic and real data; confounding variables like smiling, pose, and lighting can amplify or mask biases (R2, R3).
- **Well-written and well-motivated**, with relevant, up-to-date related work (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Causality claim and synthetic dataset biases.** The paper claims causal conclusions, but the GAN used to generate CausalFace is trained on a real dataset that may contain biases. Thus, confounders may persist in the generated images, weakening the causal interpretation. Reviewers 1 and 4 specifically note the lack of discussion about GAN biases and the assumption that synthetic data automatically eliminates confounders. Reviewer 4 considers this a fundamental flaw, while Reviewers 2 and 3 find the experimental design convincing. This disagreement is noted.
- **Limited scope and generalizability.**
  - Only one real dataset (FairFace) and three demographic groups (Asian, Black, White) are used. Reviewer 2 recommends including UTK Face, CelebA, and especially MIAP (natural settings) to strengthen the argument and highlight the impact of confounders in more varied data.
  - The paper focuses on sentiment-related attributes (warmth, competence, communion) that are sensitive to confounders; other association biases (e.g., gender-occupation) that may be less sensitive are not studied (R2).
  - The title should specify “for face images” (R3).
  - Reason for limiting FairFace to only three groups is not explained (R4).
- **Clarity and missing definitions.** Several concepts are insufficiently explained in the main paper:
  - Connection between social psychology frameworks (ABC Model, Stereotype Content Model’s warmth/competence dimensions) and the bias measurement methodology in CLIP is not clearly articulated; a diagram or detailed walkthrough is needed (R1).
  - *Markedness* lacks a precise mathematical definition; a formula like WEAT should be given (R2, R3 also notes ambiguity).
  - *Mean cosine similarities*, *relative preference frequency*, and *neutral prompt* are not clearly defined (R3).
  - *Intensity* is used without definition (R3).
  - Specific prompts used to generate levels of pose, lighting, and smile are not described (R2).
  - Thresholds in Section 3.4 (e.g., 0.7 for age) are unexplained (R3).
  - Figure B.1 is unclear; the x-axis of Figure 3a is not defined; commentary on Figure 3b about black males needs clarification (R3).
  - The phrase “legally protected attributes” is used without acknowledging that legal interpretations vary across jurisdictions (R4).
- **Paper not self-contained.** Essential details like the cosine similarity computation (Appendix A1) and results discussed in Section 4.1 (Appendix B) are relegated to supplementary material, which should only contain extra information (R4).
- **Missing comparisons and metrics.**
  - Applicability to other vision-language models is not addressed (R1).
  - Standard bias metrics like max-skewness or NDKL are not used; why they were omitted is unclear (R1).
  - A *control group* of words is needed to test whether the observed positive correlation between positive and negative terms is due to some images being correlated with all words (R3).
- **Interpretation of findings.** Reviewer 3 challenges the claim in Sections 4.5 and 5 that age- and gender-induced variations are not strong factors. The reviewer argues that the social importance of these attributes is not about the magnitude of deviation but about socially determined reasons for measuring disparities and harms; the paper’s framing misses this point.
- **Experimental choices not justified.**
  - Why compare smile levels -1.5 vs 3.0 instead of extreme ends -2.5 vs 4.0 (R2)?
  - What is meant by “neutral lighting” when it appears at the middle of the scale (R2)?
  - Why is the pattern for black males considered “unexpected” – should not the other groups’ lack of amplification be the unexpected pattern (R2)?
- **Novelty.** Reviewer 4 finds the novelty limited, noting that correlations between image and word embeddings in CLIP have been extensively studied; using attributes from social psychology may be insufficient as a main contribution, and the causality claim is not well justified. Reviewers 1 and 3 consider the work original. This disagreement is noted.
