## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes using LLMs as a window into the mental structure of psychopathology. The authors prompt LLMs (Mistral, Llama, Dolphin) with descriptions of disorders (depression, anxiety, apathy, schizophrenia, etc.) and then have them answer clinical questionnaires (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7). They show that prompted models score above diagnostic thresholds and that correlations between questionnaire scores mirror human co-morbidity patterns (using RSA). They then analyze lexical output patterns from a word-association task using GloVe embeddings and simplex volume as proxies for representational structure, comparing these to human data. Finally, a case study with schizophrenic patients shows qualitative similarity in semantic structures between patients and LLMs prompted for schizophrenia. One reviewer (score 1) strongly questioned the methodology; another (score 6) found the analysis thorough but superficial; two reviewers (scores 5 and 5) found the topic interesting but flagged fundamental concerns.

### Strengths
- **Timely and relevant.** Reviewers agreed that exploring LLM-human correlations for psychopathology is interesting and timely. Reviewer 1 (score 1) noted the value of carefully correlating LLM behavior against existing human data.
- **Thorough design and model comparison.** Reviewer 2 (score 6) praised the thorough analysis across three open models (Mistral, Llama, Dolphin), 100 runs per condition, and statistical significance testing. Reviewer 3 (score 5) also highlighted the comparison across models and runs as increasing representativeness.
- **Good presentation.** Reviewer 3 called the article well-written and well-structured, with rich empirical data and intuitive graphics. The presentation of results was rated highly by Reviewer 3 (4/4).
- **Careful boundaries.** Reviewer 3 appreciated that the authors delineate what their results do and do not imply, especially given the sensitive topic of mental disorders.
- **Consistency with empirical findings.** Reviewer 4 (score 5) noted that results for depression and anxiety are consistent with prior empirical observations, demonstrating potential for simulation.
- **Societal benefit.** Reviewer 4 found it interesting that work attempts to use LLMs for societal benefit.

*Contrast:* These strengths contrast with the much more negative view of Reviewer 1 (score 1), who doubted the methodology entirely, questioned the figures, and saw no clear takeaway. The other reviewers (2, 3, 4) were more positive about the topic and presentation while still noting major issues.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear and unjustified definition of “internal representation.”** Multiple reviewers (1, 3, 4) criticized the paper’s central claim that lexical output patterns are a proxy for LLM internal representations. Reviewer 1 noted that if representation means weights, the same LLM is used with different prompts, which contradicts the claim; if it means weights + activations, this is not argued. Reviewers 3 and 4 explicitly stated that research (e.g., Turpin et al. 2023) shows output under a given decoding strategy does not reflect internal representations. The claim is “very erroneous” (Reviewers 3, 4). A more direct approach comparing patient neural activation patterns to LLM embeddings was suggested.
- **Fundamental circularity in experimental setup.** Reviewers 3 and 4 pointed out that the paper prompts models to generate psychopathological language and then analyzes the manipulated output to draw conclusions about the link between representation and psychopathology. Because the desired signatures are induced by the prompt, the analysis is circular. Representational tuning (e.g., via fine-tuning) rather than prompting would be more informative.
- **Methodology concerns: contamination and relative comparisons.** Reviewer 1 argued that high pathology scores in “No Pathology” conditions (line 260) likely stem from test data contamination (questionnaires present in pretraining). The paper’s test of co-morbidity correlations uses RSA in a relative manner (selecting the model with highest correlation, 0.59) without an absolute human-baseline or significance bar. The use of RSA is not cited or justified.
- **Equations (1) and (2) unmotivated; GloVe embeddings unjustified as gold standard.** Reviewer 1 found no literature citation or concrete evidence that the word-pair similarity metrics reflect internal representation. GloVe is used as a “gold” representation for semantic diversity without justification, evaluation, or manual verification.
- **Inconsistent “No Pathology” baseline across prompting techniques.** Reviewer 2 observed that in Figure 2, the “No Pathology” scores vary substantially across naive, react, and chain-of-thought prompts (e.g., for Social Anxiety, Mistral → Dolphin → Llama show decreasing naive scores but increasing react scores). Since “No Pathology” is not influenced by any prompt template, the authors should explain why these values differ and why they sometimes cross the diagnostic threshold.
- **Correlation results lack meaningfulness tests.** Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 1 noted that the observed correlations (RSA, heatmaps) are post-hoc and do not demonstrate that the approach can generate concrete, testable hypotheses. The paper shows correlation but not causation or novel insight. Reviewer 2 argued for a “prospective” test: use the LLM to design a novel hypothesis and verify it with human data, not just replicate known patterns.
- **Analysis only on output words, not hidden states.** Reviewer 2 pointed out that the paper analyzes the words of the answers, not the transformer hidden states that shape token probabilities. Correlations are interesting but lack justification for being meaningful.
- **Limited model and generation exploration.** Reviewer 2 noted that the paper uses only three open models with a single parameter scale; more modern models (GPT-4, Llama-3) should be tested, and scaling with parameter size should be investigated. Only three temperature values are used; other decoding methods (beam search, contrastive search) are not explored.
- **Heatmaps (Figs. 8-12) show many off-diagonal perfect scores (1.0) and large changes across prompting templates.** Reviewer 2 questioned which values are meaningful and whether it makes sense to evaluate min/max. The side-effects (co-morbidity patterns) limit the specificity of the approach, and a more detailed analysis is needed to show which patterns mirror human data.
- **Inconclusive case study.** Reviewer 2 found Figure 5 inconclusive due to outliers in both human and LLM “Patient” data; mean may be less adequate than median. Standard error bars are hard to detect. A qualitative exploration of outliers could lead to concrete hypotheses.
- **Missing key information.** Reviewer 2 requested clarity on whether foundation or instruction-tuned models were used, since instruction tuning can affect system prompt adherence. The license and consent for human data were questioned by Reviewer 1.
- **Appropriateness for ICLR.** Reviewer 1 questioned whether the paper belongs at a machine learning venue (ICLR) rather than a psychology journal, as many claims are about psychology and the reviewer felt unqualified to evaluate them. The target audience and practical takeaway are unclear.
- **Minor issues.** Grammar/spelling mistakes (lines 020, 051, 313, 394, 396, 430, 482, 502, 510, 518), confusing notation (“cos” for cosine similarity, Kappa and Delta switched in lines 369-370), figure quality (Figs. 8-12 need improvement), and mislabeled axes in Figure 3 (top/middle/bottom vs. left/middle/right, “between” to remove).
