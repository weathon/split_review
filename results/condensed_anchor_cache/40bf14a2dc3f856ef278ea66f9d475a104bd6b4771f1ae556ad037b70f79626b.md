## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces BenchStock, a benchmark with standardized datasets from US and China stock markets, and evaluates 23 ML/DL models for stock prediction over three decades using portfolio backtesting. Reviewers acknowledge the comprehensive model selection and detailed experimental setup, but raise major concerns about novelty, methodological depth, missing comparisons to existing benchmarks (e.g., M6 competition), narrow data scope (price/volume only, daily frequency), flawed metrics, and insufficient experiments to support key claims.

### Strengths
1. A detailed dataset with clear preprocessing and standardized datasets from two large markets (US and China) (R1, R4).
2. Thorough benchmarking framework covering a wide range of 23 models from traditional ML to latest DL (R1, R4, R5).
3. Large-scale experiments over 30 years, assessing both short- and long-term performance (R4).
4. The paper is well-written, well-structured, with clear logic, diagrams, and tables (R3, R5).
5. Sound application of machine learning methodology (R3).
6. Large number of models tested (R2).
7. The choice of experimental data is rigorous and ensures reliability and robustness (R5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited novelty and contribution**: The paper offers nothing new that isn’t already known or doesn’t already exist in a significantly better form (R3, R4, R5). A minority reviewer (R2, score 1) strongly criticizes that public stock data is easily accessible, so consistent preprocessing alone is insufficient for a unique benchmark.
2. **Missing consideration of existing literature**: The paper does not reference the 2024 M6 competition, which is the SOTA benchmark for stock prediction and portfolio optimization with 100,000 submissions from 50+ countries on a standardized dataset (R3).
3. **Unsubstantiated and arbitrary conclusions**: The four main findings are either well-known or lack practical implications. For example: (a) “Most methods outperformed the S&P 500 in the US but lost in China” – no trading firm would use the same strategy in such different markets; (b) “Prediction accuracy was not correlated with portfolio return” – practitioners care more about return; (c) “Advanced DL did not outperform traditional approaches” – DL is more useful with external data, so this conclusion is unreliable within this scope; (d) “Performance was highly dependent on testing period” – this is already known. These observations fail to provide practical guidance (R4). The conclusions appear arbitrary and lack sufficient experiments to substantiate (R5).
4. **Flawed metric selection**: Only finance-related metrics are used, which are subjective and heavily influenced by specific trading strategies and local exchange policies. The paper lacks time-series-related metrics for properly evaluating model performance (R5). Using portfolio performance as a metric for forecasting is questioned: forecasting volatility is probably more important than point forecasts of price (R3, citing arXiv:2310.13357).
5. **Lack of statistical justification**: The Information Coefficient (IC) measures correlation between predicted and actual returns but fails to eliminate the possibility that performance is driven by randomness rather than genuine predictive power (R5).
6. **Narrow feature scope**: Only price and volume data (open, high, low, close, volume, amount) are used, excluding news sentiment, economic indicators, financial statements, analyst reports, and alternative data. This limits predictive power and practical applicability (R1, R2, R4, R5). The benchmark is not usable by most stock prediction models that incorporate other data types (R2).
7. **Insufficient data and evaluation dimensions**:
   - Single frequency (daily) only. Missing high-frequency (minute-level), mid-frequency (hourly-level), and low-frequency predictions (R5). Reviewer 1 also suggests incorporating higher-frequency data where signal-to-noise ratio is higher (R1).
   - Only single-step forecasting evaluated; missing multi-step forecasting for evaluating models’ ability over extended horizons (R5).
   - Limited to only two markets (US and China). Stock prediction for other advanced and developing countries is crucial, and preprocessing challenges are often more pronounced elsewhere (R2, R4).
8. **Experiments are simple evaluations of existing methods**: The framework appears to be a combination of existing tools (Figure 1), making it easy to replicate. The experiments lack deeper analysis such as ablation studies or underlying mechanisms (e.g., why DL underperforms on Chinese markets, role of feature engineering/labeling) (R1, R4). The study also lacks testing across multiple frequency dimensions and multi-step evaluations, making it incomplete for a benchmark (R5).
9. **DL on Chinese markets contradictory**: Deep learning methods performed very badly on Chinese markets, which seems to contradict existing works and industrial practices, but no explanation is provided (R1).
10. **Lack of generalizability**: Findings may not apply to other markets with different characteristics and regulatory environments (R4).
