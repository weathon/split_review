## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces MetaTool, a novel methodology for training LLMs to use tools by incorporating self-supervised meta-tasks that teach fundamental tool understanding (causality, constraints). The data generation process predicts masked elements in tool execution, enabling scalable creation of high-quality training data without expert annotations. Six meta-tasks are proposed: Effect, Decision-making, Reversion, Input Boundary, Output Boundary, and Counterfact. Experiments on tool-based planning (SAW, BW, LOG) and chatting (ToolBench, BFCL) show performance improvements over baselines and results comparable to ChatGPT while using smaller models (8B). An ablation study confirms the contribution of each meta-task. One reviewer was notably more critical (score 3), particularly about training fairness and experimental comparisons, while others were more positive (scores 5,6,6) but raised several concerns.

### Strengths
- Novel meta-learning approach for tool learning that focuses on general, task-independent understanding of tools, reducing reliance on labeled data. (R1, R3, R4)
- Thorough experimental setup covering both tool-augmented planning and tool-oriented chat scenarios, with clear improvement over baselines. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- Scalable self-supervised data generation process that does not require expert annotations. (R4)
- Well-designed six meta-tasks (Effect, Decision-making, Reversion, Input Boundary, Output Boundary, Counterfact) that capture diverse aspects of tool understanding. (R3, R4)
- Detailed ablation study validates the effectiveness of each meta-task. (R1, R4)
- Good empirical results: MetaTool (8B) achieves performance comparable to ChatGPT on tool-based tasks. (R2, R3, R4)
- The tool-oriented scenario (BW, SAW, LOG) provides a new environment for evaluating tool-using capability. (R2)
- The paper demonstrates solid understanding of related work and includes comprehensive references. (R2)
- The explanation of meta-tasks is clear and easy to follow, with helpful visuals. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- Training fairness concerns (noted by one reviewer with score 3): The restriction to only updating query/value projection layers (line 224–226) may limit learning capacity and make comparisons with full-parameter baselines unfair. The LLaMA3-solution baseline was trained with fewer updates (10k×3) compared to other models ((10k+10k)×3), making direct comparison questionable. The use of early stopping to prevent overfitting may introduce variance; more comprehensive results are needed to rule out hyperparameter selection effects. (R1)
- Novelty and distinction: Some reviewers questioned the novelty of the self-supervised meta-task approach, as prior works (Toolformer, TALM) have explored similar ideas. The paper does not adequately distinguish its specific meta-tasks from these existing methods, making it difficult to assess the true advancement. (R2; R3/R4 found it novel)
- Real-world relevance: The benchmarks are largely simulated environments, which may not capture the unpredictability of real-world tool use. The practical relevance of the six meta-task settings in real-world scenarios is not sufficiently discussed. (R2, R3)
- Comparisons: Lacks comprehensive comparison with a wider range of SOTA tool-learning models, especially those using self-supervised learning or other multi-task/hierarchical approaches. Specifically, the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) has many baselines trained from LLaMA-3.1-8B that are not compared against. (R2, R3)
- Missing experimental details:
    - Two-stage results on ToolBench and BFCL are not reported; also missing LLaMA3-8B-instruct results on BFCL. The experimental setup should be clarified for each benchmark. (R1)
    - The self-play and tree search techniques for data generation (line 199–201) lack implementation details; the method section should describe how ReAct is used to generate thought-tool-input tuples. (R2, R4)
    - The concept of "solution data" is not clearly defined and is introduced abruptly; notation for different data types (solution, meta-task, etc.) is inconsistent across the paper. (R4)
    - The actual prompts used for meta-set construction are not provided, especially for generating informative context states. (R4)
    - The effect of context generation complexity on model performance is not analyzed; qualitative examples would help. (R4)
- Writing and presentation issues:
    - Several typos: "Bert" should be "BERT" (line 146), "BlocksWolrd" -> "BlocksWorld" (line 256), "ChaGPT" -> "ChatGPT" (line 334), "ReACT" -> "ReAct" (line 363). (R2)
    - Line 208: comma should follow math formula. (R2)
    - Missing arrows (↑/↓) to indicate direction of better metrics. (R2)
    - Line 225: Unclear which part of training requires full-parameter training; the model is trained using QLoRA? Clarification needed. (R2)
    - Tables and figures are organized confusingly: Table 3 (tool-augmented scenarios) is placed in Section 3.1, while Figure 4 (tool-oriented scenarios) is separated in Section 3.3. All tables/figures should have hyperlinks. (R2)
    - The analogy to BERT's masking is not pertinent, as the model is still trained autoregressively, not to predict missing context. (R2)
    - Missing specification of model versions: which version of ChatGPT (e.g., gpt-3.5-turbo)? No comparisons with GPT-4 or Claude-2. (R2)
    - Clarification needed: what does "based on ChatGPT" mean for evaluation metrics (Pass Rate, Win Rate)? Pass Rate seems automatic. (R2)
- Scalability and guidance: The self-supervised meta-task setup may be difficult to apply to new tools or domains without additional guidance. Computational cost for large toolsets is not discussed. (R3)
- While ablation is good, it would be interesting to see comparisons with other recent multi-task or hierarchical learning approaches that also aim to improve generalization. (R3)
- One reviewer suggests adding more meta-tasks (e.g., probabilistic reasoning, continuous learning) to further improve generalizability. (R3)
