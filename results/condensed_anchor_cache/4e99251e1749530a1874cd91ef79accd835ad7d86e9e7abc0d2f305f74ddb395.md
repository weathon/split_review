## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a multi-task fine-tuning approach (Task Fusion Decoder, TFD) on top of pre-trained visual encoders (R3M, MVP, EgoVLP) using three surrogate tasks from Ego4D: object state change classification (OSCC), point-of-no-return temporal localization (PNR), and state change object detection (SCOD). The goal is to learn a representation better suited for downstream robotic manipulation. The method is evaluated on MetaWorld, Adroit, Kitchen, and real-world FANUC tasks. Reviews ranged from reject (score 3) to accept (6). One reviewer (score 3) was highly critical, citing poor clarity and inconclusive results, while others (scores 5,6,6) found the approach promising but identified notable weaknesses in exposition, evaluation rigor, and missing ablations.

### Strengths
- Addresses the important problem of adapting large pre-trained models for manipulation tasks; fine-tuning on hand/object interaction tasks from Ego4D is a sensible way to leverage human data.
- Surprising finding: fine-tuning on the same dataset with a different objective (spatial/temporal surrogate tasks) yields significant downstream improvements (up to ~25% success rate) over the base encoders.
- Flexible: can be grafted onto multiple pre-trained visual backbones; not tied to a specific encoder.
- Thorough evaluation: three backbones, three simulation environments, and real-world tasks. Results show average improvement across most tasks.
- Exploits human task data without requiring direct correspondences between human and robot actions.
- Paper is generally well-written (R2, R3) with clear Figures 2 and 3, helpful color-coded tables, and a clear methodology for downstream evaluation.

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and exposition**: The training/testing pipeline is insufficiently explained in the main text. How the backbone connects to TFD, how fine-tuning is done, and how benchmark networks are set up are unclear (Appendix A.1 is essential). Figures 2,3,9,10 need improvement – inconsistent notation, missing global/temporal features, unclear what “+” denotes.
- **Task definition and motivation**: The three surrogate tasks (OSCC, PNR, SCOD) are not clearly defined early; acronyms are not explanatory. The cognitive science motivation (Kirkham et al.) is too thin to justify the specific task choices. Questions raised: what is a “state change”? Why are tasks 1 and 2 different (PNR already implies classification)? Does SCOD apply only at state-change frames or continuously? Why is “point-of-no-return” used? How is the KL loss in (4) computed? Why exactly 10 tokens in TFD? Why assume only one state-change frame?
- **Incomplete evaluation**: 
  - No ablation per individual task head – only a combined spatial vs. temporal split, leaving the reader unsure which head contributes what.
  - No error bars or significance tests reported for success rates (Tables 1, 3).
  - Learned sigmas (task weights) are not reported; it is unclear if they are global or per-scene.
  - Results are mixed: on one of five environments performance decreases, and on a second it does not benefit (R1). Toggle on/off tasks consistently underperform – possible reason?
  - Qualitative evidence (t-SNE, attention maps) is not informative due to known projection/attention issues; stronger evidence would be decoding robot policies into state-change events or bounding boxes.
- **Claims and framing**: Overselling – calling the approach a “paradigm shift” is overreach (R1, R3). The introduction’s claim of “human alignment without human labels” is not supported (R2). The phrase “human-like synesthesia” and the statement that “humans focus on one perception in specific environments” need removal or citation.
- **Missing comparisons and related work**: 
  - Missing comparison to multi-task transformer architectures (e.g., Bhattacharjee et al, CVPR 2022).
  - Missing discussion of Bahl et al. (CVPR 2023) on directly predicting hand-object interaction locations.
  - Reviewer 4 suggests exploring pre-trained VLMs for state-change reasoning instead of the proposed method, and evaluating on longer-horizon tasks.
- **Computational cost**: Training takes 3 days on 5 A6000 GPUs – expensive for the observed gains (R3).
- **Reproducibility**: Real-world test interaction counts not given; release of real-world dataset not committed.
