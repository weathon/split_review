## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces a Trajectory Score Matching (TSM) method to reduce the accumulated error in Interval Score Matching (ISM) for text-to-3D generation. TSM uses DDIM inversion to generate two paths from the same starting point instead of one, and further proposes pixel-by-pixel gradient clipping when using Stable Diffusion XL to stabilize training. The paper is well-written and clearly organized. Reviewers were mixed: three gave score 3 (borderline reject) and one gave score 5 (marginally positive), though all pointed to significant weaknesses in motivation, theoretical rigor, experimental justification, and fairness of comparisons.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and clearly organized.
- It offers a sound theoretical background on TSM, with an intuitive explanation of how comparing two trajectories from the same starting point reduces error accumulation from DDIM inversion compared to ISM.
- TSM is a novel technical innovation that addresses the accumulated error issue in ISM.
- The authors demonstrate advantages of TSM in reducing accumulated error and improving stability, supported by experiments.
- The pixel-by-pixel gradient clipping method for SDXL guidance is a practical engineering contribution.
- The paper clearly distinguishes the differences between TSM and ISM.
- The discussion of related work helps readers understand the progress in the field.

### Weaknesses

- **Unclear motivation.** The term “pseudo ground truth inconsistency” is never clearly defined (R1). The motivation for using dual paths appears somewhat ad‑hoc (R3). The overall goal of reducing prediction error is inadequately explained (R4).
- **Insufficient justification of TSM’s advantage.** No concrete failure modes of ISM caused by DDIM inversion error are shown (R1). Figure 2 still shows inconsistencies for TSM (variations in hair, face color, neck) and both ISM and TSM produce mostly identical results, so the advantage is not convincingly demonstrated (R3, R4). If the goal is simply to reduce error, setting \( t = \mu \) would eliminate the error to zero; reducing \( \delta_T = t - s \) in ISM could also achieve error reduction (R4). An experiment comparing ISM and TSM using the same \( t - \mu = \delta_T \) is necessary (R4).
- **Weak theoretical foundation.** The naive distillation objective in Eq. (7) lacks intuitiveness (R4). The proof of Proposition 1 appears incorrect because it directly compares vectors instead of their norm lengths; without a complete proof it is difficult to accept that TSM effectively reduces “errors” (R4). Figure 3 does not clarify the differences, and the main benefit seems to be reduction of gradient variance, which is not justified by quantitative measurements (R4). It is unclear whether classifier‑free guidance was accounted for when computing the reconstruction term in Figure 3 (R4).
- **Insufficient comparisons and ablation.** Comparisons between TSM and ISM appear only in Figure 5, which is insufficient to demonstrate effectiveness (R1, R3). Ablation studies are lacking; the qualitative results in the appendix show only marginal improvement (R1). No video demos of the generated 3D results are provided (R3). The paper claims two contributions (TSM and pixel‑level gradient clipping) but ablation for each is insufficient (R1).
- **Unfair comparisons and lack of standardized evaluation.** The quantitative evaluation in Table 1 uses different base diffusion models: TSM uses SDXL while baselines (e.g., LucidDreamer) use SD2.1, making the comparison unfair (R1, R2, R4). The major performance gain in Table 1 likely comes from the stronger prior model, not the proposed contributions (R1). When TSM uses SD2.1, it shows only minimal improvements over ISM (R1, R4). What diffusion model was used by LucidDreamer in Table 1? An eye‑to‑eye comparison with the same diffusion model is essential (R3, R4). The dataset used for Table 1 is not specified; it should be a common benchmark such as the T³ benchmark (R3). The A‑LPIPS metric in Section 4.2 is not common; the authors need to cite relevant work and provide a detailed explanation (R4). The number of prompts and seeds used in Section 4.2 is not reported; at least 25 samples are needed for a robust measurement (R4).
- **Missing time‑cost analysis.** SDXL is significantly slower than SD2.1 in 2D generation, implying even slower 3D generation, but the paper does not discuss comparative time consumption experiments (R2).
- **Limited discussion of failure cases.** The paper briefly mentions issues like light handling but provides no detailed analysis of when the model fails or limitations in complex scenarios (R2).
