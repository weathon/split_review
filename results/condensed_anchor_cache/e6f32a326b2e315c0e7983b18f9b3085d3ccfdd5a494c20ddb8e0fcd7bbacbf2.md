## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose Wavelet Optimal Transport (WOT), a method that aligns unpaired single-cell multi-omic datasets by using spectral graph wavelets (SGW) to compute intra-domain distances at multiple scales, combined with Gromov-Wasserstein optimal transport. Two variants are introduced: E-WOT (entropy-based heuristic filtering) and L-WOT (learned filters via alternating optimization). Experiments on simulated and real single-cell datasets (SNARE-seq, scGEM) show improvements over baselines such as SCOT, SCOTv2, UnionCom, Pamona, MMD-MA, Cross-modal AE, BindSC, and Seurat. The problem is well-motivated, and the method is novel, but significant concerns remain about hyperparameter tuning, presentation clarity, theoretical depth, and experimental completeness.

### Strengths
- **Problem motivation**: Aligning unpaired single-cell datasets across modalities is an important and challenging problem; the paper clearly justifies the need for methods robust to noise, dropout, and non-isometry.
- **Novel combination**: Using spectral graph wavelets for multi-resolution dataset alignment is a novel approach, and the framework generalizes Gromov-Wasserstein under specific conditions (Remark 1).
- **Empirical effectiveness**: WOT consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods (e.g., SCOTv2, UnionCom, Pamona) in high-noise, non-isometric, and dropout settings across synthetic and real single-cell multi-omic datasets (SNARE-seq and scGEM). On SNARE-seq, E-WOT achieves label transfer accuracy 0.945; on scGEM, L-WOT achieves 0.663.
- **Flexibility**: The method allows diverse design choices (kernels, wavelets, scale aggregation, filters) and two optimization strategies (E-WOT and L-WOT) to adapt to dataset characteristics.
- **Mathematical rigor**: The algorithm is well-grounded, relying on projected gradient descent and alternating minimization; theoretical noise-robustness is discussed.
- **Coverage of scenarios**: Experiments include simulated data, shape correspondence (SHREC20), and two real single-cell multi-omic datasets, with comparisons to multiple relevant baselines.

### Weaknesses
1. **Hyperparameter tuning and fairness of comparisons** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). The method has many hyperparameters: kNN graph size (k), RBF bandwidth (σ), scale parameters (S), entropic regularization (ε), aggregation function (min/max/sum), wavelet generating function (low-pass heat kernel, tight-frame Meyer, etc.) with its own hyperparameters, threshold δ for L-WOT, KDE bandwidth for E-WOT, etc. The authors state experiments are “fully unpaired” with no validation data for tuning, yet Appendix C reports different hyperparameters per dataset (e.g., aggregation function varies). The guidance in Appendix C.1 is insufficient: it only cites a self-tuning heuristic from Demetci (2022b) that accounts for k and ε, leaving many parameters unaddressed. If hyperparameters were tuned using the test data, then baseline comparisons are unfair—the correct baselines should be the optimally tuned numbers from Demetci (2022a): SCOTv2 0.927, SCOT 0.982 on SNARE-seq; SCOTv2 0.643, SCOT 0.576 on scGEM—which exceed or match several WOT results. Even after rebuttal, the hyperparameter tuning procedure does not account for parameters like ρ (balancing OT terms), which is critical for real unpaired datasets with disproportionate cell-type representation. The fixed hyperparameter setting limits applicability; an adaptive strategy or sensitivity analysis is needed.
2. **Presentation and audience disconnect** (Reviewers 2, 3). The paper is too technical without sufficient connection to single-cell biology. Terms like “scale” are never explained in the biological context (e.g., what do different wavelet scales represent for single-cell data?). Section 3 on the algorithm is nearly isolated from the application. The evaluation metrics (geodesic distance, label transfer accuracy, FOSCTTM) are used without clear explanation of their meaning for practitioners. Figure 1 is unclear in illustrating both the task and framework. The paper also contains misspellings (e.g., “throughput” for “throughout” in line 012).
3. **Theoretical and ablation depth** (Reviewers 1, 4). The method combines established concepts (SGW + GW + filtering) with limited new theoretical analysis. Important questions remain: (a) Which scales are most relevant and how much do they contribute? Performance variability between E-WOT and L-WOT (e.g., L-WOT much better on scGEM, E-WOT better on SNARE-seq) is not explained beyond superficial speculation about dedifferentiation. (b) No clear rationale for choosing aggregation method (sum vs. max) or how it interacts with filtering and scale selection. (c) Convergence of L-WOT is not discussed; the bilevel optimization (non-convex inner/outer loops) may be sensitive to initialization. (d) Entropy-based filtering (E-WOT) uses fixed KDE bandwidth (0.4); no justification or exploration of adaptive bandwidth (Scott’s, Silverman’s). (e) Chebyshev polynomial approximation for SGW is used without analysis of its accuracy vs. exact computation or impact on results.
4. **Experimental scope and comparators** (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). (a) **Missing baselines**: No comparisons with more recent single-cell paired alignment methods such as Harmony and scDML. Section 2.1’s description of related work weaknesses is insufficient. (b) **Limited real-world data**: Only SNARE-seq and scGEM are used; results would be more convincing with diverse datasets from different tissues and sequencing technologies. (c) **Two-dataset limitation**: The method is designed only for aligning two datasets, while multi-dataset alignment is common. (d) **SHREC20 experiments**: How were baselines (SCOT, SCOTv2, UnionCom, Pamona) hyperparameters chosen? Task-specific baseline methods (e.g., shape correspondence methods) are missing in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. (e) **No validation-data comparison**: The paper does not address whether WOT’s advantage persists when validation data is available for all methods (i.e., is the benefit from a better self-tuning procedure or inherent algorithm quality?). (f) **High-dimensional scalability**: Common single-cell datasets have dimensions >20K (even after feature selection, 1-3K genes); experiments use only 10-30 dimensions. How does the method handle high dimensions? (g) **Figure 8**: It does not show whether SNARE-seq and ATAC-seq datasets were mixed successfully (need coloring by dataset origin).
5. **Computational and practical considerations** (Reviewers 1, 4). Runtime and memory benchmarking is insufficient beyond Appendix E; no breakdown of cost per step (graph construction, wavelet decomposition, OT). The fully-connected graph construction is expensive; scalability for large datasets is unclear. For practical use, the large number of tunable parameters and lack of automatic tuning heuristics (beyond k and ε) limit ease of adoption. Additionally, the claim that previous methods do not explicitly reduce noise (Section 1) is inaccurate: UnionCom, Pamona, SCOT, SCOTv2 use kNN on dimensionality-reduced data, and cross-modal autoencoders learn low-noise latent spaces, albeit naively.
6. **General applicability vs. single-cell focus** (Reviewers 3, 4). The method seems general-purpose for noisy, non-isometric spaces, but the paper positions it only for single cells without testing other domains (e.g., shape correspondence is already shown, but not as a primary contribution). If it is general, broader experiments and discussion would strengthen the contribution. If limited to single cells, the paper must address unique challenges like high dimensionality and multiple dataset alignment.
