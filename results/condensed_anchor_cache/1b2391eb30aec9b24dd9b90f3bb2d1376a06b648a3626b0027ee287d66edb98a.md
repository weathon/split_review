## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approach to Departure Metering (DM) for mixed-mode runways (arrivals and departures sharing a runway). The problem is cast as an MDP, with a state representation using a spatio-temporal event graph that captures airside hotspots, a pushback-rate action (0–4 in discrete levels), and a reward combining taxi efficiency and runway utilization. The agent is trained with PPO on simulated traffic from Singapore Changi Airport. Results on a typical operational day show 1–3 minute average peak taxi time reductions and ~27% fuel savings relative to uncontrolled pushback.

### Strengths
- The airside event graph state representation, inspired by temporal constraint networks, captures spatial-temporal aircraft movements and multiple conflict types (following, crossing, head-on), providing a sophisticated modeling approach.
- The pushback-rate action space (rather than binary on/off metering) is more practical for real-world air traffic control.
- The method is evaluated on realistic, high-fidelity traffic scenarios from Singapore Changi Airport, including quantitative fuel consumption analysis.
- Significant performance gains are demonstrated over uncontrolled pushback and competing approaches (tabu search, baseline DRL), especially under high traffic density.
- The domain-inspired MDP choices are well motivated, and the paper clearly explains the problem and approach.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of generalization.** The method is validated only on Singapore Changi Airport. No experiments or analysis are provided for airports with different layouts, traffic patterns, or operational procedures, making transferability and scalability unclear.
- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons.** The evaluation lacks comparisons with other DRL methods (e.g., MARL, alternative RL algorithms), simpler optimization approaches (e.g., model predictive control / rolling horizon optimization), or more fine-grained ablation studies. The choice of PPO is not justified, and it is not tested whether the framework is agnostic to the RL algorithm.
- **Questionable necessity of DRL.** Some reviewers argue that the problem is centralized, has a low-dimensional state space, and important safety constraints, which make a deterministic optimization method (e.g., MPC with explicitly defined trade-offs) more appropriate and interpretable. The black-box nature of DRL makes constraint satisfaction and policy interpretation difficult.
- **Ambiguous definition of “hotspots.”** The paper does not specify the precise criteria (aircraft density, congestion duration, spatial extent) used to identify hotspots, which can lead to a convoluted state space and suboptimal policies.
- **Inadequate reward function detail.** The reward design, including component weights (taxi time vs. fuel savings vs. runway utilization), is not transparently defined. This makes it hard to assess alignment with objectives or potential biases that incentivize unintended behaviors.
- **Action space concerns.** The pushback rate is discretized into only five levels (0–4); one reviewer incorrectly perceived the action space as continuous, indicating unclear presentation. Some reviewers believe a continuous or more fine-grained action space would allow more nuanced control.
- **Missing analysis of mixed-runway utilization.** The algorithm does not explicitly exploit the mixed-mode runway property (e.g., balancing departure slots with arrival streams). Moreover, for a large airport like Changi, it is unclear whether runways are actually operated in mixed mode.
- **Oversimplified airside dynamics.** The action space is limited to pushback rate control; other impactful actions (e.g., adjusting taxi routes, prioritizing flights, coordinating with ground control) are not considered. The model also ignores the cost of holding aircraft at gates (e.g., freeing gates for arriving aircraft).
- **Lack of algorithm novelty.** The work is a case-study application of existing DRL methods; no new algorithm or general methodological contribution is provided.
- **No comparison with multi-agent RL.** Questions about how the single-agent approach compares with MARL, how agents would communicate, and how credit assignment / reward shaping would work in a multi-agent setting are left unanswered.
- **No discussion of real-world integration.** How the policy would perform with human controllers in the loop vs. full automation is not addressed.
- **Hyperparameter tuning.** The paper does not discuss how hyperparameters are set (e.g., manual tuning vs. automated search), raising reproducibility concerns.
