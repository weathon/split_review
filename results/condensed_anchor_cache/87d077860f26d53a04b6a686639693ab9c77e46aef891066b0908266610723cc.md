## Merged Review

### Summary
LLM-Exp proposes using two off-the-shelf LLMs to adaptively adjust the exploration distribution in deep Q-learning. One LLM analyzes a recent action-reward trajectory (without state information) to summarize the agent’s learning status; a second LLM generates an action distribution for exploration based on that summary. The distribution is updated every K episodes. The method is tested on Atari benchmarks with several DQN variants and LLM backbones.

### Strengths
- The idea of using LLMs to improve policy exploration is interesting and novel (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- The paper targets the important problem of the exploration-exploitation tradeoff in RL (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3).
- The method is clearly presented, well described, and easy to understand and implement (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).
- The method is a simple plug-in design compatible with DQN and its variants, and experiments include multiple DQN backbones and several LLM choices (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- Diverse evaluation across multiple Atari games (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Triviality and limited technical contribution.** One reviewer (score 5, more positive overall) found the LLM use “kind of trivial,” arguing that LLMs’ broad world knowledge could be better used for developing generalist RL agents rather than just for exploration. Another reviewer (score 3) judged the technical contributions as limited, noting the core pipeline is essentially prompt design (Figure 1). (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Unconvincing necessity and insufficient baselines.** The necessity of using LLMs over existing lightweight exploration methods (e.g., ε-greedy, Gaussian noise, Random Network Distillation) is not well demonstrated. The paper lacks comparisons to other LLM-based RL approaches (e.g., CLIP embeddings by Tam et al., 2022; LLMs as value functions or policies) and to common exploration techniques. Performance of DDQN+LLM-Exp falls significantly short of state-of-the-art Atari benchmarks—the original DDQN paper (2015, page 10) reports much stronger results, and more recent Atari studies are not matched. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
- **Action space and domain limitations.** LLM-Exp is restricted to discrete action spaces and is demonstrated only on Atari. Extending to continuous action spaces (e.g., robotics, MuJoCo) is unclear and considered unlikely to work well because action distributions are high-dimensional and exploration requirements vary per state. The prompts are specialized for Atari, so universality to general domains remains unknown. (Reviewers 1, 3, 4)
- **Design and input choices.** Feeding only action-reward trajectories without state information is counter-intuitive and likely limits the performance upper bound. The use of two separate LLMs (one for status analysis, one for strategy recommendation) is not justified; an ablation with a single LLM showed similar performance while saving substantial compute, raising questions about the necessity of two LLMs. (Reviewers 1, 4)
- **Experimental rigor and reporting.** No measure of spread (standard deviation) is reported in tables, making it difficult to assess significance. Experiments were run with only three random seeds, fewer than the typical five or more in Atari studies. Improvement is small on most environments except a few (Pacman, Freeway, Alien, Hero). Because a task description is given, the LLM may have memorized known solutions; it is unclear whether the method would work on novel environments without well-known solutions. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Related work coverage.** The review does not thoroughly address recent advances that apply LLMs directly within RL frameworks. (Reviewer 3)
- **Parameter sensitivity.** No discussion or guidance is provided on optimizing the sampling parameters M and K for different environments; the method’s sensitivity to these parameters is noted. (Reviewer 3)

*Contrast: One reviewer (score 5, confidence 4) was more positive overall, finding the idea interesting and the presentation clear, but still raised significant concerns about triviality and domain limitations. The other three reviewers (scores 3, confidences 3–4) were less positive, emphasizing lack of rigor, insufficient baselines, and weak experimental evidence.*
