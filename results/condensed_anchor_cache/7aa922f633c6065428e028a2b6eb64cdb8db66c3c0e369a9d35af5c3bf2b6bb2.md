## Merged Review

### Summary

Four reviewers give scores of 6, 3, 6, 3, indicating strong disagreement. Two reviewers (R1, R3) find the method strong and well-presented; two (R2, R4) raise major concerns about novelty, experimental validation, and baselines. The paper proposes a rehearsal-based video continual learning method that stores compressed neural codes (via a VQ-VAE) instead of raw frames, combined with a code-refreshing mechanism to handle representational drift. The method is evaluated on EpicKitchens-100 and Kinetics-700 for noun and action classification under incremental learning and pretraining settings.

### Strengths

- The problem statement is interesting and practically relevant, addressing the high memory footprint of video for rehearsal-based continual learning in applications like robotics and surveillance (R1, R3). The approach of storing compressed codes rather than raw frames is a reasonable and novel way to reduce memory demands (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- Combining a set of frozen decoders (one per task) with a continuously trained encoder (compressor) to mitigate representational drift is an interesting and novel configuration (R3). The code-refreshing mechanism is a sensible addition (R2).
- The paper is well-written and nicely motivated, with a clear structure and a helpful Figure 2 (R1, R3). The discussion of biological inspiration is insightful (R3).
- The experimental setup is clearly explained and exhaustive: three settings (IID sampling, incremental learning, CL with pretraining) on two large-scale long-video benchmarks (Kinetics-700, EpicKitchen-100). Memory constraints and compression rates are clearly defined (R1, R2).
- The proposed method outperforms the baselines by large margins on all benchmarks (R1, R3). While one reviewer (R2) strongly contests the baseline choices, others (R3) find them appropriate. Ablation studies in the appendix are appreciated (R4).
- The work has potential significance for memory-limited real-world applications (R2).
- (R4) The use of compressed representations in this context is original.

### Weaknesses

- **Memory trade-offs not quantified**: The paper does not quantify the additional memory required to store decoder weights from previous tasks compared to the savings from compressed codes. Similarly, the memory size of the autoencoder parameters relative to the replay buffer is not reported, leaving overall trade-offs unclear (R1, R3). This is a critical omission for a method claiming memory efficiency.
- **Sufficiency of a single latent code for long videos**: No analysis or ablation is provided on how the compressed representation performs across varying video lengths or diversity (e.g., short vs. long videos, scenes with many vs. few action changes). The paper should clarify whether there is a maximum video length beyond which performance degrades significantly (R1). The number of frames per video used in training/evaluation is not stated (R1).
- **Limited novelty in memory efficiency solutions (minority view – R2)**: The paper does not compare against prior memory-efficient video CL methods such as **vCLIMB** (which uses frame selection and temporal consistency regularization) or **FrameMaker** (which uses frame condensing with prompts). The absence of these comparisons weakens the claimed novelty and memory-efficiency contribution. (R2) *Note: R4 considers the approach original; this is a clear point of disagreement.*
- **No comparison to rehearsal-free methods (minority view – R2)**: If memory efficiency is the goal, rehearsal-free methods like **ST-Prompt** (vision-language models with temporal prompts) or **DPAT** (decoupled prompt-adapter tuning) avoid memory buffers entirely. The paper does not discuss or compare against such methods, which would provide a more complete assessment. (R2)
- **Inadequate baselines (minority view – R2)**: Using **GDumb** (2020) as the primary baseline is insufficient. More recent and sophisticated rehearsal-based methods (e.g., **ER-ACE**, **L2P**) should be included. (R2) *Note: R3 states that baselines are appropriate; this is a point of disagreement.*
- **Insufficient justification of benchmark and novelty claims (minority view – R2)**: The paper introduces a new benchmark with a pre-training phase followed by incremental learning, but a similar setup already exists (Park et al., 2021). No clear rationale is given for why the new benchmark is necessary or superior. The claim of being *“the first to extend CL to large-scale naturally-collected long videos”* is unsubstantiated: prior works have used ActivityNet, Kinetics, Something-Something, and EpicKitchens (e.g., vCLIMB, DPAT). A rigorous comparison to existing setups is needed. (R2)
- **Insufficient related work on video CL**: The related works section under “Continual Learning with Images and Videos” cites only one prior video CL method. To support the claim of being the first practical large-scale long-video approach, a more thorough review is required, including works like **CLAD** (Verwimp et al., 2023) and **Label-efficient online continual object detection** (Wu et al., 2023) (R3).
- **No statistical uncertainty**: Results are reported without confidence intervals, standard errors, or error bars on Figure 3. Multiple training runs and variability estimates are needed to assess reliability (R3). R4 also notes a lack of significance analysis.
- **Limited experimental validation (minority view – R4)**: Only two tasks (noun and action classification) on two datasets are evaluated. To demonstrate generalizability, more datasets (e.g., Ego4D, Something-Something v2) and more tasks (e.g., dense prediction, segmentation, optical flow) should be included. (R4)
- **Lack of analysis of learned representations**: The paper provides minimal analysis of how the compressed representations evolve as tasks are introduced – e.g., whether they change gradually or abruptly, and whether they remain meaningful for old tasks. Visualizations of the learned representation space would be valuable. The main ablation only studies previous-task buffer size; other ablations are relegated to the appendix without sufficient discussion of their significance (R4).
- **Clarity issues**: The paper is hard to follow in places. The problem setup (definition of tasks, how tasks evolve over time) is not clearly defined. The caption of Figure 2 is insufficient; the model should be understandable from that figure alone (R4). Additionally:
  - No basic statistics on the datasets (e.g., video lengths, number of actions/objects, labeling scheme, handling of transitions) are provided (R3).
  - The method is inconsistently referred to as “BootstrapCL” in tables but not introduced in the text (R3).
  - The dimension “224×224×14” for video clips is unexplained (grayscale? 14 frames?) and appears inconsistent with the claim of processing long videos (R3).
  - It is unclear whether the continual learning setting is class-incremental or task-incremental (i.e., does the model need the task identity during evaluation?) (R3).
  - “Average accuracy” is not defined – is it the average over tasks at the end of the sequence, or averaged after each task increment? (R3).
  - The buffer sample selection strategy is not described for the baselines (e.g., are samples randomly selected from each task?). The statement that “no frame selection or sampling strategy” is needed for very large videos is ambiguous (R3).
  - The compressor’s “continuous” training is ambiguous: is a single encoder updated with each task, or is a separate encoder trained from scratch for each task, or is the previous encoder frozen and copied? (R3). Equation 10 implies the same encoder for all tasks, but equations 6–7 show different encoder versions per task, contradicting the text (R3).
  - There are typos and formatting issues (e.g., “a concatenation of m samples from each of the task”, “we store the resulting the codes”). References are incomplete, lacking conference/journal names (R3).
  - The average forgetting (AvgF) metric is referenced but not defined in the paper (R3).
  - The terms “compressor”/“decompressor” and “encoder”/“decoder” are used interchangeably; consistency is needed (R3).
