## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Self-Rationalization, an iterative method for improving LLM-as-a-judge models by having them generate multiple judgments with rationales for the same input, curating preference pairs from their own outputs (using ground-truth alignment, majority vote, or meta-judge scoring), and performing two rounds of SFT followed by DPO, all without additional human-labeled data. Evaluated on RewardBench, BiGGen Bench, and Feedback Bench, the approach shows modest gains over SFT baselines and achieves comparable or slightly better performance than some larger models, with improved rationale quality in human evaluation.

### Strengths
- Self-refinement via RLAIF for judge models is an important research direction; generating rationales enhances both performance and interpretability.
- The approach is practical and resource-efficient, requiring no additional human-labeled data for preference construction, making it broadly applicable.
- The method demonstrates that even for models under 10B parameters, a preference dataset can be built without external data using rationale trajectories and post-filtering (e.g., majority voting on scores), which is useful for domains without exact answer matches.
- The paper provides extensive evaluation across multiple benchmarks (RewardBench, BiGGen Bench, Feedback Bench) and includes comparisons against both comparable and larger models.
- Iterative DPO is shown to improve rationale quality over SFT in human evaluation.
- The ability to handle custom fine-grained criteria and produce ratings with accompanying rationales is a practical advantage.

### Weaknesses
**Novelty and relation to prior work**
- The concept of generating rationales for LLM judges is explored in concurrent works (Ankner et al. 2024, “Critique-out-Loud Reward Models”; Zhang et al. 2024, “Generative Verifiers”) and the paper does not clearly differentiate its contributions from these or discuss their pros/cons.
- The methodology heavily resembles Self-Rewarding LLMs (which also uses iterative DPO on self-generated judgments), although that work used pairwise judgments and synthetic data generation. The basic self-improvement pipeline (generate → preference → DPO → iterate) is the same, and  the paper does not adequately highlight its unique contributions (e.g., using seed training data for preference vs. synthetic generation, pointwise vs. pairwise assessment).
- The training dataset and SFT initialization follow Prometheus; the main novelty relative to prior RLAIF judge models is the heuristic for preference selection, which some reviewers view as incremental.

**Limited and unconvincing performance gains**
- The improvements over simple SFT and single-stage DPO are modest: in Fig. 2, the win rate over SFT is only 55% (5% above random), and in Table 2, gains over single-stage DPO are minimal, raising questions about the effectiveness of iterative refinement and rationales.
- On RewardBench (Table 3), the gain from including rationales is only 2 points (72.0 → 74.0); pairwise-trained Skywork-Critic-Llama-3.1-8B achieves 89.0, suggesting that rationales do not provide a clear advantage over simpler approaches.
- A statement in line 322 appears to be reversed (says “performs significantly worse” when the context suggests “significantly better”), indicating an error.
- The claimed reduction in training samples and compute resources (line 311) is not substantiated with specific comparisons.
- The claim that long rationales introduce noise (line 317) is not supported by experimental evidence.

**Insufficient baselines and evaluations**
- Missing comparisons with iterative self-improvement approaches such as Self-Taught Evaluators.
- No pairwise evaluation on standard benchmarks (RewardBench, PreferenceBench, InstruSum, HHH) despite training for both pointwise and pairwise tasks.
- Missing baselines like NCSOFT/Llama-3-OffsetBias-8B and a Llama 8B version of Self-Rewarding LLMs.
- Only a single model family and size is used; the results may not generalize across different architectures or scales (e.g., smaller or larger models, other base LLMs).

**Lack of analysis and clarity in preference construction**
- No exploration of how the diversity of generated rationales (number, quality, sampling temperature, nucleus parameters) affects downstream performance; it is unclear whether simply generating more (similar) rationales is beneficial.
- Relying solely on high-temperature sampling to produce rejected rationales may not generate sufficiently diverse or challenging negative examples; using prompts to elicit incorrect rationales could improve training.
- The difference between pointwise and pairwise assessment is not justified experimentally; potential issues with pointwise training (e.g., scores becoming “capped” at maximum after iterations, as noted in Meta-Rewarding) are not discussed.
- The margin definition for preference selection (line 371) is not fully explained: how is the margin set, what happens when all differences fall below the margin (are those samples discarded?), and which specific pairing method (correct-answer vs. majority vote vs. meta-judge) is used in the main results (Tables 2, 3)?
- The use of weight-merging instead of training on a mixed dataset is not justified; similarly, the choice to use different data quantities across the two iterations (line 259) is unexplained.
- A minority reviewer (R5) argues that without analysis of whether oversampling rationale trajectories is more efficient than using a small human preference filter, the work lacks justification over prior studies (e.g., Meta-Rewarding Language Models) and may therefore be insufficiently novel.

**Writing and clarity issues**
- The introduction is redundant (first paragraph introduces RLAIF, second revisits RLHF limitations); the logical chain from rationale generation to calibrated evaluation is vague (line 52).
- The Background section does not clarify why pointwise vs. pairwise assessment is so important.
- Several citations are incorrectly formatted (e.g., line 483).
- The caption for Table 1 uses unclear phrasing (“whether synthetic seed prompts need to be generated”).
- The same reference is cited twice (Kim 2023 and Kim 2024a in line 247) when they appear to be the same work.
- Several specific experiment details are missing: how majority vote score distribution is calculated (line 213), what “use ground truth score to guide selection” means (line 293), what “ground truth rationale” refers to (line 345), amount of data used in Figure 2.
- Training time comparisons (Self-Rationalization vs. baselines) are not provided.
