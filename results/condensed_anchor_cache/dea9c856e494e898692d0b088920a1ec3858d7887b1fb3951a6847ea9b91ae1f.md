## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper explores whether diffusion language models (LMs) can match autoregressive LMs on general language tasks when scaled in model size and finetuned on instructions. The authors adapt pretrained masked LMs (XLM-R, sizes 86M–9.7B) into generative discrete diffusion models via the RDM method. They perform task-specific finetuning on two machine translation datasets (IWSLT14, WMT14) and one summarization dataset (Gigaword-10K), then instruction finetuning on FLAN 2022 for zero-shot and few-shot in-context learning evaluation. Results show that larger diffusion LMs consistently improve performance on the supervised tasks and can achieve competitive zero-shot results after instruction tuning, with some indications of reasoning abilities.

### Strengths

- Important topic: one of the first studies to systematically investigate scaling behavior (model size, instruction finetuning) of diffusion LMs, potentially stimulating further research on non-autoregressive LMs (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Diverse and comprehensive set of experiments covering task-specific finetuning (MT, summarization), instruction finetuning, zero-shot, few-shot in-context learning, and reasoning – giving a complete picture of diffusion LM capabilities (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- Demonstrates that diffusion LMs adapted from pretrained MLMs (via RDM) can achieve strong performance on supervised tasks and generalize after instruction tuning; the diffusive adaptation (lightweight continual pretraining) is a practical strategy under computation constraints (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Scaling model size from 86M to 9.7B consistently improves performance across all downstream tasks, confirming that diffusion LMs benefit from scale (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The final qualitative example showing a diffusion LM generating in a causal order for chain-of-thought reasoning (contrasting with autoregressive linear generation) is novel and interesting (Reviewer 1; but Reviewer 4 notes it is only a single example).
- Paper is well-written and clearly explains prior work on discrete diffusion and the RDM method (Reviewers 1, 3).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited methodological novelty.** The core method – using pretrained masked LMs as a knowledge base and finetuning them with a diffusion objective – is essentially the same as prior works (RDM-Diffusion [2], DiffuSeq [3]) and only differs by using XLM-R instead of BERT (Reviewers 2, 3). No comparison to training a diffusion LM from scratch is provided, so it is unclear if diffusion adaptation is effective (Reviewer 4).
- **Unsurprising scaling results.** The observation that larger XLM-R backbones improve performance is not surprising because XLM-R itself scales; the benefit likely comes from pretraining, not from diffusion adaptation (Reviewer 1, 2). The paper claims to study scaling with data but only varies model size and instruction tuning, not data volume (Reviewer 1).
- **Incomplete and confusing experimental comparisons.** Table 1 has missing cells, notably for the XLM-R-BASE autoregressive baseline, and the comparison to encoder-decoder models (T5) is unclear because of different architectures, sizes, and pretraining setups (Reviewer 1, 3, 4). Important baselines at comparable scale (e.g., GPT-style decoder models, vanilla autoregressive finetuning) are absent (Reviewer 3). Table 2 uses supervised AR as reference instead of zero-shot AR (Reviewer 4). The paper also lacks comparisons to other non-autoregressive LMs and to MCMC sampling from MLMs (Reviewers 4, 2).
- **In-context learning results are weak.** Adding more in-context examples shows little or negative improvement, suggesting these diffusion LMs are good zero-shot learners but poor in-context learners (Reviewer 1). No evaluation on the raw pretrained diffusion LM (before instruction finetuning) is provided to isolate ICL ability (Reviewer 4). The limited context length of the original model (XLM-R) may hinder few-shot performance (Reviewer 4).
- **Reasoning analysis is anecdotal.** The discussion of causal generation order rests on a single qualitative chain-of-thought example; a systematic evaluation on a benchmark or probe dataset is needed to draw concrete conclusions (Reviewers 1, 4). Overall reasoning performance is insufficient to demonstrate capability, and deficiencies may be due to insufficient training rather than inherent model limitations (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing ablation on diffusion configurations.** The paper does not explore the number of diffusion steps, noise levels, or the length predictor – all factors that affect generation quality, diversity, and inference latency (Reviewer 3). It is unclear whether larger models reduce the need for more steps or improve length prediction (Reviewer 3). Inference computation cost compared to autoregressive LMs is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
- **Unexplained or contradictory trends.** BBH-NLP does not follow the overall scaling trend in Figure 4 (Reviewer 3). The performance gap between oracle and predicted length is opposite for the two MT tasks in Figure 3, with no explanation (Reviewer 3).
- **Terminology confusion.** Calling BERT-like encoder models “decoder-only” is misleading; no comparison to actual decoder-only (GPT-like) models is made (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient discussion of prior scaling work.** This is not the first work on scaling text diffusions; prior scaling laws [2] should be contrasted (Reviewer 2). The paper also does not discuss potential ceilings from the base MLM’s own scaling limitations (Reviewer 2).
- **Overall evaluation effectiveness.** Because the adapted diffusion LMs use outdated checkpoints (XLM-R) and diffusion adaptation likely loses performance relative to training from scratch, the current results may be considerably inferior to what diffusion LMs could achieve under state-of-the-art training recipes – limiting the conclusions about true capabilities (Reviewer 4).
