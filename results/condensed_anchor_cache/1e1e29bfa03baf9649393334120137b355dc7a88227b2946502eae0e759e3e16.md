## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes LoRA-Gen, a framework that uses a larger cloud-side LLM (LLaMA-8B) to generate meta tokens from task-specific prompts; these tokens are then used by a routing model to compose LoRA modules (from a set of pre-trained experts) into adapter weights for a smaller edge-side language model. The method aims to achieve task specialization while compressing the input context and improving inference efficiency. Evaluations on common-sense reasoning and intelligent agent tasks show improved accuracy and latency compared to LoRA and LoRA-MoE baselines, with a 2.1× speedup on TinyLLaMA-1.1B reasoning tasks and a 10.1× compression ratio with Gemma-2B on agent tasks.

### Strengths
- **Novelty and problem motivation**: The core idea of using a large cloud-side model to generate parameters for a smaller edge-side model is a realistic and underexplored setting for on-device specialization (R1, R4). Hyper-networks for LoRA modules enabling projection to unseen tasks is an interesting and promising direction (R4).
- **Layerwise LoRA-MoE design**: The architectural contribution of composing LoRA experts from meta tokens is innovative and adds to the field of model adaptation (R2).
- **Strong empirical results on efficiency**: The method achieves significant inference speedup (2.1×) and compression gains (10.1×) over previous methods, primarily due to context compression (R2, R3). The latency reductions are valuable for resource-constrained deployments (R2).
- **Generalization to unseen tasks**: LoRA-Gen demonstrates knowledge transfer from large to small models and flexible adaptation (R3).
- **Clear hyperparameter setup**: Reproducibility is supported by the reported configuration (R3).
- **Evaluation coverage**: Experiments include both reasoning classification tasks and complex agent-based scenarios (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing critical implementation details**:
  - The meta-token generation process and their representation are not adequately explained. It is unclear whether they are generated in a training-free manner or require training, and whether they have a layer-specific mapping to the edge-side LM (R1, R2, R4). The description “by generates” (line 218) should be “by generating” (R4).
  - The ‘direct’ method in Table 8 is not introduced or differentiated (R2).
  - The cloud-side LM’s role during inference is ambiguous: whether it is used for every inference or only during LoRA weight generation affects latency/resource claims (R2). The paper states “our method is cost-free during inference” (line 259), but this is misleading if the method is task-specific and requires prior knowledge of the task (R2).
- **Unfair latency comparisons and task specification**:
  - Baseline methods (LoRA, LoRA-MoE, MixLoRA) are task-agnostic or designed for multi-task scenarios, whereas LoRA-Gen is task-specific. The latency advantages may stem from task specification rather than architectural improvements, making direct comparisons questionable (R2). The claim of 10.1× speedup (line 100) appears to refer to prompt compression ratio, but actual latency reduction is only 2.4×, showing a discrepancy (R4).
- **Statistical rigor concerns**:
  - Results lack confidence intervals, variance, or statistical significance testing (R1, R2, R4). Given small models (1.1–2B) and small datasets (e.g., WinoGrande), significance testing (e.g., bootstrap test) is necessary (R4). Validation set results are not reported, which is needed to show that hyperparameters were not selected on test set (R4).
- **Missing baselines**:
  - The method’s latency gains come primarily from context compression, but no comparisons to prior context compression methods (gisting, AutoCompressors, ICAE, 500xCompressor) are provided. At least one such baseline is required to assess whether gains are substantial (R4).
- **Training compute cost unreported**:
  - LoRA-Gen requires additional training compute from the cloud-side model, but FLOPs or training time are not compared against baselines. This omission makes it difficult to assess the trade-off between training cost and inference gains (R4).
- **Performance inconsistencies and modest multi-task gains**:
  - The method does not consistently outperform baselines: on SIQA it is inferior, and improvements on Gemma-2B are inconsistent (R1). Multi-task learning gains are modest given the added complexity of indirect LoRA generation, so the paper’s claims would be stronger if focused more on efficiency benefits (R3).
- **Terminology and presentation issues**:
  - “Edge-side language models” is used throughout without a proper introduction (R1).
  - Meta tokens are described with extra terminology but appear to be just regular LLaMA tokens from the prompt—this should be clarified and simplified (R4).
  - Dataset sizes and task names should be explicitly stated; grouping diverse tasks under “Commonsense Reasoning Datasets” is misleading (R2).
  - Sections 4.1 and 4.3 have redundant dataset introductions (R2).
  - Citation errors: e.g., LLaMA3 incorrectly cites Touvron et al. (2023b) instead of Dubey et al. (2024); many citations should use `\citep` instead of `\cite` (R1, R4).
  - Formatting errors: “Sotmax” in Eq. 4 (R3), “which maintaining” in Section 4.3 (R3).
- **Ablation and hyperparameter questions**:
  - In Table 8, the auxiliary loss coefficient (α) yields worse accuracy at 0.1 than without it, and performance increases as α decreases to 0.01. It is unclear whether even smaller values would continue to improve performance (R3).
