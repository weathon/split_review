- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 3, 3, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CURE, a multi-norm certified training framework for union certified robustness against \(L_\infty\), \(L_2\), and geometric perturbations. It introduces a deterministic \(L_2\) certified training method (modifying SABR) and three multi-norm variants (Joint, Max, Random), plus gradient projection (GP) and bound alignment. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and TinyImageNet report up to 23.9% improvement in union certified accuracy and better generalization to unseen geometric perturbations. Reviewers strongly disagree on contribution and theoretical grounding: three reviewers rate the paper 3 (reject-level) and find the work incremental, lacking theoretical justification, and suffering from clarity issues; one reviewer rates it 6 (accept-level), calling the problem novel and the approach promising with valuable insights.

### Strengths
- Addresses a challenge not tackled before: training certifiably robust models for multiple perturbation types simultaneously. (R4)
- The framework (including gradient projection and bound alignment) yields strong empirical wins on multi-norm certified robustness and on geometric perturbations. (R2, R4)
- Extensive evaluations on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and TinyImageNet for \(L_\infty\), \(L_2\), and several geometric transformations, with documented settings. (R2)
- Hyperparameters and pseudocode are provided in the appendix, aiding reproducibility. (R2)
- Experimental results demonstrate effectiveness of the proposed method on union robustness. (R1)
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow. (R3, R4)

### Weaknesses
- The core contribution is incremental: the new loss is merely an adaptation of the unified loss used in general adversarial training, and the deterministic \(L_2\) method replaces a hyper-box with a hyper-sphere in SABR without analysis or theoretical justification, making it a baseline rather than a new method. (R1, R2)
- The three multi-norm variants (Joint, Max, Random) naturally arise from combining separate \(L_2\) and \(L_\infty\) methods and appear to be baselines, not genuine contributions. (R2)
- CURE-Finetune is simply CURE-Scratch applied to a pre-trained model with small changes; it does not warrant a new name. (R2)
- The claim of “universal” certified robustness is not justified: only \(L_2\) and \(L_\infty\) norm bounds are considered; results across a wider range of \(L_p\) norms and other perturbation types (e.g., geometric) are missing. (R1, R4)
- No theoretical analysis is provided that explains how the proposed loss functions contribute to improved certified robustness, or why the multi-norm framework is specifically effective for certified training versus general adversarial training. (R1, R3)
- The notation is confusing (e.g., “\(A_u \rightarrow A_q\)” in line 283, equation 2 is clumsy) and many equations are unnecessary (e.g., equation 7 takes a union of parameters, equation 8 a linear combination). Definitions in 4.1 and 4.2 are rough, lack formality, and do not contribute to understanding. (R2, R3)
- The \(L_2\) certified training method suffers a significant drop in natural accuracy, mitigated only by using a very small \(\lambda_2\), making it very close to standard adversarial training. The reviewer suggests that using the \(L_\infty\) box as a proxy for \(L_2\) robustness can cause the propagated distance to be 4–6 times larger than \(\epsilon\) (e.g., for CIFAR-10 \(\epsilon=2/255\), \(\lambda=0.1\)), introducing unnecessary regularization. Alternative strategies for constructing the \(L_\infty\) box should be analyzed. (R4)
- Bound Alignment is complex and poorly motivated: it is unclear why aligning bounds across perturbations is beneficial, and there is an inconsistency between Definition 4.2 (which seems to use bounds for the whole \(\epsilon\)-ball) and Algorithm 5 (which computes loss_KL for the small box). (R4)
- The paper lacks computational complexity analysis, which is especially important given the high cost of certified training. (R3)
- Experiments could be more comprehensive: comparison with adversarial training augmented by DDPM-generated data (referenced as DM-Improves-AT) is missing, and such data might also improve certified robustness. (R3)
- The logical relationship in lines 282–284 requires clarification: is it a necessary or sufficient condition? In the worst case \(A_u = A_q = 0\), does optimizing \(A_u \rightarrow A_q\) converge to the worst? Why is optimizing \(A_u \rightarrow A_q\) better than direct optimization toward \(100\%\)? Can the authors prove that the ideal case \(A_u = A_q\) holds for any training set drawn from an unknown distribution, and that this solution is optimal over the training set? (R3)
- Line 297–298 claims “similarly, \(\gamma\) can be regarded as…” without justification; the causality is not evident. (R3)
- Missing technical details: the implementation of IBP loss computation (deriving lower/upper bounds) is not self-consistent, and the \(L_\infty\) subselection rate \(\lambda_\infty\) is only cited as “same as SABR” with no given value. (R2, R3, R4)
- Several additional questions are raised that overlap with missing experiments or ablations: (R4)
  - Can geometric perturbations be directly included in the training framework?
  - The results on MNIST+Translation are unexpectedly low – any insights? What about CIFAR-10?
  - When is GP applied? Appendix A says “after training with the final epsilon values” – is that after epsilon-annealing?
  - Could the effect of Bound Alignment be achieved by giving higher weight to samples from \(\gamma\) in the \(L_r\) loss?
  - The learning rate is reported as \(1\times10^{-4}\), but SABR uses \(5\times10^{-4}\) – is this a mistake or a deliberate change?
- The paper’s overall contribution is considered incremental without substantial theoretical innovation; it does not meet the bar of ICLR. (R3) (One reviewer, R4, disagrees and sees the work as novel and promising.)
