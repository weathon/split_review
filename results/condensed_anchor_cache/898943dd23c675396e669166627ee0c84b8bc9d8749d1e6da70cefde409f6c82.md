## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether transformers can learn Bayesian networks in-context. The authors provide a theoretical construction of a 2-layer transformer that can estimate conditional probabilities of a Bayesian network from in-context samples and autoregressively generate new samples. Empirical experiments on simulated data (three graph structures: general graph, tree, chain) with binary variables are conducted to validate the theory. Reviewer ratings span from 1 to 5, with substantial disagreement: Reviewers 1 and 3 are positive (both score 5), Reviewer 2 is strongly negative (score 1), and Reviewer 4 is mildly negative (score 3).

### Strengths
- The problem of whether transformers can learn Bayesian networks in-context is interesting and relevant to the ICLR community (Reviewers 1, 4).
- The research question – whether transformers can estimate multivariate discrete distributions in-context – has not been studied before (Reviewer 2).
- The paper provides a theoretical construction of a simple 2-layer transformer that can estimate conditional probabilities of Bayesian networks and generate new samples from the inferred model (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The experimental framework is well-defined, with comparisons across different variable numbers and Bayesian network types (general graph, tree, chain), and detailed description of qualitative differences (Reviewer 3).
- The benchmark of Bayesian networks for studying ICL capabilities is interesting (Reviewer 4).
- The paper simplifies the tricky problem of learning a Bayesian network using a curriculum approach, introducing only a few variables at each stage (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Misrepresentation of the problem**: The paper claims to learn Bayesian networks, but the chain-rule factorization used is generic for any distribution and does not capture the conditional independences that define the true Bayesian network. Bayesian networks are anyway not identifiable from observed data (Reviewer 2). Other reviewers assume the framing is acceptable, so this constitutes a strong minority criticism.
- **Unclear significance**: The broader implication of showing that transformers can estimate joint distributions of discrete variables in-context is not properly contextualized. What questions does this help answer in broader machine learning? (Reviewer 2).
- **Gap between theory and practice**: The theoretical construction shows that a specific parameterization exists, but it does not guarantee that standard training procedures (gradient descent) will converge to such a parameterization. This limitation should be acknowledged more explicitly (Reviewer 1, also echoed by Reviewer 3 who notes no evidence that trained transformers implement the proposed algorithm).
- **No evidence that trained transformers implement the proposed algorithm**: The paper does not analyze attention patterns or conduct causal studies (e.g., ablations) to verify if the trained model uses the construction from Theorem 4.1 and Section 6, as done in prior work (Reviewer 3).
- **Unrealistic assumptions about causal order**: The curriculum and the theorem require a priori knowledge of the causal order (variables indexed accordingly). Does the main theorem hold when the ordering is unknown, e.g., variables are permuted uniformly in the samples? This significantly limits practical applicability (Reviewer 1, also Question).
- **Lack of real-world data validation**: Experiments are only on simulated data; even a few real-world datasets would help assess practical relevance (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing clarity in technical setup**:
  - The training objective is not specified: is the transformer a causal decoding model trained to minimize negative log-likelihood of the next variable given previous ones? This is essential (Reviewer 2, also Question from Reviewer 2).
  - Notation is undefined: e.g., \(0_{dm}\), \(\mathbf{e}_{N+1}\); dimensions in Eq. 3.2 are mismatched – each \(\mathbf{x}_{ij}\) is a \(d\)-dimensional one-hot, but the last row uses vector \(p\) of dimension \((M+1)d\) (Reviewer 2). Also, Algorithm 3.1 input size seems to be \((2M+1)d \times (N+1)\) while the Read Function takes \((2M+1)d \times N\) (Reviewer 4).
  - The output of the final linear layer does not produce probabilities that sum to 1; for categorical variables the output should be a vector in the \(d\)-dimensional simplex (Reviewer 2).
  - In Section 5.1, the dimensions of \(p\) and \(p_q\) change compared to Eq. 3.1 (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing details on empirical comparisons**: The phrases “naive Bayes” and “Bayesian inference” are used without explanation of how they are applied to the density estimation problem or how their accuracy is calculated in Figures 2, 4, 6 (Reviewers 2, 3). Also, “the probability distribution of those graphs” (lines 261-262) is not parseable (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited empirical scope**: Only three graph structures are considered (general graph, tree, chain); more varied and diverse graphs should be evaluated. Variables are only binary; the analysis should be extended to larger vocabularies (Reviewer 4).
- **Contradiction between theory and experiments**: A 1-layer transformer appears sufficient in experiments, while the theoretical construction requires 2 layers. The paper should elaborate on this discrepancy (Reviewer 4).
- **Robustness not addressed**: The paper does not consider more realistic settings, such as sensitivity to positional embeddings (Reviewer 3).
- **Notation and presentation issues**: The paper is hard to follow due to confusing notation; training and test dataset generation (Section 5.1 Datasets and Metrics) is unclear (Reviewer 4). Several typos: L152 “will-known” → “well-known”, L198 “paramter” → “parameter”, L294/L667 missing citation and “nad” → “and” (Reviewer 1); missing link in “Curriculum Design” paragraph (Reviewer 4).
