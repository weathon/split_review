## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces RM-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating reward models that measures sensitivity to subtle content differences and resistance to style biases (length, markdown formatting). It constructs chosen/rejected responses from the same model (GPT-4o), injects subtle errors in rejected responses, and controls style via prompting (concise, detailed, markdown). The style-substance evaluation matrix yields easy/normal/hard accuracy metrics. Experiments on ~40 reward models show even top models average only 46.6% under style bias, and RM-Bench performance correlates better with downstream policy model alignment (Tulu-v2.5) than RewardBench.

### Strengths
- **Originality**: First benchmark controlling for style in reward model evaluation; the methodology is simple, structured, and generic (can extend to other categories and datasets).
- **Motivation & Methodology**: Well-motivated contrast to RewardBench; explicit prompts to control style bias; style-substance matrix directly quantifies bias and its impact on reward predictions.
- **Comprehensiveness & Significance**: Large-scale evaluation of many public reward models (Appendix K) reveals that even the best are far from robust, exposing a key weakness. Correlation with policy model performance (DPO, RLHF) is better than prior benchmarks, supporting RM-Bench’s validity.
- **Clarity & Rigor**: Paper is clearly written, figures are effective, and three main takeaways are well-evidenced. The evaluation design (style-controlled generation, domain-specific error injection) is sufficiently described for reproducibility.

### Weaknesses
- **Data & Reproducibility**: The exact dataset is not publicly available; a canonical version on HuggingFace would aid reproducibility (R3). The prompts used to generate plain/concise/markdown variants and the quality-check procedures are missing (R1, R2).
- **GPT-4o Reliance**: Benchmark construction relies solely on GPT-4o for generation. Including other top models (Claude, Gemini) would improve deployment readiness (R1). The paper acknowledges this only in the Limitations appendix.
- **Correlation Analysis Limited**: The correlation with policy model performance (Section 5, Table 4) uses only Tulu-v2.5; results should be verified with at least one more base model (e.g., Qwen, LLaMA) (R1). Similarly, correlation for each domain (math, code, safety) separately would be informative (R3).
- **Role of Style-Controlled Variants Unclear**: The contribution of style-controlled pairs (y^{L,M}) to accuracy and correlation is not isolated. Compare RM-Bench performance with and without style variants (i.e., using only y^{L,M} pairs) to understand their impact (R2). This would also address whether the improved correlation over RewardBench is due to style control (R3 wants an ablation).
- **Missing Ablation / Contradictory Results**: The Nectar dataset DPO result appears to contradict the overall conclusion about substance > style; clarification needed (R2). The claim that style bias is the key factor would be strengthened by ablating style control (R3).
- **Methodology Details Insufficient**:
    - Length control (Table 2): target length ranges, and how core information is preserved after summarization, are not specified (R1).
    - Human filtering drop (519→183) in Section 3.1: is this due to chosen responses being incorrect, or jailbreak failure? Elaborate on effectiveness (R2).
    - HumanEvalPack: does multiple-sampling rejected samples improve over using existing buggy solutions? (R2)
    - Section I instruction says “introduce one error” but examples have several; could this cause over-filtering? (R2)
    - Section 4.3: correctness/verbosity scores are per-prompt and may not be comparable; using accuracy r(y_c) > r(y_r) would be more interpretable (R2).
- **Benchmark Hacking Concern**: Augmenting reward model training with style-controlled generation (as in Section 3.4) could inflate RM-Bench accuracy without improving policy model performance; discuss this risk (R2).
- **DPO Analysis**:
    - How does DPO model perform using hard accuracy instead of average accuracy? (R2)
    - Reference policy initialization (π_ref = 1) may be suboptimal; compare with maximizing likelihood of preferred completions (R2).
- **Presentation Issues**: Minor typos (e.g., “Border Impacts” → “Broader Impact”, “open-end” → “open-ended”, missing words in Section 3.1) should be corrected (R1).
- **Figures/Tables Clarifications Needed**:
    - Figure 4: what do the four data points signify? (different preference datasets?) Clarify and show similar graph for Code Hard or Code Normal accuracy (R1).
    - Figure 8 (Appendix H): y-axis label – relative accuracy or score? (R1).
    - Overlap in scatterplot (Figure, Section 5): explain despite almost identical average tokens for short/long responses in Math/Code (Table 2); provide min, max, median lengths (R1).
