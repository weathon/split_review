## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents REvolve, an evolutionary framework that combines LLMs with human feedback to generate reward functions for RL. It uses genetic operators (mutation, crossover, selection) and human labelers as the fitness function, extending Eureka (which does not maintain populations or use crossovers). Experiments are conducted on three simulated tasks: autonomous driving, humanoid locomotion, and Adroit hand door opening. Ablations test the effect of human ratings as fitness functions, and full Elo scores between REvolve, Eureka, and their ablations are reported.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written, with a well-illustrated technical approach (Figure 1 and Algorithm 1).
- Experiments cover a wide range of task types (continuous/discrete action spaces, diverse observation spaces) and include comparisons with many baselines.
- Good ablation study that isolates the effect of human ratings as fitness functions for both REvolve and Eureka.
- Human evaluations are conducted and full Elo scores are reported, including comparisons with ablations.
- Detailed experimental setup is provided in the appendix.
- The evolutionary algorithm (mutation, crossover, selection) overcomes the greedy search limitation of Eureka.
- REvolve leverages LLMs to generate explicit reward functions while using human preference to guide generation implicitly, enhancing interpretability and alignment.

### Weaknesses
- The role of the “fitness score” is ambiguous: it is used as a supervisory signal for the genetic algorithm in Sections 2–3, but becomes an evaluation metric in Section 4. This raises concerns:
    - If the fitness score effectively measures agent performance, why not use it directly as the reward function?
    - If the fitness score guides reward generation, it is unfair to compare against baselines that do not receive this supervision during training.
- The paper provides limited insights beyond the result that costly human feedback achieves better performance; there is no deeper analysis justifying why the approach is necessary for the chosen tasks.
- Pipeline complexity and uncertainties: no reported runtime for the full process, and no discussion of cases where the reward function fails to converge due to difficulty in summarizing human language feedback.
- Natural language feedback is extracted from a predefined checklist of attributes that the user ticks, requiring manual engineering per task. This may bias LLM prompting and constrains feedback to a small discrete set.
- The total human effort is not quantified. REvolve requires: (a) defining the checklist per task, (b) providing hundreds of pairwise ratings and natural language feedback over N=5 generations. This likely far exceeds the effort to define a single fitness function in Eureka. (Reviewer 2 notes that the promising aspect is that REvolve auto outperforms Eureka auto.)
- Additional tuning parameters (mutation probability p_m, number of islands) add to practitioner effort.
- The proportion of LLM-generated reward functions that were executable is not reported (Eureka reported many non-executable functions).
- The relative importance of the pairwise preference assessment vs. natural language feedback is not ablated.
- Open-form natural language feedback (unconstrained by checkboxes) was not tested.
- If the same human evaluators provided feedback during training and evaluated the final policies (Table 2), non-auto methods gain an unfair advantage due to evaluator familiarity.
- The crossover mechanism for selecting “most effective” reward components is unclear.
- No evaluation comparison is shown across generations; it is unknown whether REvolve consistently outperforms Eureka or learns faster (e.g., Figure 3 left shows auto growing faster than human feedback; Figure 3 right shows auto growing exponentially while others grow linearly).
- Feasibility on real robot tasks is questionable: sim2real is difficult for high-DOF robots, and real training would be expensive given the high demand for human feedback.
- The paper lacks a comparison to implicit reward design methods, such as learning a reward model from human preference (RLHF), in terms of performance, interpretability, and computational requirements.
- In Figure 3, if the auto variant surpasses human feedback given more generations, would human evaluators still prefer human feedback? More generations are needed to see a clear trend.
