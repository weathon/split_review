## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers generally agree that the paper demonstrates highly effective adaptive jailbreaking attacks achieving near-perfect attack success rates (ASR) across a wide range of safety-aligned LLMs, including closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5, Claude) and open-source models (Llama-2/3, Mistral, Vicuna, Gemma, etc.). The method combines custom prompt templates with random search on adversarial suffixes (using logprobs where available) and a prefilling attack for Claude. The self-transfer approach and the extension to trojan detection (winning the SaTML’24 competition) are noted as positive aspects. However, opinions diverge on scientific novelty: some reviewers see the work as a straightforward engineering effort rather than a novel contribution, while others value the demonstration of saturation in attack capability. Concerns are raised about the lack of systematic guidelines for prompt design, missing baselines, inconsistent comparisons with prior work, limited discussion of limitations and defenses, and organizational issues. Several reviewers would raise their scores if these points are addressed.

### Strengths
- **High attack success rate across many models**: Achieves 100% ASR (by GPT-4 judge) on Vicuna-13B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3-Mini, Nemotron-4-340B, Llama-2-Chat-7B/13B/70B, Llama-3-Instruct-8B, Gemma-7B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, R2D2 (from HarmBench), and all Claude models (via prefilling). GPT-4 Turbo reaches 96% ASR. This demonstrates that even the latest safety-aligned models are vulnerable to simple adaptive attacks.
- **Effective on black-box closed-source models**: The method successfully jailbreaks GPT-4o and Claude models, which do not expose logprobs, using transfer or prefilling attacks. The prefilling attack achieves 100% ASR on all Claude models.
- **Self-transfer approach is insightful**: Initializing the random search on adversarial suffixes found for easier examples and then transferring to harder prompts improves efficiency and effectiveness.
- **Extension to trojan detection**: The same random search technique (with restricted token sets) won first place in the SaTML’24 Trojan Detection Competition, demonstrating the generality of the approach.
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The paper tests a wide range of models (both open and closed source) and provides detailed results in Table 1 and throughout.
- **Simple, straightforward method**: The attack is easy to understand and reproduce, with code provided.
- **Important implications for the community**: The near-perfect ASR indicates attack saturation, suggesting the community should prioritize better defense/alignment over additional marginal improvements in attack methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and scientific contribution**: The core technique (random search on a suffix, custom prompt templates) is not novel; similar methods have been explored. The paper is seen by some as a technical report rather than a research paper with rigorous scientific design, challenges, and solutions. No guidelines or systematic methodology for adaptive attack generation are provided (contrary to claims in lines 93-98).
- **Heavy reliance on ad‑hoc custom prompt templates**: The attack success heavily depends on manually crafted prompt templates (e.g., Fig. 1) that instruct the model to bypass safety mechanisms. The paper does not provide a scientific way to obtain these templates, nor ablations of their components. For GPT-4o, a separate template is used, limiting generalizability. The method is not adaptive in prompt design; the same template is reused for most models.
- **Missing baselines and unfair comparisons**: The paper does not compare against key prior attacks such as Many Shot Jailbreaking, AutoDAN, or PAIR on a level playing field. Reported prior work ASRs use different data and evaluation methods, making it impossible to fairly situate the results. Table 1 lacks clear references to the compared prior attacks.
- **Evaluation setup is unclear**: It is not specified which 50 samples from AdvBench are used, whether the same set is used for training and testing, or which subset is used for each model. The judgment model (GPT-4) is mentioned but details on false positives, sensitivity of the 10/10 jailbreak score, and whether the judge is consistent across models are missing.
- **Limited adaptivity and robustness analysis**: The attack strategies are tailored to individual models, requiring frequent updates. The random search space is large, but no discussion is given on how to effectively search the token space or the computational cost. The benefit of self-transfer initialization is not separated from simply using double the random search budget.
- **No testing against known defenses**: The paper does not evaluate whether the attack is robust to defenses like SmoothLLM or RPO. The attack is also easy to detect via monitoring (e.g., the request “don’t start with \”I can’t assist with that\”” is suspicious), but this is not discussed.
- **Trojan detection section is disjointed**: The link to jailbreaking is not clearly explained, and no citations for backdoor attacks in LLMs are provided. The section feels separate from the main narrative.
- **Overclaiming and minor inaccuracies**: The abstract and TL;DR claim 100% ASR on all leading safety-aligned LLMs, but GPT-4 Turbo achieves 96%. Table 1 may overclaim when using modified system prompts for Claude (pg. 3) without clarifying the exact setup. Some model versions are not specified (e.g., which GPT-4o version, which Llama-3 checkpoint).
- **Lack of ethical considerations and disclosure**: The paper demonstrates powerful attacks but does not include a discussion of ethical concerns, responsible disclosure, or efforts to inform model developers.
- **Organization**: Section 5 (trojan detection) is poorly contextualized, making it hard to follow how it fits with the rest. The summary in Table 1 is confusing (“prev” vs. “Ours” without clear reference to prior work). Abbreviation “RLHF” is introduced without definition (line 423).
