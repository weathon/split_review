## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Penalized Autophagy Estimation (PLE), a method to improve fairness and mitigate model autophagy disorder (MADness) in generative models. The key idea is to add a regularization term that penalizes discrepancies between a generative model’s weights when trained on real data versus its own synthetic data. Because the naive loss is intractable, the authors use hypernetworks to generate the generative model’s parameters, providing a scalable implementation. Experiments on synthetic distributions, MNIST (VAE), and CIFAR-10 (BigGAN) show improvements in fairness metrics (class-ratio bias) and stability in self-consumed training loops. The paper also includes theoretical discussion connecting MLE bias, fairness, and MADness. Two reviewers assign 5, two assign 3, indicating a split in overall assessment.

### Strengths
- Novel theoretical contribution connecting statistical bias in MLE to fairness and MADness issues in generative models.
- The proposed method (PLE) is intuitive, easy to understand, and offers an original solution to mitigate MADness.
- Comprehensive empirical validation is reported, covering multiple types of distributions and models (VAE, BigGAN), with results showing meaningful improvements in both fairness and stability.
- The paper is well-structured, clearly written, and makes concepts like hypernetworks and MADness accessible to a broad audience.
- The discussion of fairness and bias in generative models, including LLM relevance, addresses an important and timely problem.
- Results regarding fairness (class-ratio correction on MNIST) are considered experimentally compelling.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited experimental scope and inconsistency:** Fairness is evaluated only on MNIST (VAE); MADness mitigation is evaluated only on CIFAR-10 (BigGAN). No evidence that the method mitigates MADness on MNIST or improves fairness on CIFAR-10. The deep generative experiments are restricted to CIFAR-10. Testing on newer architectures (e.g., diffusion models) is absent.
- **Missing comparisons to existing fairness/bias mitigation methods:** The paper does not compare conceptually or empirically with prior work such as FairGAN, Fair generative modeling via weak supervision, RepFair-GAN, or TabFairGAN. No justification for why PLE is preferable to simpler statistical techniques (e.g., upweighting minority class loss).
- **Insufficient justification for how the method reduces unfairness:** It is unclear how constraining the MLE to yield consistent parameters on real vs. synthetic data (Equation 4) mitigates bias, especially when the original training data itself is already biased. No theoretical explanation is provided.
- **Concerns about the validity of the hypernetwork approach:** The hypernetwork may collapse to a trivial solution (weights near zero, biases near θ*), making retraining redundant. A suggested diagnostic test: using only “airplane” samples from CIFAR-10 to generate images via the hypernetwork—if the method works, the output should be only airplanes; otherwise the network may barely change or produce garbage.
- **No generated images from CIFAR-10 are shown** in the paper or appendix, making it hard to visually assess quality.
- **No ablation study on the key hyperparameter λ (fixed at 0.1):** Systematic variation of λ is missing; its impact on generation quality, when MADness occurs, and on fairness is unexplored. Reviewers also ask whether λ=0.1 has theoretical justification or was chosen empirically.
- **Unclear trends in MADness experiments:** In Figure 2, FID still increases with PLE (though slower); no explanation for this remaining increase or for the spike at iteration 4. In Figure 4, some distributions show stable performance while others show increasing MADness; reasons are not explained.
- **Arbitrary choice of minority class on MNIST:** The minority class (digit 6) was chosen arbitrarily; the paper should instead select the class with the highest false-negative rate when classified as minority (as done in prior work).
- **Hypernetwork dependency increases complexity and practical usage burden** without clear evidence that it is superior to simpler alternatives.
- **Theoretical vs. practical gap:** Although LLMs are discussed extensively (Section 1.3), the method is not applied to text generation. A direct application to LLMs would strengthen relevance.
- **Presentation issues:** The introduction’s motivation and problem setup are not well structured, making the core contribution hard to grasp initially. The appendix contains background material (sections B, C, D, E) not directly linked to the main contribution; these could be replaced by references. Several typos exist (e.g., “Autophogy” → “Autophagy”, duplicate words, missing punctuation).
