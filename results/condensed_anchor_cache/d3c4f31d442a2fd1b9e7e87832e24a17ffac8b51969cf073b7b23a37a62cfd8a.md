## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between pretraining data frequency and the formation of linear representations (specifically Linear Relational Embeddings, LREs) for factual recall relations in language models. Using OLMo 1B, OLMo 7B, and GPT-J (6B), the authors empirically show that:
- The quality of linear representations is strongly correlated with subject-object co-occurrence frequency in pretraining data, more so than with individual term frequencies.
- Linear representations form at predictable frequency thresholds (e.g., 1–2k co-occurrences for 7B models), and this threshold depends on model scale.
- These representations can be used to predict term and co-occurrence frequencies in the pretraining corpus, even across different models and datasets, offering a potential method for analyzing closed-source model training data.
- A tool for searching tokenized text is also released.

All four reviewers (scores 6,6,6,6) find the work valuable for understanding why some relations exhibit linear structures while others do not, and for providing a new perspective on interpretability and data provenance. However, they note limitations in scope, generalizability, and the strength of some claims.

### Strengths
- **Important research question:** Exploring the origin of linear representations in LMs is a key problem in interpretability. The paper identifies a correlation between linear representations of factual recall relations and subject-object co-occurrence frequency in pretraining data.
- **Strong empirical evidence:** The correlation between average co-occurrence frequency and LRE quality surpasses individual correlations with subject or object frequencies, highlighting the importance of co-occurrence.
- **Robustness across training stages:** The finding that linear representations can form at any pretraining stage as long as the co-occurrence threshold is met is particularly intriguing.
- **LRE methodology:** The paper builds on Linear Relational Embeddings, which effectively approximate LLM computations for factual triplets, and shows how frequency directly impacts their formation.
- **Predictive utility:** The ability to use LRE features to predict term frequencies from the pretraining corpus provides a promising technique for analyzing closed-source models.
- **Tool release:** A tool for searching tokenized text is provided to support understanding of training data.
- **Comprehensive related work:** Reviewer 4 notes the discussion of related work is comprehensive.
- **Investigation of few-shot accuracy:** The relationship between few-shot accuracy and existence of linear representations is studied.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited scope and generalizability (multiple reviewers):**
  - Only 25 factual relations from the Relations dataset are analyzed. It is unclear whether findings hold for other relation types (abstract concepts, events, etc.) or for other linear/non-linear structures beyond LREs (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
  - The paper focuses exclusively on LREs; other forms of linear or non-linear representation might behave differently (Reviewer 3). The limitations of LREs in capturing complex relationships (e.g., non-linear transformations, attention mechanisms) are not explored.
- **Weakness of causal and predictive claims (Reviewer 4, with support from others):**
  - The claim that the frequency threshold decreases with model size is only tested on two model sizes (OLMo-1B and OLMo-7B); GPT-J (6B) has a smaller threshold than OLMo-7B, contradicting a simple scaling trend (Reviewer 4).
  - The claim that linear representations form at “predictable” thresholds regardless of training stage is not well supported: it is not shown that the threshold (mean causality >0.9) is consistent across different checkpoints; scatter plots per stage with fitted slopes are missing (Reviewer 4, see also Reviewer 1’s question on training dynamics).
  - Predicting term or co-occurrence frequencies is a direct consequence of the correlation and not a major standalone contribution (Reviewer 4). Moreover, co-occurrence frequency prediction achieves only marginal improvements over the mean baseline (Reviewers 3, 4). The regression model may simply be learning to predict the mean frequency (Reviewer 3).
  - The claim that the regression “transfers well” across models (e.g., “around the same accuracy”) is not supported: Table 1 shows a substantial drop when training on OLMo and evaluating on GPT-J, larger than the gap between LRE features and the mean baseline (Reviewer 4). Table 2 only gives a few examples, not aggregated statistics (Reviewer 4).
  - The efficiency of the released tokenized-text search tool is not discussed or compared to existing methods like WIMBD (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing justification and experimental details (multiple reviewers):**
  - Figure 3 and Table 1 results do not match: the mean frequency baseline differs, and LRE features in Table 1 (0.76) seem better than LRE+LM in Figure 3 (~0.67) for OLMo; no explanation is given (Reviewer 4).
  - Table 2: “Predictions are better for relations that are closer to those found in fitting the relation (country-related relations)” — it is unclear what “closer” means and how it was measured (Reviewer 4).
  - Table 2: “Some relations, like star-constellation perform very poorly, possibly due to low frequency” — no evidence that low frequency is the cause (Reviewer 4).
  - Figures: The two left plots in Figure 2 need clearer explanation (why some points darker, meaning of grey lines, different shapes for GPT-J). Table 1 headers “Train OLMo” and “Train GPT-J” are not self-explanatory (Reviewer 4).
  - Why only 25 relations? No discussion of how representative they are (Reviewer 4).
  - Section 4.3 describes observations but lacks a clear research question or implication (Reviewer 4).
- **Context and confounding factors (Reviewers 2, 3):**
  - The LRE experiments are performed with few-shot in-context examples (e.g., “X plays the Y” examples); it is unclear if findings generalize to generation without ICL (Reviewer 2).
  - Other factors (context, syntax, semantics, template memorization) are not controlled and may confound the observed correlation (Reviewer 3). A discussion of these factors and their potential influence is missing.
- **Theoretical depth (Reviewer 1):** The paper provides one empirical perspective but lacks theoretical discussion on training dynamics and the observed frequency thresholds.
- **Minor issues (Reviewer 4):** Typos (line 455 “be” missing, line 416 paragraph does not end properly).
