## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a Vision-Language-based Survival Analysis (VLSA) paradigm for computational pathology, aiming to overcome data scarcity and weak supervision in gigapixel whole-slide image (WSI) analysis. VLSA leverages pathology vision-language (VL) foundation models, introduces prognostic language priors from GPT-4o, ordinal survival prompt learning for continuous time-to-event labels, and an ordinal incidence function prediction target. The authors present extensive experiments on five datasets with ablation studies and interpretability via Shapley values. Reviewers recognize the novelty of adapting VL models to survival analysis with ordinal design and the value of interpretability, but raise substantial concerns about experimental rigor, fairness of comparisons, justification of key design choices, and the sufficiency of evidence for claimed advantages. Reviewer 1 (score 5) is more positive about overall outperformance but flags binning and metric issues; Reviewer 2 (score 6) and Reviewer 3 (score 6) are more critical about baseline selection, justification, and clinical evaluation.

### Strengths
- First work to adapt vision-language foundation models to survival analysis in computational pathology, with ordinal prompts tailored to the task.
- The introduction of prognostic language prior (from GPT-4o) is empirically validated to significantly improve survival analysis performance.
- The framework is complete and novel, comprising ordinal survival prompt learning, ordinal incidence function, and language-encoded aggregation guidance.
- Interpretability is provided via Shapley values combined with natural language descriptions, which is important for clinical trust and transparency.
- The paper is overall well-written and addresses a practically important problem; the proposed method outperforms reported benchmarks in several settings.

### Weaknesses
- **Data efficiency claim vs. experimental evidence:** The paper conflates computational efficiency (smaller model, MACs) with data efficiency (performance under limited training samples). The claim that the method addresses limited training data is not convincingly demonstrated; the core advantage of language priors over vision-only foundation models (e.g., UNI) for data scarcity is not rigorously tested. A direct comparison controlling for feature dimensionality and pretraining dataset size is needed.
- **Ordinal incidence function (IF) justification:** The assumption of a consecutive probability decline for bins away from the event time is questionable when events can occur multiple times; the model only addresses first events. Furthermore, the ordinal survival prompt ignores censorship status—assigning the same prompt to censored and uncensored cases with identical follow-up time is inappropriate (e.g., a short follow-up censored case should not be placed in “very poor prognosis”).
- **Baseline comparisons are inadequate and unfair:**
  - The number of time bins is set to sqrt(N events) with insufficient justification, and no comparison with other binning strategies is provided. Since baselines like PatchGCN originally used 4 bins, the comparison is not conducted under identical binning conditions, which may bias results.
  - The VLM baselines (CoOp, OrdinalCLIP) are trained on natural images, not pathology data; this introduces a confounding variable. Comparisons should include VLMs pre-trained or fine-tuned on pathology data.
  - The claim that “highly-expressive networks cause overfitting” is not supported because no such state-of-the-art models (e.g., CAMIL, R2T, DTFD-MIL, HAG-MIL, MHIM-MIL) are compared. Additionally, the language prior constitutes an extra modality; multimodal survival models that integrate WSI and text (e.g., MCAT, MOTCat, MMP, CMTA, MoME) should be adapted and compared to isolate the impact of the pathology foundation model.
  - A stronger baseline with naive prompts that simply treat survival as a classification task is missing.
- **Marginal gains from ordinal prompt components:** Tables 3 and 8 show that gains from the two ordinal prompt designs (ordinal survival prompt and ordinal incidence function) are small or even negative. The authors should justify why these components are necessary.
- **Few-shot learning comparison is unfair:** CONCH (used in VLSA) aligns visual and text spaces well, whereas CoOp and OrdinalCLIP are equipped with ABMIL which is not text-space-aligned. For a fair evaluation, prompts from CoOp/OrdinalCLIP should be evaluated on the same CONCH backbone only.
- **Incomplete survival analysis evaluation:** The paper omits standard clinical protocols such as Kaplan-Meier survival curves for risk stratification groups, log-rank test statistics, hazard ratios, and confidence intervals. These are essential to assess clinical relevance.
- **Preprocessing and data issues:** Patches in Appendix E contain mostly white-space; clarification on filtering (e.g., blanks, blur, artifacts) is needed.
- **Missing code:** The code is not released, which hinders reproducibility.
- **Figure/equation clarity issues:**
  - In Equations 2 and 3, it is unclear how the model enforces multi-level representation learning; no explicit constraint is described.
  - Figure 3(a) is difficult to interpret—axes and explanations are missing.
  - The term “cross-attention” in line 163 is misleading; it should be similarity matching.
  - Figure 1 has rendering issues in “context prompt”.
- **Potential data contamination:** CONCH is pretrained on large public pathology data; the authors should ensure that test samples in the few-shot setting were not seen during pretraining.
- **Spelling error:** Line 44 – “exciting progress” (likely “exciting progress”).
