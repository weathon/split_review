## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes DynVLA, an adversarial attack that injects dynamic perturbations into the vision-language connector of MLLMs by adding a Gaussian kernel to attention maps. It is evaluated across open-source models (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT4, LLaVA) and a closed-source model (Gemini) on classification, captioning, and VQA tasks. The four reviewers disagree on overall quality: two assign a score of 5 (positive) and two assign a score of 3 (critical). The method is praised for targeting vision-language alignment and for its thorough evaluation, but criticized for limited novelty, weak transferability beyond Q-former architectures, overclaimed results, missing comparisons with state-of-the-art transferability methods, and insufficient ablation.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written and clearly structured.
- It provides a comprehensive evaluation across multiple model architectures (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT4, LLaVA) and both open- and closed-source models (e.g., Gemini), spanning different tasks (classification, captioning, VQA).
- It introduces a novel approach that targets vision-language alignment rather than vision-specific perturbations, which addresses an important and practical problem in MLLM robustness.
- The methodology is well-founded, with the Gaussian kernel providing a refinement to the attention mechanism.
- The study is timely and relevant to multi-modal AI security.

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited transferability contradicts core claim.** The method achieves substantial improvements only on Q-former-based models (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT4). On LLaVA (Table 4) the improvement is only ~1%, and on V1.6-LLaMA3 it is 0.1% (1 successful attack out of 1000 images). These results undercut the paper’s claim of strong transferability across diverse architectures. [R2, R4]

2. **Unfair and missing baselines.** The main experiments use an extremely large PGD step count (T=2000), which causes severe overfitting and reduces transferability; the proposed Gaussian kernel likely acts as a regularizer. The paper does not compare with standard PGD setups or with established methods for improving adversarial transferability (e.g., MI, DI, SGA, CroPA). Three of the four baselines are from before 2020, and no comparison with the latest transferability-enhancing techniques is provided. [R2, R4]

3. **Overclaimed statements.** DynVLA is claimed to “not require any prior knowledge of the model,” yet all surrogate models in Tables 1–3 share Q-former connectors; tests on structurally different models like GPT-4 are absent. Additionally, the paper claims to be the first to attack Gemini, but Attack-Bard already demonstrated successful transfer attacks on the predecessor (Google-Bard) using similar techniques. [R2]

4. **Targeted output choice is unrealistic.** The attack uses “unknown” as the targeted output label. Reviewer 3 questions this choice as unrepresentative of typical MLLM outputs, and Reviewer 1 notes that in Figure 4 the attack forces outputs to contain “unknown,” which hurts the attack’s practical relevance. [R1, R3]

5. **Stealthiness concern.** Adding a Gaussian kernel alters the forward pass; in a standard inference there is no such kernel, making the attack easily detectable by users. [R1]

6. **No defense analysis.** The paper does not evaluate whether the attack is robust to common defense mechanisms. [R1]

7. **Limited novelty of core technique.** While effective, the Gaussian kernel perturbation is a straightforward smoothing of attention weights and lacks deep theoretical justification or algorithmic innovation. [R3]

8. **White-box dependency.** The method requires access to the model’s internal attention maps, which is not available for black-box models, and its reliance on attention mechanisms limits applicability to other MLLM architectures. [R3]

9. **Insufficient ablation and control experiments.** The paper does not demonstrate transferability in a conventional PGD attack setup (T not extreme). It also does not isolate whether the perturbations target vision-language alignment or the vision encoder; a random-noise baseline with the same perturbation magnitude is needed to verify the claim. [R2, R4]

10. **Limited dataset diversity.** Experiments focus on a few datasets and model architectures, raising questions about broader generalizability. [R3, R4]

11. **Undefined baseline term.** In Tables 1–3, the term “baseline” is not defined, leaving ambiguity about the exact comparison condition. [R4]
