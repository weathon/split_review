## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CoMOGA (Constrained Multi-Objective Gradient Aggregator), a constrained multi-objective RL algorithm. The key idea is to treat the maximization of multiple objectives as a constrained optimization problem (COP) by converting the objectives into constraints (with preferences) and then integrating existing safety constraints. Within a trust region, a quadratic program is solved using linear approximations of the constraints, and constraints that become infeasible are removed. The method is extended to a preference-conditioned universal policy via amortized optimization. The paper provides a convergence guarantee in tabular settings. Experiments on synthetic and simulated tasks demonstrate that CoMOGA achieves constraint satisfaction and outperforms prior methods.

### Strengths

1. **Practical relevance**: The problem of multi-objective RL under safety constraints is highly relevant for real-world applications (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).

2. **Novel and well-motivated approach**: The transformation of objectives into constraints to avoid gradient conflicts is a novel and straightforward idea (Reviewers 1, 3).

3. **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is well-written, easy to read, and uses effective figures to illustrate the concepts (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).

4. **Theoretical analysis**: The paper provides a convergence guarantee in tabular settings, adding to the soundness of the method (Reviewers 3, 4).

5. **Thorough experiments**: The experimental evaluation is extensive, covering multiple environments, and the metrics (e.g., hypervolume, constraint satisfaction) are clearly defined. The method consistently outperforms existing baselines (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).

6. **Minority positive view**: Reviewer 2 (score 8) is particularly enthusiastic, praising the thorough experiments and clear evaluation metrics; this contrasts with Reviewer 4 (score 5) who is more critical about implementation details and reporting.

### Weaknesses

1. **Computational efficiency and scalability concerns** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4 – strong/medium magnitude). Specific issues:
   - Gradients of all objectives and constraints must be computed per policy update, scaling linearly with number of objectives (N) and constraints (M), creating a bottleneck for high-dimensional tasks (Reviewer 1).
   - Estimating terms like \(x^\top H x\) (involving the Fisher information matrix) requires inner products and extra memory; no time complexity analysis is provided (Reviewer 1).
   - Equation (9) uses the inverse Fisher information matrix over policy parameters \(\theta\), which is high-dimensional and computationally expensive; the paper does not explain how this is handled in practice (Reviewer 4).
   - Additional overhead from solving a quadratic program and projecting the policy back into the feasible region (Reviewer 3).
   - Memory requirements for storing gradients and intermediate computations are not discussed (Reviewer 1).
   - It is unclear whether the transformation yields net gains in computational complexity or convergence rate; overhead may offset benefits (Reviewer 3).

2. **Insufficient practical implementation details** (Reviewers 4, 1 – high magnitude). The paper lacks a detailed derivation bridging the theoretical COP framework to a concrete deep-RL algorithm. Missing specifics include: how gradients of the objective and constraint functions are computed, how the inverse Fisher information matrix is approximated, and how finite sample sizes and arbitrary parameterizations (as done in e.g., TRPO) are accounted for (Reviewer 4). The estimation of the Fisher information matrix is not elaborated (Reviewer 1).

3. **Unfair ablation and missing comparisons** (Reviewers 2, 3 – high magnitude).
   - In the ablation study (Section 6.5), CAGrad uses the Lagrangian method for constraints, but CoMOGA uses its own constrained optimization; this inconsistency makes it impossible to isolate the effect of the core contribution (Reviewer 3). A fair ablation would use the same optimization method or justify the choice.
   - The paper should include direct comparisons with similar gradient aggregation methods for MORL, such as CAGrad and PCGrad, especially in the gradient aggregation section (Reviewer 2).
   - The authors do not explain why CAGrad+Lagrangian is treated as an ablation study rather than a baseline, nor why CoMOGA significantly outperforms it (Reviewer 2).

4. **Potential limitations of converting objectives to constraints** (Reviewer 3 – medium/high magnitude). The core idea may be undermined by two factors:
   - **Linear approximation**: The trust-region linear approximation may not capture the true curvature of objective functions, leading to inaccurate constraint boundaries and violations that reintroduce gradient conflicts (Reviewer 3).
   - **Value function bias**: Biases in value function estimation produce noisy gradients, further challenging constraint satisfaction (Reviewer 3).
   - Moreover, transforming objectives into constraints complicates the feasible region, potentially creating non-convex regions, sharp corners, or discontinuities that hinder gradient-based optimization and increase the risk of local optima (Reviewer 3).

5. **Inconsistencies and missing details in hypervolume (HV) reporting** (Reviewer 4 – high magnitude). These issues raise concerns about the validity of comparisons:
   - In Figure 8, HV results are shown only up to \(3 \times 10^6\) training steps, while prior work (e.g., PD-MORL) reports up to \(10 \times 10^6\) steps; this may not reach convergence (Reviewer 4).
   - HV units are mismatched: PD-MORL uses units of \(10^6\), whereas this paper uses units of 0.1 for HV, creating confusion and making PD-MORL appear worse than its original report (Reviewer 4).
   - The normalization process for objectives before computing HV is not clarified, undermining comparability (Reviewer 4).
   - Figure 7 includes many Pareto-dominated points that do not contribute to optimal trade-offs and complicate interpretation (Reviewer 4).
   - The results for Hopper and HalfCheetah in Figure 8 differ significantly from the original PD-MORL results, requiring explanation (Reviewer 4).

6. **Missing hyperparameter sensitivity analysis** (Reviewer 3 – medium magnitude). The paper introduces hyperparameters (e.g., local region size, number of preference samples) but provides no sensitivity analysis. This limits practical applicability, as users must tune for each new task (Reviewer 3).

7. **Underspecified preference sampling and critic training** (Reviewer 2 – low/medium magnitude). The paper does not specify how preferences are sampled (e.g., uniformly) and whether critics are trained with the same targets (e.g., Retrace) across all methods (Reviewer 2).

8. **Conceptual premise questioned** (Reviewer 1 – low magnitude). The abstract claims an RL agent should consider multiple objectives, but the reward hypothesis suggests a single scalar reward is sufficient. The authors do not motivate why separate objectives are needed instead of aggregating them (Reviewer 1).

9. **Additional theoretical gaps and questions** (Reviewers 1, 3, 4):
   - The equivalence between the original update (2) and the constrained formulation (3) for multi-objective case may hold only when a feasible solution exists; it is unclear whether the norm constraint can always be satisfied while monotonic improvement constraints cannot (Reviewer 1).
   - Why not directly use a norm constraint \(||d||_H \le \epsilon\) (as in TRPO-style optimization) rather than scaling the gradient? Possibly because estimating \(H^{1/2}\) is difficult (Reviewer 1).
   - If both (5) and (6) use linear approximations, how does (6) correct the policy after (5) violates constraints? (Reviewer 1).
   - Does the algorithm converge in continuous state/action spaces? (Reviewer 3).
   - How does CoMOGA handle the high-dimensional inverse Fisher information matrix in practice? (Reviewer 4).
