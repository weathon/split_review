## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Physics-RW, a benchmark for evaluating physical reasoning in general world models using real-world videos across four classical physics categories (mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, optics). It includes classification (yes/no) and video generation tasks, and tests several open- and closed-source models in zero-shot and fine-tuned settings. Reviewers generally agree the benchmark addresses an important gap, but raise concerns about task design, evaluation metrics, model selection, and novelty. Two reviewers (Ratings 3,3) are more critical, while four (Ratings 5,5,5,5) are more positive overall.

### Strengths
- **Real-world video data**: The benchmark is the first to use real-world videos (not simulator-generated) for physical reasoning, providing more realistic evaluation (Reviewers 2,4,5). Collecting such data is a non-trivial effort (Reviewer 5).
- **Broad physics coverage**: Covers four major classical physics categories—mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and optics—allowing a thorough assessment across diverse phenomena (Reviewers 1,2,4,5,6).
- **Dual-task setup**: Includes both classification (inference) and video generation (prediction) tasks, enabling nuanced evaluation of interpretive and predictive reasoning abilities (Reviewers 2,4,5). This extends evaluation to the multimodal domain (Reviewer 2).
- **Comprehensive model evaluation**: Tests a range of open- and closed-source models with controlled factors (response format, frame sampling) (Reviewers 1,4,6). The zero-shot and fine-tuning experiments show potential for improvement (Reviewer 1).
- **Interesting finding**: Models tend to respond with “yes” disproportionately, revealing a systematic bias (Reviewers 1,3,5).
- **Human test included** (Reviewer 5).
- **Easy to follow writing** (Reviewer 5).
- **Detailed analysis** of limitations and possible improvement methods (Reviewers 1,3,6).

### Weaknesses
- **Binary yes/no classification too simplistic**: The classification task reduces complex physical reasoning to a binary answer. Several reviewers suggest richer formats (multiple-choice, fill-in-the-blank, or requiring model justification) to better assess understanding (Reviewers 1,3,6). The prompts in Figure 1 provide substantial guidance, reducing the need for models to reason independently (Reviewer 6).
- **Poor model performance**: Many models score below 50% ACC despite balanced yes/no distribution (Table 3). Reviewers request explanation—e.g., whether this stems from many “don’t know” answers or systematic bias (Reviewers 1,4). The tendency of models like Video-LLaVA to always answer “yes” (cheating) undermines evaluation validity (Reviewer 3). Robust evaluation methods (e.g., requiring correct answers on both affirmative and negative descriptions) are needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited video generation evaluation**: Only two models tested; missing baselines such as stable video diffusion and other open-source video generation models (Reviewers 2,4,5,6). Drawing conclusions from only two models does not provide a comprehensive view across architectures (Reviewer 6).
- **FVD as evaluation metric for generation is insufficient**: FVD measures semantic similarity, not physical correctness. Papers like VideoPhy and PhyGenBench have already explained this limitation (Reviewers 3,4,6). Moreover, physical interactions are often localized; FVD scores may be dominated by unrelated regions (Reviewer 6). A more targeted evaluation is needed.
- **Problem definition and terminology confusion**: The paper uses inconsistent terms—“video understanding and generation” vs. “reasoning ability” vs. “classification and video generation” (Reviewer 2). The definition of “world model” and its relation to VLMs, video generation, etc., is unclear (Reviewer 2). The abbreviation T1–T4 hard to follow (Reviewer 2). The choice of four categories is not justified (Reviewers 3,5); more phenomena (e.g., fluid dynamics, chemical reactions) could be included (Reviewer 4).
- **Task design rationale unclear**: Why model video generation as video-to-video when the motivation starts with Sora (a text-to-video model)? Does text-to-video also measure physics understanding? The logic is confusing (Reviewer 3). Criteria for assigning videos to classification vs. generation tasks are not explained; Table 2 shows many more classification than generation videos (Reviewer 1). For generation, some videos have multiple plausible continuations—how were proper videos selected? (Reviewer 5).
- **Missing analysis on why models fail**: Deeper insights into why VLMs are poor at physical reasoning are needed—e.g., is training data the main reason? (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 suggests experimenting with using Video-VLM to extract captions and then an LLM for yes/no judgment, as LLM common sense may improve results.
- **Human evaluation concerns**: Manual review of model responses (line 259) makes results hard to reproduce and benchmark usage difficult (Reviewer 3). Tools like VLMEvalKit could be used (Reviewer 3). More human evaluation analysis needed: do human judgments align with quantitative results? Can the benchmark be replicated? (Reviewer 2).
- **Overclaim in results**: Claiming that Table 4 shows models lack understanding of physical laws is an overclaim because video-to-video is inherently more difficult than text-to-video, skewing evaluation (Reviewer 3).
- **Novelty of improvement methods**: The mitigation strategies (fine-tuning, prompt injection) are widely accepted techniques and may not add unique value (Reviewer 6).
- **Data leakage and dataset size**: Potential data leakage if models were trained on similar real-world videos (Reviewer 5). Dataset size and comparison to previous benchmarks are not provided (Reviewer 4). Release timeline and website are unclear (Reviewer 4).
- **Zero-shot inference definition**: What is “Zero-shot Inference” in Table 1 and why do all previous methods not support it? (Reviewer 5). Merging Table 3 and Table 5 could improve presentation (Reviewer 5).
