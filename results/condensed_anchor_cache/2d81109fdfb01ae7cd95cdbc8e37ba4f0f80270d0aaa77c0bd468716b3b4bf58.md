## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores the use of AI foundation models (FMs) to improve climate model parameterizations, specifically focusing on gravity wave (GW) effects. The authors leverage a pre-trained weather foundation model (Prithvi WxC, 2.3B parameters) by fine-tuning its encoder and decoder components to predict gravity wave momentum fluxes that are typically unresolved in coarse-resolution climate models. Using higher-resolution ERA5 data, they create a data-driven parameterization that learns GW fluxes from high-resolution climate models to represent them in coarse models. The model receives background atmospheric conditions and outputs GW momentum fluxes. They compare against a baseline Attention U-Net trained from scratch using three evaluation tests: predicting global flux distributions, region-specific flux spectra across atmospheric heights, and temporal evolution at known gravity wave hotspots. Results show the fine-tuned FM outperforms the baseline, especially in stratospheric regions where the original FM was not pre-trained. The Hellinger distance for extreme events is 0.06 for the fine-tuned model versus 0.11 for the baseline. The authors frame this as a proof-of-concept for using FMs to develop improved parameterizations for other unresolved processes like clouds and precipitation.

### Strengths
- Introduces a fine-tuning framework with rigorous evaluation methodology: three increasingly stringent tests (global distributions, region-specific flux spectra across pressure levels, temporal evolution at hotspots). This template could be valuable for assessing other climate parameterizations. (R1, R3)
- Uses tail-Hellinger distance to specifically evaluate extreme events in flux distributions, showing careful consideration of climate-relevant metrics. (R1)
- Empirical results demonstrate the fine-tuned FM outperforms the baseline Attention U-Net, especially generalizing to stratospheric gravity wave behavior in regions outside the original FM pre-training range. (R1, R4)
- Clear and accessible presentation; well-organized with effective bridging of ML and climate science concepts. Figures, especially Figure 1 and Figures 5–8, are well designed. (R1, R3, R4)
- Successful demonstration with limited fine-tuning data (four years of ERA5, ~35k training samples), suggesting a practical pathway for low-data regimes. (R1)
- Significance for climate science: introduces a more lightweight and better-generalizing approach to data-driven parameterizations compared to training from scratch. (R3)
- Thought-provoking results; the difference between baseline and fine-tuned model is visually striking. (R2)
- Applies state-of-the-art foundation models to a relevant societal problem; fine-tuned FM produces accurate predictions even outside the FM’s training regime. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- Limited machine learning innovation: primarily applies standard fine-tuning; the scheme is already described in the cited Prithvi WxC paper as a typical downstream task. Contribution to the ML community is modest. (R1, R2, R3)
- Similar applications (e.g., ClimaX, Aurora) have already demonstrated that weather-trained models can be fine-tuned for various downstream tasks; findings align with expected outcomes. (R1, R2)
- Missing comparison with other foundation models (ClimaX, AtmoRep, Aurora) to understand the impact of FM choice. Selection of Prithvi WxC over alternatives is insufficiently justified; Aurora offers finer resolution and state-of-the-art weather prediction performance. (R1 Q8, R2, R4-1)
- No ablation studies: no analysis of sensitivity to hyperparameters, training strategies, or contribution of different model components. (R2, R4-4)
- Baseline comparison is problematic: (a) baseline Attention U-Net uses one-quarter fewer input variables than the fine-tuned model, confounding data selection with model quality; (b) baseline is convolutional (35M parameters) while fine-tuned model is a transformer (2.3B), so architectural and capacity differences obscure whether gains come from pretraining, architecture, or scale. A transformer-based baseline with comparable parameters is needed. (R3-2, R4-2, R4-3)
- Additional baselines needed: e.g., equation-based methods or mixed probability methods mentioned in 'Related Work', traditional numerical approaches, or other ML techniques. (R2, R3-2)
- Prithvi WxC operates at coarse resolution (0.625°×0.5°); the downstream prediction of gravity wave fluxes is also at very low resolution (coarse-grained to ~280 km). This limits practical impact for real-world climate modeling. (R2, R4-5)
- The parameterization is not tested in a numerical climate model; actual verification of coupling and long-term stability is missing, which is key for assessing relevance. (R4-7, R1 Q7)
- Coarse gravity wave fluxes may be of limited value for numerical climate models; the spatial and temporal resolution required to resolve gravity waves is not specified. (R4-6, R4 Q1)
- Daily average GW momentum flux experiments (Figures 5 and 10) show notable differences in small-scale features between the deep learning model and ERA5. Adding MERRA-2 distribution would clarify whether the model learns GW information from ERA5 rather than inheriting features from the pre-trained model. (R3-3)
- Figure 5 dotted lines indicate 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, but the data distributions do not seem to match these percentiles visually; possible miscalculation or misinterpretation. (R4-5)
- The claim that a Hellinger distance of 0.05 or less is "pretty good" (line 325) is not justified with a reference or data-driven reasoning. (R4-6)
- Daily flux predictions show difficulty with small flux values (Figure 5b); no proposed solution. (R1 Q2)
- No convergence plot of baseline vs fine-tuned model to compare training behavior. (R4-4)
- No code provided with the submission; reproducibility is limited. (R1 Q9, R4)
- Venue fit: the primary contributions are to climate science rather than ML methodology; the work would be better appreciated at climate science venues (e.g., JAMES, GRL). (R1)
- Data splitting unclear: training/validation/test split ratios and method (temporal vs random) not specified. (R3 Q3)
- Definition of "data-scarce" for ~35k training samples is questionable compared to typical sizes in other ML domains; context for climate modeling dataset sizes is needed. (R3 Q2)
- Claim that the method could extend to cloud parameterization and precipitation (line 105) is not substantiated with experiments; these tasks have distinct challenges and data reliability issues. (R3 Q1)
- Rationale for freezing both encoder and decoder during fine-tuning not explored; full fine-tuning could potentially improve performance given the 2.3B parameter model size. (R1 Q1)
- Models effectively approximate equations (1)–(3); it is unclear why deep learning is needed instead of solving those equations directly. (R4 Q3)
- Typo in line 289: "evolutionlution" should be "evolution". (R4)
