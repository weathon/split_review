## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies scaling laws for multilingual decoder-only language models, proposing a power‑law that links dataset size, model size, and language sampling ratios. It hypothesizes that test cross‑entropy loss for each language family depends only on its own sampling ratio (the “minimal cross‑group transfer” hypothesis). Experiments cover >100 models from 85M to 1.2B parameters across 23 languages grouped into 5 families (Romance, Germanic, Slavic, Indic, Sino‑Tibetan). The derived optimal sampling ratios are shown to improve normalized loss over three naive baselines at small scales and are claimed to generalize to larger models. Reviewers note the topic is important and the experimental effort is substantial, but raise concerns about overclaiming generality, insufficient downstream evaluation, limited evidence for the core hypothesis, small absolute gains, missing baselines, and limited language diversity.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an overlooked area: scaling laws for multilingual general‑purpose LMs, extending prior work focused on monolingual or translation models. (R1, R4)
- A substantial experimental effort: >100 models trained at multiple scales (85M, 397M, 810M, 1.2B parameters). (R1, R2)
- The proposed scaling law offers a simple way to account for cross‑lingual transfer by focusing on language families, potentially reducing the complexity and computational burden of multilingual data mixing. (R2 S1, R3 S1)
- The finding that \(\gamma_i(N, D)\) is independent of model size \(N\) and dataset size \(D\) is interesting. (R2)
- The derivation of optimal sampling ratios is a practical result that can inform real‑world multilingual training decisions. (R2 S2, R3 S2, R4 S2)
- The paper is clearly written, with well‑structured text, figures, tables, and formulas. (R3 S3)
- One reviewer (R2) notes that the optimal sampling ratios yield marginal improvement (< 0.1) over the three naive baselines.

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaimed generality and strong claims**  
  * The abstract and introduction claim scalability to “an arbitrary number of languages,” but experiments are limited to 23 languages out of >6000, and low‑resource languages within families are reduced (Line 190‑191). (R2, R4)  
  * The paper spends half a page arguing it is better than previous work; faithfulness to the phenomenon matters more than simplicity. (R4)  
  * Claims that the scaling law is for “general‑purpose” LMs are not backed by tests on diverse downstream tasks. (R3 W1)

- **Insufficient evaluation on downstream tasks**  
  No experiments beyond loss and machine translation; testing on summarization, question answering, etc. would strengthen the claim of general‑purpose applicability. (R1 questions, R3 W1)

- **Limited evidence for the language‑family hypothesis**  
  * Experiments supporting Hypothesis 1 are confined to two language‑family pairs: (Romance, Indic) and (Slavic, Sino‑Tibetan) with only three sampling ratios {0.2, 0.4, 0.6}. Pairs like Germanic‑Romance or Romance‑Slavic (more similar) are not tested. (R2, R3 W3)  
  * Language families are genetic groupings, but cross‑lingual transfer is known even across families (e.g., English (Germanic) for cross‑lingual transfer, areal effects, Japanese‑Chinese shared characters) [citing N09‑1067]. The hypothesis is not tested with random groupings of languages. (R1 questions, R4 W2)  
  * 22 out of 23 languages belong to different branches of Indo‑European; only Chinese (Sino‑Tibetan) is from a truly separate family. Script differences (Latin vs. Cyrillic) are not discussed despite impact on tokenization. (R4 W2)  
  * One reviewer (R1) notes confounding factors in Figure 2: Romance/Germanic are closely related and using Indic languages with different scripts adds variables.

- **Marginal gains and inconsistent results**  
  * The improvement in normalized loss from the optimal sampling ratio is very small (Table 4: 5.912 uniform vs. 5.895 proposed, difference 0.017). The practical significance is unclear if gains do not translate to downstream tasks. (R3 W2)  
  * In Table 6 (1.2B model), the total normalized loss for uniform sampling appears inconsistent: summing individual family losses (1.142+1.180+1.273+1.142+1.134 = 5.871) gives a different value than the reported 5.946. If the sum is correct, the proposed ratio may not generalize to larger models. (R3 W2)  
  * Another reviewer (R2) acknowledges the improvement is marginal (<0.1).

- **Missing important baselines**  
  The three baselines are naïve. More established methods, e.g., Eq. (7) in Fernandes et al. (2023), are not compared. (R2)

- **Missing analyses and ablations**  
  * How do the scaling laws perform on out‑of‑distribution data (e.g., outside CommonCrawl)? The test set is from the same dataset. (R3 questions)  
  * Low‑resource families like Niger‑Congo are not considered. (R3 questions)  
  * Could results be skewed by high‑resource languages (English, Spanish, Russian, Hindi, Chinese) within each family? Would the strategy benefit less‑represented languages like Catalan, Kannada? (R3 questions)  
  * How to interpret that optimal sampling ratios differ between 85M and 1.2B models (Table 5)? (R2 questions)  
  * The rule‑of‑thumb often up‑weights low‑resource languages, but some work suggests making English dominant (e.g., 50%) for reasoning; how does the proposed strategy relate to these guidelines? (R4 questions)

- **Presentation issues and typos**  
  * Captions of Figures 1 and 5 are unclear: “N” in Figure 1 is not specified (0,2,4,8? 8 corresponds to 85M); Figure 5 y‑axis, x‑axis, and the difference between left and middle plots need explicit explanation. (R1)  
  * Figure 4 axes are labelled \(L(p)-p\) but should be \(\log(L(p))-\log(p)\); lines are nearly parallel, so reporting slope rates would be helpful. (R2)  
  * Typos: Line 452 \(q_j\) → \(q_i\); Line 515 “asses” → “assess”. (R2)  
  * Line 531 “100 models” – clarify if this is the number of models investigated. (R2 questions)

- **Model scale is small compared to current practice**  
  Training data up to 50B tokens, model size up to 1.2B parameters; large multilingual LMs like EuroLLM‑1.7B are trained on 4 trillion tokens. (R4 questions)
