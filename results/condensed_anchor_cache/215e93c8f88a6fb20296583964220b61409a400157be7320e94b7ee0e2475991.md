## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DeepFDM, a method that combines finite difference approximations with a convolutional neural network to learn coefficients of a known PDE family from initial condition to solution. It also introduces a data generation procedure with distribution shifts quantified via Hellinger distance. Reviewers generally find the topic of benchmarking neural PDE solvers important, but raise significant concerns about the problem setup, limited generality, unfair comparisons, missing experimental details, and lack of justification for choices. Reviewer 1 is more positive (score 5, confidence 3) about accuracy and benchmarking procedure; other reviewers are more critical (scores 3, confidence 4–5) about novelty, fairness, and clarity.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important need for systematic benchmarking of neural PDE solvers against numerical methods and for evaluating out‑of‑distribution (OOD) performance.
- DeepFDM achieves lower errors than FNO, U‑Net, and ResNet on the considered PDE family while using fewer trainable parameters (Reviewer 1).
- Provides a structured procedure for generating training/test data with quantified distribution shifts using Hellinger distance, which is valuable for OOD evaluation.
- Offers interpretability by learning PDE coefficients.
- The comparison approach is novel and can be fairer to neural PDE solvers when those solvers are trained on real‑world data (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Combined contributions**: The paper merges two distinct contributions (a benchmark data‑generation procedure and the DeepFDM method) without clear separation.
- **Novelty of idea**: Parameterizing finite‑difference schemes via CNNs has already appeared, e.g., in the smoothing operator of multigrid methods (arXiv:2102.12071); this prior work is not discussed.
- **Restricted problem setup**: DeepFDM is only applicable to time‑dependent PDEs with periodic boundary conditions and bounded coefficients, and it learns only from initial conditions. The paper does not discuss how this limits generalizability to other PDE classes, such as hyperbolic equations with shocks, nonlinear PDEs, or complex boundary conditions.
- **Unclear problem definition**: The paper starts from a solution‑reconstruction problem but actually solves an inverse coefficient‑estimation problem. It is not clearly stated whether the focus is the forward or inverse problem.
- **Unfair comparisons**: Comparing DeepFDM (which uses known PDE structure) with purely data‑driven methods (FNO, U‑Net, ResNet) is not a level playing field; the results are not convincing.
- **Missing experimental details** (Reviewers 2, 4):
  - Dataset size, spatial/temporal resolutions, and distinction between 1D and 2D problems (Table 1).
  - Learning rate, optimizer, and whether models were trained autoregressively or with one‑step errors.
  - How spatially varying PDE parameters were generated (e.g., number of basis functions, standard deviation).
  - How distribution shift was introduced (increase/decrease of standard deviation, number of basis functions, equal or different variances).
- **No inference‑time evaluation**: Faster inference is a key motivation for neural surrogates; this is not reported or compared.
- **Scalability not discussed**: Scalability of DeepFDM relative to competitors is omitted.
- **Figures and initialization**: In Fig. 5, the training loss curves start from different initial points, suggesting different initializations that could make the comparison unfair (Reviewer 1). Also, DeepFDM does not show oscillations unlike other methods; the reason is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Choice of Hellinger distance**: The motivation for using Hellinger distance over KL divergence (which also admits closed‑form for Gaussians) is not justified.
- **Literature review issues**: Section 2.1 is disorganized and contains incorrect claims, e.g., “DeepONet learns PDE solution operators … the PDE is fully known and the PDE residual is included in the loss” – this is incorrect for data‑driven DeepONet (Reviewer 4). The claim that “Neural PDE operators … assume the PDE form is unknown” conflicts with the previous point.
- **Related work on distribution shift**: An existing paper (Zhu et al., 2023, CMAME) on reliable extrapolation of neural operators with distribution shifts is not cited.
- **Unclear notations**: Symbols such as `m` in the dataset, `A`, `A*`, and `\hat{A}` are not clearly defined.
- **No metrics for coefficient fields**: If the inverse problem is considered, there are no metrics for the reconstructed coefficient fields.
- **Missing Appendix B**: The manuscript references Appendix B but it is not present.
- **Typo**: “coarsened” should be “downsampled” when describing higher‑resolution generation.
- **Limited applicability as baseline**: DeepFDM is only a useful baseline when the neural PDE solver is trained on real‑world data; in typical surrogate‑modeling scenarios where PDE parameters are known during training, it is not a fair comparator (Reviewer 2).
- **Unanswered methodological questions** (from reviews):
  - How are neural PDE solvers conditioned on coordinate‑dependent PDE parameters? By adding spatial coordinates as inputs?
  - What are the trade‑offs between finite difference and automatic differentiation in the training loss, given evidence that AD often outperforms FD?
  - Given that Fig. 5 does not show significant improvement, what advantages does DeepFDM offer in training dynamics?
