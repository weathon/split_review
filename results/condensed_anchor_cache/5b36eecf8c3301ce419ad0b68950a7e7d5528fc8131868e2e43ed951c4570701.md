## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes IRSA (Iterations by Regimenting Self-Attention), a highly structured prompting technique that uses repetitive execution traces to trigger GPT-3 family models to perform iterative algorithmic execution (e.g., sorting, dynamic programming). It introduces three strategies: (1) using a strong repetitive example of an execution path, (2) fragmented prompting with partial traces, and (3) skip attention to bypass parts of generated text. Experiments show that GPT-3.5 with IRSA outperforms GPT-4 with naive prompting on the evaluated tasks. The paper also discusses implications for education and prompt engineering. Reviewers generally found the topic interesting and the approach potentially significant, but raised major concerns about generalization, evaluation, formalization, and differentiation from prior work. Reviewer 4 was notably more positive (rating 6) than the others (ratings 3, 5, 3), emphasizing importance and soundness while still noting clarity issues.

### Strengths

- The paper explores an important question of broad interest: the exact capabilities and limits of LLMs in performing iterative, stateful reasoning. (R1)
- The proposed prompts (IRSA) are intuitively clear and novel to the best of reviewers' knowledge. The strategies (IRSA, fragmented prompting, skip attention) are well explained with examples. (R1, R2)
- The experiments demonstrate that IRSA prompts help LLMs correctly simulate algorithms requiring loops, and that GPT-3.5 with IRSA can achieve better results than GPT-4 with a naive prompt, highlighting the significance of prompt engineering. (R1, R2, R4)
- The analogy of GPT as a Turing machine is effective. (R2)
- The paper includes a good amount of detail in the main text and appendices. (R1)
- The paper is well-organized and the language is technical yet understandable for domain readers. It also introduces related work on LLM prompting. (R4)

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of scaling and generalization analysis.** The evaluation is restricted to short inputs (e.g., sorting lists of length 5) and a small set of problems (handful of programs, 100 Python arithmetic problems). It is unclear how performance (correctness) varies with input size, trace length, or more complex programs. The paper does not explore whether the method can handle long traces that exceed context windows, or how the choice of example input affects results. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Execution path assumption is too strong.** IRSA relies on providing an execution path (stack trace) of the target program for one input. It is not discussed how this path is generated, whether the method works if the LLM has not seen similar traces in training data, or how one selects the example. Obtaining such a path may be as hard as executing the program itself. (R1, R3)
- **Unclear differentiation from prior work.** The approach bears strong resemblance to the scratchpad method (Nye et al., 2021) which also uses trace information. The paper lacks a detailed related work section that spells out exact differences and advantages over existing structured prompting techniques. It is not clear whether gains are due to IRSA's specific structure or could be achieved by other methods. (R1, R2, R3)
- **Lack of formal formulation.** IRSA is presented largely through examples, without a clear algorithmic or formal description. The terminology ("hardens the attention", "skipping unnecessary attention") is loose and not precisely defined. This makes it difficult to independently implement the method, evaluate its limitations, or generalize it to new programs without significant manual effort. (R1, R3)
- **Insufficient evaluation and missing ablations.** The evaluation is not comprehensive or systematic. Key ablations are missing: applying IRSA to GPT-4 (why only GPT-3?), testing with repeating digits vs. non-repeating digits, using multiple vs. single examples, and analyzing performance on inputs not seen in training. The paper claims (Section 2.1) that a variant of Bubble Sort solves 76% of puzzles, but does not provide evaluation results supporting this statement. The evaluation metric is not always clarified (e.g., captions of Tables 1-3 should specify "accuracy"). (R2, R3, R4)
- **Practical applicability concerns.** For education, having an LLM markup an existing execution trace of actually running the program would be more correct. The method may not scale to long execution traces due to context window limits. An automated method for turning a general program into an IRSA prompt is not provided. (R2, R3)
- **Presentation issues.** The overall structure could be adjusted, research gaps more highlighted, and prompt fonts could be made consistent (Prompt 1 has larger font than others). The methodology can be elaborated for better clarity. (R4)
- **Missing experiments on input variability.** The effect of repeating vs. non-repeating digits in the Bubble Sort task is not tested. The impact of the choice of example and multiple examples is not explored. (R3, R4)
