## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Token Distribution Dynamics (TDD) to interpret and control autoregressive LLMs. TDD assesses input token saliency by projecting tokens into the embedding space and tracking probability distribution shifts over the vocabulary. Three variants are introduced—forward, backward, and bidirectional—each intended to provide different insights into token relevance. The method is evaluated on BLiMP datasets (11 out of 67 showing improved AOPC/Sufficiency over baselines) and demonstrated on two practical tasks: zero-shot toxic language suppression and sentiment steering. Experiments are conducted across GPT-2, GPT-J, BLOOM, and LLaMA (up to 7B parameters).

### Strengths
- **Simple, efficient method.** All reviewers agree that the core idea is simple and computationally lightweight.
- **Interesting approach.** Using token distribution dynamics (difference between probability of a token and an alternative token) to reveal token influence is novel and timely (R1, R3).
- **Comprehensive model evaluation.** The method is tested across multiple autoregressive models: GPT-2/J, BLOOM, and LLaMA (R1, R2).
- **Improved results on 11 datasets.** TDD achieves better AOPC and sufficiency than attention rollout, contrastive gradient, etc. on BLiMP (R4).
- **Practical applications demonstrated.** Toxic language suppression and sentiment steering show real-world utility (R1, R2, R4).
- **Well-written and reproducible.** Paper is clear, with sufficient detail to reproduce experiments (R2). Reviewer 2 (score 8) was notably more positive, emphasising these presentation and reproducibility strengths.
- **Clear motivation.** The paper clearly identifies limitations of existing saliency methods (misalignment with generation objectives, linearity assumptions) and positions TDD as a remedy (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Heavy dependence on alternative word wₐ.** The method requires a well-defined alternative token, which is only naturally available in datasets like BLiMP that provide sentence pairs with exactly one-word differences. It is unclear how wₐ is identified in the Section 5 steering experiments (R3). The use of space tokens or ‘positive’/‘negative’ as alternatives in controlled generation is considered too simplistic for practical applications (R4).
- **Causal claims not substantiated.** Despite claiming that TDD “elucidate[s] causal relationships,” the analyses do not control variables or measure treatment effects. The assumptions (especially the first and second listed on page 4) are strong and lack theoretical or experimental validation (R3, R4).
- **Missing important baselines and related work.** No comparison with prompting for style transfer (R1), existing LLM-based controllable generation via prefix prompting (R4), or integrated gradients (IG) for attribution (R4). Attribution is only evaluated for next-token prediction, not for a full generated sequence, which is arguably more practical (R4). The closely related AtMan method (Deiseroth et al., NeurIPS 2023) is not discussed (R2).
- **Insufficient discussion of TDD variants.** The paper does not clarify when forward vs. backward vs. bidirectional is most appropriate, nor does it specify which variant is used in the steering experiments (R2, R3, R4). Practical implications for explanation fidelity and computational cost are not elaborated.
- **Evaluation limitations.** Only automatic metrics are used; no manual evaluation of generated text quality, which is critical given the lack of ground truth (R4). Indicators are simple and may not fully capture text quality. An error appears in Table 3, column 5—the highlighted number is wrong (R4). Table 1 and Table 3 captions are too sparse (R2).
- **Scalability not addressed.** No analysis of performance on larger models (e.g., Llama-2 13B, 70B) or computational cost (efficiency, memory) (R1). Scalability for multi-attribute control is also not considered (R4).
- **Robustness untested.** The effect of using multiple alternative tokens simultaneously is not explored (R2).
- **Writing style concerns.** Excessive adverbs (e.g., “elegantly simple yet remarkably effective”) reduce the rigor of the presentation (R3).
