## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes **Self-Proving models** that, for a given input, output both a prediction and an interactive proof of correctness verified by an external, trusted verifier \(V\). The framework combines interactive proof systems with learning theory. Two learning algorithms are given: **Transcript Learning (TL)** (strong supervision from successful proof traces) and **Reinforcement Learning from Verifier Feedback (RLVF)** (weaker supervision). Theoretical convergence guarantees (Theorem 4.1) are provided under certain assumptions. Experiments on **GCD** computation using a small GPT model demonstrate the approach.

### Strengths
1. **Well-motivated and novel framework.** The idea of leveraging interactive proof systems (and related tools from TCS like PCP, property testing) to build models that prove correctness on a per-instance basis is attractive and may spur further research into AI safety.  
2. **Principled combination of verification and learning.** The setup integrates a formal verifier with a learning model in a natural way, and the use of interactive proofs adds flexibility compared to fixed proof systems.  
3. **Theoretical contribution.** The paper provides theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.1) of the learning process, including error bounds on the two learning approaches. The reduction to SGD and the communication complexity constant \(C\) are clearly explained.  
4. **Clarity and accessibility.** The paper is well written, the background on interactive proofs is covered sufficiently for non-experts, and the related work discussion helps situate the contribution.

### Weaknesses
1. **Strong and unrealistic assumptions in Theorem 4.1.**  
   - The theorem assumes \(A(\theta)\) is concave, which does **not** hold for typical autoregressive models (e.g., GPT used in experiments). This drastically simplifies the optimization landscape and undermines practical relevance.  
   - It assumes the existence of \(\theta^*\) with \(A(\theta^*) \ge 1 - \epsilon/2\), i.e., that a self-proving model achieving high accuracy exists. This is not justified and may not hold for many problems or architectures.  
   - The theorem lacks a confidence parameter \(\delta\); since parameters are estimated from finite samples, the possibility of “bad” samples should be addressed.

2. **Limited and questionable experimental evaluation.**  
   - Only the **GCD** task is used. As the authors note, proofs in this case do not involve verifier questions (they are simple intermediate steps of Euclid’s algorithm). GCD is easily solvable by non-ML algorithms, so it does not convincingly demonstrate the need for or advantage of self-proving models.  
   - A second example beyond GCD would have strengthened the results (e.g., to illustrate what annotations mean in other domains).  
   - The experiments do not explore scaling to more challenging problems (e.g., more general arithmetic, formal theorem proving) where probabilistic guarantees might be meaningful.

3. **Unrealistic soundness assumption and implication for per-instance guarantees.**  
   - The definition of \(s\)-soundness assumes the verifier’s false-positive error is bounded for **every** input \(x,y\) and any prover \(P\). In practice, such a worst-case guarantee is extremely hard to achieve unless the verifier is deterministic (\(s=0\)). If the error depends on the input distribution, the claimed guarantee for a specific \(x_0,y_0\) (line 262) collapses.  
   - The paper does not explain how to construct a probabilistic verifier with this property, nor where the randomness comes from. Running a probabilistic verifier multiple times could reduce errors, but this would change the learning setup.

4. **Unaddressed practical concerns about the verifier.**  
   - The approach **requires an existing verifier** implementation. If a verifier is already available, the practical scenarios where learning a model to produce proofs is beneficial (rather than just running the verifier) are not discussed.  
   - The computational overhead of generating proofs is not explored; if proving correctness costs more than solving the problem directly, the approach may not be useful.  
   - If the verifier itself is imperfect (e.g., has bugs or incomplete specifications), the learned model’s proofs become meaningless.

5. **Presentation gaps.**  
   - **Algorithms 1 and 2** are relegated to the appendix even though they are central contributions.  
   - Algorithm 1’s objective uses expected probability \(\mathbb{E}[q_\theta(\text{trace})]\) instead of expected log probability (cross-entropy). The reason for this choice (consistency with Theorem 4.1?) is not explained.  
   - Several minor typos are present (e.g., L284: “EOS in \(\Sigma^*\)” should be “EOS in \(\Sigma\)”; L926: \(a_0 := y\) should be \(a_0 := y^*\); L928: missing asterisks).

6. **Novelty of the overall setup.**  
   - While the theoretical error bounds are novel, the basic prover/verifier framework is well known from argumentation, theorem proving (e.g., Isabelle), and earlier work. The paper could better clarify what is new in this specific instantiation with ML models and an interactive verifier.  
   - One reviewer (rating 3) found the contribution significantly limited, contrasting with the higher ratings of others, indicating substantial disagreement about the paper’s impact.
