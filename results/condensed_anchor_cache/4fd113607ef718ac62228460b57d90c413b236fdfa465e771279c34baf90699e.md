## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates why AdamW often outperforms SGD when fine-tuning large pretrained vision models (ViT, ConvNeXt) on downstream tasks, especially those with distribution shifts. The authors identify that the performance gap arises because gradients in the first embedding layer are much larger than in the rest of the model for models pretrained with AdamW. Freezing this embedding layer (less than 1% of parameters) allows SGD (with or without momentum) to match or exceed AdamW’s accuracy while reducing memory usage (e.g., ViT-L uses ~33% less GPU memory). The method achieves state-of-the-art results on five distribution shift benchmarks: WILDS-FMoW, WILDS-Camelyon, BREEDS-Living-17, Waterbirds, and DomainNet. All five reviewers found the paper well-written and the experiments convincing, though one reviewer (R1, score 8) rated it more positively than the others (all score 6). Most reviewers noted the clear motivation, thorough analysis, and practical memory savings, but raised concerns about generality, evaluation scope, and some presentation details.

### Strengths
- **Well-written and clearly motivated**: The paper is easy to read, and the problem is well introduced (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5). All claims are backed by experimental results (R1).
- **Extensive experiments and analysis**: The work includes thorough experiments and ablation studies, including observations on optimizer mismatch between pretraining and fine-tuning (R2, R3, R5).
- **Simple yet effective method**: Freezing the embedding layer is a minor modification that yields significant performance gains for SGD with less memory than AdamW (R1, R2, R3). The memory savings are practically important as models grow larger (R3).
- **State-of-the-art results**: The approach achieves SOTA on multiple distribution shift benchmarks (R4, R5).
- **Novel insight**: The paper identifies the high grad norm of the embedding layer under AdamW pretraining as the root cause of SGD’s poor performance, and shows that freezing fixes the gap (R1, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generality**: The analysis focuses on models with an embedding layer (e.g., ViT, ConvNeXt), which may not be permanent in vision, and does not address architectures like recommendation models (DLRM) where the embedding layer dominates parameters (R1). It remains unclear if the findings transfer to NLP models (e.g., Llama2) (R2).
- **Narrow task scope**: All fine-tuning experiments are on classification; segmentation and detection are not considered (R3). Conventional in-domain transfer tasks (e.g., ImageNet-1k, iNaturalist, VTAB) are missing—the paper only benchmarks distribution shift datasets, so the method’s effectiveness for common visual transfer is unknown (R5).
- **Marginal improvements in some cases**: On CIFAR-10 with CLIP, the absolute differences between SGD, AdamW, and SGD (freeze-embed) are ≤0.4%, yet the paper claims large relative improvements (e.g., “20% lower error”), which is misleading (R5). In some settings, SGD (freeze-embed) can still underperform, and the explanation for these failures is insufficient (R4).
- **Limited hyperparameter tuning**: The grid search only varies learning rate (6 values) while holding weight decay fixed (0 for SGD, 0.01 for AdamW), despite different pretraining strategies (SSL, CLIP, supervised) often requiring distinct settings (R5).
- **Weak support for certain claims**: The statement that “SGD performs well when fine-tuning data is closer to pretraining” is drawn from a single data point (CLIP), and the paper also misstates that all models except CLIP were trained on ImageNet-21K (DINO uses ImageNet-1k) (R5).
- **Contradictory memory numbers**: The abstract claims 12 vs 16 bytes/parameter (1.33×), while the introduction says AdamW maintains 2× to 3× more state than SGD (R5).
- **Missing clarity and details**: The gradual unfreezing method in Table 5 is not clearly described (R3). The paper does not report mean and standard deviations over multiple seeds (R4). The related work omits parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches like visual prompting and reprogramming (R4).
- **Lack of practical guidance**: It is unclear when practitioners should choose SGD (freeze-embed) over AdamW or vanilla SGD, especially for different datasets and tasks (R1, R4, R5).
- **Unanswered questions about mechanisms**: Whether low learning rates on early layers could achieve the same effect, whether large gradients persist throughout fine-tuning or only at initial steps, and why SGD still outperforms in some experiments (R1, R3). The normalization of gradient norms in Fig. 2 is not specified (R1). The dependence on models being pretrained with AdamW (what if pretrained with SGD?) is not tested (R2).
- **Memory overhead scaling**: The claim that memory overhead of AdamW relative to SGD increases with model size needs justification, as the relative difference should be constant (R1).
