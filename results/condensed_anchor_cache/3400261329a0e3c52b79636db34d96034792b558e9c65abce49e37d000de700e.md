## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper quantifies variance in LLM evaluation benchmarks, focusing on seed variance (different random initializations) and monotonicity during pretraining. It retrains 10 LLaMA 7B models from scratch (210 checkpoints), analyzes 32 open models, and studies metrics like confidence intervals, signal-to-noise ratios, and item response theory (IRT). The main findings are: (1) continuous metrics (e.g., log-likelihoods) exhibit lower variance and higher monotonicity than discrete metrics; (2) simple reformulations like framing MMLU as a cloze task reduce variance for smaller (~7B) models; (3) efficient benchmarking methods (subsampling, IRT-based selection) do not meaningfully reduce variance and may increase it. The paper recommends factoring variance into model comparisons.

### Strengths
- Addresses a timely and important topic: understanding and reporting variance in LLM evaluations is crucial given close model performances.
- Provides direct empirical estimates of seed variance across multiple benchmarks (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, GSM8K, HumanEval) and models, offering a useful reference.
- Shows convincing evidence that continuous metrics (log-likelihoods) have higher signal-to-noise ratios and monotonicity than discrete accuracy, and demonstrates that simple formatting changes (e.g., MMLU completion format) reduce variance.
- Cautionary result on the unreliability of IRT-based methods for reducing variance in LLM evaluations is practically relevant.
- Clearly written and easy to understand (cogent presentation), with demonstrations that variance can obscure pretraining progress.
- Covers multiple variance metrics (seed variance, confidence intervals, monotonicity) and explores techniques from human testing literature, explaining why they fail.

### Weaknesses
**1. Overclaiming scope and lack of generalizability**
- The title and abstract suggest a broad study of “variance in evaluation benchmarks,” but the paper only investigates *training seed variance*. Other sources (e.g., prompt formatting, option order, decoding strategies, data augmentation, optimization choices) are not studied or compared. Several reviewers ([R2, R5, R4]) note this overclaiming and ask for a clear distinction between training and inference variance. The paper does not show that seed variance is the most critical factor relative to other known variance sources (e.g., [R5] cites prompt design and benchmark detail sensitivity).
- The variance estimates are derived from a single model scale (LLaMA 7B, 210B tokens). It is unclear if these numbers transfer to other sizes (e.g., 1B, 13B, 70B) or training durations ([R3, R4, R6]). R4 questions whether 7B variance is representative of models trained under different FLOPs budgets. R6 and R3 request experiments at other scales or at least a discussion of scaling trends.
- The findings are based on base (uninstructed) models. Benchmarks like MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag use instruction-like formats, which base models handle poorly. This may confound the observed variance differences (e.g., cloze formatting may help just because base models struggle with multiple-choice format) ([R6]). R6 asks for evidence with instruction-tuned models.

**2. Missing comparative analysis with other variance sources and practical relevance**
- The paper does not compare seed variance with other well-documented variance sources such as prompt design, option ordering, or decoding stochasticity ([R2, R4, R5]). R5 requests a comparative analysis to show whether seed variance is a dominant contributor. R4 notes that interaction between seed variance and other random factors (e.g., randomized options) is unexplored.
- R1 and R5 argue the paper does not convincingly demonstrate how quantifying seed variance leads to actionable insights or changes current evaluation practice. R1 gives an example of alternative (ontology-guided interventions) that might be more impactful. R5 points out that retraining multiple seeds is compute-heavy and may be impractical, questioning the utility of the proposed approach.
- The paper does not show that the reported variance estimates can actually invalidate previously reported improvements (e.g., on the 32 models apart from Llama-7B) ([R5]).

**3. Methodological concerns and missing details**
- The computation of Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNRs) for discrete vs. continuous metrics is not explained precisely. It is unclear whether mean and std are computed across seeds or another dimension, and whether the noise component is correctly isolated ([R3]).
- The monotonicity metric used (unnormalised) does not account for chance-level monotonicity. R3 suggests using chance-corrected measures (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) and references Geirhos et al. R3 also notes monotonicity was explored in FineWeb.
- The difference between the discrete standard deviations in Table 2 and those in Table 1 is not explained (same metrics, same models) ([R3]).
- For generative tasks (GSM8K, HumanEval), it is unclear what decoding strategy was used and whether likelihoods are token-length normalized ([R3]).
- R3 notes a typo in Fig 3 caption (“discriminability” misspelled) and asks for variance on the correlation between train/test item discrimination, and whether the “best”/“worst” model split is confounded by data contamination.
- The analysis of IRT-based methods is weakened because the smaller IRT test sets may themselves increase variance. R3 asks for a control: random subsets of equal size instead of IRT-selected items. R5 also questions whether item analysis methods were ever claimed to reduce variance; if not, evaluating them on variance reduction may be unfair.

**4. Weak support for key claims**
- The claim that continuous metrics are better suited for early-stage model comparison is not backed by evidence that they indeed improve prediction from early to late training or from small to large scales ([R6]).
- The criticism of efficient benchmarking (subsampling, IRT) is not fully convincing. Table 7 shows Kendall’s tau remains high and stable; Tables 3 and 4 show relatively small increases in variance or decreases in monotonicity after subsampling, except for GSM8K discrete ([R5]). The rationale for subsampling to extreme 100 samples is unclear; R5 asks whether aggregating multiple 100-sample estimates could reduce variance while retaining efficiency.
- The emphasis on MMLU is problematic because one of the compared metrics (discrete accuracy on multiple-choice) is near random for small 7B models; continuous log‑likelihood simply surpasses random performance earlier, which may not constitute a general variance advantage ([R5]).

**5. Missing experiments and other concerns**
- No analysis of how variance changes across pretraining stages (intermediate checkpoints) for different model sizes ([R4]).
- No examination of how variance varies with model size, number of shots, choice of shots, or task type ([R3, R4]).
- Disagreement noted: while some reviewers find the paper valuable (R3 rating 6), others (R1, R5, R6) see limited impact due to the narrow scope and weak generalizability. The paper does not resolve whether seed variance is practically the most important variance source.
