## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers generally acknowledge the novelty and motivation of the universal geometric graph-of-sets representation and the GET architecture. However, they raise substantial concerns about clarity, insufficient comparison with recent state-of-the-art methods, reliance on domain-specific block construction, small dataset scales, lack of complexity and capacity analysis, and unclear advantages over atom-level or distance-based approaches. One reviewer (score 8) is very positive and finds only minor flaws, while two reviewers (score 5) are more critical, and one (score 6) has moderate concerns with high confidence.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and well-motivated (all reviewers).
- The proposed geometric graph of sets provides a universal representation for 3D molecular complexes, unifying encoding across proteins, small molecules, and RNA/DNA (R1, R2, R4).
- The GET model is E(3)-equivariant and captures interactions at both block and atom scales through bilevel attention (R1, R3).
- Extensive experiments across multiple interaction types (protein–protein, protein–small molecule, protein–RNA/DNA) support generalization (R1, R2, R3).
- The hierarchical approach and equivariant design are original (R2, R3).
- Overall experimental results are good and significant (R2, R3).
- The paper is well structured (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient comparison with state-of-the-art methods.** Missing comparisons with GemNet, Equiformer, EquiformerV2, LEFTNet, Allegro, MACE, and other recent equivariant GNNs (R1, R4). When comparing to older baselines (SchNet, DimeNet++, Tables 2, 3), the paper should include model complexity and capacity differences (R3).
- **Reliance on domain-specific block construction** (e.g., residues as nodes in proteins) may limit universality; no systematic exploration of alternative block construction methods is provided (R1). Reviewer 2 asks for elaboration on how the bilevel representation is built (e.g., K in KNN graph) and whether better block-construction methods exist besides KNN. Reviewer 3 questions why hierarchical processing is better than atom-level analysis given Transformers’ long-range capabilities.
- **Limited dataset scale for some experiments.** PPA and PBA datasets are small, raising concerns about statistical significance and generalizability to larger, more challenging real-world scenarios (R1).
- **No experiments on bare molecules** (e.g., property prediction); the current focus on complexes limits assessment of model versatility (R1).
- **Computational complexity concerns.** High cost noted in Table 9; lack of detailed analysis of computational bottlenecks or optimization strategies (R1).
- **Clarity and technical detail issues.** Equation (3) is not well-defined (MLP/RBF) and appears closely related to SchNet/PhysNet (R3). Unclear how Equation (5) differs from Jin et al.’s pooling (R3). Lack of motivation for keeping coordinates instead of using interatomic pairwise distances or C-RMSD (R3). Equation (10)’s E(3)-equivariance is not clear without consulting the appendix (R3). Including Figure 4 in the main text would improve clarity (R1). Adding statistics for the PDBBind benchmark in the paper would help (R1).
- **Typos.** “TorchMDNet (ET)” is referred to as “TorchMD” (R4). Typo in Sec. 1 (“approache”) (R2).
- **Practical implications** for applications are not discussed (R4).
- **Minority view:** One reviewer (score 8) reported no obvious weaknesses beyond the above minor flaws, while the other three reviewers (scores 5, 5, 6) found significant shortcomings in comparison scope, clarity, and experimental rigor.
