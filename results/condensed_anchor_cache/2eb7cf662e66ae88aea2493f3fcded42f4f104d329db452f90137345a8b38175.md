## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents Spread Preference Annotation (SPA), which reduces the cost of human-annotated preference data for LLM alignment by using the model’s own logits to directly score pairwise preferences, without an external reward model or in-context learning. SPA iteratively generates responses, annotates them with a confidence-based denoising scheme (Eq. 9–10), and aligns via a modified DPO objective. Experiments with Mistral 7B on 3.3% of UltraFeedback preferences achieve strong AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench results, outperforming full-data DPO and several baselines (iterative DPO, LLM-as-judge, explicit reward + RLHF). The method extends to Phi and Llama (Table 5) and shows robustness to seed data variance (Table 4). Reviewer ratings: one reviewer gave 10 (confidence 4), two gave 8 (confidences 4 and 3)—the higher‑scoring reviewer found no major weaknesses; the other two raised several clarifications and explanatory gaps.

### Strengths
- The paper is well‑written, clearly contextualizing SPA within the alignment literature and the fundamentals of DPO (Section 3).  
- The core idea—leveraging the LLM’s implicit reward (logits) from a small seed dataset for direct preference annotation—is simple, intuitive, and novel compared to self‑rewarding or reward‑model approaches.  
- Practical contributions: the self‑refinement denoising mechanism (Eq. 9 for confidence prediction, Eq. 10 for smoothed preference label); reproducibility details (Section 5, L311–321); minimal code changes to DPO; and planned open‑source release.  
- Strong empirical results on AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT‑Bench, including comparisons to three baseline categories (iterative DPO, LLM‑as‑judge, explicit reward + RLHF, L288–291).  
- Demonstrates generalizability beyond Mistral 7B (Phi, Llama; Table 5) and robustness to seed data choice (Table 4).  
- The reduction in human annotation effort (3.3% of UltraFeedback) is both promising and robust across runs.

### Weaknesses
- **Length control (Reviewer 1):** The “length control” setting in AlpacaEval 2.0, used in most results, is introduced at Section 5.2 without sufficient explanation.  
- **Gold‑label definition (Reviewer 1):** The meaning of “gold label” in Tables 1 and 5 is not explicitly stated (presumably UltraFeedback preferences).  
- **De‑coupled noise detection (Reviewer 2):** Section 4.2 is unclear about how $z_{\tilde{\theta}}$ replaces $z_{\theta}$ in self‑refinement. The final usage of approximated logits and labels should be stated in the main text.  
- **Why small data works / outperforms full DPO (Reviewer 2):** No explanatory discussion is provided for why SPA succeeds with only 3.3% of data and even surpasses DPO trained on the full UltraFeedback set (e.g., does it elicit latent human knowledge, or is the 3.3% subset already sufficient?).  
- **LLM‑as‑judge model missing (Reviewer 2):** Table 2 does not specify which model was used for the “LLM‑as‑judge” baseline; it should clarify whether ${\pi}_{i-1}$ was tried.  
- **Seed data selection (Reviewer 3):** The method for choosing the initial seed data is unexplained (random sampling? how are differences in human preferences handled?).  
- **Response generation diversity (Reviewer 3):** Section 4.1 describes sampling $y_1$ and $y_2$ but does not specify whether diversity techniques are employed or only random sampling.  
- **Reliability of expanded data (Reviewer 3):** No explicit verification or guarantee is given for the reliability of the self‑annotated preference data.  
- **Formatting issues (Reviewers 2, 3):** References and appendix have lines extending beyond the page border; a typo “Lengh‑control” appears in Figure 2.
