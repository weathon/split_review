## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes SET-MI, a membership inference (MI) method that leverages the set assumption: documents sharing a common attribute (e.g., creation date, license type) are either all present or all absent in the training dataset. By aggregating membership scores over such sets, the method improves upon individual MI techniques. The authors construct five benchmarks (Wikipedia, arXiv, languages, licensing, instruction-based datasets) and show an average 0.14 AUROC gain over four prior MI methods. Additional experiments analyze the impact of model size, deduplication, sequence length, and robustness to noise. The method is simple and orthogonal to existing MI approaches.

### Strengths
- The idea is novel, simple, and effective, and is orthogonal to previous MI methods, achieving consistent performance enhancements across almost all benchmark settings.
- The paper is relatively clearly written and well-structured; key concepts like the set assumption are effectively clarified through examples.
- Two relatively large datasets (Wikipedia and arXiv) are used, alongside five diverse benchmarks covering Wikipedia, Arxiv, languages, licensing, and instruction-based datasets.
- Extensive experiments demonstrate consistent improvements over individual MI methods, especially with larger models, duplicated datasets, and longer document sequences.
- Further analytical experiments show robustness of SET-MI to some extent, including evaluation in noisy settings.
- The method improves membership inference for black-box models, which could be useful for downstream tasks like privacy auditing.

### Weaknesses
- The novelty of the method appears limited; SET-MI simply aggregates and averages individual membership scores across document groups, which is more of an incremental improvement.
- The set assumption is strong and lacks a solid theoretical foundation. Even if a whole set is intended to be included, data preprocessing (e.g., deduplication, removal of low-credibility or overlapping content) may remove some documents. For example, evolving news can cause substantial content overlap across sets, making the assumption questionable in practice.
- The method relies heavily on prior knowledge to select an appropriate attribute for set division; in practice, one cannot know which set assumption to use, and different assumptions can produce completely different scores for the same document, making it difficult to judge which result is correct.
- The datasets used in experiments are mostly small (except Wikipedia and arXiv) and are relatively clean and non-redundant, which may not represent typical real-world large datasets that require extensive cleaning, deduplication, and preprocessing.
- The authors acknowledge that metadata enabling set identification may not be available in practical MI scenarios, leaving this assumption unrelaxed.
- Performance declines significantly in noisy settings (where the set assumption is partially violated), indicating limitations in generalizability.
- Experiment details are not clearly written. In particular, it is not clear how membership scores are used to decide whether data is in the training set; specific decision criteria or thresholds are missing, limiting reproducibility.
- The underlying assumption that high output scores reliably indicate membership is questioned: models may produce high scores for data that closely resembles the training distribution even if not seen during training (e.g., test points sharing common patterns). Evidence to support this assumption is lacking.
- The experiments do not include existing MI benchmarks such as WIKIMIA, which would strengthen the evaluation.
- The ratio of selected tokens to total tokens in each original document is not examined; varying this ratio may capture different levels of unique document features and impact inference performance.
