## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces FeDLRT, a federated dynamical low-rank training scheme that reduces both client compute and communication costs by constructing a global low-rank basis of network weights, enabling clients to train on a small coefficient matrix. A variance correction mechanism is added to reduce client drift. The method dynamically augments/truncates the rank. Convergence guarantees (O(1/T) in the minimal gradient norm) are provided for non-convex objectives under deterministic gradients and full client participation. Experiments on LLSR, CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18/AlexNet/VGG), and CIFAR-100 (ViT) show up to an order of magnitude reduction in compute/communication with minimal accuracy loss.

### Strengths
- Strong theoretical guarantees: convergence proof to a stationary point with a clear O(1/T) rate; proofs are provided for all claims.
- Joint reduction of client compute and communication: clients optimize a small coefficient matrix rather than full weights; costs scale linearly with the number of parameters n.
- Clear narrative, well-motivated; bi-directional compression is a desirable but relatively underexplored property in FL.
- Algorithm presentation is effective, with clear pseudo-code and well-organized technical sections; discussions after each lemma/theorem help accessibility.
- Extensive appendix experiments covering multiple DNN architectures and datasets broaden validation.
- Variance correction mechanism demonstrably alleviates client drift and enables larger client participation.
- The method is a novel approach to low-rank FL with an interesting variance reduction component; one reviewer (score 8) found the computational results promising and the motivation strong.
- Empirical results show FeDLRT outperforming FedLin in most settings.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient comparison with related methods:** The paper compares only with FedLin and FedAvg, but many relevant compression and low-rank methods exist and should be included experimentally or at least discussed thoroughly. Missing comparisons include: FedPara, FedDLR, FEDHM, LBGM, FetchSGD, ComFetch, Fjord, LASER, HeteroFL, PriSM, and subnetwork training (e.g., FjORD). A detailed comparison of compute/memory/communication costs across methods is needed, not just communication. One reviewer (score 8) also noted that Table 1 compares costs of 3 algorithms but only FedLin is used in experiments.
- **Limited and weak experimental evaluation:**
    - The only non-synthetic tasks are CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, which are not standard FL benchmarks; more realistic FL datasets (e.g., those with natural non-IID splits) should be used.
    - No ablation over varying degrees of heterogeneity (e.g., Dirichlet allocation) is performed; this is a major omission for a method aiming to combat client drift.
    - The number of clients is small (≤32 in main experiments, often ≤8 in Appendix); larger client counts and partial participation should be evaluated.
    - Accuracy on CIFAR-10 (93% for 32 workers) is well below state-of-the-art; the paper does not analyze how close the method can approach SOTA with proper tuning.
    - The linear least squares regression experiment is insufficient to demonstrate FL efficacy.
- **Theoretical limitations and clarity issues:**
    - Convergence guarantees hold only for deterministic gradients; the analysis does not account for the mini-batch stochasticity standard in neural network training.
    - Results are limited to the general non-convex setting; no guarantees for strongly convex, convex, or PL-conditioned cases are provided, yet the first experiment is convex linear regression, creating a gap between theory and experiments.
    - The convergence bounds in Theorem 3 and Corollary 1 contain a residual error term that does not vanish even with diminishing step sizes, unlike many client-drift correction methods that converge to the exact stationary point without persistent error. The authors should explain the source of this residual.
    - Assumption 1 appears restrictive; its motivation and implications are not discussed.
    - Several theoretical statements (Lemma 1, Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 4) are unclear or ambiguously stated; the meaning of the inequalities and their significance needs more explanation.
    - Lipschitz continuity (L-smoothness) of gradients is not rigorously defined.
- **Variance correction novelty and literature connection:**
    - The variance correction mechanism is essentially error feedback, which is standard in compressed FL; the paper should discuss its relationship with existing error-feedback works (e.g., [5], [6] in reviewer 2’s comments).
    - Multiple important client drift reduction techniques are not discussed at all: ProxSkip, S-Local-SVRG, 5GCS, TAMUNA, and SCAFFOLD (the latter achieves drift correction without extra communication, contrary to the paper’s statement that drift correction requires additional communication).
- **Algorithm presentation and reproducibility issues:**
    - The algorithm description in Section 3.1 can be improved; Algorithm 2 (which contains key definitions/notations for Section 3.1) is placed in the appendix and never referenced in the main body.
    - Experimental details necessary for reproducibility are missing: how learning rates were chosen; how data was split across clients in CIFAR-10; how the single client used for warm-up was selected. Learning rates were not tuned per algorithm, which may bias comparisons.
    - The meaning of basis augmentation is not explained; also, why is \(\tilde{S}^\star\) not diagonal?
    - Notation is non-standard: the symbol \(C\) is used for client cohort size (mini-batch of clients) when \(M\) or \(n\) is more common.
- **Figures and tables:**
    - Figure 6 is nearly unreadable in print; preferably use tables or larger fonts.
    - Table 1 is too small; some reviewers noted “b” is used without definition.
    - Many plots can be improved for neatness and clarity; confidence intervals should be shown alongside medians.
- **Questions that overlap with criticisms/missing experiments:**
    - What are the guarantees under statistical heterogeneity (analogous to bounded gradient dissimilarity) for FeDLRT? Provide experiments with Dirichlet splits on real data.
    - How do the convergence analysis and assumptions compare with methods like LBGM that also use a global basis?
    - What is the effect of different singular value truncation thresholds? How is the threshold selected?
    - Why does accuracy increase from 1 to 4 clients then decrease with more clients (Figure 6)? Is this due to suboptimal hyperparameters or an inherent limitation?
    - Why is the proposed method only evaluated under full participation? Partial participation is more realistic.
