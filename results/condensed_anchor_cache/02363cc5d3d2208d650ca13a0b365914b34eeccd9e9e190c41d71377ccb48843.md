## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Conversational Few-Shot Prompting for instruction-tuned chat models, structuring few-shot examples as multi-turn dialogues between user and assistant instead of a single prompt. This conversational framing aims to align with the interactive nature of chat models. Experiments on diverse benchmarks (including BoolQ, MMLU) with open-source LLMs (LLaMA series, Qwen series) and varying shot settings (1, 2, 4, etc.) show improved performance, especially in low-shot scenarios, without additional fine-tuning. The authors claim reduced prompt sensitivity and enhanced instruction-following and generalization.

### Strengths
- Presents a new prompting technique that structures few-shot examples as multi-turn dialogues, aligning with the interactive nature of chat models and leveraging their conversational capabilities (R1, R2, R3).
- Comprehensive experimental evaluation across a diverse set of benchmarks, multiple open-source LLMs (LLaMA series, Qwen series), and varying shot settings (1, 2, 4, etc.) provides strong empirical evidence for effectiveness (R1, R2).
- The paper is well-structured with clear problem definition, background, and method description (R1).
- Demonstrates enhanced instruction-following and generalization without fine-tuning (R3).

### Weaknesses
- The approach is primarily a prompt engineering technique and lacks technical novelty (R2). However, one reviewer noted that the extensive experiments make it an acceptable contribution despite this (R2; contrast: another reviewer rated the paper lower overall).
- No clear explanation of why the multi-turn dialogue format improves overall performance or enhances instruction-following; the underlying mechanism is not discussed (R1, R3). For example, why would putting few-shot exemplars in a chat format be beneficial? Is there any potential mechanism behind the scenes?
- Limited insight into error cases or scenarios where the method may perform poorly; robustness across applications is not thoroughly analyzed (R1, R3). The paper does not present error analysis or discuss specific failure cases by task type or difficulty level (R3). For instance, are there specific tasks (e.g., instruction-following vs. complex reasoning) where the method is less effective or where conversational context could hinder performance? (R3)
- Clarification needed on the exact difference between standard few-shot prompting in a chat-based model (bottom right of Figure 1) and the proposed conversational few-shot prompting (R2).
- Motivation is not fully clear: why is few-shot prompting beneficial for instruction-tuned chat models that are not specifically optimized for this approach? (R2)
- In Table 1, performance decreases with larger models as the number of few-shot examples increases; an explanation is needed (e.g., perhaps larger models exhibit a higher tendency to refuse generating responses). In the BoolQ dataset, the LLaMA 70B model shows no performance difference regardless of few-shot count, suggesting the proposed method may be ineffective in that case (R2).
- In Table 2, the LLaMA-3.2-1B model achieves a score of 0 on MMLU even with varying few-shot examples; the authors should provide one or two generated examples to verify (R2).
- The paper does not compare the proposed method against other prompting techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) + few-shot vs. conversational few-shot without CoT, especially for complex reasoning scenarios (R3).
- Missing details on decoding parameters (e.g., temperature, top-p), whether any randomness was included during decoding steps, and how many runs were conducted for each evaluation task (R3).
- The placement of instructions within the conversational prompt is unclear; an example comparing how instructions are incorporated in this approach versus traditional instruction-following formats would be helpful (R3). Also, the specific prompting templates used (including format of few-shot examples and instructions given to models) are not provided (R1).
- Additional experiments comparing with other few-shot prompting techniques (e.g., prompt tuning, prompted fine-tuning) would help contextualize the relative merits and limitations of the conversational approach (R1).
- It is unclear if there are specific tasks or model architectures where this approach might be less effective; edge cases where conversational context does not help or even hinders performance are not considered (R3).
