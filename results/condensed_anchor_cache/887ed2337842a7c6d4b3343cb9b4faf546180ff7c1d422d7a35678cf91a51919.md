## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Interactive Causal Discovery Agent (ICDA), using LLMs to select edge interventions and update causal graphs interactively over multiple rounds. The approach uses LLM uncertainty to choose edges for intervention and a local update strategy that leverages binary feedback. Experiments on eight real-world graphs show ICDA outperforms a random selection baseline (by up to 0.5 F1). Reviewers acknowledge novelty but raise major concerns about the realism of the intervention setting, the reliance on potentially unreliable LLM outputs, insufficient baselines and comparisons, and whether the method fits the “LLMs as optimizers” framework. One reviewer (score 6) is more positive; two reviewers (scores 3) are more negative, citing lack of theoretical depth and experimental thoroughness.

### Strengths
- **Novel application**: Using LLMs for interactive causal discovery with uncertainly‑driven intervention selection and local updates is a fresh direction (R1, R3, R5).
- **Well motivated**: The approach is clearly motivated by the literature on LLMs for causal discovery and as optimizers (R2).
- **Extensive experiments**: Evaluation spans eight real‑world graphs, including a new protein transcription factor graph (post‑July 2024) to test memorization; multiple ablations dissect components (local updates, confidence scoring, prompt design) (R1, R2, R4).
- **Interesting trade‑offs**: Experiments underline the importance of balancing local updates (e.g., throwing the whole graph vs. only modifying intervened edges) (R4).
- **Clear writing**: Most reviewers find the paper well‑written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3, R5).
- **Outperforms random baseline**: ICDA achieves strong gains over random edge selection; improvements can be up to 0.5 F1 (R1, R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Unrealistic intervention setting**:  
  - The definition of an “edge intervention” assumes that perturbing one variable and observing a statistically significant change in another is sufficient to claim a direct causal relation, ignoring confounding and indirect effects (R2 – score 5).  
  - It is unrealistic to directly obtain the ground‑truth causal edge label in each intervention; the setting differs significantly from the standard practice in experimental design literature (R3 – score 3, confidence 4; also noted by R1 as missing comparison with statistical methods).  
  - A concrete example of a causal operation that reveals an edge without additional assumptions is requested (R2).

- **LLM reliability is questionable**:  
  - Multiple works show LLMs cannot provide faithful causal results (cited by R3: [4,5]), yet ICDA heavily depends on LLM predictions and confidences (R3).  
  - There is no guarantee that LLM‑provided uncertainty is valid; some research suggests LLM confidence may be unreliable (R1, R3).  
  - One reviewer asks for intuition on why ICDA outperforms random agents if LLM confidence is unreliable (R1).

- **Insufficient comparisons and baselines**:  
  - Lack of comparison with statistical causal discovery methods (R1, R3).  
  - No comparison with prior works on intervention selection / experimental design (R3 cites [1,2,3]).  
  - The experimental setup only includes three basic baselines: random selection, direct LLM, and static confidence selection; this is considered too simple for a top‑tier conference (R5).  
  - Only one LLM model is used in the main experiments; results with smaller models are limited to a single figure (Fig. 6) (R1, R5).  
  - A quantitative table (with confidence intervals) should accompany the graphs (R5).  
  - Some figures (e.g., Fig. 6 last subplot) lack confidence bounds (R1).

- **LLMs as optimizers framework not properly satisfied**:  
  - The next edges to query are selected algorithmically based on confidences, not by the LLM; the objective (F1) is never parsed to the LLM, and the LLM is only used post‑hoc after obtaining ground‑truth labels, not as an optimizer (R4 – score 5).  
  - The choice of local updates (only updating parents of intervened edges) is not sufficiently justified; why that specific update rule? (R4).  
  - The use of the F1 metric as a loss is not explained (R4).

- **Lack of limitations discussion**: The paper does not discuss limitations of the proposed approach (R2).

- **Minor issues**:  
  - Figures are too small / font too small (R1, R2, R4).  
  - Weird indent on L254 (R1).  
  - Missing space on line 312 (“weablate”) (R2).  
  - Misplaced citation (Sharma & Kiciman, 2020) on line 147 (R2).  
  - Algorithm line numbers are all 0; key steps in the algorithm are not defined (R3).  
  - Chronological confusion in “Building on Meek (2013), Chickering (2002)” (R4).  
  - Typo on l.403–404 “we notefFor” / “we note that” (R4).
