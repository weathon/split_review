## Merged Review

### Summary
The authors propose OOD-CHAMELEON, a method for selecting the best algorithm for OOD generalization by treating algorithm selection as a supervised classification problem over candidate algorithms. They construct a meta-dataset of datasets with various distribution shifts (covariate, label, spurious correlations) and train a model to predict relative algorithm performance based on dataset characteristics, enabling a priori selection without training multiple models. Experiments on synthetic and two real-world image datasets with five algorithms show adaptive selection outperforms any single algorithm and simple heuristics. The paper also provides insights into data/algorithm interactions. Reviewers’ opinions diverged: one reviewer (score 6) found the approach interesting, motivation clear, and performance good, while three others (scores 3, 3, 3) expressed concerns about insufficient validation, limited algorithms and datasets, lack of theoretical support, reliance on pre-known shift attributes, and practical applicability.

### Strengths
- The problem setting of algorithm selection for OOD generalization is novel (R1).
- Characterizing OOD generalization tasks as distinct objectives and selecting methods accordingly is a reasonable approach with practical implications (R2).
- The possibility of learning how to select algorithms based on dataset characteristics is very interesting (R3).
- The motivation for the work is clear (R3).
- The performance of the method is good and makes the case for using properties of the data to select different algorithms on a per-dataset basis (R3).
- OOD-CHAMELEON eliminates the need for training and evaluating multiple models, leading to efficient use of computational resources (R4).
- The method provides interesting insights into the conditions under which different algorithms are more suitable (R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient experimental validation** (R1, R4):
   - Only five algorithms are selected as candidates; more recent and influential OOD algorithms (e.g., SOTA methods) should be included (R1).
   - Only two real-world datasets are used; more diverse datasets (different modalities like text/audio, different shift types) are needed (R1, R4).
   - The model selection should be tested on a wider range of complex models and ensemble methods (R4).
   - Unclear why other baselines like Öztürk et al. cannot be compared (R3).

2. **Lack of theoretical support and learnability justification** (R2, R4):
   - The paper does not discuss why the problem of algorithm selection is learnable; there is no analysis of differentiability or continuity. The solution likely requires specialized design and deeper insights (R2).
   - The underlying concept seems to require theoretical support; selecting the appropriate model based on data characteristics appears more challenging than learning a predictive model (R4).
   - No discussion on the minimum number of datasets required or how dimensionality/diversity of dataset feature space impacts the selector; unclear how the method generalizes to target datasets with different feature spaces (R4).

3. **Reliance on pre-known shift attributes and metrics** (R3):
   - The method requires knowing which attributes are shifting a priori; finding the proposed metrics for real-world datasets is not straightforward, especially when the underlying causes of the shift are unknown (R3).
   - The choice of attributes is central; the paper does not provide a clear methodology for determining relevant attributes, and the selection is highly sensitive to this choice. The impact of noisy or irrelevant attributes is not explored, raising robustness concerns (R3).

4. **Hyperparameter dependence and optimization details** (R1, R4):
   - The prediction uses only simple dataset characteristics and a one-hot algorithm representation, ignoring many factors like hyperparameters that drastically alter performance. This limits practical applicability (R1).
   - The performance of a model depends on how well it is optimized; the paper lacks specific details on training processes, hyperparameter tuning ranges, criteria, and computational cost for each algorithm. Without this, it is unclear whether observed performance differences are due to algorithms or suboptimal settings (R4).

5. **Questions/comments about definitions and notation** (R1, R3):
   - Why is label shift needed when any distribution shift can be decomposed into covariate and concept shift? (R1)
   - In the definition of spurious correlation (Section 2.2), there is confusion between shifts on P(Y|X_c) and P(Y|X); also, Equation 2 only considers positive correlation as spurious while negative correlation could also imply strong spurious correlation (R1).
   - Notation abuse and unclear notation: confusion between random variable X and support set, function mapping definition in Section 2.2, unclear X_c subset, and notation in Line 206 (i replaced incorrectly) (R1).
   - Typos: "outperform" (line 428), "we 9" (line 905) (R3).

6. **Writing and presentation issues** (R1, R2):
   - The writing is awkward and imprecise in places, e.g., "treats the task as a supervised classification", "on unseen datasets of controllable and realistic image data", "consist in", misuse of "knew" instead of "know" (R1).
   - Imprecise language about DomainBed study (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz 2020); unclear whether "these methods" include recent algorithms (R1).
   - Some parts (algorithm description, experimental) are compact and lack clear modular structure; long paragraphs hinder readability (R2).

7. **Other concerns** (R3, R4):
   - How would one construct a meta-dataset when the shift is not obvious or the attribute is not labeled? (R3)
   - Does the coverage of the distribution over shifts impact results (e.g., unbalanced representation of shift types or magnitudes)? (R3)
   - Would the space required to store the meta-dataset become intractable if hyperparameter variations are considered? Is there a mitigation? (R3)
   - How does OOD-CHAMELEON compare to ensemble methods that directly train multiple models and aggregate their outputs? What are the practical advantages? (R4)
   - Efficiency of OOD-CHAMELEON on large-scale datasets; are there optimization solutions? (R2)
