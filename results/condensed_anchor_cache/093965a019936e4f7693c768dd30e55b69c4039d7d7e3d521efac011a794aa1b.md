## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Sparse MeZO, a memory-efficient zeroth-order (ZO) optimization method for fine-tuning large language models. It selectively applies ZO updates to a subset of parameters (the smallest per-layer weights) to reduce gradient estimation error. A memory-optimized implementation ensures inference-level memory, enabling LLaMA-30b fine-tuning on a single A100 GPU. Experiments on SuperGLUE tasks show consistent performance and convergence speed improvements over MeZO (e.g., 9% absolute accuracy gain and 3.5× speedup). Models evaluated include LLaMA-1, Mistral-v0.1, and OPT.

### Strengths
- The combination of sparsity and MeZO is an interesting and novel direction for performance improvement (R1, R2, R4).
- Sparse MeZO achieves significant and consistent gains in accuracy and convergence speed over dense MeZO across multiple setups (R1, R2, R3).
- The insight that noisy ZO optimization is more harmful for large-magnitude weights is a useful practical finding (R3).
- The algorithm is simple, efficient, and the memory-optimized implementation incurs almost zero overhead relative to MeZO, allowing large-model tuning on a single GPU (R2, R3).
- The paper is well motivated, logically structured, and clearly written (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Contribution and justification**  
  – The overall contribution is incremental, adding sparsification on top of MeZO (R3).  
  – The derivation of why small-magnitude weights are less impacted by ZO gradient noise lacks mathematical rigor and intuition. Key soundness issues: (i) why should using fewer weights help at all? For first-order methods this would be absurd; what is the difference? (ii) why exactly the smallest weights are most impactful is not justified beyond empirical observation (R4).  
  – The conclusion from Figure 2b that “ZO gradients suffer from overfitting/noise” is not sound: it compares loss change on a different batch than the one used by MeZO; the same statement could hold for SGD. A comparison on the same batch is needed (R4).  
  – Line 197 states “all parameters share the same value” – this is a false premise. The MeZO gradient estimate is a random directional derivative with high probability far from the true gradient direction. The argument about small vs. large weights based on this is unclear (R4).  
  – The sparsity vs. performance curve in Figure 4 (e.g., 70% bad, 75% good, 80% worse again) is arbitrary and non-intuitive; it may be a noise artifact (R4).

- **Threshold selection and masking details**  
  – The method for determining the “small weight” threshold is not clearly specified: is it an absolute value or a percentile? Is it fixed across layers or dynamically adjusted? How does it vary across tasks? Is it a hyperparameter requiring re-tuning? (R1)  
  – Are the masks updated every iteration? This resembles Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) where update frequency critically impacts performance (R2).  
  – Why is the threshold not set dynamically each iteration, which seems efficient and natural? (R4)

- **Missing baselines and comparisons**  
  – No comparison with first-order optimization under the same masking strategy to isolate whether gains come from masking itself or from the combination with ZO (R1).  
  – PEFT adapters (LoRA, DoRA) also reduce memory on optimizer states; for short sequences and small batches (as in SuperGLUE), activation/gradient memory is modest and can be further reduced by gradient accumulation. Comparison with these baselines, including memory usage in Table 3, is needed (R3).  
  – “MeZO-LoRA” is mentioned in Table 1 but never defined or explained (R4).  
  – No comparison of gradient updates between standard SGD and Sparse MeZO on a smaller model (~1B) (R3).  
  – It is unclear whether the 3.5× speed-up in Figure 1 uses the same learning rate for both methods, or whether it depends on differing learning rates (R2, R4).

- **Evaluation scope**  
  – Evaluation uses outdated models (LLaMA-1, Mistral-v0.1) released over a year ago. Current models (LLaMA-3, Qwen-2.5, Gemma-2) have advanced dramatically. Additionally, the method should be tested on a more challenging setup, such as instruction tuning (Alpaca, OASST) (R3).

- **Computational overhead**  
  – No specific numbers on the computational cost of generating the dynamic mask at each iteration (including threshold calculation and mask application) compared to base MeZO. Wall-clock time comparisons similar to Figure 1 are missing (R1).  
  – Whether numbers in tables are for same runtime or same number of iterations is not stated; if MeZO variants are faster, runtime-equivalent comparisons would be informative (R4).

- **Missing definitions and references**  
  – “SPSA” (Line 123) is not defined (R4).  
  – Appendix F refers to “Lemma 3.2” which does not exist in the paper (R4).

- **Minor**  
  – Section 4.1 uses \citet where \citep is intended, hindering readability (R4).  
  – Line 483: typo “on a single A100 GPU” (R4).  
  – Could Sparse MeZO be integrated with LoRA (as MeZO-LoRA was done previously)? (R2)  
  – Could more advanced masking methods like Wanda or SparseGPT further improve performance? (R2)

- **Disagreement among reviewers**  
  – While R2 and R3 praise the idea and performance, R4 considers the contribution a major issue due to vague derivation and lack of insight beyond empirical results. R3 also notes the contribution is incremental. All reviewers agree that several soundness and clarity issues must be resolved before publication.
