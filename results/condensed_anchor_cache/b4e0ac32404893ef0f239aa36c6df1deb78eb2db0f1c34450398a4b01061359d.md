## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes applying Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) from psychometrics to LLM evaluation, dynamically selecting test questions based on estimated ability to improve efficiency and enable comparisons with humans. Using three datasets (MOOC, MATH, CODE) and multiple commercial LLMs, the authors report that GPT-4 reaches the cognitive level of middle-level students, while other models exhibit behaviours like careless slipping or guessing. Reviewers are split: two (R1, R2) rate the paper low (score 3) citing unclear motivation, unverified assumptions, and questionable advantages in the LLM context; two (R3, R4) rate it higher (score 5) valuing novelty and organization but still raise concerns about efficiency justification and IRT parameter handling.

### Strengths
- **Novel application of CAT to LLM evaluation** – The idea of using Computerized Adaptive Testing is new in this domain (R3, R4).  
- **Clear presentation and organization** – The paper is well-structured and easy to follow (R2, R3, R4).  
- **Extensive evaluation and demonstration** – The evaluation covers multiple models and datasets and shows claimed advantages (R2).  
- **Model–model and model–human comparisons** – The framework enables direct comparisons between LLMs and human benchmarks (R3).  
- **Interpretable parameters** – IRT-based ability, slipping, and guessing parameters provide interpretable characterisations of model behaviour (R4).  
- **Code provided** – Reproducibility is supported (R3).  
- **Potential efficiency gain** – If efficiency is a genuine concern, CAT can converge to an accurate assessment with fewer questions (R1, with caveat that the reviewer questions whether efficiency is a real problem).

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear motivation and insufficient demonstration of efficiency**  
  - The paper claims efficiency gains but does not convincingly show why evaluation efficiency is a problem for LLMs: inference is relatively cheap compared to training, evaluations are typically one-time, and LLMs do not tire from many questions (R1, R2, R3).  
  - The motivation relies on the cost of free-response evaluation, yet the experiments use only multiple-choice questions (appendix), which are cheap to score (R1).  
  - No quantitative comparison of the number of forward passes saved versus static testing is provided (R1).  
- **Assumption that LLMs behave like humans for IRT calibration is unverified**  
  - The paper uses IRT parameters pre-calibrated on human responses without justifying that LLM response patterns match human patterns; if they do not, comparing LLMs to humans on that scale is questionable (R1, R4).  
  - It is unclear whether alternative calibration using both human and LLM responses would be more appropriate (R4).  
- **Fairness and comparability across models when test sets differ**  
  - If each model receives a different subset of questions (up to 20–30% variation), it is not obvious how scores are calibrated to ensure fair comparison; using different test sets may hinder leaderboard-style comparisons and add communication noise (R2).  
- **Lack of baseline comparisons**  
  - The paper does not compare CAT-based rankings to rankings obtained from the full static test sets, making it difficult to assess whether the adaptive procedure changes conclusions (R2).  
- **Unspecified IRT parameter handling and calibration details**  
  - It is not explicitly stated which IRT parameters (difficulty β, discrimination α, guessing c) are pre-estimated and fixed versus updated during the CAT procedure (R4).  
  - Whether calibration (β estimation) is performed separately per dataset or jointly across all three datasets is unclear (R4).  
  - The interpretation of the “student” curve in Figure 4c is ambiguous: does it represent a single student or an average? The uncertainty differences between the student and model curves require additional discussion (R4).  
- **Unsubstantiated or fringe claims**  
  - The abstract states “LLMs have shown human-level cognitive ability” and refers to “the tip of the iceberg of its latent knowledge” without supporting evidence; such claims are overreaching (R1).  
- **Writing quality issues**  
  - Grammatical mistakes and ambiguous sentences detract from clarity (R1). (Note: other reviewers found the paper clear, indicating disagreement on presentation quality.)  
- **Novelty of findings not clearly delineated**  
  - Observations such as “careless student” behaviour and GPT‑4 being strongest are interesting, but the paper does not explicitly separate which insights are new versus already known from prior work (R3).  
- **Unspecified model version and dataset handling**  
  - “ChatGPT” is not identified as a specific model version (e.g., gpt-3.5-turbo) (R3).  
  - The MATH dataset contains figures; how text-only models were evaluated on such questions is not explained (R4).
