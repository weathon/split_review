## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Easy Consistency Tuning (ECT), a method to train consistency models (CMs) efficiently by initializing from a pretrained diffusion model (DM) and progressively strengthening the consistency condition using a continuous-time schedule, decoupled weighting (L₂ with adaptive weighting inspired by pseudo-Huber), and dropout. The method achieves 2-step FID 2.73 on CIFAR-10 in 1 hour on a single A100 GPU and state-of-the-art results on ImageNet 64×64, dramatically reducing training time compared to prior consistency distillation (CD) and consistency training (CT). The paper also explores scaling laws, showing power-law improvements with compute, and discusses the “curse of consistency”.

### Strengths
- **Efficiency and strong results**: Training time is drastically reduced while achieving top performance (CIFAR-10 2-step FID 2.73 in 1 hour on A100, ImageNet 64×64 SOTA). (R1, R2, R4)
- **Simple, well-motivated, and actionable**: The method is presented clearly, with an organic motivation from the DM–CM connection and a public codebase. It has potential to become a strong baseline for future CM research. (R4)
- **Interesting insights**: The “curse of consistency” (bounding single-step error) and the scaling-law analysis (showing improved sample quality with more compute in CM fine-tuning) are novel and underexplored directions. (R2, R3)
- **Comprehensive empirical comparison**: The paper compares against many strong baselines (Table 1) and provides ablations in the appendix. (R2)

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and contribution clarity**
   - The core idea of initializing CMs from a pretrained DM was already considered in Song et al. (2023, Appendix B.3), albeit without full exploitation; this prior art is not sufficiently discussed. (R1, R4)
   - Section 3.1 appears to be background from Song et al. (2023) rather than a new contribution; the continuous-time formulation and derived weighting \(w(t,r)=1/(t-r)\) are not actually used in practice (decoupled weighting is used instead). (R1, R2, R4)
   - The proposed weighting function (L₂ with adaptive weighting from pseudo-Huber) is seen as a reformulation of Song & Dhariwal (2023); its novelty is unclear. (R1, R4)
   - The “easiness” claim is overstated: the design choices are not intuitively simpler than existing CT/iCT; the main advantage is computational efficiency. (R2)

2. **Missing ablations and experimental gaps**
   - A full ablation study in the main paper is missing; the appendix suggests diffusion pretraining alone provides most of the improvement, yet this is not discussed in depth. (R1, R4)
   - No ablation of ECT *without* DM initialization (i.e., training from scratch with the same recipe) to isolate the benefit of pretraining. (R2)
   - The scenario “pretrained DM + iCT tuning” (Figure 2) is overlooked despite its relevance to the claimed speed advantage. (R3)
   - Performance vs. training time/iteration curves are not shown; results are only at fixed iteration counts, hindering efficiency comparison and justification of stopping times in Table 1. (R1, R4)
   - Key ablations (Table 6) and the “dropout” technique are relegated to the appendix; they should be in the main paper. (R3)

3. **Rigor and precision issues**
   - Claim that “speedup comes at expense of quality” is too broad (e.g., Karras et al. (2022) reduced evaluations while maintaining performance). (R1, R4)
   - Eq. (12) is described as “generalizing Song et al.’s discrete schedule to continuous time” but is actually the exact CT loss from Song et al. (2023) untied from a specific discretization grid. (R1, R4)
   - Notation \(\Delta t \to \mathrm{d}t\) is confusing (should be \(\Delta t \to 0\)). (R1, R4)
   - The claim of “1/1000 of Score SDE’s inference cost” is technically correct but misleading because state-of-the-art DMs no longer require thousands of evaluations. (R1, R4)
   - Minor errors: Eq. (2) from Karras et al. requires \(\mathbf{f}=0\); the description of \(f(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\) as “a denoising function” needs more detail; the timestep schedule uses \(\rho=7\) not \(0.7\); numbers in Table 1 need to clearly indicate which are from other papers; Eq. (19) \(\epsilon\) should be \(c^2\). (R1, R4)

4. **“Curse of consistency” analysis**
   - The analysis only provides an upper bound on error, which does not capture true error behavior; an increase in the bound does not imply increased error in practice. (R3)
   - The connection between the “curse” and the proposed techniques (continuous-time schedule, weighting function) is not established; none of the techniques directly address it, making its inclusion poorly motivated. (R3) This is echoed by R2, who found the derivation in Section 3.1 disconnected from the method.

5. **Additional requests and questions**
   - Consider higher-dimensional datasets (e.g., ImageNet 512×512, LAION) and latent DMs (e.g., Stable Diffusion) to strengthen generalizability. (R2)
   - Inconsistent metrics in Figure 4: FD-DINOv2 on the left, FID on the right; clarify. (R2)
   - In Figure 2, why is the diffusion pretraining compute for CD and ECT less than that for SDE/DM? Is a different DM used? (R2)
   - Provide more metrics beyond FID. (R1)
   - Clarify whether the proposed model is a distillation model or not, as this is not discussed. (R4)
   - How would consistency distillation (CD) benefit from later improvements like iCT (Song & Dhariwal 2023) if tested under the same conditions? (R4)

6. **Reviewer disagreement**: Assessment varies considerably. R2 (score 8) and R4 (initially 5, later satisfied) were more positive, emphasizing strong results and scaling laws. R1 (score 6) and R3 (score 5) were more critical, particularly regarding novelty, missing ablations, and the link between the “curse” and the method.
