## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a progression of synthetic video datasets for pre-training video models (VideoMAE) without using any natural videos. The synthetic dataset is generated by simple operations on shapes (moving, accelerating, transforming) and progressively includes textures from StyleGAN and static image crops. Pre-training on these synthetic datasets closes 97.2% of the performance gap on UCF101 compared to models pre-trained on natural videos, outperforms on HMDB51, and shows improved robustness on 11 out of 14 UCF101-P datasets. The authors analyze correlations between dataset properties (frame diversity, similarity to natural data) and downstream performance. The paper is well-structured and the generation process is thoroughly described.

### Strengths
- Novel approach: using fully synthetic video data for self-supervised learning of video representations, an underexplored area that differs from prior human-based synthetic data efforts.
- Simple and well-motivated generation process (accelerating, transforming shapes, adding textures and natural crops) that allows controllable study of video properties.
- Achieves comparable performance to natural video pre-training: closes 97.2% of the gap on UCF101, outperforms on HMDB51.
- Strong robustness: outperforms UCF101-pretrained model on 11 out of 14 datasets in the UCF101-P out-of-distribution suite.
- Detailed analysis of synthetic dataset properties (frame similarity, diversity, texture types, natural crops) provides valuable insights for optimizing synthetic data.
- Transparent generation process described clearly, useful for future work in this direction.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset and evaluation scope:** Evaluated only on UCF101 and HMDB51, which are small, appearance-biased, and do not require strong motion awareness. All three reviewers agree that experiments on motion-focused datasets (Something-Something V2, Diving48, FineGym) and large-scale datasets (Kinetics-400, Kinetics variants) are needed to support claims about learning video representations. A minority reviewer (R3) strongly argues that solving UCF/HMDB does not require motion (single-frame classifiers achieve 85%/49%), so the benefits of synthetic motion are not demonstrated.
- **Only one SSL method tested (VideoMAE):** Conclusions cannot be generalized to other video SSL approaches. Reviewers 2 and 3 request experiments with at least one additional recent SSL method (contrastive, e.g., DINO, or predictive) especially given VideoMAE’s known poor linear probing performance.
- **Unfair experimental setup and misleading upper bound:**
  - The synthetic pre-training dataset is 35–140× larger (300K–1.3M videos/frames) than the UCF-101 training set used for the natural-video baseline (≈9K videos). This size discrepancy is not clearly reported in tables, making performance comparisons misleading. (R3 strongly emphasizes this; R2 also notes the imbalance in Table 2.)
  - Using a model pre-trained on UCF-101 itself as an “upper bound” is flawed because SSL methods perform significantly better when pre-trained on larger datasets (e.g., Kinetics-400). Closing 97.2% of the gap to this weak upper bound does not demonstrate competitive video representations. (R3, supported by R2’s call for large-scale pre-training.)
- **Poor linear probing performance:** The synthetic pre-trained model achieves only 24.8% linear probing accuracy on UCF101, far below contrastive methods (e.g., DINO >90%). This contradicts the claim that “useful video representations can be learned from synthetic videos.” (R3, also noted as a concern by R2.)
- **Missing comparisons with prior synthetic video SSL works:** The paper does not discuss or compare with [2] Kim et al. (How transferable are video representations based on synthetic data?) and [3] Zhong et al. (Learning Human Action Recognition Representations Without Real Humans), which are directly relevant. (R1)
- **Lack of temporal and motion analysis:** Section 5 analyzes only spatial properties (static frames, frame similarity). No experiments or analyses on how synthetic motion properties (acceleration, shape transformations) affect learned temporal representations. (R1)
- **Dataset generation choices insufficiently explored:** Questions remain about the impact of number of shapes, shape type (why only circles? squares? triangles?), inclusion of static complex shapes, and whether accelerating shapes were experimented separately. (R2)
- **No explanation for poor Dynamic StyleGAN videos result:** Table 3 reports only 68.7% accuracy for “Dynamic StyleGAN videos” but no analysis or conclusion is provided. (R1)
- **Missing results on robustness datasets:** Results on UCF101-DS (real distribution shift) and out-of-distribution sets HMDB51-P, UCF101-P, Kinetics400-P, SSv2-P are not shown. (R2)
- **No analysis of pre-training dataset size scaling:** The paper does not study how downstream performance varies with the number of synthetic videos or frames. (R2)
- **Computational cost not reported:** The on-the-fly generation strategy may consume more resources than standard pre-training; no experimental data on this are provided. (R1)
- **Unclear derivation of key numbers:** The 97.2% gap-closure figure and the experimental data in Section 4.3 are not clearly derived (missing table/figure for line 292). (R1)
- **Writing issues:** Incorrect citation in Section 3.1 (line 140), incomplete number “92.%” (line 537), and unexplained “28 datasets” (line 424). (R1)
- **Weak motivation:** The paper does not adequately discuss why synthetic pre-training is needed given the abundance of natural video data. (R2)
- **Minor presentation issues in analysis:** Figure 4 could benefit from color-coded variants for clarity. PCA visualizations are unclear (color meaning not explained; consider DINO-style overlays). (R3)
- **Statistical analysis not clearly linked to validity:** Because the accuracy numbers are unreliable (due to unfair setup), it is unclear whether the dataset correlation findings actually help video SSL. (R3)
