## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper extends prior work on in-context learning (ICL) in transformers by studying a compositional data model: the label \(y\) depends on the input \(x\) through a fixed, non-linear representation function \(\phi^*(x)\) (an \(L\)-layer MLP) followed by an instance-specific linear function. The authors theoretically construct transformers that simulate ridge regression on the transformed representations for both supervised learning and linear dynamical system settings, and empirically show that trained transformers on synthetic data achieve near-optimal ICL performance. Through probing experiments and a "pasting" experiment, they provide evidence for a three-step mechanism: lower layers apply the representation mapping, and upper layers perform linear ICL (e.g., gradient descent). The work is seen as a pioneering effort to formalize ICL with representation learning, but the novelty and generalizability of the theoretical constructions and the synthetic-only evaluation are debated across reviewers.

### Strengths

- **Extends ICL to more complex function classes.** The paper moves beyond simple linear ICL (e.g., Garg et al.) by introducing a compositional structure where a fixed non-linear representation is combined with a linear function learned in-context. This is a natural step toward understanding ICL in language models.
- **Theoretical existence proofs.** The paper provides explicit transformer constructions that can implement ridge regression on representations in both supervised and dynamical system settings. Theorem 1 (supervised) and Theorem 2 (dynamical systems) demonstrate feasibility with mild depth and size.
- **Thorough empirical validation on synthetic data.** Trained transformers consistently match the optimal ridge predictor (Figure 1b). Results hold for multiple random seeds and multiple different non-linear representations (Section 4.1.1), strengthening the main claims.
- **Mechanistic analysis is compelling.** Probing experiments (including the "pasting" experiment, Figure 4) provide evidence for the hypothesized three-step mechanism: lower layers transform inputs by the representation, upper layers perform linear ICL. The mechanism is surprisingly consistent across training runs. The analysis includes concrete copying behaviors on inputs and representations, linear ICL capability of upper layers alone, and a post-ICL representation selection mechanism in a mixture setting.
- **Clear and well-written.** All reviewers praise the clarity of the presentation and the explanation of key contributions and techniques.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty of theoretical constructions.** Given that transformers can simulate arbitrary functions via universal approximation, constructing weights to perform ridge regression on an MLP-learned representation is not surprising. The paper does not sufficiently justify why this construction is more significant than simply appealing to universal approximation, or why the specific architecture choices (e.g., gradient descent in upper layers) are necessary. (R1, R2, R4)
- **No guarantee that trained models internalize the theoretical constructions.** The existence proof does not ensure the model learns the same algorithm during training. Probing experiments provide some support but do not offer robust guarantees. An analysis of training dynamics would be needed to theoretically determine when the model aligns with the constructs. (R2)
- **Choice of non-linear representation is restrictive.** The representation is an \(L\)-layer MLP with weights as random orthogonal matrices (Lemma B.3 requires this specific structure and non-linearity). It is unclear whether results hold for other families (e.g., polynomials, exponentials, or arbitrary Lipschitz functions). The paper would benefit from clarifying that the results are specific to this setup and exploring where the mechanism fails. (R1, R3, R4)
- **Lack of analysis on robustness and compositional generalization.** The paper does not explore settings where the mechanism breaks, such as spurious correlations (e.g., non-diagonal covariance in Gaussian priors for \(x\) and \(z\)), distribution shift, or out-of-distribution linear functions (cf. Ruiqi et al. 2023). Does the model still learn the true representation or fall back? No analysis of compositional generalization is provided. (R3, R4)
- **All results are on synthetic data.** The controlled synthetic setup lacks the complexity and variability of real-world data. The paper does not provide evidence or a detailed discussion of how these findings extrapolate to realistic scenarios (e.g., language models). The mapping from the toy model (fixed MLP + linear in-context) to actual language tasks is unclear. (R1, R4)
- **Presentation issues.** Figure 4b labels (e.g., "TF_upper+1_layer_TF_embed") are unreadable. Section 3 could be condensed by presenting Theorem 2 as a generalization of Theorem 1 rather than separate. Section 2 uses \(\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\) and \(\mathbf{w}_{\star}^{(j)}\) before defining them. The paper states the representation function can be chosen arbitrarily, but Lemma B.3 requires a specific structure. (R1, R3)
- **Missing experiments and ablations.** Questions that overlap with missing analyses: (a) What happens if the representation function uses different non-linearities or if the transformer has insufficient layers/width? Does learning approximate the representation, or does the whole mechanism collapse? (R1, R3, R4) (b) How does ICL performance degrade when the number of representation layers \(L\) increases (e.g., \(L=15\) with an 11-layer transformer)? (R4) (c) In the mixture setting, how many different non-linear functions can be learned, and does the model struggle to identify the correct one? (R4) (d) Would the probing conclusions hold for other families of functions or with untrained representations? (R4)
- **Minor concerns.** The "pasting" experiment uses a linear model for the upper module (Figure 4); justification for this choice is requested (R1). The paper should formally introduce the pre-training phase (Eq (10)) earlier in the preliminaries (R1). The meaning of "OLS" in Figure 1b should be defined (R3).
