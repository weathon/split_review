## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a method for constructing confidence intervals (CIs) for average treatment effects (ATE) using two observational datasets: a small unconfounded dataset and a larger confounded dataset from the same covariate population. It builds on prediction-powered inference (PPI) to adjust the ATE estimate from the confounded dataset using the unconfounded dataset, yielding asymptotically valid CIs. Theoretical proofs and numerical experiments (synthetic and two real-world medical datasets) are provided. An extension to combinations of experimental and observational datasets is given.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important, underexplored problem: combining multiple observational datasets with different confounding structures for valid ATE CIs.
- It is well written, technically sound, and places itself clearly within the literature, explicitly stating novelty.
- The method is simple, easy to use, and applicable to a variety of estimators (e.g., AIPW, IPW).
- Theoretical results (asymptotic validity) are provided and support the method.
- One reviewer (score 8) finds the contribution strong, noting that the issue has not been covered before and is backed by precise theorems.
- Experiments show that the proposed method yields smaller CIs with better coverage compared to using the unconfounded dataset alone.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited theoretical guarantees:** Only asymptotic validity is shown; no finite-sample or Berry-Esseen-type bounds are given. There is no theoretical result on the width of the CIs or a proof that the proposed method is strictly better than using only the unconfounded dataset (R1, R3). The asymptotic normality of the confounded estimator $\hat{\tau}_2$ is questioned, as $\mathcal{D}_2$ does not satisfy ignorability—raising doubts about the CLT argument (R2). The proof of Theorem 5.2 relies on standard CLT for independent quantities and does not contain a causal-specific argument beyond that (R3). The proof for $\hat{\Delta}_\tau$ is unclear when the function $\tilde{Y}_{\hat{\eta}}$ is estimated from $\mathcal{D}_1$ and then averaged over the same sample; the argument may only hold for a fixed function (R3).
- **Incremental novelty:** Several reviewers find the novelty mild: the method amounts to applying a previously proposed PPI estimator to causal inference (R2, R3). Lemma 6.1 is a standard property of the IPW estimator, not a contribution (R2). Statements about IPW vs. AIPW in lines 357-360 are inaccurate (R2). Lemma 5.1’s assumptions are incomplete/unclear (concerning $\alpha_\mu$, $\alpha_\pi$, $1/\hat{e}$, and crossfitting), and the asymptotic variance should involve theoretical quantities (R3). Lemma 6.1 has a missing $1/n$ factor and $\hat{\pi}$ should be $\pi$ (R3).
- **Limited empirical evaluation:** The synthetic experiments use only a 1-dimensional covariate, which is unrealistic (R3). Only simple learners (linear/logistic regression) are considered; more advanced ML (e.g., gradient boosting) should be compared (R4). Experiments with more covariates, dependence structures, or when the unconfounded dataset is larger are missing (R3, R4). Coverage and RMSE relative to the true ATE (known for synthetic data) are not reported (R4). On real data, the true ATE is unknown, so RMSE computation is unclear—please define “factual outcome” (R3, R4). The claim “Our PPI-based method is effective for medical data” is too strong given only two medical datasets (R3).
- **Practical relevance concerns:** Assumptions (e.g., all confounders observed in $\mathcal{D}_1$, positivity $0<\pi(x)<1$) may not hold in practice; no guidance is given on how a practitioner can verify them (R4). The distinction between the small dataset (with unconfoundedness but unknown propensity score) and an RCT is insufficiently discussed—methods developed for RCT+observational settings (e.g., van der Laan et al., 2024) might be applicable, and their instability due to matrix inversion is not substantiated (R3). The conclusion lacks a discussion of limitations and future work (R4).
- **Technical clarifications needed:** The definition of “valid” in line 269 is ambiguous (R3). A reference for the statement “PP CI is smaller than classical CI when model $f$ is sufficiently accurate” (l.151-152) is missing (R3). Equation (5) should be compared analytically to the classical CI using only $\mathcal{D}_1$; can it be proven strictly smaller in specific settings? (R3). The term $\tilde{Y}_{\hat{\eta}}$ is built on $\mathcal{D}_1$ and its empirical mean is computed over $\mathcal{D}_1$, making the CLT argument non-trivial (R3). There are numerous typos: missing opening parenthesis (l.124); missing “a for” (l.162); $p$ used for both number of predictors and ratio of dataset sizes (l.177, l.334); “This steps computes analogous the above” (l.363); “We now evaluate our the effectiveness” (l.409); “Hence, our method performs thus best” (l.469) (R3). Citation brackets are missing on lines 48-49; gray text in Table 1 is not applied to all columns (R4).
- **Audience fit:** One reviewer notes the paper is more suited for a statistical journal (e.g., Biometrika) and may not be highly relevant to the ICLR community, as it does not discuss neural networks or representation learning (R2).
