## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a new loss function for approximating Nash equilibria in normal-form games that is amenable to unbiased Monte Carlo estimation via symmetrization. This enables standard non-convex stochastic optimization (SGD, bandit methods) for equilibrium finding, with provable guarantees. Experiments on games up to 286 actions show competitiveness with prior state-of-the-art.

### Strengths
1. **Novel loss function enabling unbiased sampling** – The symmetrization technique allows unbiased gradient estimates without reading all payoffs, a solid technical contribution towards scaling Nash computation to large games despite theoretical hardness.
2. **Loss upper-bounds exploitability** – Both theoretical proofs and empirical results demonstrate that minimizing the loss yields approximate Nash equilibria with bounded exploitability.
3. **Scales to larger games** – Experiments are conducted on games with up to 286 actions, showing feasibility beyond what was previously possible.
4. **Two algorithms explored** – Vanilla SGD and a bandit-based method (BLiN) are analyzed; the bandit approach comes with theoretical guarantees (high-probability exploitability bound).
5. **Clear presentation** – The paper is well-written, with step-by-step intuition and helpful illustrations (Figures 1, 2) that make the problem and solution accessible even to non-experts.
6. **New principled framework** – The loss function opens the door to applying many more stochastic optimization algorithms to equilibrium computation.

### Weaknesses
1. **Loss function only captures fully mixed equilibria** – The loss inherently measures deviations from a fully mixed strategy profile; zero loss corresponds to quantal-response equilibrium, not exact Nash. The reliance on quantal-response introduces approximation that may be unsuitable when the quantal-response parameter is hard to tune or interpret. (Reviewer 2)
2. **Limited empirical evaluation on real-world games** – Most experiments are small synthetic games from prior research. Asymmetric payoff structures, many players, or complex action spaces may reveal limitations not seen here. Established game theory or economics benchmarks are not used. (Reviewer 2)
3. **SGD suffers from saddle points** – Vanilla SGD can get trapped in saddle points in certain games, a common non-convex optimization issue. The paper does not explore advanced methods like momentum or adaptive learning rates that specifically address this. (Reviewer 2)
4. **Empirical section (6.2) lacks detail** – The baselines compared against are not explicitly stated in the body text; they are only referenced as "baselines used in Gemp et al. 2022’s simulations," making it hard to assess significance. Hyperparameter settings for baselines and the proposed method are missing, hindering reproducibility. (Reviewer 1)
5. **Flow of Section 6 is disjointed** – SGD is introduced, then the bandit algorithm, then immediate discussion of bandit performance. The lack of a clear narrative makes it hard to follow the experimental logic and compare relative strengths. A suggested restructuring is to present SGD experiments before bandit discussion. (Reviewer 1)
6. **Minor typo** – In the definition of projected-gradient, it should be \(\Pi_{T\delta^{d-1}}(z) = z - \frac{1}{d} \mathbf{1}^\top z \mathbf{1}\). (Reviewer 1)

**Contrasting minority view**: One reviewer (Reviewer 3) considered that there are no major weaknesses in the work, finding the experiments sufficient and the presentation clear. The weaknesses listed above primarily reflect the assessments of Reviewers 1 and 2.
