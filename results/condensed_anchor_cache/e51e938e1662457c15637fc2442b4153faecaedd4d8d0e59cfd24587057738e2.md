## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses privacy risks in diffusion models against membership inference attacks (MIAs). It first observes that fine-tuning DMs on clean self-synthesized data increases vulnerability (SecMI AUC from 0.715 to 0.752), while fine-tuning on fully perturbed self-synthesized data improves privacy but degrades generation quality (CLIP score drops from 51.9 to 29.9). To balance privacy and quality, the authors propose MixSyn, which fine-tunes DMs on a mixture of clean and perturbed self-synthesized images using two mixing strategies: image-level mixing (Mixup-I) and pixel-level mixing (Mixup-P). The perturbation is generated via PGD on the conditional loss (L_cond), following Anti-Dreambooth. Experiments on CelebA with Stable Diffusion 1.4 show that MixSyn can reduce MIA success while maintaining image quality (Mixup-I: 0.552 SecMI AUC, 51.7 CLIP; Mixup-P: 0.498 SecMI AUC, 51.8 CLIP). The method is compared across fine-tuning approaches (Textual Inversion, LoRA, DreamBooth, DreamBooth+LoRA) and against two MIAs (SecMI, PIA).

### Strengths

- The paper is easy to read, the motivation is clearly articulated, and the proposed method (MixSyn) is straightforward.
- The systematic investigation of privacy risks in diffusion models is valuable: it demonstrates that fine-tuning on clean self-synthetic data increases vulnerability (SecMI AUC 0.715→0.752) and that fully perturbed data improves privacy but degrades quality (CLIP score 51.9→29.9), highlighting the need for a balanced approach.
- Mixup-I and Mixup-P are simple yet effective: Mixup-I achieves 0.552 SecMI AUC while maintaining 51.7 CLIP score; Mixup-P reaches 0.498 SecMI AUC with 51.8 CLIP score.
- The method compensates for the quality drop from perturbation and shows practical effectiveness in defending against MIAs.
- The experiments comprehensively compare multiple fine-tuning methods (Textual Inversion, LoRA, DreamBooth, DreamBooth+LoRA) to validate the hypothesis that self-synthesized fine-tuning increases vulnerability.
- The paper tests the proposed method against two MIAs (SecMI and PIA), and Figure 4 provides valuable insights into the tradeoff between AUC and CLIP score across different mixup ratios.
- The practical relevance of defending against MIA in real-world scenarios is a notable contribution.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty and theoretical depth**:
  - The core perturbation strategy is directly borrowed from Anti-Dreambooth without major improvements. The mixing strategies are straightforward random selection without advanced methods, and no new design ideas or improvements to the fine-tuning process are introduced.
  - There is a lack of theoretical justification for why maximizing L_cond via PGD protects privacy; the connection between noise patterns and privacy protection needs formal analysis, and no definite privacy guarantees are provided.
  - The paper lacks in-depth exploration of why mixing is effective and what differentiates Mixup-I from Mixup-P; mathematical derivation and theoretical analysis are missing.

- **Insufficient experimental scope and comprehensiveness**:
  - Only one perturbation method (PGD) is examined; other techniques such as PhotoGuard, AdvDM, and Mist should be compared to strengthen the findings.
  - Validation is limited to a single dataset (CelebA) and a single diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 1.4), which is insufficient to demonstrate generalizability.
  - The privacy evaluation is limited to MIAs on a specific target set (40 celebrities); this scope should be moved to the main text with clearer justification of the precise privacy improvement.
  - Important ablations are missing, e.g., effects of dataset size, number of training iterations, and the minimum perturbation ratio needed to guarantee privacy protection.
  - The paper does not consider a naive attack using training loss, which is the most intuitive metric for overfitting.
  - Questions remain about the impact of combining Mixup-I and Mixup-P, and the randomness in sampling for Mixup-I and Mixup-P (e.g., which images/regions are perturbed) should be addressed with more extensive experiments.

- **Experimental design and clarity issues**:
  - The task setting is questioned: fine-tuning is part of an attack strategy (e.g., S2L), so adversaries could still exploit vulnerabilities in the pre-trained model even if the fine-tuned model is robust; the practical value of the defense is unclear.
  - Many experimental details are missing: definition of member/hold-out sets, why eidetic memorization samples are used, number of images for customization, meaning of “largest sample sizes,” whether clean and corrupted images share layouts, and definition of CLIP-R.
  - There are abnormal results: in Tables 2 and 3, AUC is below 0.5 while TPR is above 0.1, which is strange; ROC curves and log-scaled ROC curves should be provided for validation. The meaning of AUC for the pre-trained model is also unclear.
  - The positioning of privacy benefits is ambiguous: the mention of artists forming unions suggests an ethical angle that may be inappropriate in this context. The victims of privacy attacks (developer vs. user) are not clearly defined.
  - The assumption that only self-synthetic data can be used is restrictive; it is unclear whether the findings (RQ1) hold for real datasets.
  - Writing clarity issues: Table 1 caption inaccurately mentions “effects of perturbation” when no perturbations are applied; “masks” is used to describe additive noise; DreamBooth is presented as default rather than an example; Figure 4 caption could be more descriptive.
  - Figure/Table placement is poor (e.g., Figure 2 mentioned on page 6 but placed on page 4; Tables 3 and 4 should be closer).
  - Overlooked related works (e.g., “Extracting training data from diffusion models” from USENIX Security 2023).
  - The idea of mixing is intuitive, but treating perturbed and clean samples differently (e.g., different training objectives) might be more optimal; current design seems suboptimal.
  - Minor typos: missing reference for BRISQUE; Equation 1 has an incorrect arrow (x_{t+1} → x_t).
