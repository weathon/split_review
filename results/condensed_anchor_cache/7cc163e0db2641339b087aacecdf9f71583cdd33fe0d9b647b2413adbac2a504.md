- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 6, 3, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a hierarchical, online query-driven cardinality estimation method for workloads with repetitive queries. It represents queries as DAGs, partitions them using template-based hashing at multiple granularity levels, and trains local online models for each partition. At inference, it traverses from fine-grained to coarse-grained models with fallback to a default model. Using the CardBench benchmark, the authors report improvements over PostgreSQL and MSCN. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the hierarchical approach is novel, but raise significant concerns about experimental rigor, comparison with state-of-the-art, evaluation metrics, presentation, and motivation. Reviewer 2 is notably more critical (rating 3), while Reviewers 1 and 3 rate 6 and 5 respectively, acknowledging promise but requesting major improvements.

### Strengths
- S1: The paper addresses a practical and important problem: cardinality estimation for repetitive query workloads common in data warehouses (all reviewers).
- S2: The hierarchical localized model structure is a novel approach that can improve accuracy for repetitive queries by training separate estimators per query template; online learning avoids upfront training costs (all reviewers).
- S3: Evaluation on six types of workloads with up to five-table join queries shows higher Q-error than MSCN and PostgreSQL; the fine-grained H1 model achieves faster convergence and highest accuracy (Reviewer 1, Table 3).
- S4: The fallback mechanism (fine to coarse to default) is beneficial in practice, especially for high-percentile cases where ML methods often underperform; Table 4 shows deeper fallback improves Q-error (Reviewer 1).
- S5: The featurizer’s ability to characterize predicates is useful for the query-driven approach (Reviewer 1).
- S6: The method demonstrates strong cardinality estimation performance and good convergence stability; faster convergence with lower overhead compared to traditional estimators, making it suitable for practical deployment (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- W1 (Metrics): Only Q-error is reported. Reviewers 1 and 3 request additional metrics for comprehensive evaluation: P-error (Reviewer 1), mean absolute error (MAE) and relative prediction error (RPE) (Reviewer 3).
- W2 (Baselines): Comparisons are limited to MSCN (2019) and PostgreSQL. Both Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 request inclusion of state-of-the-art data-driven methods (e.g., [1]) and recent query-driven methods like QueryFormer. Reviewer 2 emphasizes that this insufficient comparison (especially the absence of a data-driven method trained on large data) limits the paper’s impact.
- W3 (Unfair experiment design): In the “Imperfect Admission Experiments” (Section 4), each method is evaluated on different query subsets: PostgreSQL on the entire set, MSCN on a subset excluding disjunctions, while the proposed method appears evaluated on a subset of simple repetitive queries. Reviewer 2 notes this makes comparisons invalid.
- W4 (Missing time reporting): Reviewer 2 requests end-to-end time (planning + execution) with breakdown (feature extraction, model lookup, training, inference) to assess practical overhead.
- W5 (Missing training time / model size): Reviewer 2 notes these are critical for feasibility, especially for online models in dynamic environments and memory footprint.
- W6 (Counter-intuitive behavior): In Figure 4, q-error is higher in certain intervals with larger training sample sizes. Reviewer 2 points out that this lacks explanation, raising concerns about stability and reliability.
- W7 (Workload details): The authors do not provide details on how query repetitiveness was generated, the distribution of templates, or the number of constant variations. Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 3 request experiments with lower query redundancy to assess generalizability.
- W8 (Motivation): Reviewer 2 finds the motivation inadequate: the paper does not explain why existing learned estimators struggle with repetitive workloads, nor why a hierarchical structure is effective. A theoretical or empirical justification is missing.
- W9 (Presentation issues): Multiple problems noted:
    - How the DAG is generated from a query is unclear (which plan? logical/physical? impact of different plans?) (Reviewer 2).
    - Undefined terms (e.g., d_{\psi} in Section 2) (Reviewer 2).
    - Inconsistent notation (e.g., “query graph” and “query plan” used interchangeably) (Reviewer 2).
    - Typos (e.g., “geoping” and “hases” in Section 5) (Reviewer 2).
    - Contradiction: Section 2.5 states template isomorphism uses only edge information, but Section 3.1 claims “query graphs found in the same H1 template differ only by constant values” (Reviewer 1).
    - Hash size calculation: Equation 10’s case distinction seems invalid because hash length is fixed at h per Equation 6 (Reviewer 1).
    - Unclear how the plan tree is constructed; e.g., are (A⋈B)⋈C and A⋈(B⋈C) considered non-isomorphic? (Reviewer 1).
    - Reviewer 3 asks for clarification of the specific models used at each hierarchy level.
- W10 (Misstatements about existing work):
    - The paper states “50% of real world clusters have more than 90% queries repeated in templates,” but the cited work [4] says 80%. Reviewer 2 notes this misrepresentation undermines credibility.
    - The claim that “NN-based estimators perform well only with large amounts of query samples” is too broad and mischaracterizes data-driven NN estimators (Reviewer 2).
- W11 (Benchmark choice): Reviewer 1 questions the use of CardBench, noting it is an arXiv paper and not yet a standard benchmark; suggests using the widely accepted join order benchmark or providing strong justification.
