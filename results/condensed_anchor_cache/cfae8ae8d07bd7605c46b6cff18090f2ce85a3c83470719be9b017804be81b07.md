## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces the Canonical Representation Hypothesis (CRH), which posits six alignment relations among representations, weights, and gradients that universally govern representation formation in neural networks. When CRH is broken, reciprocal power-law relations (Polynomial Alignment Hypothesis, PAH) emerge. The theory shows that a balance between gradient noise and regularization drives the system to stationarity, producing canonical representations. The framework unifies phenomena such as neural collapse and the neural feature ansatz. While the framework is general and well-supported by theoretical derivations and experiments, several weaknesses regarding assumptions, clarity, motivation, and scope are noted across reviewers.

### Strengths
- CRH provides a new, unifying framework to interpret basic statistical aspects of representation formation, generalizing and organizing previous alignment phenomena (e.g., neural collapse as a specific case).
- The framework is very general: it avoids reference to specific network designs, loss functions, or activation functions, increasing its potential impact.
- The paper is well written, clearly structured, and carefully presented; figures are informative and support the content.
- Theoretical derivations are detailed and add credibility; the paper includes a significant number of theoretical results that distinguish it from most ML work.
- Experimental results are rigorous, extensive, and strongly support the theory, holding across different network types (ResNet, LLMs).
- The insights section and clear statement of limitations are valued.
- The paper emphasizes backward representation—a relatively unexplored area—and identifies two specific mechanisms that disrupt alignment, quantified via the PAH.
- The authors are thorough in examining all possible combinations of forward and backward alignment relations.
- The work has potential interdisciplinary applications (e.g., neurosciences) and offers predictions relevant to widely used models.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and significance**: The motivation for why alignment of weights, gradients, and representations matters for understanding why deep neural networks work is insufficient (R4). The paper fails to clearly show how CRH contributes to the bigger picture; the connection to "interpretable" solutions is unclear (R4, line 49). The term "hypothesis" may be misleading—it functions more as a measurement framework than a testable hypothesis (R3).
- **Assumptions**: Critical assumptions for the theory, such as the self-averaging conditions (Hypothesis A.1, A.2), may not hold in general; the paper would benefit from an empirical assessment of how severe the departure from self-averaging is in a concrete relevant case (R1). In practice, stationarity is often not reached—this needs comment (R4, line 149).
- **Clarity and organization**: Section B (Theory and Proofs) in the supplement is difficult to follow; e.g., Lemma 1 uses notation (A tilde in eq. 21) that is not introduced first—adding explanations would improve readability (R1). Early references to later sections (e.g., Figure 9 before context) and frequent shifts in reference disrupt flow (R3). More detailed explanations for "neural collapse" and "alignment" are needed (R3). Figure 4 lacks a color bar for the weight decay parameter, complicating interpretation (R3). Several minor writing issues: line 23 typo "emergence + of"; line 44 sentence is incomprehensible without context; line 68 "weight matrices evolve during training" seems tautological—what is the point?; line 117 missing verb; line 159-160 unclear (R4).
- **Scope and generalization**: The CRH is better supported for later layers than earlier layers (R2). The current empirical tests focus on specific types of networks and tasks; further tests across more diverse datasets and architectures are needed to confirm practical universality (R1).
- **Missing comparisons and contradictions**: The paper should discuss or address potential contradictions between neural collapse/invariance to irrelevant features and works such as Zimmermann et al. (2021) and arxiv/2410.21869 (R4, line 217). A relevant related work is missing: https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00810 (R4).
- **Interpretation of phases**: The implications of different alignment phases (e.g., positive vs. negative exponents between Za and Ha) need greater elaboration—how do these phases correspond to observable behavioral or performance changes? What other factors beyond the two identified could disrupt alignment? (R3). Additionally, the three branches in Figure 7 are unexplained—why do they appear? (R2, question).
- **Questions on initialization and learning regimes**: The paper does not address how different initialization strategies might impact alignment phenomena (R3). The relation to rich/lazy learning literature is unexplored; could the phases further define distinct learning regimes? (R3).
- **Minor contradictions**: The paper claims a result is "surprising" (line 232) when it is exactly what the theory predicts—this requires clarification (R4).
