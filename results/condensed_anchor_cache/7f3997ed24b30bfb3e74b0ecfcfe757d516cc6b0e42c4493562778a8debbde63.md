## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Glimpse-based Active Perception (GAP), a method for visual reasoning inspired by human active vision. GAP sequentially extracts salient image regions using a saliency map, processes them at high resolution via multi-scale or log-polar sensors, and feeds both visual content and location information into a downstream Transformer or Abstractor. The approach achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance on several visual reasoning benchmarks (SVRT, ART/CLEVR-ART) and out-of-distribution variants, with improved sample efficiency and OOD generalization. The architecture is simple, well-motivated, and relates to human perception. However, the evaluation is limited to synthetic datasets with clean backgrounds; comparisons to modern vision-language models, broader OOD / cross-task generalization, and computational analysis are missing.

### Strengths

- The paper provides interesting insights into visual reasoning problems and offers a well-motivated method grounded in human active vision theories (e.g., error neurons linked to surround modulation, fixation-based relational reasoning). The logical connection from motivation to design is clear.
- The proposed GAP model is relatively novel and inspiring for the visual reasoning community. It contributes a new approach that makes explicit use of glimpse locations alongside appearance to represent relations.
- The approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on several visual reasoning tasks, especially compared to prior models that do not use pretraining (GAP-Abstractor outperforms them by large margins). It also shows better sample efficiency and OOD generalization.
- The paper evaluates on four datasets spanning binary figures to real-world images (including OOD settings), and considers diverse visual sensors (multi-scale, log-polar) and downstream architectures (Transformer, Abstractor).
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with both textual and visual explanations.
- The approach is simple, and the exploration of architectures that encode positions and not just appearance is interesting due to potential capabilities and relation to human vision.

*Note: The two reviewers who gave scores of 8 are more positive about novelty and significance, while the three reviewers with scores of 6 acknowledge contributions but raise stronger concerns about limited novelty and evaluation scope.*

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and related work:** Although the approach is somewhat novel, it is similar to prior work such as AdaGlimpse (ECCV 2024), which also focuses on what and where to look. There is extensive prior work on foveated vision and local feature extraction (e.g., Larochelle et al. 2010, Ba et al. 2014, Xu et al. 2015), and the paper does not compare to existing foveated vision methods on these tasks. The paper also omits discussion of “Look, Remember and Reason” (ICLR 2024), which uses surrogate tasks to decide where to look.
- **Missing comparisons to vision-language models (VLMs):** Current SOTA VLMs like LLaVA (NeurIPS 2024) can actively attend to images within the context window. The paper should include comparisons to such models on the evaluated benchmarks, or at least a discussion of how explicit spatial reasoning (GAP) compares to the implicit reasoning of pretrained VLMs. This would also help assess cross-task generalization.
- **Limited evaluation on real-world/complex datasets:** Three of the four evaluation datasets (SVRT, ART/CLEVR-ART) are synthetic with simple, uncluttered backgrounds and highly local structure. The paper would benefit from evaluation on more realistic datasets such as COCO, GQA, or Super-CLEVR. The solutions to the chosen tasks strongly rely on object positions, which favors methods that make positions explicit.
- **Missing cross-task generalization analysis:** Generalization is tested only for OOD variants within the same task family (e.g., SVRT #1-OOD). The paper does not address how learned glimpse patterns and relational reasoning transfer to tasks with different visual primitives or requiring temporal dynamics or physical understanding. This limits the assessment of true reasoning ability.
- **Incomplete experimental details:**
    - It is not explicitly stated in the main paper which sensor (multi-scale vs. log-polar) is used for each dataset-architecture combination; this is only in the appendix. Also, there is no explanation of why a particular sensor performs better or worse for a given setting.
    - The dataset size for results in Table 1 is restricted to 500/1000 training examples with no justification. Figure 4 suggests training can be larger.
    - In Table 3 (on a subset of ART), not all models from previous experiments (e.g., Attn-ResNet) are included. The reason is unclear.
    - References are missing from tables and figures (reviewer 2).
- **Limited analysis of the saliency mechanism:**
    - The paper does not explore alternative methods for computing the saliency map, even though the method’s effectiveness depends on the map’s accuracy.
    - The impact of using a hard vs. soft mask M(x_t) is not investigated.
    - Why GAP-Abstractor improves OOD generalization for same-different and more abstract relations is not explained.
    - The authors note that glimpses can be distracted by spurious salient locations (e.g., edges of shades, high reflection). Could performing GAP on feature maps (rather than images) mitigate this? No results are provided.
- **Lack of computational performance comparison:** The GAP mechanism involves multiple steps (saliency map generation, inhibition of return). No comparison of computational cost with baseline models is given.
- **Presentation clarity issues:** The tasks RMTS and ID are not explained before Section 5.4 results. The term “active” is questioned, as the architecture relies on a fixed (non-adaptive) glimpse extraction scheme rather than recurrent or adaptive selection.
- **Limited exploration of task scope:** The SVRT #1-OOD experiment is performed only for subset #1; it should be extended to other subsets to verify generality. Also, analysis of errors compared to LLM-based reasoning would be beneficial.
