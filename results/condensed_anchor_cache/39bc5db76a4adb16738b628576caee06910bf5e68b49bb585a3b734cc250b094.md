## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper reassesses the Knowledge Neuron (KN) thesis in pre-trained LLMs, which claims that factual knowledge can be localized to a small number of MLP neurons and that editing those neurons controls factual generation. The authors extend the analysis to syntactic knowledge using minimal pairs from BLiMP, and find analogous “syntax neurons.” Through previously and newly proposed metrics (bi-directionality, synonym‑agnosticism) they argue that these identified neurons do not robustly represent knowledge but rather token correlations from training data. The paper is clearly written with intuitive examples, comprehensive experiments, and novel methodology (e.g., applying editing to syntax, t‑tests). Reviewers agree the work brings clarity to interpretability and highlights limitations of current KN‑based editing. One reviewer, however, had concerns about the significance for the ML community and the scope of the contribution.

### Strengths
- Clear writing with intuitive examples and graphical illustrations. (R1, R3)
- Comprehensive experiments convincingly demonstrate the existence of syntax‑knowledge neurons and that neither factual nor syntax neurons robustly represent “knowledge.” (R1, R2, R3)
- Broadens the definition of knowledge neurons and connects to prior linguistic phenomena, a novel application of editing techniques to syntax. (R2, R3)
- Introduces rigorous new evaluation practices: minimal pairs, t‑tests, bi‑directionality, and synonym‑agnosticism. (R2)
- Systematic study of whether editing effects are local or systematic, unlike much prior work. (R3)
- Brings clarity to the interpretability of transformers. (R3)
- Thorough and diverse analysis. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Significance and scope (minority more negative view):** One reviewer (R1, score 6, confidence 3) found the paper technically sound but struggled with its significance for the machine learning community, suggesting borderline rejection for ICLR (though strong acceptance for ACL). The other reviewers (R2, R3, both score 8) rated the contribution as excellent. (R1)
- **Neuron selection procedure limits to 2–5 neurons a priori**, excluding the possibility of distributed representation. The reviewer suggests investigating distributed representation would increase impact and reach. This echoes the “grandmother” neuron critique from neuroscience. (R1)
- **Inconsistent messaging:** The paper states (p.7) that LMs process factual and syntactic knowledge using the same mechanism, while the high‑level conclusion argues there are no “knowledge neurons.” A more consistent narrative is needed. (R1)
- **Lack of explicit contributions section:** The paper relies heavily on a small number of prior works, making it hard to assess novelty. The assessment of syntax and new metrics is clearly new, but an explicit summary of contributions would help. (R1)
- **Illustrative examples (Figs 2–6) focus mostly on determiner‑noun agreement;** showing successful examples for subject‑verb and gender/number agreement would be more convincing. (R1)
- **Definitions of formal and functional competence** appear too late; moving them earlier (after Figure 1) would improve readability. (R1)
- **Disjoint section 4:** It feels not fully fledged and somewhat separate from the rest of the paper. (R2)
- **Presentation of experiments could be cleaned up:** The first paragraph of Section 3.2 is too loaded (attribution scores, regularity, causal interventions, frequency cues). Breaking it into separate paragraphs would improve clarity. (R3)
- **Results taken from prior work not always clearly marked:** Table 1 and Figure 5(c) appear to be from prior work; this should be explicitly stated. (R3)
- **Missing references and discussion:** The paper could cite prior work on decision‑making circuits (Clark et al. 2019, Murty et al. 2023, Wu et al. 2023). A more extended discussion on future directions for interpretability research (e.g., studying attention and layer structures holistically) would be valuable. (R3)
