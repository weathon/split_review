## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies multi-task reinforcement learning and few-shot learning by proposing a framework that learns a set of policies (a “policy committee”) such that at least one is near-optimal for most tasks. It provides theoretical results for low-dimensional settings and a gradient-based practical approach, along with sample complexity bounds for few-shot learning. Experiments on MuJoCo and Meta-World benchmarks compare against multi-task, meta-RL, and personalized RL baselines.

### Strengths
- The idea of learning a committee of policies rather than a single policy for multiple tasks is novel and has potential to improve adaptability in real-world multi-task RL (R1, R2).
- The investigation is thorough, including both theoretical analysis (covering low/high-dimensional and parametric/nonparametric scenarios) and empirical results on popular benchmarks (R1, R3).
- The Related Work section is well-articulated and covers a broad spectrum of relevant literature (R2, R4).
- The method targets the important trade-off between optimality and generalization when tasks are diverse, and the approach is justified and motivated by theoretical results (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical justification for when a policy committee helps over a single policy.** The only mention is a brief paragraph (lines 153–159). There is no analysis of task distribution properties that make a committee beneficial (e.g., outlier tasks) or why the method outperforms on MuJoCo where no outliers exist (R2). The paper does not clearly articulate how the proposed method achieves a better balance compared to context-based meta-RL methods that already learn task-specific patterns (R4).
- **Assumption of access to task parameters and mapping to MDPs.** The clustering requires parametric task representations, and the theory assumes a mapping from parameters to MDPs—both strong assumptions that are not discussed sufficiently. The “naive” Greedy Elimination Algorithm does not need the mapping, but this advantage is not highlighted (R2). It is unclear whether the method can work without any parametric information (R4). The baselines (meta-RL) do not use task parameters, making the comparison potentially unfair; a justification is needed (R4).
- **Mismatch between theory and practice.** In the theory, each policy in the committee is assumed optimal for a specific task, but in the experiments each policy is trained on *all* tasks in its cluster. This modification is significant—the Lipschitz assumption in Lemma 1 may not hold for tasks like pick-and-place—yet no theoretical guarantees are provided for the empirical setting (R2).
- **Presentation and clarity issues.** Section 3, especially 3.2 (“Clustering”), lacks an explicit description of how clustering is done (R1). The Greedy Intersection Algorithm and the latter part of Section 3.3 are unclear (R2). Definitions and lemmas would benefit from preceding motivation (R2). Notation for meta-RL and multi-task RL (Γ and T) could be unified (R2). Typos: line 155 “to to”, line 143 “denote an optimal policy”, line 215 “task π_i” (should be τ_i), line 516 “Figure 5.3(a)” (should be “Figure 2(a)”) (R2).
- **Insufficient empirical analysis and ablations.**
    - The only ablation of the clustering algorithm is Figure 2(a), which shows a marginal improvement over KMeans. Comparisons with other clustering methods (GMM, DBSCAN) and ablations over distance metric and number of clusters are missing (R2).
    - Recent meta-RL baselines are omitted (e.g., [1], [2], [3] cited in review) (R2). The results in Figure 1’s first two plots appear inconsistent with the claims (R2).
    - The comparison to VariBAD/MOORE is not fair in terms of parameter count; a baseline with matched total parameters or a random split of tasks across the committee would strengthen claims (R2).
    - Missing details: number of seeds (R2), hyperparameter tuning for in-cluster policies (R2), training steps (why only 1.2e7 for MuJoCo when VariBAD trained longer) (R2). Few-shot adaptation sample numbers across baselines should be reported (R4).
- **Lack of discussion on hyperparameter choice.** Does a larger committee size K always improve performance? How to choose K in practice? (R1, R2).
- **No connection to reward-free RL.** For the case of identical transitions and different rewards, the paper does not compare with reward-free RL methods (e.g., [1], [2], [3]), especially regarding assumptions on action space, linearity, sample complexity, and dependence on number of policies (R3).
- **Restriction in Gradient-Based Coverage approach.** The cluster representation is restricted to tasks in set T, unlike the Greedy Intersection algorithm. Theorem 5 holds only under this restriction, which may cause a larger error gap with respect to unseen tasks. No explanation or discussion of implications is provided (R3).
- **Zero-shot generalization and policy selection not explained.** For a new task, how is the appropriate policy identified from the committee? (R4). The few-shot learning results with K=1 are very different from VariBAD/MOORE, but the paper does not clarify whether PACMAN with K=1 is truly identical to those baselines (R2).
- **Minor reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 4 is more negative (score 5) compared to others (score 6) and emphasizes the unfair comparison and unclear advantage over context-based methods, while Reviewer 2 highlights the theory-practice gap and insufficient empirical rigor. Reviewer 1 and 3 are more positive overall but note specific presentation and connection gaps.
