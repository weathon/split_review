## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses conformal prediction (CP) under general distribution shift (covariate and concept). It derives a Wasserstein distance-based upper bound on the coverage gap and decomposes the bound into two terms that decouple the effects of covariate shift and concept shift. Algorithms (WR-CP) are proposed to minimize the bound via importance weighting and regularized representation learning, with finite-sample concentration bounds. Experiments on six datasets claim coverage gap reduction to 3.1% and 38% smaller prediction sets compared to the worst-case approach.

### Strengths
- The problem is important and timely: CP under general distribution shift (both covariate and concept) is less researched but common in practice (R1, R2, R3).
- The Wasserstein-based bound and its decomposition into covariate/concept shift components are novel and provide a clearer structure for understanding the coverage gap (R1, R2). R2 notes the bound indicates changes across different miscoverage levels α. R3 finds the bounds and algorithm increase CP applicability.
- The optimization via a Wasserstein regularizer is practical: the penalty term does not involve non-differentiable sample quantiles of conformity scores (R1, R3). R1 highlights the importance weighting and representation learning approach as practical for real-world applications.
- The paper provides theoretical guarantees and concentration bounds (R1). R2 agrees the theory extensively validates the results.
- The method shows empirical improvements: coverage gap reduction to 3.1% and 38% smaller prediction sets than worst-case on average across six datasets (R1). R2 and R3 note this claim but question experimental fairness (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
- **Hard-to-interpret concept-shift term η**: R1 finds the term capturing concept shift difficult to interpret and asks for geometric intuition. R3 questions the necessity of separating covariate and concept shift, arguing it requires strong assumptions and the advantages for optimizing prediction intervals are unclear.
- **Reliance on density estimation**: The covariate shift component of the optimization uses importance weighting, which requires good density estimation (difficult in practice). R2 questions practicality and computational overhead; R3 asks about KDE instabilities in Eq. 19 (tractability).
- **No formal guarantees on final prediction sets**: After training with WR-CP, there are no formal coverage or robustness guarantees on the resulting prediction sets. R2 considers this a major limitation given the problem framing. R2 suggests applying a fixed CP calibration procedure on top of the changed model to regain guarantees.
- **Experimental comparison is unfair and insufficient**: R2 argues the comparison with worst-case CP (WC-CP) is unfair because WC-CP has guarantees while WR-CP changes model parameters. A fair comparison would apply a fixed calibration procedure to both. The claimed 38% reduction in set size is not adequately supported. R3 adds that a baseline trained on all available (original+shifted) samples is missing. The experiments do not convincingly assess performance against SOTA or prior works.
- **Unclear contribution placement in CP pipeline**: R2 notes the paper does not clearly distinguish between training-phase conformal-based optimization (changing model parameters) and calibration-phase procedures (black-box model with guarantees). Related works on conformal training (e.g., Stutz et al. 2022, Yan et al. 2024) and multi-source/federated CP are missing.
- **Assumption of test samples during training**: R3 points out the optimization assumes samples from the shifted distribution are available at training time, which is not standard in distribution-shift problems. It is not explained why one cannot simply train on the combined calibration and test data.
- **Exchangeability concerns in Section 5**: In the multi-source mixture setting, R3 queries whether calibration and test samples are truly non-exchangeable. If the test distribution is a mixture of training components, it is unclear why sampling from the test set differs from sampling from the training set.
- **Uniform averaging may suboptimal**: When test distribution is an unknown mixture of training components, the Wasserstein regularizer averages uniformly over all components, potentially leading to suboptimal regularization for components with small weight in the mixture (R1, R2).
- **Missing clarification on WD vs. TVD**: R3 requests a clearer explanation of why the Wasserstein distance (infimum over joint distributions) is superior to the total variation distance (supremum over sets) for indicating coverage gap changes across α.
- **Proof generality**: R3 asks whether the proofs of Theorems 1 and 2 depend on the specific conformity measure.
- **Minor issues**: Figure 1 is missing a (c) panel (R1, R2). Line 153 has a typo: “adaption” should be “adaptation” (R2).
