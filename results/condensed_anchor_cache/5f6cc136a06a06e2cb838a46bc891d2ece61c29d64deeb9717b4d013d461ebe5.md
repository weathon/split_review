## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes IOMT, a fine-grain optimizer switch method that uses a Gaussian process surrogate model, a transferability assessment, and an acquisition function to automatically switch optimizers and hyperparameters during DNN training. Experiments on several datasets and models (CIFAR10, STL10) show improved convergence (e.g., 10% on STL10) and accuracy (e.g., 3% on CIFAR10) compared to static optimizers and hybrid methods. Reviewers generally find the topic compelling and the approach novel, but raise significant concerns about insufficient theoretical justification, unclear methodological details, limited experimental scope, and lack of ablation/sensitivity analyses.

### Strengths

- The motivation for switching optimizers during training is good and the problem of optimizer selection is clear.
- The switching strategy based on a Gaussian process surrogate is novel, with a modified acquisition function introduced.
- The method is empirical validated on several datasets and DNN models, showing effectiveness.
- The paper is easy to read and coherently guides the reader.
- The comparative analysis against static optimizers and hybrid methods appears thorough and fair.
- The examination of transferability is a valuable addition, hinting at broader applicability.

### Weaknesses

- **Theoretical / mechanistic understanding.** The paper does not sufficiently explain why optimizer switching leads to better generalization (R1: faster convergence does not imply improved generalization; need analysis on avoiding overfitting or finding better local minima). The claim that different optimizers make divergent choices at saddle points is central but not rigorously validated on large-scale tasks; the approach lacks theoretical analysis and intuitive explanations for its success (R2, R3, R4). Overfitting concerns are not addressed—no mechanism prevents overfitting if training runs longer (R3).

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity and ablation gaps.** The method is sensitive to hyperparameters (initial step size, switch step size τ, number of PCA components, n_ini), but no sensitivity analysis or ablation study is provided (R1, R4). Specifically: the initial weighted-random selection phase is crucial for surrogate quality, yet not studied; variance halving is presented as core but not ablated; the choice of hyperparameters n_ini and τ is not justified (R4). The search space is small (5 optimizers × 3 learning rates = 15 choices), raising scalability concerns (R3). It is unclear how effective the method would be with more hyperparameters or when training time is allowed to vary (R3).

- **Methodological clarity.** Several critical details are missing or ambiguous:
  - **PCA calculation:** The paper does not explain how a sufficient set of θ parameter vectors is collected for eigen-decomposition. When parameters have dimensionality D, at least D′ (number of PCA components) data points are needed, and it remains unclear how these are obtained (R1, after rebuttal still unsatisfied).
  - **Optimizer internal states:** How are momentum / adaptive moments inherited or reset when switching optimizers? (R1)
  - **Definitions missing:** w_p and w_d are not defined in the main text or appendix (R3).
  - **Transferability assessment:** Its definition and use to predict training cost are not discussed (R2).
  - **Joint optimization ambiguity:** Lines 14-19 are ambiguous about whether optimizers and hyperparameters are jointly optimized or only optimizers (R4).
  - **Figure 1 and Figure 7:** Figure 1 shows only a single function evaluation and does not effectively illustrate how optimizers differ at saddle points (R2, R4); Figure 7 does not clarify whether different final parameter states yield comparable accuracy (R4).
  - **Performance prediction:** It is unclear what metric (accuracy or something else) the surrogate model predicts (R2).

- **Experimental scope and baselines.**
  - Datasets and tasks are limited: need larger datasets (ImageNet, COCO2017, VOC), more tasks (object detection, VQA, NLP), and larger models (stable diffusion, LLMs) (R2). The observed 3% improvement on CIFAR-10 may reflect plateaued progress; adversarial benchmarks like ImageNet-A would better demonstrate the adaptive advantage (R4).
  - Comparisons lack a simple HPO baseline: tuning the learning rate with cosine annealing is a common and easy strategy; beating it would justify the need for optimizer selection (R4).
  - More competitors should be compared, and training cost experiments are needed (R2).
  - Ablation studies are missing on variance halving, surrogate model fit (e.g., tracking fit quality during training), and switching profiles under different surrogate strategies (e.g., pure exploitation) (R4).
  - No visualization of the learning trajectory is provided (R2).

- **Novelty and related work.** While some reviewers find the approach novel (R1, R3), others note strong connections to AutoML (R2) and to Dynamic Algorithm Configuration (Adriansen et al. JAIR’22) and learned optimization (R4). These relationships are not discussed, and the paper does not clarify how it differs from prior work in SMBO or dynamic configuration.

- **Presentation issues.** Typos/grammatical errors (e.g., “Iterleaving” should be “Interleaving”) (R1, R2). Reference format is inconsistent (R2). Figure 1 and Figure 7 clarity issues (R2, R4). Explanation in the introduction (lines 40-48) could more explicitly link optimizer diversity to saddle-point decisions (R4).
