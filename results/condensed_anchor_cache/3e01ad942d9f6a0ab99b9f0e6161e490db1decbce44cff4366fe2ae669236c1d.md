## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper attempts to compare and unify different measurements of “robustness” for deep neural networks. The authors define robustness via four definitions: Generalization Gap, Clean Test Accuracy, Corrupted Test Accuracy, and Attack Success Rate. They conduct extensive experiments on the Imagenette dataset using a variety of previously proposed metrics (complexity, sharpness, margin, etc.) and evaluate their correlations with these robustness definitions. The main finding is inconsistency: no single measure correlates consistently across all definitions, leading the authors to recommend using different measures for different robustness aspects. They also investigate sharpness-aware minimization and conclude that the relationship between sharpness and robustness is heavily influenced by batch size and other hyperparameters.

### Strengths

- The tackled problem is important: deploying DNNs in practice requires robustness, and a proper measure for estimating robustness is currently lacking.
- The paper is clear and well-written; figures are clear and typeset well.
- The scope of experiments is large and comprehensive: the authors compare many candidate measures—complexity, sharpness, margin, etc.—across multiple architectures and hyperparameters, covering a heavy practical lift. This goes beyond prior work that typically studies only one or two measures.
- The findings contribute valuable insights to the community and may lead to new, implicit methods for improving robustness.

### Weaknesses

**Ambiguity in definitions of robustness.**  
- The paper equates robustness with generalization (Section 3.1, lines 112-115), which is rare and not standard; robustness has multiple definitions (adversarial, natural corruptions) that are distinct from generalization (refer to Jiang et al. 2019, Goodfellow et al. 2014).  
- Clean test accuracy and generalization gap are included as “robustness” definitions without clear justification. There is a known trade-off between clean accuracy and adversarial robustness (Tsipras et al. ICLR 2019, Zhang et al. ICML 2019), yet the paper claims higher test accuracy implies greater robustness.  
- The connection between generalization gap and robustness is insufficiently explained: the statement “A larger Generalization Gap … indicates poor robustness” (Ln113) needs justification and references.  
- The paper’s conclusion that no single measure is representative across all definitions is weakened because these are distinct concepts that cannot naturally be unified; an increase in generalization gap does not necessarily correspond to a decrease in clean accuracy, especially when training accuracy declines.

**Incorrect or incomplete definitions.**  
- Attack Success Rate (ASR) is defined in a way that would always yield 100%; a formal correction is needed. Typical ASR is percentage of instances where adversarial perturbation alters the prediction.  
- In Corruption Test Accuracy (Ln123) the target remains \(t_i\), while in Attack Success Rate (Ln129) the target shifts to \(t_i^{adv}\); this inconsistency requires clarification.

**Unclear distinction between “definitions” and “measures”.**  
- All terms in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 appear to be measurements that can relate to robustness. The paper should clarify the conceptual difference: measures in 3.1 (corruption test accuracy, attack success rate) more directly reflect robustness, while those in 3.2 are indirectly connected.

**Evaluation scope and generalizability.**  
- All conclusions are drawn from a single small dataset (Imagenette), using only ResNet-18 and ResNet-34 architectures. This raises questions about generalizability to larger datasets (e.g., full ImageNet) and other architectures (e.g., ViT).  
- Adversarial robustness is evaluated only with FGSM and PGD, which may overestimate robustness as gradient-based methods can fail (Mosbach et al. 2018). The authors should include AutoAttack (Croce et al. ICML 2020).  
- The paper does not consider other OOD generalization algorithms beyond sharpness-aware minimization (e.g., invariant risk minimization) or other OOD benchmark datasets (e.g., CMNIST, PACS, Waterbird).

**Novelty and significance.**  
- Many of the relationships between different robustness metrics have been studied individually in prior work; the main contribution is the scope of experiments, but the result that no metric fits all definitions is unsurprising, especially since adversarial robustness trade-offs are well known.  
- The significance of the contributions is unclear: what are the main takeaways for researchers or practitioners? The paper recommends using different measures for different definitions, but if one cares specifically about corruption accuracy or ASR, why not just use those directly?  
- The paper lacks theoretical analysis explaining why sharpness-aware optimization or complexity measures relate to robustness, leaving the contribution feeling more like a summary than new insight.

**Presentation and figure interpretation.**  
- Correlation plots are difficult to interpret because some metrics indicate better robustness with smaller values while others with larger values. It would help to plot correlations with \(-1 \times\) metric for those where smaller is better, and to apply the same to generalization gap (smaller gap = better robustness).  
- Some metrics appear redundant (e.g., log sum of Frobenius norms, log sum over margin, sum of Frobenius norms, all on pg. 21). If they are not redundant, the authors should explain why all are needed. The large number of metrics makes the aggregated results hard to follow.

**Contradictory trends not necessarily “inconsistent”.**  
- Some measures show positive correlation with both test accuracy and generalization gap. This is not necessarily inconsistent, because a higher train accuracy can explain both phenomena simultaneously. The paper should address this directly.

**Threshold for correlation.**  
- The paper uses \(|r| > 0.2\) as informative without justification; why that threshold?

**Missing sections.**  
- The title mentions “Improving” robustness, but the paper does not clearly address improvement beyond observing that sharpness matters. The “improving” aspect is underdeveloped.
