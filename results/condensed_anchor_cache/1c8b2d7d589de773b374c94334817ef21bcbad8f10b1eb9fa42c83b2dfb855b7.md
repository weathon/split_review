## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the rank of attention score matrices in Transformers, empirically and theoretically identifying two phenomena: a low-rank barrier (an approximate upper bound of ~0.63n on the rank, where n is sequence length) and a model-reduction effect (diminishing returns on rank as head dimension d_h increases, with saturation when d_h = Ω(log n)). Experiments use synthetic data and small vision datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN). All reviewers agree the topic is interesting and the results on random/initialized settings are clearly presented. However, there is strong disagreement on the generality and practical relevance of the findings: two reviewers (2,3) find the assumptions too restrictive and the connection to real transformer performance unconvincing, while two others (1,4) see the work as a useful theoretical contribution but note limitations in scope and generalization.

### Strengths
- The paper studies a fundamental problem in Transformer efficiency (Reviewer 1, 3, 4) and offers a new theoretical perspective on the limitations of attention ranks, which is insightful for understanding model capacity (Reviewer 3).
- The two identified phenomena—low-rank barrier (upper bound ~0.63n) and model-reduction effect (saturation at d_h = Ω(log n))—are interesting and shed new light on the expressive power of attention matrices (Reviewer 3, 4).
- The paper provides both empirical validation (on random data and small vision datasets) and rigorous theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, bound ~0.63n) (Reviewer 1, 4). The technical parts are clearly written and easy to follow (Reviewer 4).
- The findings are valuable for designing more efficient Transformer-based architectures (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited generalizability and scope of experiments** (overlapping concerns from all reviewers):
   - Experiments are restricted to small vision datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN) with fixed input sizes. It is unclear whether the findings transfer to NLP or audio domains, where sequence lengths vary and positional encodings/masking are common (Reviewer 1). Larger-scale experiments, especially on text datasets where embedding dimension can be varied, are needed (Reviewer 4).
   - Only the first transformer block is analyzed in some experiments (Reviewer 2). The effect of deeper layers and multiple heads is not systematically explored; Section 3.1 is unclear about how ranks are aggregated across heads (Reviewer 2, Question).
   - The paper does not explore different attention mechanisms (e.g., sparse attention) or variations in multi-head formulation (e.g., key/query dimension differing from value dimension, or h=1 possibly recovering full rank) (Reviewer 1, 2). Figure 4b shows a variant exceeding the 0.63 bound (up to 0.68 with d_h<5), but this is not discussed (Reviewer 2, W1.1).
   - The paper lacks a discussion on how the observed low-rank barrier might interact with the positional encodings and masking strategies commonly employed in NLP Transformers (Reviewer 1).

2. **Overly strong assumptions in theoretical analysis** (Reviewer 2, 3, 4):
   - The theoretical result (Theorem 1) assumes exactly orthonormal input sequences, which is a very strong assumption. The paper only justifies this via near-orthogonality for large d (citing Tian et al. 2024), but does not extend the proof to almost orthogonal data or quantify allowed deviation from orthogonality (Reviewer 3, 4). This limits practical relevance; the bound might not hold for real, correlated embeddings (Reviewer 3). Additionally, the assumption of orthonormal inputs significantly simplifies the analysis, but it is unclear how deviations from orthonormality (e.g., the anisotropic token embeddings noted by Ethayarajh, 2019) would affect the observed phenomena (Reviewer 3).
   - The analysis further assumes normal distributions on weights and tokens, and treats query and key rows as independent and identically distributed, which is query-independent (W1.4 of Reviewer 2). The claim that rows of QK^T are i.i.d. N(0, KK^T) is not justified (Reviewer 2, W1.4). This query-independence contradicts the fundamental motivation of transformers where different queries should have different attention patterns (Reviewer 2). Also, the assumptions on key/query projection matrices seem more restrictive than those on inputs and require appropriate discussion (Reviewer 2, C3).
   - Reviewer 2 provides a counterexample: with K=Q=X, each ||x_i||_2=1, and low temperature (top-1 attention), the attention matrix becomes the identity I_n (full rank), violating the 0.63n bound. Similarly, an off-diagonal shift (each token attending to its predecessor) yields near-full rank. The paper does not explain why such cases are implausible or how its analysis excludes them (Reviewer 2, W1.2). This suggests the bound may be an artifact of the assumptions rather than a universal limitation.

3. **Lack of motivation and practical implications** (Reviewer 2, 3):
   - The paper does not convincingly explain why the rank of the attention matrix matters for model performance or expressive power. No clear relationship is established between rank and any performance metric (accuracy, perplexity, etc.) (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 asks: what problems can a transformer not solve because of this rank-bound? Specific examples are needed to connect theory to practice.
   - It is unclear how the "model-reduction effect" can be leveraged for efficiency (e.g., to determine optimal head dimensions, prune heads, or compress models). The paper only observes saturation but does not propose or test any practical method (Reviewer 3). Given that softmax already induces low-rank structure (as noted by Reviewer 2's question), the novelty and actionable insight are uncertain.
   - Reviewer 2 asks how rank relates to expressivity, and notes that linear attention can achieve full rank but is not desired; the paper does not address this.

4. **Theoretical result independent of head dimension** (Reviewer 2, W1.3):
   - Theorem 1 has no dependence on d_h, which seems at odds with the stated goal of studying the effect of head dimension on rank. The derivation effectively reduces the attention matrix to one with a single non-zero entry per row (uniform random selection), yielding the 0.63n bound via a standard combinatorics coupon-collector argument (n(1-(1-1/n)^n)). This form is not representative of useful attention mechanisms, and it is not clear how d_h matters. The paper should reconcile this discrepancy or provide a finer-grained analysis that ties the bound to d_h (Reviewer 2). For example, with h=1, one might expect full rank, but this is not discussed (Reviewer 2, W1.1).

5. **Presentation issues and missing context** (Reviewer 2, 4):
   - The abstract and introduction give the impression that the low-rank phenomenon is general, but the main theoretical and empirical results are for random/initialized transformers, not trained models (Reviewer 4). This should be clearly stated upfront.
   - Figure 1 caption is not self-sufficient; a complete description should be provided (Reviewer 2, C1).
   - Table 2's caption includes "less than or around 0.01" in relation to the log-dependence argument, which is less convincing; for sequence length 200, more than linear increase in d_h was needed, implying a different story (Reviewer 2, C2).
   - Missing references to related works on theoretical rank analysis of attention: KDEFormer (Zandieh et al., 2023) and HyperAttention (Han et al., 2024) (Reviewer 2, Question). Also, Performers (Choromanski et al., 2020) and scatterbrain are relevant for the low-rank motivation from softmax (Reviewer 2, Question).

6. **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (2, 3) consider the paper's soundness low (1 and 2) due to assumptions and lack of practical relevance; two others (1, 4) rate soundness 4, considering the contributions valuable despite limitations. The paper needs to address the concerns of the negative reviewers to strengthen its claims.
