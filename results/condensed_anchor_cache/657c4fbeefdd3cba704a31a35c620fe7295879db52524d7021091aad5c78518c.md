## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes LabSafety Bench, a benchmark of 765 multiple-choice questions aligned with OSHA protocols to evaluate LLMs (and large vision models) on lab safety knowledge. It compares 17 models (including GPT-4o, open-weight, proprietary) and human participants. A minority reviewer (score 6) praises the specificity and practical contribution, while most other reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3, 5) question the motivation, threat model, significance, and evaluation methodology. Core disagreements center on whether the benchmark measures meaningful risk of unsafe actions and whether multiple-choice questions are a valid proxy for real-world lab safety reasoning.

### Strengths

* The paper targets a highly specific, essential aspect of LLM application (lab safety) where errors have severe real-world consequences, filling a critical gap in LLM evaluation. (R1)
* Creation of LabSafety Bench tailored to lab safety is a novel and practical contribution. (R1)
* The validation study is welcome and useful. (R2)
* Substantial engagement with performance evaluation results, including qualitative review of errors and lower-performing models to understand approaches for enhancing support. (R2)
* Addresses an important topic as LLMs are increasingly integrated into scientific workflows. (R3)
* Benchmark is comprehensive, covering multiple aspects of lab safety and aligned with established OSHA protocols. (R3)
* Evaluation is thorough, including both text-only and multimodal questions, and comparing against human performance. (R3)
* Sound study with carefully designed experiments and evaluations; huge human effort to compose and examine the data; benchmark valuable for developing useful LLMs for safety guidance. (R4)
* Good clear presentation; motivation, methodology, and evaluation results convincing. Interesting finding that CoT and few-shot have minimal impact on LLM performance. Identifies model difficulties in Radiation Hazards, Physical Hazards, Equipment Usage, and Electricity Safety. (R4)
* Problem is well motivated and justified, and seems important considering the use of LLMs in many safety-critical areas. (R5)
* Lab safety taxonomy based on OSHA requirements seems helpful and useful for benchmarking. (R5)
* Evaluation has good diversity of LLM models (17 foundation models, including open source, proprietary and scientific models) and different prompting techniques. (R5)
* Code and data are released open source. (R5)

### Weaknesses

* **Motivation and threat model** – There is no offered model of how users reach unsafe actions. The assumption that a user queries an LLM and then takes action based on the response is considered naive. Motivating accidents listed (e.g., chemical fabrication pipeline accidents) are generally not the kind that improved LLM function would avoid. The paper does not clarify how an LLM query fits into a safe lab process or why a trained lab scientist would unquestioningly follow an LLM’s guidance. (R2) (R3: Main use case – students asking LLMs about lab safety – is itself problematic; students should consult official protocols and trained personnel.) (R3) (R1 asks: what are the tasks and roles LLMs play in lab environments, and why is it important to benchmark safety? Should be highlighted in intro.) These concerns temper the claimed practical significance; a minority reviewer (R1) finds the benchmark highly novel and practical, but most others see the motivation as insufficiently justified.

* **Validity of multiple-choice as a proxy** – Multiple-choice questions may not fully capture complex decision-making required in real lab safety. In practice, users engage with LLMs very differently (e.g., free-form queries, not multiple choice). The benchmark may not measure whether an LLM can provide safe actionable advice in realistic workflows. (R1, R5) (R5 explicitly asks why multiple-choice questions were chosen and comments on limited applicability as proxy.) (R2: Tests against benchmarks do not define knowledge about avoidance of risk; the paper should caveat the measurement against a defined purpose.)

* **Human evaluation concerns** – Selection of human participants and the examination procedure are not described in sufficient detail. (R1) The paper does not specify what lab safety training the humans had or whether they were tested on questions matching their specific subfield (e.g., broad biology safety vs. a narrow subfield). (R5) Including junior scientists (undergraduates) may make the LLM comparison unfair; LLMs should be compared to human experts only. (R5) Human evaluation does not allow participants to consult external materials, which is problematic because if humans could achieve 100% by using references, they would not need LLMs. (R3) Additionally, no ethics statement for human subjects research is provided. (R2)

* **Benchmark construction and bias** – Using LLMs (GPT-4) to regenerate benchmark questions raises concerns: if LLM text is detectable, rewriting may bias the selection of multiple-choice answers by models during evaluation. (R2) Potential biases from using GPT-4 in the curation process should be addressed. (R3)

* **Missing related works** – Several relevant works on LLM safety evaluation are not cited: Xie et al. "Sorry-bench", Li et al. "Inference-time intervention", Xie et al. "Online Safety Analysis". (R1)

* **Lack of insights and solutions** – Findings such as “LLMs are not fully reliable in lab safety” are not surprising given the general concerns about LLM reliability in safety-critical domains. The paper offers little study of methods that could improve performance (e.g., integrating symbolic representations or knowledge bases into generation). Potential solutions would be more valuable. (R4, R2)

* **Unclear value of model comparisons** – Several pages are spent comparing LLM performance (e.g., GPT-4o vs. other models) without explaining the purpose. Since LLM competition develops fast, such comparisons may become obsolete quickly and are not considered necessary or important. (R4) (R4 asks: what is the necessity of comparing LLM performance?)

* **Benchmark evaluation absent** – Although the paper claims the benchmark as a contribution, it lacks an evaluation of the benchmark itself (e.g., whether it is good enough to train LLMs or investigate new approaches). (R4) (R4 asks: how can we assess the quality of the benchmark?)

* **Additional minor issues** – Reference (OSHA, 2011l) is hard to read due to orthographic similarity of 1 and l. (R2)
