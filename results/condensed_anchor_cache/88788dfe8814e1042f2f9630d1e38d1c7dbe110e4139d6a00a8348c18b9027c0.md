## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers are split on this paper: two rate it highly (8, 6) with high to moderate confidence, while two rate it low (3, 3) with one high and one low confidence, and one gives a neutral 5. There is consensus that the idea of using matrix-free algorithms and preconditioning for second-order PINN optimization is interesting and that empirical results show improved accuracy and convergence. However, major concerns exist about the correctness of the theoretical core (Lemma 3.2), insufficient experimental validation, unclear novelty relative to existing Hessian-free methods, and presentation issues. Several reviewers explicitly question the paper’s main claims and technical soundness.

### Strengths
- The paper clearly and concisely addresses the scalability limitation of prior natural gradient methods for PINNs, which previously required constructing and inverting a dense square matrix of dimension equal to the number of parameters. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5)
- The proposed matrix-free approach (Hessian-free natural gradient) avoids storing the full Hessian, enabling scaling to networks with up to a million parameters. (Reviewers 1, 2, 5)
- The preconditioning scheme based on a low-rank approximation of the Gauss‑Newton Hessian, obtained via adapted Lanczos decomposition and orthogonal iteration with the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula, is a novel and non‑trivial contribution to the problem of finding effective preconditioners for these systems. (Reviewers 1, 2, 5)
- Empirical results demonstrate significant improvement in training loss (orders of magnitude) and faster convergence compared to LBFGS, Adam, SGD, UGNNG, and DGNNG across benchmark PDEs (e.g., 2D Navier-Stokes, Poisson equation). Figures 3 and 4 show particularly strong convergence speed. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 5)
- The method is well-founded in numerical linear algebra techniques. The combination of low-rank reduction of cost, preconditioning to improve condition number, and matrix-vector products is reasonable and potentially impactful. (Reviewers 1, 3, 5)
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow overall, with a rich experimental section. (Reviewers 1, 2, 5)

### Weaknesses
**Theoretical correctness concerns (major):**
- Lemma 3.2, a key result about the conditioning number of the preconditioned system, is stated incorrectly and its proof in the appendix contains fundamental errors. One reviewer (2, high confidence) states the lemma “is simply false” and the proof is full of “very strange errors”, including a nonsensical decomposition in Step 2 and an inability to correctly identify maximum and minimum eigenvalues in Step 5. Another reviewer (4) also questions the implication in Step 4 ($M_k v_i = m_i v_i \Rightarrow M_k^{-1} v_i = \frac{1}{m_i} v_i$) and asks what happens if $M_k$ is not invertible (i.e., $m_i=0$). These flaws undermine the theoretical justification of the preconditioner. (Reviewers 2, 4)
- The claim that the spectrum of a small MLP in Figure 1 is representative of Gauss‑Newton spectra for general PINNs is not adequately justified. Prior work (Papyan 2020) shows that taking care of only the top few eigenvalues is often insufficient; from Figure 1 it is not clear that the remaining spectrum becomes well-conditioned. The Gauss‑Newton matrix is a sum of $N$ rank-$O$ matrices (upper bounded by rank $NO$), so for large problems capturing all necessary leading eigenvalues is unlikely. (Reviewer 2, high confidence)
- The paper states (line 161) that the method “circumvents the prohibitive cubic cost” of matrix operations, but conjugate gradient methods still have $O(n^2)$ per iteration and require $O(n)$ iterations, leading to cubic complexity in practice. The main advantage is lower space complexity, not time complexity. (Reviewer 2)

**Missing experiments and ablation:**
- No experiments on higher-dimensional PDEs beyond 10‑D Poisson; real-world problems are often higher-dimensional, and this would strengthen the scalability claim. (Reviewer 4)
- The choice of PDE benchmarks (Poisson, Navier-Stokes) is limited; it is unclear how the method would perform on other common PDEs such as Burgers, Advection, Euler‑Bernoulli, or nonlinear diffusion. (Reviewer 4)
- The paper lacks an ablation study to isolate the contributions of preconditioning, the matrix-free computation, and the line search. It is unclear how much of the improvement is due to the preconditioning versus other components. (Reviewer 5)
- It is not stated whether the results in the figures are from one run or multiple seeds; plotting mean and standard deviation over several random seeds would be more informative. (Reviewer 4)
- The conjugate gradient tolerance is chosen as 1 (Appendix B.3), which seems high and may affect convergence quality. (Reviewer 2)

**Unclear novelty and contribution:**
- The term “Hessian‑Free Natural Gradient Descent” is confusing because the method still uses a low-rank approximation of the Hessian. A comparison with directly using a low-rank approximation as the pseudo-inverse of $\mathcal{H}(\theta)$ would help clarify the advantage. (Reviewer 1)
- The proposed algorithm is not specifically tailored to PINNs; it does not exploit any physics-informed structure. Several reviewers question whether this is mainly an application of existing Hessian‑free natural gradient techniques from general machine learning to PINNs, and whether the primary novelty lies in the preconditioning. (Reviewers 3, 5)
- The relation between the mathematical techniques in Sections 3.2 and 3.3 and the Hessian matrix or physics-informed machine learning is not clearly explained, making the paper harder to follow. (Reviewer 5)
- The claim of “faster convergence rate” is made without specifying what that rate is or deriving it. (Reviewer 4)

**Presentation and technical errors:**
- In Section 2.2, the discretization process and the explicit form of the empirical residual are not clearly shown, which may hinder understanding. (Reviewer 1)
- In Section 2.3, the first equation is missing a diagonal scaling matrix between $J(\theta)^T$ and $J(\theta)$ (with entries $\frac{1}{N_\Omega}$ for domain points and $\frac{1}{N_{\partial\Omega}}$ for boundary points). (Reviewer 1)
- There is a typo in Section 2.3: “$\mathcal{H}v = J^T w$” should be “$\mathcal{H}\nabla L(\theta) = J^T w$”. (Reviewer 1)
- The Sherman‑Morrison‑Woodbury formula is presented as $M_k^{-1} v = \frac{1}{\lambda} v - \frac{1}{\lambda^2} V_k D V_k^\top v$ but it should contain $D^{-1}$ rather than $D$; also there is no guarantee that $D$ is always invertible. (Reviewer 4)
- Inconsistent citation formatting (missing parentheses) disrupts readability. (Reviewer 4)
- Typographical errors such as “Eq. equation,” “THE GAUSS-NEWTON GAUSS-NEWTON HESSIAN,” “section4.1,” and “minimaland” appear. (Reviewer 4)
