## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a layer-swapping method for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in LLMs. Starting from the same pre-trained model (Llama 3.1 8B), separate “experts” are fine-tuned on English math instruction data and on generic instruction data in a target language. The top and bottom transformer layers of the math expert are replaced with layers from the language expert, with intermediate layers merged via souping (averaging). This design is motivated by an interpretative analysis showing that math tuning primarily changes middle layers, while language tuning changes first and last layers. The merged models are evaluated on the MGSM math benchmark across four languages (Swahili, Telugu, Bengali, Japanese) and achieve gains of up to 10% over individual experts and other merging methods. The approach is post-hoc, requiring no additional training beyond initial fine-tuning.

### Strengths
- The layer-swapping method is novel, efficient, and practical: it recombines task and language expertise post-hoc without complex adjustments, outperforming the individual SFT experts, the base LLM, and general model souping in three out of four languages.
- The approach directly addresses the challenge of lacking task-specific data in low-resource languages, requiring only generic instruction data in the target language.
- The paper includes additional experiments on Swahili and a limitations section that address several initial concerns, and the writing is clear, well-justified, and easy to follow.
- The preliminary analysis of parameter changes during fine-tuning (math changes middle layers, language changes first/last) is interpretative and provides intuitive support for the layer-swapping design.

### Weaknesses
- Evaluation is limited to one model architecture (Llama 3.1, 8B), one task (mathematical reasoning on MGSM), and only four languages (Japanese, Telugu, Swahili, Bengali). This narrow scope makes the generalizability of the method unclear. Missing experiments on other tasks (e.g., question answering, translation, code generation) and with other models (e.g., encoder-decoder architectures, models with different pre-training) are needed. One reviewer noted the contribution as lower (2) and emphasized the need for broader evaluation.
- The assumption that high-quality, human-generated generic instruction data is available for low-resource languages may not hold in practice. The authors did not test the use of machine-translated instruction data for the language expert, which would increase the practical relevance of the method.
- There is insufficient ablation and analysis of the layer-swapping configuration choices. The paper lacks a detailed study of how varying the number of swapped layers or the size of the transition zones affects performance, making it unclear how to determine optimal configurations for different tasks or model sizes.
- Comparisons to other modular fine-tuning techniques (e.g., adapters, LoRA) are missing. Such comparisons should include not only performance but also computational costs and number of trainable parameters to contextualize the method’s relative strengths.
- For Japanese, layer swapping yields lower average performance than the individual math expert. The authors did not share results on other benchmarks (BELEBELE, FLORES, MBPP, MMLU) for the Japanese expert, which could help explain this case.
- Several questions raised by reviewers correspond to missing analyses: (a) the effect of longer fine-tuning on which layers change and on transfer success, (b) whether a two-stage SFT (first language then task, or vice versa) would outperform joint SFT, (c) how the re-composed model behaves on typologically similar languages, and (d) how the method scales to larger models (e.g., more than 32 layers) and whether the same performance gains are expected.
