## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores using weak LLMs (small models) as feedback providers for alignment, positioning this as a middle ground between costly human annotation and expensive large-LLM annotation. The weak LLM is fine-tuned with DPO on a small labeled preference dataset; its implicit reward model then annotates unlabeled data, and the resulting dataset is used to align a student LLM. Experiments across model scales (down to 125M parameters) and families (OPT, Pythia, Llama) show that weak LLM feedback can match or exceed human feedback and GPT-4 feedback. The paper also provides qualitative and quantitative analysis of feedback quality discrepancies.

### Strengths
- Research topic is critical and timely given high costs of human and large model annotations.
- Paper is well-written, clear, polished, and easy-to-follow, with helpful background on alignment techniques and a dedicated discussion distinguishing this work from prior studies (e.g., Burns et al. 2024).
- Proposed framework is cost-effective, reducing both computational and human annotation costs.
- Experiments are comprehensive and convincing, evaluating weak LLM feedback across various model scales and families and demonstrating consistent effectiveness and generalizability.
- Weak LLMs perform as well as or better than human annotators and GPT-4 in generating preference feedback, as shown by multiple metrics (gold reward model, GPT-4 win-rate, human evaluation).
- Research has significant practical implications for scalable and sustainable alignment.
- In-depth analysis offers insights into quality discrepancies between human and weak LLM feedback.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The weak-to-strong approach is methodologically similar to Burns et al. (2024) (reward modeling and binary classification), and the contribution may be incremental over RLAIF pipelines with smaller models. Authors need to better articulate differences and additional insights beyond "using a smaller model." Reviewer 5 further notes that works like Self-Rewarding Language Models do similar things with "weak-ish" models, further weakening uniqueness.
- **Choice of DPO not motivated**: Since only a reward model is needed for annotation, training a generation model via DPO seems unnecessary; a standard RLHF reward model would be simpler. The impact of DPO vs. RLHF reward model should be studied.
- **Missing comparisons**: No direct comparison with RLAIF using weak LLMs as the reward model, nor with the original RLAIF pipeline. Comparisons with higher-quality human data (e.g., revised HH-RLHF) are needed to address known noise in the dataset.
- **Limited and potentially biased evaluation**: Evaluation relies on only two automated metrics: a "gold reward model" (whose "gold" status is not justified; it may also make incorrect judgments on subtle differences) and GPT-4 win-rate (where order bias should be controlled by swapping positions). More diverse evaluation models (e.g., RewardBench, models with different architectures/training data) and metrics are needed.
- **Lack of consistency analysis**: No analysis of weak LLM feedback across multiple runs, different initialization seeds, or different models from the same family. Stability and repeatability are not assessed.
- **Generalization concerns**: Small models may overfit to the specific dataset; generalization across domains is not studied. If weak LLM feedback does not transfer, annotation data for each domain may still require large human cost.
- **Limited task scope**: Only general helpfulness/harmlessness is tested; applicability to more complex tasks (math, coding) is not shown.
- **Framing issues**: "Weak LLM feedback" is positioned as a "middle ground" between human feedback and AI feedback, but it is still AI feedback with a smaller model. The narrative should better disentangle "weak" from "small"; a small model trained on quality preference data might become a moderate reward model, not necessarily "weak."
- **Background section takes space**: Section 2 on DPO/PPO basics is somewhat trivial for an alignment paper; could be moved to appendix to focus on results.
- **Actionable insights are sparse**: The in-depth analysis mainly confirms that both weak and strong models struggle with noisy data, providing limited practical guidance.
- **Missing experimental detail**: The default ratio \(|\mathcal{D}_l|/|\mathcal{D}|\) used in main experiments is not specified (only given in ablations).
- **Figure error**: The x-axis label in Figure 7 (Appendix C) should be \(\beta\), not "student model size."
- **Unexplained outperformance**: The paper claims weak LLM annotations can surpass human-labeled data but does not fully explain why (likely due to noise in human labels, which needs more discussion).
