## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes FLARE, a method that fine-tunes ReLU activations (replacing Softmax) and FIRE positional encodings into pre-trained models (specifically GPT-2) to enable efficient long-context inference on edge devices. The authors claim that this combination yields better validation loss, faster inference, length generalization, and allows mathematical fusion into a single efficient operation (98.9% zero lower-triangle in the probability matrix). They benchmark custom CUDA kernels (3.8× faster than Softmax FlashAttention) and ASIC synthesis using a 130nm PDK (8× frequency, 0.1% power, 0.11% energy per cycle). Experiments were conducted on GPT-2 fine-tuned on OpenWebText.

### Strengths

1. **Relevant problem and practical motivation.** Deploying LLMs on resource-constrained edge devices is timely and the proposed acceleration of long-context attention addresses a genuine bottleneck (R2, R3, R4).
2. **Effective integration of ReLU with FIRE.** Combining ReLU-based attention (a well-studied idea in itself) with FIRE positional encodings is a novel combination that has not been previously explored for fine-tuning into existing models (R1, R3). Two reviewers (R3, R4) found the combination interesting and practically relevant, while two others (R1, R2) saw it as incremental.
3. **Solid implementation and hardware profiling.** The custom CUDA kernel (ReLUFlashAttention) shows 3.8× speedup over Softmax FlashAttention, and the hardware PPA analysis (130nm PDK) demonstrates significant improvements in frequency, power, and energy (R1, R3). The experiments on memory usage, speed, and power efficiency are well-structured and clearly presented (R3).
4. **Clear exposition.** The paper explains ReLU and FIRE techniques progressively; the writing is clear and easy to follow (R2, R3). R1 also noted a reasonable writing flow.

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of novelty relative to prior work.** The core components (ReLU replacing Softmax) are well-studied (e.g., [1]) and the results align with expectations from that work (R2). The paper does not clearly distinguish whether it uses the sequence-length scaling factor from [1] and, if not, fails to analyze the impact of omitting it (R2). For the FIRE integration, the original FIRE paper [3] already includes a section on "FINETUNING ON LONG TEXT BENCHMARK"; the paper claims (line 260-261) no previous attempts to finetune FIRE encodings, but it is unclear if [3] finetuned the positional encodings themselves or only the model weights. This comparison is missing, which diminishes the novelty claim (R2). Overall, two reviewers (R1, R2) rated the contribution as incremental (score 3), while two (R3, R4) rated it higher (score 5), indicating disagreement on the extent of novelty.
2. **Evaluation limited to a single small model (GPT-2).** All fine-tuning and inference experiments were conducted on GPT-2. It is unclear whether gains generalize to larger models like Qwen, LLaMA, or LLaMA-2. The authors had access to 80GB A100s and could have used FSDP to fine-tune larger models (R3). R1 also noted the 20K fine-tuning steps are model-dependent, with no guarantee the method stabilizes across architectures, and the paper itself mentions instability concerns from ReLU (L151).
3. **No downstream task evaluation.** Only validation loss is presented; there are no benchmarks on standard NLP tasks (e.g., MMLU, QA, summarization) to demonstrate that the fine-tuned model retains quality (R3). The missing curves in Figures 3, 5, and 7 (as noted by R1) further reduce the completeness of the training analysis.
4. **Lack of real edge-device testing.** Despite claiming inference efficiency for edge devices, only hardware PPA simulation (130nm PDK) is provided. No experiments on actual edge platforms (e.g., Android phones, Raspberry Pi) are performed, even for a single attention block (R3). R2 also noted that the end-to-end execution time overhead (including zero-skipping) is missing; a breakdown of execution time across model components would help assess the overall impact.
5. **Hardware evaluation using an outdated PDK (130nm).** The PPA analysis uses a 130nm technology node, which is several generations old. Results could differ significantly on more recent nodes (e.g., 45nm, 15nm open-source PDKs). This limits the credibility of the claimed power and area benefits (R2).
6. **Missing detailed analysis of hardware-area impact.** The PPA study lacks the percentage area of the FLARE/Softmax blocks relative to a full DNN accelerator. Similarly, power consumption breakdowns for individual components within the blocks are not provided (R2).
7. **Insufficient clarity in method description.** The paper does not explain all design decisions for FLARE, nor does it clearly separate novel contributions from existing techniques (R4). Notation is missing (e.g., "n" undefined in L83-L90) and informal language appears (e.g., "don't" in L322) (R1). Figure captions (Figs. 8, 9) are not centered, and overall presentation quality was rated low by R1 (score 1) and R4 (score 2), though R3 found it clear.
8. **Incomplete experimental comparisons.** Section 8 only compares ReLU and Softmax; other activation or positional encoding combinations are not tested (R1). The claim that the fused operation yields 98.9% zero operations is presented without comparison to alternative sparsity-inducing methods.

These weaknesses reflect that two reviewers (R1, R2) found the paper significantly lacking in novelty and evaluation depth, while the other two (R3, R4) saw value in the integration and hardware results but still noted limitations.
