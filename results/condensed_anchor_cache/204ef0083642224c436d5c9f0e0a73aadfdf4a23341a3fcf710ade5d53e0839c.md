## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes V3, an unsupervised method that disentangles content and style representations from sequences of observations using variance-versus-invariance constraints within a vector-quantized encoder-decoder architecture. It exploits the inductive bias that content varies more within a sample but maintains a consistent vocabulary across samples, while style remains invariant within a sample but varies across samples. Results on music audio, handwritten digits, and simple animations show strong disentanglement, out-of-distribution generalization, few-shot learning, and interpretable codebook alignment with human knowledge.

### Strengths
- Novel unsupervised approach that does not rely on external labels or supervision, leveraging universal statistical differences between content and style. (Reviewers 1,2,3,4,5)
- Generalizes across multiple domains and modalities: music audio (NSynth), handwritten digits (SVHN, Written Phone Numbers), simple animations (SPRITES), and images. (1,2,5)
- Content representations exhibit interpretable properties, achieving near one-to-one alignment between machine codebook entries and human knowledge. (1,2,3)
- Strong out-of-distribution generalization and few-shot learning, sometimes surpassing supervised counterparts. (1,2)
- Loss function is concise and well-motivated, aligning with human cognitive characteristics; the variance-invariance constraints are clearly justified. (2,4)
- The loss framework is not limited to V3 and can be applied to other models for content-style learning. (2)
- Paper is well-structured, clearly presented, with strong logic, clear problem statement, methodology, and experimental evaluation. (2,4,5)
- Makes a novel contribution over existing work, most of which requires external labels for style or content. (4)

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumptions limit applicability**: The method assumes (i) content elements are distinct and content varies more within a sample than across samples while style is invariant within but varies across samples, and (ii) data can be pre-segmented into fragments. These assumptions are restrictive for domains with overlapping content (e.g., polyphonic music with multiple instruments, natural scenes with occlusion, images with multiple objects having varying styles and positions), continuous unsegmented data (e.g., continuous speech with blurry phoneme boundaries, video where actions blend), and scenarios where content variation is too small (e.g., Chinese calligraphy with few characters, paintings with one object) or too large (e.g., short passages in different languages where content still varies greatly). Multiple reviewers (1,2,4) explicitly flag that the method may misinterpret style as content or vice versa in these cases, and the method’s reliance on observing variation within a sequence is untested when such sequences are not naturally present.
- **Experiments limited to toy/synthetic datasets**: All experiments are on small, carefully crafted datasets (Written Phone Numbers, SPRITES, NSynth, SVHN cropped). No real-world datasets such as human speech (e.g., VCTK for voice conversion), face photos (e.g., FFHQ), natural images, paintings, or real music recordings are evaluated. This raises concerns about scalability, robustness to noise, and applicability to real-world complexity. (1,3,4,5) Even the most positive reviewer (5, score 8) notes that 3 of 4 experiments are on synthetic data and recommends testing where assumptions do not hold.
- **Lack of principled codebook and hyperparameter design**: No guidelines or sensitivity analysis are provided for how to determine codebook size \(K\) and codebook dimension, making the method difficult to reproduce and adapt to new datasets. Hyperparameters such as the relativity parameter \(r\) and V3 loss weight \(\beta\) are chosen dataset-specifically without intuition or sensitivity study. (1,2,3)
- **Insufficient ablation analysis**: The ablation study shows that some loss components degrade performance, but no deeper investigation into why or how the components interact. (2) Moreover, the necessity of vector quantization (VQ) is not justified; no ablation variant without VQ is shown. (2)
- **Difficulty distinguishing similar contents and styles**: The model may struggle with fine-grained discrimination between similar content or style instances. It may collapse similar styles into a single representation, limiting style transfer or recognition tasks. A reviewer suggests analyzing how much content information can be recovered from the style representation and vice versa to ensure sufficient disentanglement. (2)
- **Missing related work comparisons**: Two important related works are not discussed or compared: "Retriever: Learning Content-Style Representation as a Token-Level Bipartite Graph" and "Rethinking Content and Style: Exploring Bias for Unsupervised Disentanglement." A detailed comparison on basic assumptions and scope should be included. (2)
- **Minor issues**: Typo on line 463 ("contennt" should be "content") (1). Page 5 claims codebook usage distribution is not differentiable, but a soft, differentiable softmax-based alternative exists (e.g., VQ-VAE’s soft selection) (4). Statement "Content should be more variable within samples than across samples" on line 242 is imprecise; it should refer to sample-wise aggregated content (4).

*Missing analysis/previously questions folded in:* The paper does not evaluate on more complex datasets (e.g., FFHQ) or discuss limitations when dealing with multiple contents/styles within a single sample (1,2). No analysis is provided on whether style representations inadvertently capture content (coupling) (2). The impact of using different numbers of content and style representations is not explored (2). The retrieval experiment setting in Section 4.2 (nearest neighbors with PR-AUC and best F1) needs elaboration (4).
