## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes to exploit the lazy training regime (linear dynamics) and low-rank structure of the model’s Jacobian to design a backpropagation‑free gradient descent algorithm. It also presents scalable methods for measuring laziness (rate of change of the Jacobian) and verifying low‑rankness of the Jacobian without storing the full matrix. The algorithm precomputes a low‑rank decomposition of the Jacobian at initialization (using a reference set) and then trains with that fixed decomposition, avoiding backpropagation.

### Strengths
- Explores combining NTK theory with low‑rank approximations to increase training efficiency (R1).
- The paper is well‑presented and easy to read (R2, R4).
- Provides a useful review of NTK theory, especially for readers unfamiliar with the topic (R3).
- Proposes a relatively efficient way to compute low‑rank gradients without a full‑size SVD (R3).
- Presents an interesting alternative to backpropagation when the lazy/low‑rank assumptions hold (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient experimental validation.**  
  – The only dataset tested is MNIST, using only fully‑connected layers of varying widths. No experiments were conducted on more challenging datasets (e.g., CIFAR‑10, ImageNet) or on architectures such as CNNs or transformers, even though the paper claims general applicability to such models (R1, R3).  
  – No training curves, comparisons with standard methods like SGD, or analysis of sensitivity to hyperparameters are provided (R2, R4).  
  – The achieved accuracies on MNIST are not state‑of‑the‑art, and the setup does not reflect the resource‑efficient large‑model scenarios the paper targets (R3).  
  – The algorithm is claimed to be a training pipeline, but there is no experimental evidence that Eqn. (5) can train a network from start to end even on small tasks (R2).  
- **Strong assumptions not adequately justified.**  
  – Lazy training is known to correlate with poor generalization [1]; the paper neither discusses this limitation nor the trade‑off between computational efficiency and generalization performance (R2).  
  – The low‑rankness assumption may hold at initialization, but the paper does not quantify how the effective rank changes during (even near‑lazy) training, nor does it compare performance against standard backpropagation to assess degradation if the rank increases (R4).  
  – It is unclear whether the laziness assumption holds for architectures with skip connections or batch normalization; the paper lacks rigorous theoretical or empirical justification for extending the method to such models (R1).  
- **Limited novelty of the algorithm.**  
  – Algorithm 1 essentially combines dimensionality reduction and a constant‑Jacobian assumption, reusing existing ideas (low‑rank approximations, NTK regime) without a clear advancement (R1).  
  – The core technique of approximating the Jacobian via a reference set is not fundamentally new (R1).  
- **Practical implementation concerns.**  
  – The eigenvector computation, even on a reduced matrix, may still be costly if the reference set size is not small; the paper does not analyse how the size of the reference set impacts cost and accuracy (R1, R4).  
  – Details on how to select the reference set are missing, and robustness/efficiency of this step remain unclear (R1, R4).  
  – Algorithm 1 does not mention any update of the Jacobian during training; it is not explained why one should not recompute the Jacobian after a fixed number of iterations (R4).  
  – For large networks, the overhead of pre‑computing and storing the reduced Jacobian may be non‑trivial (R1).  
- **Missing verification of the linear dynamics assumption.**  
  – The paper should first test whether the linear regime holds for the chosen architectures and datasets by comparing Eqn. (5) without any low‑rank approximation against standard training on small problems (R2).  
  – It remains unclear whether the linear approximation error is small enough to make the algorithm practical, and under which conditions it breaks down (R2).  
- **Lack of reproducibility and clarifying details.**  
  – Key experimental details (data preprocessing, network architectures, hyperparameters, optimisation algorithms) are missing, making it difficult to assess or reproduce the results (R1).  
  – Notation is unclear and the writing contains typos (e.g., lines 375, 334), harming readability (R1).  
- **Minority positive reviewer (score 5) still raised significant concerns.** That reviewer acknowledged the efficient SVD‑free method and the NTK review, but agreed that the strong assumptions and very limited experimental evaluation (only MNIST fully‑connected) are major weaknesses (R3).
