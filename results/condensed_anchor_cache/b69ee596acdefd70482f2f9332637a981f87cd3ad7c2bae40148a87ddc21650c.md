## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces SummaryMixing, a linear-complexity replacement for multi-head self-attention (MHSA) in speech encoder architectures. The method computes the temporal mean of the sequence and combines it with local information from a cgMLP branch, building on the Branchformer framework. Experiments on multiple ASR benchmarks (CommonVoice, Librispeech, AISHELL, TED-LIUM 2, GSC) and SLU (SLURP) show that SummaryMixing can match or exceed the accuracy of self-attention while reducing training/inference time by up to 28% and halving memory use. Reviewers disagree substantially: one reviewer (score 8) finds the approach surprisingly effective and a strong generalization of HyperMixer, while another (score 3) considers the novelty incremental and the gains marginal; the other two (scores 6 and 5) are moderately positive but raise significant concerns about assumptions, completeness, and presentation.

### Strengths

- The approach is simple yet effective: replacing MHSA with temporal averaging and a local MLP achieves competitive or better performance across many ASR and SLU datasets (CommonVoice, Librispeech, AISHELL, TED-LIUM 2, GSC, SLURP).
- Results are very competitive against multiple strong baselines, including Conformer, Branchformer, ContextNet, Branchformer+fastattention, and Branchformer without attention.
- The method is a generalization of HyperMixer to speech tasks, and its effectiveness is surprising given its simplicity.
- Low computational cost: up to 28% faster inference and half the memory consumption compared to standard Branchformer.
- Good reproducibility: open-source implementation and use of publicly available data.
- The paper fits the ICLR scope and proposes an interesting direction for reducing model complexity with linear layers.
- Encoder-only experiments (CTC setup) help demonstrate that MHSA complexity is not strictly necessary in the encoder.
- The paper highlights the surprisingly good performance of the simplest baseline (Branchformer without attention), which is an honest and informative observation.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient literature survey**: The paper does not adequately cover existing efficient transformer methods for speech (e.g., Squeezeformer, Efficient Conformer, Emformer) or low-rank/kernel attention approaches. A dedicated survey section is missing.
- **Novelty concerns**: The contribution is incremental on top of BranchFormer and HyperMixer; the core idea of using a pooled summary is not clearly distinct from global pooling or other feature aggregation methods. The difference mainly lies in the choice of linear functions.
- **Performance gains marginal**: The improvement over Fastformer is small, and no statistical significance analysis (confidence intervals, variance) is provided. It is unclear whether the gains justify introducing a new architecture.
- **Fundamental assumption not well supported**: The claim that MHSA in the encoder is unnecessary relies on Zhang et al. (who say it's not needed in higher layers only) and Peng et al. (who use self-attention for global context). The argument that uniform attention weights justify a simple average is not rigorous; the paper lacks analysis of actual attention weight distributions and their implications for information flow.
- **Lack of experiments on spontaneous speech**: All SLU experiments use read speech (SLURP). Temporal averaging may be harmful for spontaneous conversational speech (e.g., SLUE-VoxPopuli). The paper needs to evaluate degradation with increasing spontaneity and noise.
- **Dependence on transformer decoder**: The main results use a 6-block MHSA decoder, which may capture global context even if the encoder does not. The paper cannot conclude that self-attention is redundant unless verified in an encoder-only (CTC) setting without decoder replay. Needed: CTC results with vanilla Conformer/Transformer, and without LM shallow fusion. Consider also RNN-T decoders.
- **Missing evaluation on long-context ASR**: How does performance (WER) vary with sequence length? As the sequence grows, the summary vector blends more frames, potentially degrading discriminability.
- **Vague architecture description**: Details of encoder layers, decoder, loss (CTC), and model size adjustment are unclear. The paper often refers readers to SpeechBrain recipes, leaving shallow readers confused.
- **Insufficient explanation of HyperMixer/MLP Mixer**: The distinction from SummaryMixing is not well explained; a detailed description of HyperMixer architecture and parameters is needed to assess novelty.
- **MHSA implementation specifics missing**: For speed comparisons, the paper should disclose which MHSA implementation was used, as many efficient variants exist.
- **Writing clarity issues**: Abstract should expand acronyms (ASR, SLU). Figure 1 is confusing because “T” may be misinterpreted as heads. The term “head” in Multi-head SummaryMixing is misleading (it resembles chunk-wise attention rather than parallel attention heads). The mathematical notation on page 3 (“X ∈ ℝ becomes X ∈ ℝ^{T×D/n}”) contains a typo.
- **Missing citations**: Several recent works show linear transformers achieve comparable ASR performance (e.g., Sun et al. “Locality Matters”; Samarakoon et al. Nyström attention; Rekesh et al. Fast Conformer). These should be acknowledged and compared.
- **Missing experiments**: Application to RNN-T not explored. Online ASR comparison not provided. KWS results are mentioned but not shown in the results section. 
- **Scope narrow for ICLR**: The paper focuses exclusively on speech, while the original HyperMixer included NLP tasks; general AI/ML audience may not be engaged.
