## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes the Decomposed Concept Bottleneck Model (DCBM), which replaces the pre-defined text concept embeddings used in prior Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) with learned visual concept vectors called Concept Decomposition Vectors (CDVs). CDVs are trained via projection of image features in a CLIP-like VLM latent space. To interpret these CDVs, the authors introduce a quintuple notion of concepts and a concept-sample distribution, and they use either an LLM-based text composition or prompt-conditioned segmentation. Experiments are conducted on nine datasets across four categories (natural, semantic, fine-grained, specialized). A human study (n=27) evaluates interpretability along six metrics (sparsity, groundability, factuality, fidelity, meaningfulness). The approach aims to overcome limitations of text-only concepts (subjectivity, insufficient visual representation) while retaining bottleneck-model interpretability.

### Strengths
- **Novel approach to interpretability:** Leveraging vision-language models to learn visual concepts instead of relying on pre-defined human text concepts is a promising direction (Reviewer 1, low confidence; Reviewer 4, high confidence). The conceptual innovation of a paradigm shift from text-based to visual concept interpretations is clearly articulated (Reviewer 2, confident).
- **Exhaustive experimental validation:** The paper evaluates DCBM on nine datasets of four types (natural, semantic, fine-grained, specialized)—a commendable breadth, even though results are not universally improved over baselines (Reviewer 2, confident; Reviewer 4, confident). Reviewer 4 specifically finds Sec. 4.2 informative and Sec. 4.3 the most impactful, where CDV clearly outperforms prior methods on all six interpretability metrics.
- **Intuitive motivation and explanation:** Figures 1 and 2 provide a clear overview of the method and the limitations of prior text-concept CBMs (Reviewer 4). The logic of tuning interpretable concept embeddings closer to the instance/domain under test makes sense (Reviewer 3).
- **Potential for future improvements:** The idea of automatically discovering concepts (no human specification) is noted as a strength by Reviewer 1, though the current implementation needs improvement.

### Weaknesses
- **Poor presentation and incomplete content:** The paper is poorly written, with grammatical errors, misspellings, and confusing vocabulary (Reviewer 1, low confidence). Reviewer 1 states that experiments supposedly performed in Sec. 4.4 are not described and refer to a missing appendix. Reviewer 3 finds the flow hard to follow and Fig. 3 confusing. Specific errors: an unfinished sentence in the Introduction (“The adversarial training ensure CDVs”), epsilon ($\epsilon$) never defined (Eq. 7), “Eq.” not consistently formatted, and references incomplete (e.g., Oikarinen & Nguyen missing venue ICLR 2023 and full author list) (Reviewer 2). In contrast, Reviewer 4 finds the paper presented well (especially Figs 1,2, and the mathematical formulation).
- **Unconvincing empirical benefits:** Performance improvements over the Labo baseline are minor (Reviewer 2). Linear probe often achieves the best accuracy in Table 1, but the paper’s highlighting seems to ignore this and offers no explanation (Reviewer 4). * Notation for linear probe in Table 1 is unexplained (Reviewer 4). Average performance across all nine datasets is not shown (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 finds the overall results underwhelming.
- **Insufficient validation of concept necessity:** It is not proven that previous text-based interpretable embeddings are truly insufficient; as VLMs improve, the text approach might solve the same issues without the added complexity (Reviewers 2 and 3, referencing the “Bitter Lesson”). Figure 2’s failure examples for text concepts might be solved by simple prompt engineering, e.g., “what are useful visual features…” or “eliminating concepts not observable from an image” (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing comparisons and references:** Important related work is omitted: Visual Classification via Description from Large Language Models (ICLR 2023) and its subsequent works should be compared empirically and theoretically (Reviewer 2). Only two 2023 papers are cited; references are out-of-date (Reviewer 2).
- **Human study limitations:** The user study with only 27 participants is limited (Reviewers 2, 4). Results may be subjective and sensitive to selected samples; statistical significance is uncertain (Reviewer 4). Details are lacking: how human labels were collected, filtering criteria, questions asked, answer distributions (Reviewer 1). The rationale for requiring “diverse geography” given the concepts are in English is questionable (Reviewer 4). No radar chart is provided for the Linear Probe baseline in Fig. 8 (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 notes the user study is necessary but insufficient without understanding incentive structures.
- **Redundant and non-distinguishing interpretations:** The text-interpreted results (e.g., “brown” in Fig. 6, “material of glass” in Fig. 5) are highly redundant across concepts. Image segment interpretations do not qualitatively show how they help distinguish classes or give confidence to a model developer (Reviewer 3).
- **Unexplained methodological choices:** The concept-sample distribution intuition is not explained (Reviewer 1). The regularization loss used in experiments is not specified (Reviewer 1). Section 4.3’s experiment objective and setup are unclear (Reviewer 1). An algorithm pseudocode would improve presentation (Reviewer 4).
