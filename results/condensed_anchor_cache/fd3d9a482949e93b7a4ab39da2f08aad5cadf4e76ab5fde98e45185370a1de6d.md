## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses Long-Context Question Answering with Citations (LQAC) at the sentence level. It introduces LongBench-Cite, an automated benchmark for sentence-level citation evaluation; CoF, a pipeline using proprietary LLMs to construct the LongCite-45k SFT dataset; and fine-tuned LongCite-8B and LongCite-9B. The trained models are reported to outperform GPT-4o on the benchmark. The paper notes that requiring citations degrades answer correctness, and that SFT improves both response quality and citation quality.

### Strengths
- **Interesting task and complete work.** Studying fine-grained (sentence-level) citations is a useful direction for verifiability. The paper presents a relatively complete exploration: proposed task variant, evaluation metric, existing-model evaluation, dataset construction, model training, human evaluation, and analysis (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Strong results.** The fine-tuned 8B/9B models achieve state-of-the-art citation quality, surpassing GPT-4o and other proprietary models. This is a clear positive result (R1, R4). Human evaluation also supports the models’ effectiveness (R1, R2).
- **Observation of correctness–citation tradeoff.** The paper shows via a “correctness ratio” that requiring sentence-level citations degrades QA correctness—a point not highlighted in prior work (R1, R2).
- **Clear structure and reasonable experimental design.** The writing is well-organized and the choices of models/datasets/evaluation are generally sound (R2, R3, R4).
- **Inclusion of human evaluation.** Manual annotation of citation precision/recall strengthens the findings, though the correlation analysis relies on only three data points (R1).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution.** The LQAC task essentially combines long-context QA with fine-grained (sentence-level) citation, both explored separately in prior work. The paper lacks sufficient discussion and comparison with existing works on fine-grained citation [2][3] and long-context generation with attribution. The core methodology (distilling from advanced LLMs) heavily overlaps with [2][3], differing mainly in context length and sentence splitting (R2, R3, R4). Many reviewers saw little new conceptual contribution (R2: “unoriginal conclusions”; R3: “low level of innovation”).
- **Insufficient emphasis on unique long-context challenges.** The paper does not clearly articulate or evaluate the distinctive difficulties of long-context scenarios, such as citation consistency across contexts, citation redundancy, potential contradictions, or the difficulty of evidence-searching over very long inputs (R3, partly R2). The benchmarking only uses one strategy (LAC-S), which cannot disentangle poor instruction-following from weak evidence-searching ability (R2).
- **Clarity, naming, and missing details.** Several confusing noun pairs appear (e.g., LongBench-Chat vs. LongBench-Cite; LongCite vs. LongSFT) (R4). The pipeline description for dataset construction is hard to follow: the retrieval method is vague (“Zhipu” but no embedding model, similarity metric), hyperparameters *l*, *k*, *n* are not justified, and the pipeline validation process is not clearly manual or automatic (R1). The construction of LongBench-Cite is unclear—whether it is a simple concatenation of existing datasets (R1). Typos exist (e.g., Line 82 “LongBench-Chat” should be “LongBench-Cite”; Figure 4 mislabeled) (R2).
- **Over-reliance on proprietary LLMs and high cost.** GPT-4o or GLM-4 is used for nearly every step: evaluating correctness and citation, filtering sentences, and all CoF pipeline steps. This introduces potential systemic bias (e.g., favoring responses that match the evaluator’s style). The human evaluation correlation is not fully convincing (only three data points). The entire SFT data construction depends on expensive calls to GLM-4, reducing reproducibility (R1, R2). The paper should include a limitations section discussing possible biases and alternate evaluation methods (R1).
- **Evaluation correctness metric conflates dimensions.** Correctness is scored on both accuracy and comprehensiveness, which are separate aspects; fluctuations could stem from errors or incomplete coverage. These should be assessed independently (R2). Scoring also uses inconsistent standards across datasets in LongBench-Cite (1–10, 1–5, 1–3 ranges) and different evaluation approaches (few-shot vs. zero-shot), without clear normalization for Table 3 (R2).
- **Lack of prompt sensitivity analysis for proprietary models.** Since the proprietary models are only prompted with a specific format (sentence-index tags), it is unclear if tuning prompts would substantially improve their performance. The paper does not discuss prompt variation or sensitivity (R1).
- **Missing baseline comparisons.** The paper does not fairly compare against prior methods that achieve sentence-level citation through content selection and sentence planning (e.g., method in [2] using Gemini) (R2).
- **Quality of self-instruct QA pairs unverified.** The first step of CoF generates questions and answers via self-instruct, but the diversity and quality of these QA pairs are not analyzed. Generated questions may focus too much on local information, leading to insufficient answers (R2).
- **Granularity concerns.** With 128k tokens per example and ~20–30 tokens per sentence, there are over 4,000 sentence indices—arguably too fine-grained for practical use (R2). Moreover, the choice of fixed 128-token chunks for chunk-level citation is not motivated; splitting by sentence boundaries could avoid incomplete sentences (R4, R1: “why not match sentence length?”).
- **Missing analyses.** Several reviewer questions point to unanswered aspects that weaken the paper’s evidence: why GLM-4-9B-chat performs poorly on GovReport (Table 2); whether other citation statistics change (number of citations, proportion citing multiple sources) (R2); whether a “lost-in-the-middle” phenomenon occurs (R1); whether models can cite non-contiguous sentences (R1); and whether CoF could itself serve as the LQAC method (R1). The Cohen’s Kappa calculation method is also not clearly explained (R1).
