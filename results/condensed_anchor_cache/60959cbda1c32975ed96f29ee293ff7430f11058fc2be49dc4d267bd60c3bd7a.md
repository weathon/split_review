## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes a variable selection method based on a weighted maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) framework. Weights are assigned to variables and optimized within a regularized MMD objective (ridge penalty); the optimized weights serve as importance measures and can be used for two-sample testing and binary classification. An accelerated variant derived from a first-order Taylor expansion is also presented. Theoretical results cover consistency of weights for the accelerated algorithm and its convergence. Experiments on two synthetic datasets and one real gene expression dataset (GSE2034) show improvement over the original unweighted MMD and Lasso-based variable selection.

### Strengths

- The paper is clearly motivated and the writing is generally clear and easy to follow.
- The method is non-parametric and model-free, allowing a broad range of applications.
- Experimental results show sizable improvement over the original MMD framework.
- The idea of using weighted MMD for model selection is novel.
- The paper presents an interesting approach to nonlinear variable selection within the MMD framework and develops a faster variant for implementation.
- The overall procedure is sound; the proof is generally correct with detailed assumptions stated, and the numerical study supports the algorithm design.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient theoretical grounding.** The work does not provide a theoretical guarantee that the optimal weights are beneficial for two-sample test power or classification accuracy (R3, main concern; echoed by R2 and R5). Only consistency and convergence of the approximate algorithm are given; there is no generalization or approximation guarantee for the original method, no guarantee on statistical power when using the approximation, and no optimality gap between the non-convex objective (3) and its approximation (6) (R2, R3, R5).
- **Empirical evaluation is limited.** Only one baseline (unweighted MMD) is compared for two-sample testing, and experiments are exclusively on synthetic data (R2). For classification, only two baselines (Lasso and unweighted MMD) are tested on synthetic data and one real dataset (GSE2034) (R1, R2, R4). Many nonlinear Lasso variants (e.g., Sparse Additive Models, Feature-wise Kernelized Lasso, referenced in [2,3]) are not compared (R4). No error bars or standard deviations are reported despite randomness from random subsets and data splits (R1, R2). Type-I error studies are not included in the main text (R4). An ablation study on whether the ridge penalty is beneficial is missing (R4).
- **Variable selection procedure is unclear.** The optimization (4) uses an L2 penalty and does not naturally encourage sparse weights; it is not explained how weights translate to variable selection – is there a thresholding step? (R2). The properties of \(w_\lambda^*\) are not sufficiently investigated (R4). Why ridge instead of L1 (which is standard for sparse selection)? No theoretical justification is given (R4, R5).
- **Presentation issues.** The derivation in Section 2.4 (Equations (4) and (5)) is intricate and unclear; readers would benefit from a clearer explanation (R1). The title “Regularized MMD” is somewhat ambiguous and does not fully capture the main idea (R4).
- **Methodological ambiguities.** The tuning of \(\lambda\) in Algorithm 1 is unclear: is it done on the training set or via cross-validation involving a test set? (R2). Also, the evaluation of \(\ell(\lambda)\) (Eqs. 4/5) – which data split is used? (R5).
- **Other concerns.** The accelerated algorithm is applied to the non-convex objective (3) and only an approximate version (6) is solved; it is not discussed whether this is just gradient descent on (3) (R1, R5). Hyperparameter sensitivity is not examined (R1). The code is not provided (R5). The possibility of extending the workflow to more general settings (e.g., regression with HSIC) is not discussed, though it is a natural question (R4).
