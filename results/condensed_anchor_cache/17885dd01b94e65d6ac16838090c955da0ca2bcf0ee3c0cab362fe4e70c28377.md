## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes the Dilated Convolution Neural Operator (DCNO) for learning operators on multiscale PDEs. It uses an encode-process-decode architecture, alternating Fourier layers (for low-frequency information) and dilated convolutional layers (for high-frequency information). A weighted loss that emphasizes high frequencies is also introduced. Experiments cover Darcy flow, Navier-Stokes, Helmholtz, and an inverse problem. The paper includes ablations, but all three reviewers question novelty, the fairness of comparisons, and the support for the central high-frequency claim. Reviewer 2 provides the most positive assessment of presentation and related work; Reviewer 1 rates the contribution as poor (1), while Reviewers 2 and 3 rate it fair (2). Reviewer 3 notes that author updates to address initial concerns were acknowledged but did not fully resolve doubts.

### Strengths

- **Broad experimental scope**: The paper evaluates DCNO on a range of physical systems (Darcy flow, Navier-Stokes, Helmholtz) and baseline architectures, with additional ablations in the appendix (R2 S3, R3). Inclusion of an inverse problem is noted as a welcome addition (R3).
- **Clear presentation and context**: The related work is reviewed thoroughly, making it easy to contextualize (R2 S1). The methodology section is clear and provides sufficient detail (R2 S2). The overall presentation is easy to follow.
- **Practical relevance**: The goal of accurate and resource-aware PDE prediction has practical applicability for industry and academia (R1). The aim of handling multiple scales is attractive, even though reviewers noted the paper does not fully deliver on that front (R3).
- **Available code**: Source code is provided to improve reproducibility, though not independently verified (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**:
  - The architecture is largely an extension of an encoder-decoder with Fourier and convolutional layers; the contribution relative to prior work, especially Cao (2021) for the decoder, is unclear (R1, R2). The paper does not clearly articulate how DCNO differs from existing approaches or what specific innovation it brings.
  - Dilated convolution itself is a well-established technique, and the paper fails to justify the uniqueness of the proposed combination (R1).
- **Unfair / incomplete baseline comparisons**:
  - Weighted loss is used only for DCNO, while baselines are trained with standard L2 loss. A fair evaluation would require training all baselines with the same weighted loss, as it significantly impacts high-frequency performance (R2 E1, Appendix B.3.1, D).
  - The number of parameters varies widely across methods; dil-ResNets in particular are given far fewer parameters. It is not clear whether other baselines (e.g., dilated ResNets) would outperform DCNO if given a comparable parameter count (R3). Key parameter choices for baselines are not reported, and the effect of underparameterization is suggested by Tables 4 and 7 (R2 E3).
  - Only single training runs are reported; no mean or standard deviation across multiple runs is provided, leaving the evaluation subject to stochastic variation (R3).
- **Central high-frequency claim not supported**:
  - Fig. 1b only compares DCNO to FNO, which is known to perform poorly on high frequencies. When considering other methods (Fig. A.1), DCNO performs only on par (R2 E2).
  - Fig. 1c is misleading because DCNO was trained with the weighted loss focusing on high frequencies, while FNO in Fig. 1d was not. Without the weighted loss, DCNO’s frequency behavior degrades significantly (Fig. C.1), and other baselines (MWT, HANO) even show smaller errors in crucial lower frequencies (R2 E2).
  - Ablation shows that a DCNO variant with no dilations achieves similar performance (Tab. 4), casting doubt on the claimed role of dilated convolutions (R2 Q1). More rigorous frequency-domain evaluations (e.g., on Navier-Stokes cases) are needed (R2 E2, R3).
- **Lack of qualitative evaluation**: For the Darcy case, no qualitative results (e.g., visual comparisons of solutions) are presented; it remains unclear how averaged MSE quantities relate to solution quality (R3).
- **Limited generalization assessment**: All test sets are randomly split from the training data; generalization to new parameter ranges (e.g., different Reynolds numbers) is not evaluated (R2 E4). For Navier-Stokes, data is downsampled from 256×256 to 64×64, which may average out high-Re effects (R2 E5).
- **Missing justification for design choices**:
  - The decoder lacks C layers; no rationale is given (R1).
  - Each convolution layer includes three convolutional neural networks; the reason for this specific choice is not justified (R1).
  - The weighted loss requires manual balancing of frequencies; its problem-specific nature limits generality (R3).
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Wording issues: “convoluted” should be “convolved” (C layers description); unclear sentence in Section 4.2; “allows us to selectively skip” should be “allows for selectively skipping” (R2).
  - Several citations lack publication information (e.g., Cai et al., 2019; Freese et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023) and citation format is inconsistent (R2).
  - Fig. 4.1 and 4.2 should use log scaling on colorbar and axis respectively; subplot formatting is inconsistent (R2).
  - The structure of the appendix could be improved (R2).
- **Minor disagreement in evaluation**: While not a weakness per se, one reviewer (R2) notes that page formatting differs from standard submissions, though this was not considered a major issue.
