## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MMSci, a multimodal, multidisciplinary dataset curated from 131K peer-reviewed, open-access Nature Communications articles spanning 72 scientific disciplines. The dataset includes 742K figures (schematic diagrams, simulations, macroscopic/microscopic photos, experimental visualizations) and supports benchmarks for scientific figure captioning and multiple-choice figure–caption matching tasks. Extensive evaluations of six proprietary and over ten open-source MLLMs show high task difficulty and a significant performance gap, with some open-source models at chance level on multiple-choice tasks but fine-tuning Qwen2-VL-2B achieving GPT-4o-level accuracy; captioning remains challenging. A pre-training case study in materials science demonstrates potential for generating stable, valid material structures. Reviewers generally acknowledge the dataset’s novelty, scale, and quality but raise concerns about benchmark design, evaluation methodology, analysis depth, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
*   **Dataset quality and scale:** The dataset is large-scale (742K figures from 131K articles) and diverse, covering 72 scientific disciplines with high-quality, peer-reviewed source material from Nature Communications (a novel distribution distinct from arXiv preprints), adding significant value to scientific figure understanding research.
*   **Challenging benchmarks:** The benchmarks (figure captioning, multiple-choice matching) are thoughtfully designed to assess models’ abilities in complex scientific content, revealing limitations in current models; the multiple-choice task uses authenticated labels from articles, a realistic setting.
*   **Demonstrated improvement via fine-tuning:** Fine-tuning the LLaVA model (and Qwen2-VL-2B) on the training set yields tangible improvements, achieving performance comparable to high-level proprietary models; the pre-training experiment on materials science strongly demonstrates the dataset’s utility for generating valid and stable material structures.
*   **Extensive experimental evaluation:** Comprehensive experiments evaluate a wide variety of open and closed models with different metrics and settings; the supplementary material includes a well-structured list of benchmark examples, enhancing transparency.
*   **Clear presentation:** The paper is well-structured and written in a clear, accessible manner.

### Weaknesses
*   **Benchmark task design lacks originality and depth:** The entire benchmark consists of figure–caption matching permutations (subfigure-to-subcaption, caption-to-subfigure) – no original question-answering tasks exist. This could bias models toward memorizing associations rather than correctly perceiving image details and retrieving relevant knowledge. The QA tasks focus on differences across figures/captions rather than requiring fine-grained domain knowledge needed to analyze details within a figure (e.g., recognizing irrelevant details like “2.5 mm x 2.5 mm” in Figure 3, caption part b).
*   **Human baseline is flawed:** The authors recruited computer science graduate students to provide human baselines on challenging natural science questions that likely require domain-specific knowledge incompatible with CS training; it is unclear what accuracy a domain-expert human would achieve.
*   **Taxonomy comparison is misleading:** MMSci claims 72 subjects compared to CharXiv’s 8 or SciCap’s 1, but uses a more granular approach (e.g., “Cell Biology” separate from “Microbiology”). For fair comparison, authors should group these into five broader subjects (per Table 6) to align with other dataset definitions.
*   **Captioning evaluation limitations:**
    *   The LLM-based evaluation reports final scores using only 100 randomly selected samples, limiting statistical significance and potentially leading to unreliable conclusions.
    *   Several standard captioning metrics are missing: BLEU, CIDEr, SPICE (common in figure captioning), as well as more advanced LLM-based metrics like LAVE, L3Score, or PrometheusVision.
    *   The fine-tuned model achieves significantly higher ROUGE scores but underperforms its baseline counterpart on FActScore; the paper only briefly explains this as learning semantic/stylistic patterns, missing a discussion of negative impact on factual knowledge retrieval.
*   **Model comparison is incomplete:**
    *   The comparison pits large closed-source models (GPT-4V, Gemini-Pro) against relatively small open-weight models limited to 8B parameters; larger open-source models (e.g., Qwen2-VL-72B) are missing, making it hard to assess true dataset potential, especially with near-random performance of smaller models.
    *   Advanced reasoning models like GPT-o1 or chain-of-thought prompting are not tested, which could provide crucial insights for LMM development on complex scientific reasoning.
*   **Discipline-specific analysis is lacking:** Despite multidisciplinary being a key feature, the experiments lack in-depth analysis of performance variation across disciplines (e.g., why Astronomy and Planetary Science yields high accuracy while Materials Science, with more data, performs worse). Factors driving these differences are unexplored.
*   **MMSCICAP task grounding is questionable:** Providing only the abstract rather than figure-specific sentences or the entire relevant section undermines task grounding; abstracts suffice only for teaser figures, otherwise the missing figure-paired text may disproportionately increase task difficulty.
*   **Materials science case study is hard to interpret:**
    *   The baseline performance of LLAMA-2-7B (without tuning) is not clearly reported; many numbers in Table 5 and Figure 6 appear already high, making it unclear how much improvement there is.
    *   Proprietary model performance on this task is not evaluated.
    *   The “Stable DFT” column numbers for LLAMA models (from Gruver et al.) appear inconsistent with numbers reported in Gruver et al.
*   **Missing experiments and evaluations:**
    *   No evaluation of potential data leakage between training and test sets (e.g., similar images with different sub-charts across samples).
    *   No generalization experiment: authors should evaluate existing VLMs fine-tuned on MMSci on other knowledge-based benchmarks like MMMU to see if the model learns broadly or only fits the Nature Communications distribution.
    *   Beyond material generation tasks, the authors have not explored how the fine-tuned model performs on other scientific tasks (downstream applications).
    *   No verification of top-5 model statements using a different evaluator (L359).
*   **Visualization issues:** Figure 4 is extremely difficult to follow due to overlapping performance curves; a better visualization or table is needed. Figure 3 can be improved to more clearly highlight tasks and ground truth captions.
*   **Dataset curation transparency:** The paper lacks detail on the data annotation process, quality control stages, and how regex extraction was validated to ensure all figures and structures were captured correctly (including handling edge cases/errors).
*   **Figure readability after downscaling:** Given many figures contain small text, the authors should verify readability after downscaling to 336×336 (CLIP ViT-L/14-336) and provide standard error for Figure 6 (MMSci+MMC4 Vis+Text vs Text gap appears small).
*   **Missing citations:** The related work section should cite papers on relevant metrics (LAVE, L3Score, PrometheusVision) and the SciOL and MuLMS-Img dataset.
*   **Minor issues:** Line 98 “figures types” should be “figure types”; inconsistent terminology: “Materials Science” in Figure 4(a) vs “Material Science” in line 438; Figure 3 caption refers to “right” side but caption does not appear on left.
