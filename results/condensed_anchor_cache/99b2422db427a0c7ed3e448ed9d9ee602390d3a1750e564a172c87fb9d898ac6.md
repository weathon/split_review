## Merged Review

### Summary

DrS (Dense reward learning from Stages) learns reusable dense rewards for multi-stage tasks from sparse rewards and optional demonstrations. It uses binary stage indicators to classify trajectories by stage, trains a discriminator per stage to distinguish successful trajectories, and defines the reward as stage index plus discriminator output. Experiments are conducted on three ManiSkill task families (PickNPlace, TurnFaucet, OpenCabinetDoor) with over 1000 task variants. Baselines include hand-engineered dense reward, VICE-RAQ, and ORIL. The paper is clearly written and well-motivated.

### Strengths
*   Novel overall design that exploits stage indicators and the specific form of the learned reward function (reviewer 1).
*   Significantly reduces human engineering effort for tasks with identifiable stages, contributing toward automating reward design (reviewers 1, 2, 3).
*   Outperforms several reasonable baselines (VICE-RAQ, ORIL) and competes with hand-engineered rewards in some cases (reviewer 1).
*   Extensive experiments over three task families with 1000+ variants, plus thorough ablations in the appendix (varying number of training tasks, input modality, dynamics, action space, robot morphology, reward function form) (reviewers 1, 4).
*   The goal of learning reusable dense rewards from training tasks for unseen tasks is important; capturing task-family representations and decomposing tasks into stages is logical (reviewer 3).
*   Paper is clear, easy to read, and well-motivated (reviewers 1, 4).

### Weaknesses
*   **Reliance on demonstrations**: All experiments used demonstrations; it is unclear how sample efficiency would hold without them, especially if early stages cannot be solved by random exploration or a noisy policy from previous stages (reviewer 1). The method also requires success and failure trajectories for each stage, which can be expensive to collect (reviewer 2). A reviewer asked for experiments without demonstrations or with varying numbers (reviewer 1).
*   **Limited generality**: The method heavily depends on well-defined stage structures; it fails with only one stage (ablation Figure 6) and is not applicable to tasks without clear stage boundaries (e.g., locomotion, Mujoco tasks) (reviewers 1, 2, 4). The need for separate discriminators per stage increases computational overhead and memory, limiting scalability to real-world problems (reviewers 1, 2). Experiments are confined to ManiSkill; results on other domains (e.g., Minigrid navigation) would be more convincing (reviewer 4).
*   **Generalization may come from state abstraction**: Experiments use low-dimensional states as reward input, allowing the reward function to ignore point clouds and still generalize. There is no evidence that learned rewards generalize from other modalities such as pixels or high-dimensional point clouds (reviewer 2). Pixel experiments were omitted without clear justification; one reviewer suspects pixel-based rewards would not generalize well (reviewer 2).
*   **Optimality concerns**: Classifying trajectories as success/failure based on the maximal stage reached may not incentivize optimal task completion (e.g., cannot distinguish a direct path from a circuitous one). The single classification per trajectory ignores transitions within a trajectory, potentially misaligning rewards with task requirements (reviewer 4).
*   **Missing comparisons and ablations**: No ablation on robustness to noisy or suboptimal demonstrations (reviewer 3). Lack of discussion on related work using LLM-based rewards or SayCan (reviewers 2, 4). Not shown that DrS enables new skills unachievable by human-engineered rewards (reviewer 2). Insufficient explanation of why GAIL (without stage indicators) cannot learn reusable rewards (reviewer 4). Training time not reported (reviewer 4).
*   **Technical concerns**: In Algorithm 1, each transition’s reward is computed using the discriminator selected based on the trajectory’s stage index, which could assign different rewards to the same next state in different trajectories, potentially causing training instability (reviewer 4). What kind of dense reward does the method actually produce? (reviewer 4). Figure 3 is not referenced in the text and is unclear (reviewer 3).
