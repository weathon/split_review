## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper provides an asymptotic analysis of Generalized Distributed SGD (GD-SGD) under time-varying communication graphs and various sampling strategies (i.i.d., shuffling, Markovian). The authors prove asymptotic consensus and identify the optimal model parameter, then analyze the impact of sampling strategies on the limiting covariance matrix via the Loewner ordering. The key insight is that whereas non-asymptotic analyses of GD-SGD with Markovian sampling depend on the mixing time of the *worst* agent, the asymptotic analysis can benefit from *every* agent's sampling quality (Corollary 3.4). Experiments on CIFAR10 (logistic regression in main text; “convex-like” non-convex in appendix) and on synthetic data support the theoretical results.

### Strengths

- The paper is well-written and the theoretical analysis appears rigorous.
- The work studies an interesting generalized framework (GD-SGD) that unifies numerous prior results as special cases (SGD, D-SGD, variants with different communication patterns and local steps).
- It highlights an important difference between asymptotic and finite-time bounds: the latter can be misleading when comparing client sampling strategies, because they are dominated by the worst-agent mixing time.
- The dependence of the limiting covariance on each agent’s sampling strategy (not just the slowest) is a valuable insight that moves beyond worst-case analyses in the existing literature.
- Theoretical results are aligned with known findings (linear speedup, asymptotic network independence) and extend efficient Markovian sampling results from vanilla SGD to GD-SGD.
- The paper explores the importance of both sampling strategies and communication patterns within a unified asymptotic analysis.

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and technical contribution:** The theoretical results appear to be straightforward extensions of existing works (Morral et al., 2017; Koloskova et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022). Consensus under Assumption 2.5 (connectivity of the expected communication graph) is a well-established result in distributed optimization. The main proof techniques (central limit theorem analysis, handling of consensus error) are largely adapted from the cited works; the expansion to more general communication patterns does not represent a significant leap in proof techniques. The paper does not sufficiently highlight the technical challenges overcome or what is fundamentally new compared to [Li et al. (2022)] and [Hu et al. (2022)].

- **Trivial comparison of sampling strategies:** The analysis of different sampling strategies in Corollary 3.4 is only qualitative, based on existing results [Hu et al. (2022)]. The relationship (e.g., i.i.d. sampling being better than shuffling, shuffling better than Markovian) can be easily generalized from both asymptotic and non-asymptotic prior works and therefore provides no new insight. A rigorous quantitative comparison is missing.

- **Limited practical relevance:** The discussion below Corollary 3.4 is too technical and does not explain practical implications. The paper fails to discuss: which federated learning applications can benefit from non-i.i.d. or Markovian sampling; whether Markovian sampling can be implemented efficiently (additional computational cost); and how to apply it in online settings where data is not stored locally. Without this context, the work comes off as a mechanical combination of two existing techniques (asymptotic analysis and consensus-error analysis).

- **Experiments are not comprehensive:**
  - Only one real-world dataset (CIFAR10) is used. This limits generalizability; experiments on diverse datasets and model architectures (beyond logistic regression and a “convex-like” non-convex model) are needed.
  - The non-convex experiments in the appendix are “convex-like” (e.g., a simple neural network is not tested). More realistic non-convex models (e.g., deep neural networks) should be included.
  - Data heterogeneity is not simulated, making it harder to observe differences between agents—a benefit the paper claims for asymptotic analysis over non-asymptotic.
  - The step-size schedule in experiments uses \(a=0.9\) for the non-growing local steps case, but no justification is given for why this value is chosen; results with different step-size schemes (tuning \(a\) optimally for each instance) could shift the asymptotic regime and change the relative performance of sampling strategies.
  - The experiments only validate the asymptotic regime when local steps are not growing; results with growing local steps should also be examined more carefully.

- **Incremental algorithm design:** The GD-SGD algorithm itself is not novel; many studies have considered similar generalization of communication topologies (e.g., Koloskova et al., Wang et al.). The consistency guarantee under Assumption 2.5 is standard.

- **Asymptotic limitation:** The analysis is entirely asymptotic. It does not provide practical guidance on finite-time convergence rates, which is crucial for real-world applications. It is unclear whether these asymptotic results can be extended to non-asymptotic ones.

- **Missing details:** The introduction could benefit from a separate related work section to better differentiate contributions. The authors do not discuss the technical hurdles specific to extending serial asymptotic analyses to the distributed setting (beyond the inevitable consensus-error handling). No comparison of computational cost or implementation considerations for the proposed sampling strategies is provided.

- **Minority negative remark:** One reviewer (score 3) strongly emphasized that the theoretical contribution is marginal and the experimental evaluation is too narrow; another (score 6) conceded that the work closely builds on existing tools but appreciated the asymptotic viewpoint. The more negative reviewer explicitly called the experiments insufficient for demonstrating the claimed benefits of the asymptotic analysis.
