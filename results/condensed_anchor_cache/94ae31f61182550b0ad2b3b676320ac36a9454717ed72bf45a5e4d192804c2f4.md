## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes SVAk_ADD, a novel method to approximate Shapley values by sampling T coalitions, fitting a k-additive surrogate game (where interaction indices beyond size k are zero), then computing exact Shapley values of the surrogate in polynomial time. Empirical evaluation on feature‑importance tasks (classification/regression) compares MSE with exact Shapley values against baselines under different budget constraints. The method is motivated by the exponential complexity of exact Shapley computation and the need for efficient approximation.

### Strengths
- **Novel approach**: Using a k‑additive surrogate game to approximate Shapley values is novel, simple, and intuitive (Reviewers 1,2,4,6). It makes clever use of existing results (Reviewer 1).
- **Well‑motivated and well‑presented**: The problem is naturally motivated, the background and preliminaries are sufficiently discussed, and the paper is easy to follow (Reviewers 1,2,6).
- **Implementation-friendly**: The algorithm is easy to implement and performs reasonably well (Reviewer 5).
- **Empirical validation**: The method achieves a competitive approximation ratio under various sample budgets; results validate the approach (Reviewer 1). The k‑additive assumption itself seems reasonable for many settings (Reviewer 3).
- **Relevance**: The work is relevant to the XAI community and addresses an important efficiency problem (Reviewers 4,5,6).

### Weaknesses
- **No approximation guarantees**: The paper provides no theoretical bounds on estimation error or convergence (Reviewers 1,4,6). In game‑theory literature, abstractions without such bounds are of limited usefulness (Reviewer 4).
- **Unclear and non‑monotonic performance**:
    - The MSE error curve is not monotonic in the number of samples T; for some datasets the approximation becomes worse with more samples (Figure 3). This counterintuitive behavior is concerning (Reviewers 1,3,5,6). Baseline methods show monotonic improvement, and the proposed method does not (Reviewer 6).
    - The method does not consistently outperform baselines, and its accuracy appears capped by the k‑additivity assumption (Reviewer 3). The lack of consistent advantage and the non‑monotonicity raise doubts about practical significance (Reviewer 1).
    - The paper does not explain the condition differences (e.g., convexity, potential function) that cause the non‑monotonicity, nor does it provide heuristics for early stopping (Reviewer 6).
- **Running‑time and implementation issues**:
    - The running time is stated as “polynomial” but not formally characterised (e.g., dependence on k, n, T). No running‑time comparison with baselines is given (Reviewers 1,2). The claim of polynomial time needs clarification, especially because Algorithm 1 appears to compute p_A for all subsets A, which would be exponential (Reviewers 2,4).
    - The sampling procedure for coalitions is not explained in a way that guarantees polynomial time (Reviewer 2). The optimisation tool and the number of variables/constraints are not specified (Reviewer 2).
- **Arbitrary choice of k and lack of guidance**:
    - The choice k=3 is arbitrary and lacks theoretical justification. No sensitivity analysis with respect to k is provided (Reviewer 3). The computational advantage mainly holds for small k (2 or 3); for higher k (closer to n) the polynomial advantage disappears (Reviewer 6).
    - The paper gives no method to assess how close a given game is to being k‑additive, nor guidance on when the assumption holds or how to test it (Reviewers 3,6). The question “under what conditions does increasing k improve approximation?” is left unanswered (Reviewer 6).
- **Incomplete experimental evaluation**:
    - Only MSE of Shapley values is reported; actual feature‑importance outputs (e.g., bar plots) are not shown, despite the XAI framing (Reviewer 4). The MSE numbers (e.g., up to 20000) are hard to interpret as good or bad (Reviewer 5).
    - The experiments are limited to small models (n=10–13) where exact Shapley values can be computed. Larger experiments with models where exact computation is infeasible are missing (Reviewer 5).
    - The regime where the method outperforms (very small T) is not clearly justified as practically relevant (Reviewer 1). The paper uses varying sample sizes (hundreds to 16k) without explaining why those numbers were chosen (Reviewer 3).
    - No downstream evaluation (e.g., effect on model performance or explanation quality) (Reviewer 1).
    - The method is only tested on feature‑importance tasks; data‑point valuation is not considered (Reviewer 1).
- **Missing comparisons and related‑work coverage**:
    - The difference from highly related work “k‑add SHAP” is not sufficiently explained (Reviewer 6).
    - Other work that also restricts interactions to small sets (e.g., Bordt et al. 2023, Shapley‑Taylor index) is not discussed or compared (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited technical novelty (minority view)**: One reviewer found the contribution limited, noting that the core idea is largely inspired by Pelegrina et al. 2023a and that the algorithm is straightforward once the k‑additive restriction is adopted (Reviewer 5). (Other reviewers considered the approach novel.)
- **Writing and presentation flaws**:
    - Grammar issues appear (e.g., “value” vs. “evaluation” in one sentence; unclear wording) (Reviewer 4).
    - Equation 11 uses “M” without calligraphic notation (Reviewer 4).
    - Plots are too small to read; many should be moved to appendix (Reviewer 2).
- **No theoretical justification for sample size**: The argument for using T = n(n²+5)/3 is neither proven nor empirically studied (Reviewer 5).
