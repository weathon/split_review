## Merged Review

### Summary
FIRMBOUND adapts the Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT) to finite-horizon early classification by estimating the Bayes-optimal backward induction solution from data. The framework uses density ratio estimation (DRE, specifically SPRT-TANDEM with Log-Sum-Exp loss) to estimate the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) and convex function learning (CFL) to estimate the conditional expectation (continuation risk). A faster alternative based on Gaussian process (GP) regression is also proposed, which reduces training time but may compromise statistical consistency. Theory (Theorem 3.2, Appendix J) establishes consistency of FIRMBOUND with CFL. Experiments on i.i.d., non-i.i.d., binary, multiclass, synthetic, and real-world datasets show that FIRMBOUND achieves near-optimal speed-accuracy tradeoffs, advancing the tradeoff boundary and reducing decision-time variance. Four human reviewers gave scores of 6, 6, 6, and 8, indicating general positive reception but with one reviewer (Reviewer 4) being notably more positive.

### Strengths
- SPRT under finite horizon is a practically relevant and technically hard problem.
- FIRMBOUND is well grounded in Bayes optimality via backward induction (Theorem 2.1). The conditional expectation in the backward induction equation is concave, making CFL a natural and consistent estimator (using results from Siahkamari et al., 2022).
- DRE (SPRT-TANDEM) directly estimates density ratios, avoiding individual density estimation; this reduces degrees of freedom and provides stable LLR estimates for both i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. data.
- The minimizer of the Log-Sum-Exp Loss yields a consistent estimator of the log density ratio.
- GP regression offers a computationally faster alternative to CFL, significantly reducing training time while keeping deployment overhead low, though with potential loss of statistical consistency.
- The combination of DRE and CFL yields a consistent and Bayes-optimal method for early classification tasks (Theorem 3.2, Appendix J).
- Experiments are extensive: synthetic data and multiple real-world datasets; results show FIRMBOUND achieves optimalities in Bayes risk and speed-accuracy tradeoff, and reduces decision-time variance. Figures are dense but informative.
- The paper explains ideas in a clear sequence, although some parts are dense.

### Weaknesses
- Paper is very lengthy (60 pages with appendices); reviewer suggests it might be better suited for a journal like JMLR.
- The consistency proof (Theorem J.1) relies on assumptions from Siahkamari et al. (2022) for learning convex functions; these assumptions are not low-level or easy to interpret, and the proof is not highly innovative.
- The procedure is a two-step estimation: first estimate LLR (sufficient statistic), then estimate the backward induction function based on the estimated LLR. Errors in the first step can compound and make the second step harder; Figure 5 indeed shows that estimated LLRs perform worse than true LLRs.
- On real-world datasets, FIRMBOUND does not improve over vanilla SPRT. The authors attribute this to relatively monotonic sufficient statistic trajectories, but this explanation is not entirely convincing. A more rigorous analysis is needed: e.g., what specific characteristics of real datasets (compared to synthetic ones) lead to monotonic trajectories, and why that limits performance gains.
- Some parts of the paper are too dense and hard to parse. Expanding the intuition behind the backward induction equation (Theorem 2.1, Equation 5) would help: specifically, how the conditional expectation of the future minimum risk connects to the stop/continue decision, and how that expectation is used in the recursion.
- The paper would benefit from more detailed empirical comparisons with traditional methods to demonstrate practical performance gains.
- The advantage of DRE over other LLR estimation methods is not assessed or compared.
- The tradeoff between GP regression’s speed and its loss of statistical consistency is underexplored; more discussion on when GP’s speed justifies sacrificing consistency is needed.
- The multi-component framework (DRE + CFL + GP) increases implementation complexity and requires specialized tuning (e.g., ADMM for CFL). Practical obstacles for deployment at scale (e.g., in real-time, low-sample settings) are not discussed.
- There is no theoretical result on convergence rates or minimax lower bounds; these could be indicated as future work.
- It remains unclear whether GP regression can be made consistent, or under what conditions the multi-component framework maintains consistency in small-sample settings typical of real-time applications.
- One reviewer questions whether the main contribution is novel or simply a combination of three existing techniques (backward induction optimality, convex function learning, density ratio estimation).
- Reviewers also note that the following related work could be better discussed: Celisse and Wahl (2020) on discrepancy principle for kernelized spectral filters; Teng and Ertin (2016) on Wald-kernel for sequential detectors; Dai et al. (2019) on unifying GP-UCB with Bayesian optimal stopping (BO-BOS).
