## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a pipeline to extract temporally consistent 6D pose trajectories of manipulated objects from Internet videos, with the aim of transferring these motions to a robotic manipulator. The method does not require prior knowledge of the object’s exact CAD model. It first retrieves a similar CAD model from a large database using vision features (CLIP, DINOv2), then performs per-frame 6D alignment by matching the object image to rendered views of the retrieved model, estimates absolute scale via an LLM (GPT-4, offline for ~2200 objects), and leverages 2D tracking to ensure inter-frame consistency. Evaluations on YCB-V and HOPE-Video datasets show improvements over state-of-the-art RGB 6D pose estimation methods (MegaPose, GigaPose) under the assumption that the CAD model is unknown. The estimated trajectories are retargeted to a 7-axis manipulator in both simulation and real-world settings. Additionally, qualitative results on EPIC-Kitchens egocentric videos are provided.

### Strengths
- The task—predicting 6D pose from Internet videos without object-specific priors—is important and has potential for downstream applications like robot imitation learning and Embodied AI.
- The pipeline is reasonable: retrieve a similar CAD model, perform coarse-to-fine alignment, and use 2D tracking to obtain motion-consistent trajectories. The use of off-the-shelf open-set detectors, foundation models (CLIP, DINOv2), and LLM for scale estimation is effective.
- The approach for detecting and estimating 6D motion of unknown objects from RGB is novel and interesting.
- The paper is well written and easy to follow.
- Quantitative experiments on YCB-V and HOPE-Video demonstrate significant improvements over existing RGB methods (MegaPose, GigaPose) in the unseen-object setting.
- The real robot demo (pouring from one object to another) and simulation results show the usefulness of the extracted trajectories for robotic manipulation.
- The impact is dominant: the work enriches data for robotic manipulation without requiring human labor to construct specific datasets.
- The pose estimation improvement on YCB-V and HOPE-Video and transfer to a 7-axis manipulator are notable contributions.

### Weaknesses
1.  **Comparison with model-based methods using retrieved CAD models.** The paper compares against MegaPose and GigaPose, which assume known CAD models. It is unclear whether the performance gain is due to the pose estimation method itself or the specific way the CAD model is retrieved/used. Missing experiments comparing against these methods when they are given the *same* retrieved CAD model (to isolate the impact of pose estimation). This is a key concern (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).

2.  **Sampling-based rotation estimation.** The method uses a sampling-based trajectory to estimate rotation, which may limit accuracy. The paper does not report the choice of N (number of rendered views), the average/standard angular distance between sampled rotations, or how N affects accuracy vs. computational cost. An ablation study on the number of samples and impact on rotation accuracy is missing (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). In qualitative results, some rotation errors are visible, and it is unclear if they stem from the sampling strategy or the DINO feature extractor (Reviewer 1).

3.  **LLM-based scale estimation and offline procedure.** The LLM (GPT-4) is prompted offline to collect size estimates for ~2200 objects; the main paper reads as if it is prompted online per detected object. The handling of objects not in the offline set is not described, and the impact of inaccurate LLM descriptions on scale estimation is unclear. The reliability of the LLM for providing realistic object scales is questionable (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). An ablation on using different LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5, open-source) is missing (Reviewer 3).

4.  **Retrieval efficiency and scaling.** CAD model retrieval by rendering views and computing visual features (CLIP, DINOv2) appears expensive for large datasets (e.g., Objaverse-LVIS). The retrieval time, time complexity of rendering/feature extraction, and practical implications for scaling to even larger datasets are not reported (Reviewer 2).

5.  **Camera motion assumption.** The method assumes the camera is static relative to the background? The paper does not explicitly evaluate or account for camera motion. In real Internet videos, camera motion is common; the limitations of this assumption are not discussed (Reviewer 2).

6.  **Trajectory optimization dynamics model.** The optimization problem in Equation (4) does not specify the dynamic model used. The choice of dynamic model affects accuracy and stability of the optimized trajectories. It is not described how tracking of the optimized trajectory is implemented (Reviewer 2).

7.  **Metric for retrieved meshes (pCH).** The paper uses the pCH metric but does not formally define it, even in the supplemental. A formal definition is needed to understand its properties and limitations, especially when the retrieved mesh does not exactly match the ground truth mesh (Reviewer 2).

8.  **Real robot experiment details and evaluation.** The real robot experiment is anecdotal and lacks quantitative evaluation (e.g., success rate). It is unclear how the observed object trajectories are transferred to the robot, including handling viewpoint changes and embodiment differences. The specific steps to match the observed object motion to the robot’s action are missing. In Fig. 8 (upper), the bowl object pose is not correctly tracked, yet the robot still acts at a different angle; the cause of the discrepancy and its impact on performance are not explained (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 1). The end-effector translation in Fig. 9 seems inconsistent with the original video and simulation; how scale is handled in retargeting is unclear (Reviewer 1).

9.  **Clarity of original contributions.** The method uses many existing components, and the original contributions (the whole pipeline vs. detailed module designs) are not clearly distinguished. The authors should more clearly state which parts are novel (Reviewer 4).

10. **Running time.** The method is somewhat time-consuming (2s for detection/retrieval/scale per scene, 0.2s for pose per object). Comparisons of running time with other methods and analysis/recommendations for acceleration are missing. For robotic manipulation, speed is a key factor (Reviewer 4).

11. **Methodological novelty.** A reviewer (Reviewer 3) noted that the approach largely applies an existing pipeline to Internet videos, and the primary novelty may be limited (though the reviewer raised score after rebuttal). The use of LLM for scale is a specific concern, and the overall methodology may be better suited to a robotics venue than ICLR.

12. **Robotics-related questions.** Is the robot controlled on full 6D pose or only rotation? (Reviewer 1). How does the robot know where the manipulated objects are and how is this matched to the observed motion? (Reviewer 2). In Fig. 6, the rightmost real robot image appears to be a repetition of the image next to it—was the wrong image included? (Reviewer 2).

13. **Missing evaluations.** An ablation on LLM variations is missing (Reviewer 3). Efficiency/cost for a 1-minute video is not reported (Reviewer 3). No experimentation with non-CAD-based methods (e.g., 6DGS: 6D Pose from a Single Image and 3D Gaussian Splatting) (Reviewer 3). Additional evaluation on a mobile dataset (e.g., from an iPhone sensor) would strengthen generalizability claims (Reviewer 3). In Table 1, were the same scale estimates used for MegaPose/GigaPose as for the proposed method? (Reviewer 2). Are the ground-truth meshes contained in the object datasets? (Reviewer 2). Could the CAD model retrieval be used directly for classification? (Reviewer 4).
