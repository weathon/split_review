## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates root causes of hallucinations in LVLMs, categorizes them beyond object hallucination into Language, Vision, Style, and Information Transfer (IT) types, and proposes Visual Description Grounded Decoding (VDGD), a training-free method that first generates an image description, appends it as a prefix, and during decoding uses KL divergence to the description to select tokens. The paper also introduces the VaLLu benchmark for evaluating cognitive capabilities. Scores range from 6 to 8, with one reviewer notably more positive (8) while three rated 6.

### Strengths
- Extensive, in-depth analysis of hallucination issues across multiple benchmarks, models, and decoding strategies (R1, R2, R4). The analysis yields interesting and solid insights (R2, R4).
- Novel taxonomy of hallucinations (Language, Vision, Style, IT) that goes beyond simple object hallucination, providing a useful framework for the field (R1, R3).
- The problem of cognitive reasoning in hallucination is interesting and under-explored in prior work (R2).
- The paper is well-structured, well-written, and easy to follow (R3, R4).
- VDGD is a simple, training-free method with a clear motivation derived from the preceding analysis (R2, R3).
- The method shows consistent improvements across benchmarks (R2).
- The VaLLu benchmark has potential as a comprehensive evaluation resource for hallucinations (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of per-category analysis for VDGD:** The method’s effectiveness on each of the defined hallucination types (Language, Vision, Style, IT) is not demonstrated. Only overall accuracy gains are reported, making it unclear whether VDGD addresses the root causes of each type or improves via a general effect (R1, R4). This disconnect between the taxonomy and the experimental validation weakens the paper's coherence (R4).
- **Insufficient computational cost analysis:** The latency overhead of generating the intermediate description and the subsequent prefill phase is not adequately characterized. The method requires generating long descriptions even for short responses, making it potentially much slower than simple decoding-based mitigation methods. A breakdown of time per stage (caption generation, grounding, decoding) and comparisons of throughput/latency with baselines are missing (R1, R4). The architecture and computational requirements of the “small captioning model” are not clarified (R4).
- **Limited applicability to complex images:** The method’s assumption that an image can be sufficiently described by text may hold for science/char t data but likely fails for natural scenes with complex object categories, attributes, and relations. The evaluated benchmarks are heavily focused on such science-domain data (R2). One reviewer suspects that performance gains on charts may reflect that text descriptions of data are more suitable for LLM comprehension rather than improved cognitive reasoning (R2).
- **Reliance on first-generated description:** VDGD prefixes the first model-generated description, which may itself contain hallucinations. Unlike contrastive decoding methods that correct logits via a premature model, VDGD uses only vocabulary truncation, raising concerns about hallucination snowballing (R1).
- **Weak empirical support for main claims:** The claim that existing mitigation techniques boost performance on AMBER but fail on cognitive tasks (Sections 3.1/3.2) is not strongly supported: the gains on AMBER are small, and other datasets also show marginal improvements, lacking a clear performance gap (R3). Similarly, the observed decline in rank with longer text prompts (Section 4.1) is confounded by added textual context and the rank difference between datasets is small, which does not conclusively prove lack of image attention (R3). The GPT-based evaluation metric shows only a marginal correlation improvement (0.96 vs. 0.92) over existing benchmarks, insufficient to justify a new protocol (R3).
- **Algorithm and methodology issues in hallucination categorization:** In Section 3.3, the condition “base rank < 0” for language hallucination is problematic (ranks are typically non-negative). The classification prioritizes IT hallucinations first, which biases the distribution and may inflate IT counts at the expense of visual and style categories. The use of Llama-3 for visual content extraction may itself introduce hallucinations (R3).
- **Limited method experiments and ablation:** Only one table of method experiments appears in the main paper; most are relegated to the appendix. More ablation studies are needed (R2, R4). The paper would benefit from a more focused presentation with less emphss on extensive analysis that overlaps with prior work (hallucination categorization based on cause) (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient related work discussion:** The related work section is limited, missing discussion of hallucination, reasoning, benchmarks, and visual CoT methods. The method resembles Visual CoT but is not properly contrasted with relevant works (e.g., Compositional CoT, Visual Table, Visual Evidence Prompting) in terms of performance, computational cost, and applicability (R2, R4).
- **Terminology confusion:** The usage of “perception” and “recognition” may be reversed compared to standard definitions (visual perception as basic observation, recognition as more complex), which could cause confusion (R4, Q1 – noted as not affecting reviewer score).
