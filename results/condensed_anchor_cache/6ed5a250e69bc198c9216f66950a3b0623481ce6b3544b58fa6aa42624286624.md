## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the Snowflake Hypothesis (SnoH), which assigns a unique receptive field per node by pruning the adjacency matrix based on gradient and node-level cosine distance, acting as a node-level early stopping to mitigate oversmoothing in deep GNNs. Reviewers find the idea novel and the empirical evaluation extensive across backbones, datasets, and depths. However, the motivation is judged unclear by multiple reviewers, the performance gains are marginal and inconsistent (especially on ogbn-arxiv), the backbones used are far from state-of-the-art, and the writing has significant stylistic problems. There is disagreement on writing quality (Reviewer 4 calls it "well-written," while Reviewers 1 and 2 criticize clarity) and on effectiveness (Reviewer 2 finds results convincing, Reviewer 3 sees random behavior on ogbn-arxiv).

### Strengths
- Addresses the important problem of oversmoothing in deep GNNs.
- The two SnoH variants (especially SnoHv2) are simple and scale to large graphs (e.g., millions of nodes).
- Extensive empirical analysis: multiple training schemes, shallow/deep backbones (JKNet, ResGCN, PairNorm, etc.), depths 8,16,32,64, six benchmarks including dense graphs.
- Results suggest that SnoH{1,2} improve performance for many deep settings, supporting the idea that a subset of nodes benefit from early stopping.
- Novelty: the concept of node-specific receptive fields via edge pruning is a fresh perspective.
- Compatibility with diverse backbone networks and training strategies encourages broader adoption.
- The method is interpretable and offers a new way to guide depth selection.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation unclear.** Multiple reviewers question why deep GNNs are needed when shallow (2-3 layer) models already achieve strong performance. Reviewer 3 gives the concrete example [Huang et al. 2020] that obtains 73% on ogbn-arxiv with 3 layers, exceeding the paper's best results.
- **Overclaiming.** The claim that SnoH "always improves deep GNN performance" is contradicted by results: in Table 4, SnoHv1 causes performance drops on several datasets. Reviewer 3 also notes that Table 4 performance on ogbn-arxiv appears random.
- **Far from state-of-the-art.** The backbone architectures used (e.g., GCN, ResGCN) are not competitive with current SOTA on ogbn-proteins and ogbn-arxiv. Reviewer 3 requests evaluation on models that have demonstrated strong performance on those datasets.
- **Stylistic issues.** Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2 note the text is plagued by filler sentences ("We have another interesting observation"), hyperlatives ("These astonishing results clearly verify the effectiveness"), and awkward phrasing (e.g., the abstract sentence starting with "Given that the potency..."). The writing should be concise and active. (Reviewer 4 disagrees, calling the paper well-written.)
- **Table 1: best-run reporting insufficient.** Reviewer 1 recommends removing the "red numbers" (best result) and instead reporting means with variances over 5 runs; the current practice of showing only the best run is a random‑seed lottery.
- **Limited to message‑passing backbones.** The method relies on the adjacency matrix for pruning, so it cannot be applied to subgraph-based or multihop networks. No extension is offered (Reviewer 4).
- **Generalizability questioned.** Results in Table 1 show that regular GCN suffers notable performance decline as layers increase, even with SnoH, which undermines the claim of universal applicability (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing analysis of hyperparameter θ.** The paper does not examine how θ affects training across different models, nor does it provide guidelines for setting θ based on graph type (e.g., homophily vs. heterophily) or dataset size (Reviewer 4).
- **No runtime comparison.** Reviewer 2 asks for wall‑clock time or FLOPs against baselines.
- **Cosine distance choice unjustified.** Reviewer 4 questions why cosine distance is used as the stopping criterion, noting it may introduce homogeneity assumptions and harm performance on heterophilic graphs.
- **Missing evaluation on strong baselines.** SnoH is only tested on the paper’s own backbones; Reviewer 3 suggests applying it to existing strong models on ogbn-proteins and ogbn-arxiv to verify generalizability.
