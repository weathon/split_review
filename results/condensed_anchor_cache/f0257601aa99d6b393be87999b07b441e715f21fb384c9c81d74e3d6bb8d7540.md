## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Regularized Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (RegFTRL) for two-player zero-sum games, incorporating adaptive regularization (including entropy regularization as a special case). In normal-form games (NFGs), RegFTRL achieves last-iterate linear convergence to an approximated Nash equilibrium and can adjust to reach exact Nash equilibrium. In extensive-form games (EFGs), entropy-regularized Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) yields last-iterate linear convergence to the quantal response equilibrium without optimism or uniqueness assumptions. The paper also introduces FollowMu, a neural network-based implementation of RegFTRL for model-free learning in sequential environments. Empirical results on small games (Kuhn, Leduc) verify the theoretical properties and show competitive performance.

### Strengths
- One reviewer (R1, rating 8) strongly endorses the paper for its simplicity of proof and seamless extension to EFGs, evaluating the theoretical contribution as excellent.
- The guarantee is for the last iterate, which is favorable in practice (R2, R5).
- The algorithm does not require the Nash equilibrium to be unique (R2, R3, R5) and obviates the need for optimistic updates (R3, R5), which are common in prior work.
- The approach is general, accommodating various regularization functions and providing convergence results for both NFGs and EFGs (R4).
- The paper is fairly well-written and easy to follow (R4), with reasonable presentation and sound results (R5). The numerical simulations demonstrate fast convergence and are likely competitive with state-of-the-art approaches (R3).
- Studying last-iterate convergence via regularization rather than optimism or extra-gradients is valuable, as such methods tend to perform well in practice (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and connection to prior work**: Several reviewers (R1, R2, R4, R5) find the novelty limited. Part of the results have already been proposed through different analyses (R1). Linear convergence in the regularized game follows from known FTRL behavior in strongly monotone games; the idea of adaptive regularization to handle monotone games is not new (R5). Specifically, the approach is very similar to FTRL-SP (Perolat et al., 2021) which provides linear last-iterate convergence in the regularized game and sublinear convergence to exact Nash in monotone games under full-information and noisy feedback (R4). Thus the current results are subsumed: (a) no convergence rates to exact Nash equilibrium; (b) results hold only for two-player zero-sum games, not more general monotone games; (c) results hold only for full-information feedback (R4). Other relevant works (Cai et al., 2022; Hsieh et al., 2021; Dong & Peng, 2024) have proved last-iterate rates for extragradient/OGDA without uniqueness or with bandit feedback, showing regularization alone is enough (R4). The paper’s discussion of these works is inadequate; for example, comparison with magnetic mirror descent is missing (R5). The significance of the last-iterate result is also questioned, as it is just about the algorithm’s output (R2).
- **Presentation and clarity**: The paper requires substantial proofreading; typos include “continue-time” / “continues-time” (should be “continuous-time”) on page 5 (R3). Notation is inconsistent: V^{h,τ} in Section 2.1 vs. V^i in Section 2.3 (R2). The quantity r^h on page 3 is not clearly introduced or named (R3). The preliminaries could be expanded, e.g., an explicit discussion of the FTRL update would be helpful (R3). The proof environment for each theorem is unnecessary if all proofs are in the appendix (R3). The purpose of Section 2.3 is not clearly motivated (R5). One reviewer (R1, Q3) asks why the paper transitions to FollowMu in the experimental section, indicating insufficient justification for that specific algorithm.
- **Theoretical rigor**: In Section 3.3, two approaches are described (decreasing τ and changing reference strategies), but Theorems 2 and 3 assume constant τ and do not directly apply when τ is decreased. The paper merely states that “The speed of convergence will be adversely affected as the weight parameter τ decreases” without providing a specific sequence or convergence rate (R2). For the changing reference strategy (Theorem 4), there is no explicit choice of τ or rate, weakening the analysis (R2). One reviewer (R1, Q1) asks whether the algorithm requires uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium, and (R1, Q2) whether in NFGs the algorithm always converges to an ε-NE—these points indicate insufficient clarity about assumptions and guarantees. Another reviewer (R2, Q1) asks whether the Q function in equation (2) is assumed known while equation (3) uses an actor-critic approximation, revealing ambiguity in the model-free setting.
- **Incorrect or misleading statements**: The claim that “optimistic update approach often necessitates the computation of multiple gradients at each iteration, making it intricate and resource-intensive” is not true for OMWU or OGDA, which require only one gradient computation per iteration (R4). The statement in the context of Figure 1 that optimism “can impede convergence, particularly when the real-time policy exhibits chaotic behavior” is not concretely explained (R5, Q1).
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation**: The experiments on NFGs and EFGs use very small games (Kuhn poker, Leduc hold’em), which can be misleading; larger games should be tested (R5). The plots in Figures 3 and 4 start at iteration 10² and 10³, omitting early performance which is important to note (R3). The EFG experiments (Figure 4) do not compare to state-of-the-art CFR variants such as CFR⁺, DCFR, or PCFR⁺ (R3, Q4). The follow-up question from R4 about extension to monotone games, convergence rates to exact Nash, and noisy/bandit feedback highlights missing analyses and restricted generality.
