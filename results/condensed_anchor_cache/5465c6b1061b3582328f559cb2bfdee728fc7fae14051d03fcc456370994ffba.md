## Merged Review

### Summary

Four reviewers rated the paper 3, 5, 6, and 8 (confidence levels 4, 4, 3, 5). All find the paper well-written and the architecture clean. The core idea – pre-training a GPT-style transformer on actionless video (Ego4D) for video prediction then fine-tuning on robot manipulation data – is considered novel and interesting. Strong CALVIN results (88.9%→94.9% success, zero-shot 53.3%→85.4%) and real-robot validation are seen as positive. However, there is substantial criticism about missing baselines (VIMA-like, diffusion policy, R3M, VIP, MVP, Voltron, V-PTR), limited generalization evidence (simulation tasks lack scene variation, real-world tasks are simple pick-and-place only, zero-shot tested on a single condition), and missing training details. Reviewers disagree in overall assessment: R4 (8) was very positive, emphasizing scaling potential and strong performance over RT-1; R1 (3) and R2 (5) found the evidence insufficient for the core claim; R3 (6) was moderately positive but concerned about novelty claim accuracy and missing related work.

### Strengths

- **Well-written, clearly presented**, with a simple and principled architecture that is easy to implement and understand (all reviewers).
- **Novel idea**: pre-training a GPT-style transformer for video prediction on actionless human video data (Ego4D), then fine-tuning on robot manipulation tasks. This can potentially scale to diverse web videos (R1, R2, R3).
- **Strong quantitative results** on the CALVIN benchmark: improves success rate from 88.9% to 94.9%, and zero-shot unseen scene generalization from 53.3% to 85.4% (R1, R4). R4 particularly notes the improvement over RT-1.
- **Real-robot experiments** validate the simulation findings and add practical credibility (R2, R3, R4).
- **Ablation study** supports key design choices (R3). Informative figures effectively illustrate the method and its benefits (R3).
- **Good literature review** placing the work in context, and honest discussion of weaknesses and failure modes (R3).
- **Straightforward fine-tuning** that closely mirrors the pre-training step (R2).
- **Scaling promise** for larger pretraining datasets and more manipulation tasks (R2, R4). (Note: R1 and R2 were less convinced about the generalization evidence, but the potential is acknowledged.)

### Weaknesses

- **Missing baselines / incomplete comparisons**: The paper does not compare to several relevant prior works. Missing comparisons include VIMA-like encoder-decoder architectures, diffusion policy (Chi et al., Ha et al.), and pretrained visual representations (R3M, VIP, MVP, Voltron, V-PTR). R1 notes that “tons of prior work on video pretraining for robotics … it is impossible to evaluate the strength of GR-1 without comparison.” R3 adds that the claim of being the first GPT-style transformer for this setting is inaccurate given V-PTR. Without these baselines, it is unclear whether the benefit comes from video prediction pretraining or other factors (R1, R2).
- **Limited generalization evidence**: Simulation tasks all occur in a similar table-top environment with little scene variation (R2). Zero-shot generalization is only demonstrated for a single task/condition (R2). The real-world tasks are simple pick-and-place, split into separate pick and place phases (R1). Generalization is tested only on minor distractors and background changes—not on new task objects, articulated objects, pouring, scooping, or other skills present in Ego4D (R1, R2). R4 notes that generalization was not tested for longer horizons (beyond training length) or novel language instructions. Additionally, the zero-shot setup uses an ABC→D fashion (train on three tasks, test on the fourth) without clarifying why a unified model over all held-out tasks was not considered (R2).
- **Insufficient real-robot experiments**: The real-robot tasks are few, simple, and the phased decomposition (separate pick and place) provides inherent supervision. The tested level of generalization does not exploit the richness of the pretraining data (R1, R2).
- **Missing training details**: Hyperparameters for the Ego4D pretraining phase are not provided (R1). It is unclear whether the full Ego4D dataset or a relevant subset (e.g., hands/objects) was used, and the overall training set size is not clearly stated (R1, R3). The control frequency of the real robot is not reported (R1). The choice of Δt=1 during pretraining (predicting the very next frame, which is temporally close given high FPS) is not justified (R1, R3). The differences between simulation settings D vs A, B, C are not explained (R3).
- **Model description confusion**: Section 3.2.2 on masked tokens is unclear, especially the notation separating [OBS] and observation tokens (R3). The decision to predict only one action at a time rather than using a receding horizon (as in diffusion policy) is not explained (R3). The potential multimodality of video frame prediction is not addressed (R3).
- **Missing statistical significance**: No standard errors are reported for any results, making it hard to assess the significance of observed differences (R3).
- **Large performance drop with limited robot data**: When using only 10% of the robot dataset (2k trajectories), the 1-task success rate drops from 94.9% to 77.8%. The paper lacks analysis of the failure modes behind this drop (R4).
- **No ablation on pretraining data quantity**: The effect of using different amounts of Ego4D data for pretraining is not studied. It would be useful to know whether a smaller, more relevant subset is especially beneficial (R4).
- **Forgetting of pretrained knowledge**: The paper does not analyze whether the video prediction capability (or learned representations) degrade after fine-tuning on robot data. A co-training strategy mixing robot and video data might mitigate forgetting, but this is not explored (R2).
- **Open-source status unclear**: A plan for releasing code and checkpoints is not stated, which would help the community build on this work (R4).
- **Future work section too shallow**: The description of future work is limited to “combine more robot data and video data in training,” without deeper insights (R3).
- **Typos and formatting**: Several typos (e.g., “casual” for “causal”, “datset”, “highlight”) and inconsistent reference formatting (e.g., year duplication, capitalization) (R3).

*Reviewer contrast*: R4 (rating 8) was very positive, highlighting strong performance and scaling promise; R1 (rating 3) and R2 (rating 5) found the evidence insufficient, particularly regarding baselines and generalization; R3 (rating 6) was moderately positive but shared concerns about missing comparisons and the novelty claim.
