## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper investigates whether LLMs have an internal representation correlated with successful instruction-following. Using linear probes on token representations, the authors identify a latent dimension that predicts success. They manipulate this dimension via representation engineering (RE) and find it can improve instruction-following success rates without degrading response quality. Further analysis shows the dimension is more sensitive to prompt phrasing than to task difficulty or familiarity. However, the probe does not generalize across held-out instruction types, suggesting the representation is not a universal “instruction-following” direction but rather an encoding of the specific instruction type. Experiments are conducted on four open-source models (7B–13B) using an adapted IFEval dataset.

### Strengths

- **Novel and interesting findings:** The paper offers operationalizable insights into LLM internal representations, particularly the identification of a latent dimension linked to instruction-following and its manipulation to improve compliance. The follow-up analysis correlating the dimension with phrasing rather than task difficulty is especially valuable.
- **Well-structured story:** The paper is clearly motivated, with each section building logically on the previous results, from identification to manipulation to interpretation.
- **Practical usability:** The method can detect potential instruction-following failures before generation (using the first response token) and can steer models toward better compliance, with potential safety and reliability benefits.
- **Robustness across models:** Results are reproduced on multiple models (Llama, Mistral, Phi-3), demonstrating a degree of generality.
- **Transparency:** The authors generally do not overclaim their results and are transparent about limitations.

### Weaknesses

- **Methodology clarity:** The description in Sections 3 and 4 lacks precise mathematical notation. Key objects such as “input representation” (is it computed solely from output tokens?) and “dimension” (hypothetical or an actual hidden vector dimension?) are not clearly defined. The motivation for the update in line 303-304 is unexplained. Providing explicit equations and definitions would improve reproducibility.
- **Terminology confusion:** “Instruction” is used ambiguously, often meaning “constraints,” and is difficult to distinguish from “task.” The term “know internally” in the title is not explicitly defined; after reading, it appears to mean “encoded in a way correlated with correct responses,” but this is not stated. The abstract implies a yes answer, but on page 4 the paper shows the representation does not generalize across instruction types, which should be clarified earlier.
- **Dataset size and generality:** The experiments rely on a single, relatively small dataset (IFEval). This raises concerns about whether findings generalize to more complex, realistic instructions (e.g., a health bot remembering a knee injury). The instructions tested are fairly simple and may not represent general LLM use cases.
- **Limited improvement and comparison:** The performance improvement from RE (Table 4) is modest. A comparison with few-shot prompting is missing (W1). The authors note that some conclusions (e.g., Table 1/Table 2 results) align with the “Lost in the Middle” phenomenon, making them somewhat expected.
- **Generalization of RE direction:** It is unclear whether the RE direction D is universal across all instructions or must be fitted per instruction type. Section 3 states that linear probes do not generalize well across instruction types, implying RE uses different directions per instruction type—this should be clarified. Furthermore, fitting alpha for each model and instruction type greatly limits usability, as it requires a cleanly separated dataset for each task.
- **Missing counterfactual analysis:** The paper does not test the reverse manipulation (flipping the sign of alpha) to intentionally cause instruction-following failures (T2F or F2F). Such a test is necessary to demonstrate reliable usability and support safety claims (e.g., to make a model not comply).
- **Insufficient analysis of direction sufficiency:** Using only one direction may be insufficient to capture instruction-following; other meaningful directions (e.g., “dialog in first-person”) could be entangled. Higher-order approaches (PCA on representations, 2D/3D) should be tested to see if they yield more robust results. An example: in backdoored models, a single PCA direction can identify trigger inputs.
- **Phrasing sensitivity and frontier models:** The paper finds the dimension correlates strongly with prompt phrasing. However, frontier models like Claude Sonnet 3.5 are much more robust to paraphrasing, which undermines the explanatory power of the findings. More work is needed to explain why phrasing sensitivity exists and how it originates during training.
- **GPT-4 scoring calibration:** The method for constructing the quality ratio (GPT-4-based scoring from 0 to 9) is not explained. It is unclear how ratings 4 vs. 5 differ, whether in-context examples were used, and what the accuracy/reliability of the scoring is (true/false positive rates).
- **Uncertainty estimation:** The stated uncertainties in Tables 3 and 4 appear very low (negligible or zero). Bootstrap resampling or other methods should be used to provide more realistic uncertainty estimates.
- **Token position and focus:** The paper focuses on the representation of the first token in the last layer without clear justification. Why not other positions or layers? For the “middle” token analysis (Q1 in R4), it is unclear whether the middle token position varied across prompts or was fixed per sequence.
- **Visual readability:** Figures 1, 2, and 4 use small font sizes for axis labels, legends, and markers, making them hard to read.
- **Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (R2, score 8) was more positive, praising the overall structure and findings. Another reviewer (R4, score 5) was more critical, emphasizing weaknesses in generalization, usability, and the need for stronger evidence (counterfactual, uncertainty estimation). R5 (score 5) also expressed concerns about missing comparisons and the explanatory gap for phrasing sensitivity. These differing perspectives indicate that while the core idea is promising, the evidence and analysis have significant gaps.
