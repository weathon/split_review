## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Nira, a noise-guided robust aggregation method for defending against backdoor attacks in federated learning. Nira constructs a shared noise dataset (Gaussian-based surrogate dataset) distributed to clients, who train on both noise and local data while aligning local representations to the noise via a domain adaptation loss. The server filters malicious models by thresholding their accuracy and feature distance (Eq.6) on the noise dataset. Experiments on three vision datasets demonstrate effectiveness under certain settings. Reviewers disagree: one (score 3, confidence 5) finds the approach flawed and the evidence insufficient; two (scores 6, confidence 3 and 4) find it promising but raise several concerns.

### Strengths

- **Novelty**: The noise-guided defense approach is technically novel and sound (Reviewers 1, 3). It does not require an auxiliary real surrogate dataset (only noise) for filtering (Reviewer 3) and does not depend on a benign-majority assumption (Reviewer 2).
- **Presentation**: The paper is well-written, clear, and easy to follow, with helpful illustrations (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Evaluation depth**: Experiments cover a broad range of factors and baseline methods (Reviewer 1). Results on three vision datasets show good performance (Reviewer 2). Benign accuracy is improved due to noise alignment, unlike many defenses where accuracy degrades (Reviewer 3).
- **Security importance**: Backdoor attacks are an important security issue in federated learning (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses

- **Assumptions about attackers**:  
  - Reviewer 1 (score 3, high confidence) highlights a strong assumption: malicious clients are expected to follow the proposed training protocol (train on both noise and local data with the given objectives). Attackers could instead inject backdoor triggers directly into the shared noise dataset – a scenario not considered.  
  - Reviewer 3 questions the rationale for defending adaptive attacks, noting that attackers might produce an over-parameterized model that simultaneously minimizes alignment loss and backdoor cross-entropy loss (these objectives are not inherently contradictory). The authors suggest two reasons (scale-up operation and poisoned samples in cross-entropy loss), but Reviewer 3 asks the authors to disable the scale-up operation and experimentally verify the second reason.  

- **Metrics, thresholds, and robustness**:  
  - Reviewer 1 argues the claim that existing “metrics can be tainted by malicious models” is not convincingly addressed: Nira’s own metric (distance outlier, Eq.6) and thresholds could also be tainted if malicious clients form a majority. Experimental settings are limited to 12/50 (24%) attackers and 20% poison.  
  - Reviewer 2 notes that threshold selection (accuracy and distance) relies on pre-identified benign clients trained over a few rounds (appendix). This assumption should be disclosed in the main paper and introduces vulnerability if those clients are compromised.  
  - Reviewer 3 asks for clarification on why Nira’s use of malicious models in metric calculation does not cause the same failures it criticizes.  

- **Experimental scope and missing ablations**:  
  - Reviewer 1 (score 3) finds the experiments limited: only one triggered-based backdoor attack, small datasets, and low attack ratios. The ratio of surrogate to real data per client is not given, and its effect on performance is not explored.  
  - Reviewer 2 points out that the claim of being “not majority-based” is not tested with >50% malicious clients (e.g., 60-70%).  
  - Reviewer 3 suggests:  
    1. Inclusion of results with full client participation (no client selection) to show stability.  
    2. Results with only one attacker (attacker number = 1) and no attackers (attacker number = 0) under different poisoning ratios for both FedAvg and Nira, because Table 2 shows an unusually significant impact of poisoning ratio on benign accuracy.  
    3. Clarification on the scale factor in replacement attacks and evaluation of Nira’s effectiveness when the scaling-up operation is disabled (since scaling-up may not be essential for backdoor attacks).  

- **Generalization to other data types**:  
  Reviewer 2 notes the method appears limited to vision data (pixel-based feature distances, noise generation). There is no discussion of how it would adapt to NLP, graph, or other non-image data.  

- **Missing details and clarity**:  
  Reviewer 1 highlights: filtering threshold design is crucial but not explained well (why thresholds vary with time, how to adjust systematically); in Section 3.3, what “features of the model” means and how to determine \(b\) are unclear; definitions of “Acc”, “Atk Rate”, and “Main Acc” in Table 1 are not explicitly given.  

- **Rationale for noise alignment’s effectiveness**:  
  Reviewer 3 finds it unclear why attackers struggle to align data with noise, and suggests a toy experiment: fix benign data for two clients, add poisoned samples to one, perform one epoch of noise alignment, and compare accuracy on noise samples. This would help explain the defense mechanism.
