## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces T-Stitch, a training-free acceleration method for diffusion model sampling. Instead of using a single large model for all denoising steps, T-Stitch uses a smaller, cheaper model for early steps (global structure) and switches to the larger model for later steps (fine details). The key insight is that small and large models trained on the same data distribution learn similar encodings in early timesteps, supported by empirical frequency and latent similarity analysis. The method achieves significant speedup (e.g., 40% of early DiT-XL steps can be replaced by 10× faster DiT-S without quality loss on class-conditional ImageNet) and is compatible with various architectures (DiT, U-Net, Stable Diffusion), samplers, and existing acceleration techniques. It also shows unexpected benefits for improving prompt alignment in stylized SD models. Reviewers generally found the idea simple, novel, and practical, with thorough experiments. However, they also raised concerns about the lack of theoretical grounding, missing limitations analysis, limited maximum speedup (~30–40%), strict requirements on shared latent space, and unclear robustness across different training setups.

### Strengths

- **Simple, effective, and novel idea**: Leveraging model size differences across timesteps is a clean drop-in technique that reuses existing models (rather than creating new ones). Its simplicity enhances reproducibility and potential adoption.
- **Thorough empirical validation**: Extensive experiments across multiple architectures (DiT, UNet, Stable Diffusion), samplers (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver), and settings (unconditional/conditional, class-conditional ImageNet). Clear demonstrations of speedup-quality Pareto frontiers and comparisons against training-based, training-free, and search-based methods.
- **Practical and orthogonal**: Training-free, immediately deployable, and compatible with most existing fast sampling techniques (e.g., distillation, improved ODE solvers). No additional training or modification of existing models needed.
- **Foundational insight with empirical support**: Deep understanding of diffusion model behavior across timesteps, validated by latent space similarity measurements and frequency analysis (Fig. 3). The observation that smaller models can generate good global structures early is empirically sound.
- **Broader impact**: Surprising benefits for prompt alignment in stylized Stable Diffusion models, enabling natural interpolation between style and content. Opens potential for efficient model deployment in resource-constrained environments.
- **Clear motivation and well-written**: Most reviewers found the paper clearly motivated and well-organized (though one reviewer noted Section 3 was chaotic and suggested moving background to main text).

### Weaknesses

- **Missing theoretical grounding and analysis**:
  - No mathematical justification for the 40% switching threshold; the cutoff is empirically determined without theoretical guarantees for quality preservation. Reviewers questioned how this threshold generalizes across different model pairs (e.g., does it depend on the performance gap between small and large models?).
  - Insufficient explanation for *why* noise predictions align across scales in early steps (Fig. 3). What about the \(L^2\)-distance pattern – does it mimic the cosine similarity shown? The magnitude of latent vectors is also important but not analyzed.
  - Lack of analysis on optimal model size ratios and the underlying feature compatibility requirements.
  - No formal bounds or theoretical guarantees for generation quality under trajectory stitching.

- **Limited speedup and weaker comparison to strong baselines**:
  - Even ignoring the cost of running the small model, the maximum speedup is 30–40%. Reviewers argued this is not a groundbreaking improvement compared to methods like progressive distillation, which can achieve much larger speedups without storing multiple models.
  - The paper could benefit from direct comparisons to distillation or multi-step consistency models to contextualize the practical gains.

- **Strict assumptions and limited applicability**:
  - Requires both small and large models to predict score functions in the same latent space (i.e., same noise schedule, same diffusion timestep parameterization, same latent space). This restricts usage to model pairs trained under identical configurations.
  - Unclear robustness to different training setups: e.g., variance-preserving vs. cosine noise schedules, different total diffusion steps (T=100 vs. T=500). Would the method still work if the small model was trained only on a subset of timesteps (e.g., \(t\sim[70,100]\))? Such experiments are missing.
  - Cross-architecture compatibility (e.g., mixing a DiT encoder with a U-Net decoder) is not explored, and no guidelines are given when no suitable small model exists.

- **Missing systematic limitations and failure case analysis**:
  - No dedicated limitations section. Reviewers noted a critical absence of discussion on failure modes, edge cases, and potential risks. What are the primary scenarios where T-Stitch consistently underperforms (e.g., very low guidance scales, specific types of prompts)?
  - For multi-model stitching (3+ models), the decision space becomes complex and may involve search; the paper does not analyze diminishing returns when the number of models grows or when diffusion steps are few. Practical limitations of this extension are under-explored.

- **Implementation and presentation concerns**:
  - Memory overhead from loading multiple models is not addressed (models may need to be kept in VRAM simultaneously or swapped).
  - Pipeline complexity is understated; guidance for deployment in resource-constrained settings is lacking.
  - (Minor) Figure 5 has cropped bottoms.
  - (One reviewer) Section 3 is chaotic, mixing motivation, model design, and connections (e.g., to speculative decoding) in an unfocused way. Background/preliminaries should be in the main text rather than appendix.

- **Unanswered questions that overlap with weaknesses**:
  - How does the method perform when architectural differences between models are more significant? Are there specific compatibility constraints?
  - Why does prompt alignment improve for stylized models? Is this phenomenon generally applicable or limited to certain style adaptations?
  - Could training a small model specifically on later timesteps yield better results? (This suggests a potential improvement that is not explored.)
  - How does the selection of the switching point change if the goal is not a speed-quality Pareto optimum but a strict quality budget? A more rigorous method for threshold selection is needed.
