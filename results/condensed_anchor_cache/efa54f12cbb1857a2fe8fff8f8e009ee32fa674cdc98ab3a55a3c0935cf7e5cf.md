## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Policy Iteration with Turn-over Dropout (PIToD), a method to efficiently estimate the influence of individual experiences in off-policy RL by using dropout masks so that the effect of omitting an experience can be approximated without retraining. The authors evaluate PIToD’s accuracy (self-influence metrics, policy evaluation/improvement ratios) and efficiency compared to leave-one-out (LOO), and apply it to improve agent performance by removing negatively influential experiences. Experiments are conducted on four MuJoCo environments with SAC. One reviewer found the work novel, original, and well-written, and praised its computational advantages over LOO. The other three reviewers, while acknowledging the tool’s potential, gave a score of 3, citing weak theoretical foundations, flawed evaluation, insufficient experimental analysis, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- Focuses on experience replay sampling distribution manipulation, an under-represented direction in RL. [R1]
- The use of Turn-over Dropout (ToD) for sample influence estimation feels natural and justified. [R1]
- Novel approach for estimating the influence of specific experiences using ToD; to our knowledge, this machinery has not been applied to deep RL before. [R2, R3]
- Theoretical demonstration that the complement mask $w_i$ indicates an absence of influence from experience $e_i$. [R2]
- Comparison to the naive leave-one-out (LOO) approach emphasizes the computational significance of PIToD. [R4]
- Diagrams are clear and self-explanatory. [R4]
- Results showcase the efficiency advantages of PIToD very well. [R4]
- The method is simple, novel, and original. [R4]
- The paper is generally well written with a clear narrative and comfortable flow. [R4]
- The authors aimed to evaluate PIToD for a variety of purposes (self-influence, policy improvement, performance improvement). [R3]

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical foundation is weak.** Assumption 1 (Appendix A) is extremely strong and appears to define the property it seeks to prove (the gradient for every non-masked experience is unaffected by the mask, essentially assuming the desired leave-out effect). The authors provide no empirical evidence for this assumption and do not discuss implications if it does not hold. Assumption 2 seems more valid, but Assumption 1 remains unjustified. [R1, R3, R4]
- **Self-influence metrics are flawed.** The metrics in Section 5.1 (Eqns 8, 9) do not convincingly indicate positive/negative influence. $Q_{w_i}$ is never trained on $e_i$, so $L_{pe,i}(Q_{w_i}) - L_{pe,i}(Q_{m_i})$ is expected to be >0 regardless of benefit. Similarly, $\pi_{m_i} = \arg\max L_{pi,i}$, so $L_{pi,i}(\pi_{w_i}) - L_{pi,i}(\pi_{m_i})$ is almost always <0. These metrics only reflect whether learning used $e_i$, not the direction of influence. [R2, R4]
- **Evaluation for performance improvement is flawed.** In Figure 6, $w^*$ is defined as $\arg\max_w L_{ret}(\pi,w)$ (Algorithm 4), so selecting the best policy from an ensemble naturally yields high returns; this does not demonstrate that PIToD identifies negative experiences, but rather that it selects the best-performing drop-out mask. The number of MC rollouts per estimation is not reported, and it is unclear whether this rollout budget is included in the training budget shown in Figure 6. [R2, R3]
- **Insufficient experimental results and analysis in the main paper.** The main paper contains very few experiments. Critical implementation details (architectural choices, Figure 10 showing huge performance differences) and important preliminary findings (Group Mask experiments showing that single-experience influence is negligibly small) are relegated to the appendix. A direct comparison of mean return between SAC and SAC+PIToD should be part of the main paper. The purpose of each appendix experiment is not summarized, making it difficult to assess generalizability. [R2, R3]
- **Missing comparisons to existing methods.** No comparison is made to similar approaches such as gradient clipping, learning rate schedules, or Prioritized Experience Replay (PER). The paper promises better performance but does not compare to any SOTA baselines. [R1, R4]
- **Results are not adequately explained.** Figures 3, 4, and 6 lack analysis: (a) Fig. 3: What does “fit more significantly” mean beyond high dimensions? Why is Hopper’s correct experience ratio for policy improvement much worse than others, and why do Walker/Ant ratios decrease over epochs? (b) Fig. 4: The explanation for increasing TD error ratio is unclear; why does it suggest overfitting to older experiences? Heatmap scales are different across environments, making comparison difficult. (c) Fig. 6: No explanation for performance differences across environments. Bias (Fig. 14) is mainly an issue with Humanoid; specific environment characteristics that contribute to this should be discussed. [R4, R3]
- **Concerns about data removal.** Deleting “negatively influential experiences” is reminiscent of optimistic/hysteresis updates; this may hinder learning, introduce bias, and impede exploration, especially in non-linear environments. Safer alternatives such as gradient clipping or learning rate schedules are not considered. [R1]
- **Buffer management not addressed.** The paper does not consider what happens when the buffer is full. New bad experiences could replace old good ones. Can PIToD be used to choose which experiences to discard? This practical concern needs discussion. [R4]
- **Presentation issues.** Redundant mini-paragraphs and parentheses that map paper structure are distracting. No references for RL, MDP, or the LOO estimator are provided. CQL is cited unnecessarily in the introduction. The claim that “PIToD can accurately and efficiently estimate the influence of experiences” is too broad—Section 5 only estimates self-influence. The sentence in Section 4 (“Thus, some readers may suspect that the parameters dropped out by $m_i$ …”) is confusing: it seems to suggest the dropped parameters are influenced, but they are not. Section 5.2 (estimating LOO time) is misleading and could be replaced with more relevant content. [R3, R4]
- **Lack of characterization of harmful/beneficial experiences.** The paper begins this investigation but it feels like an afterthought. Given the developed tools, identifying which experiences are harmful (with links to concepts like “Ray Interference”) would be of high interest, but only Section G in the appendix and a short paragraph in Section 6 address the title question. [R3]
- **Unanswered questions that correspond to missing experiments, ablations, or comparisons:**
  - How does sample rejection based on gradient relate to the Hessian? [R1]
  - Is this approach fundamentally different from creating a policy ensemble via dropout and selecting the best-performing one? If the truly optimal $w^*$ is selected, does it necessarily mean the experience was negative? [R2]
  - For Section G (Figure 16), why is there very little change when removing adversarial experiences? Can PIToD explicitly identify adversarial experiences rather than showing the after-effect of removal? [R3]
  - Figure 10 shows huge changes with architectural choices; why is an ensemble of 20 MLPs used, and does it interact positively with PIToD? [R3]
  - Could PIToD be used together with PER? [R4]
  - Can ToD iterations be efficiently batched? [R4]
