## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Uni-RLHF, a comprehensive system for Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) that includes a universal multi-feedback annotation platform (Enhanced-RLHF), large-scale crowdsourced feedback datasets, and modular offline RLHF baselines. The platform supports multiple feedback types (comparative, attribute, evaluative, keypoint, visual) and is compatible with various RL environments. The authors collect crowdsourced annotations across 30 tasks (D4RL, Atari, SMARTS), yielding over 15 million steps. They train reward models from three feedback types (comparative, attribute, evaluative) and evaluate offline RL algorithms (IQL, CQL, TD3BC) with both synthetic (ST) and crowdsourced (CS) labels. Results show that CS-based reward models can approach or sometimes exceed oracle rewards. The work aims to provide standardized infrastructure for RLHF research.

### Strengths
- Open-sourced annotation platform and large-scale datasets (15M steps across 30 tasks) with multiple feedback types; these are valuable community resources for benchmarking offline RLHF methods (Reviewer 1, confidence 3; Reviewer 2, confidence 3).
- Extensive evaluation on D4RL, including attribute feedback datasets that support multi-objective learning, an area with fewer available datasets (Reviewer 1).
- Clear and interesting motivation: RLHF is a key strategy for AI reliability, and the ecosystem addresses the lack of standardized platforms for real human feedback (Reviewer 3, confidence 2).
- Crowdsourced labels can approximate oracle-based reward, showing flexibility of RLHF even on control tasks (Reviewer 3).
- Universal feedback format and compatibility with diverse environments facilitate integration and progress in practical RLHF (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- The claim of a "standardized feedback encoding format along with corresponding training methodologies" is overly ambitious: training methodologies are only provided for two of five feedback types, and the comparative interface lacks a preference-strength indicator (e.g., as in Anthropic’s dataset). The extension appears straightforward but is not discussed (Reviewer 1).
- Atari experiments have insufficient detail: it is unclear whether comparative or visual feedback was used (see Appendix G vs. Figure 5d), and the highlighting in Table 8 is unexplained (Reviewer 1).
- No dataset or benchmarking is provided for evaluative feedback; it is unclear if keypoint/visual feedback data on Atari was collected (Reviewer 1). However, the comparative and attribute datasets are already a good contribution (minority positive nuance).
- Star annotations in Table 2 (indicating outperformance of the oracle) are incomplete: missing stars for several entries (e.g., hopper-m, CQL, CS-TFM; antmaze-u-d, IQL, CS-MLP) (Reviewer 1).
- Blue annotations in Table 2 (indicating CS > ST) are significantly wrong: about 10 mislabelings in the IQL group alone (e.g., walker2d-m-r, IQL, CS-MLP should not be blue because the ST-MLP score is higher, 109.8 > 109.4). This undermines the claim that CS labels "tend to slightly outperform" ST labels (Reviewer 1).
- Some CS-TFM results are missing from Table 2, making it hard to assess the claim that "CS-TFM exhibits superior stability and average performance compared to CS-MLP" (Reviewer 1).
- The SMARTS experiment overstates the claim that "crowdsourcing annotations achieve the best experimental performance compared to carefully designed reward functions." Across three tasks, careful rewards outperform crowdsourcing on two of three methods; the average success rate differs by only 0.01 (<1 std) (Reviewer 1).
- Figure 4 shows only one attribute (speed) with changing strength; the torso height attribute strength stays constant (0.1). It would be more informative to vary both to demonstrate multi-objective control (Reviewer 1).
- No human-preference evaluation is performed: the paper collects "humanness" comparisons but never compares policies trained with vs. without that objective. Since RLHF is most valuable when reward is hard to define, such evaluation is essential (Reviewer 1).
- The crowdsourced data likely contains noise, bias, and annotator inconsistency, with no standardized quality control mechanism described (Reviewer 2).
- The offline RL baselines are not optimized for specific tasks; lack of task-specific tuning may limit their usefulness and generalizability (Reviewer 2).
- Reliance on human feedback introduces additional costs and delays, making the system less scalable than synthetic feedback approaches (Reviewer 2).
- The benchmark is limited to control/locomotion tasks; the ecosystem would be more attractive if extended to other modalities (e.g., text) and larger architectures (Reviewer 3).
- Only offline RL methods are evaluated; the de facto online RLHF strategy (PPO) is not ablated (Reviewer 3).
- Different query samplers are not ablated (Reviewer 3).
- Only comparative feedback is ablated in depth; the quality of reward models from other feedback types (attribute, evaluative) is not compared (Reviewer 3).
- No data cleaning procedure or filters are included in the ecosystem, although the authors acknowledge this limitation (Reviewer 3).
- The paper does not clarify the differences from RLHF-Blender, nor is there a discussion of when to use one system over the other (Reviewer 3).
- Hyperparameter selection strategy is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- No strategy for refining dataset quality is described (Reviewer 3).
- There is confusion in the attribute training model (Appendix E.2): Eq. (7) omits the subscript θ for the learned log-preferences, and it is unclear why attribute strengths v^α (in [0,1]) are compared to ζ̂^α before the softmax operation (Reviewer 1).
- The training procedure for the "expert" used for normalization in Table 2 is not described; some methods outperform this expert, which warrants explanation (Reviewer 1).
