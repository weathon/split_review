## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes two new sparsity metrics (Average coefficient norm and Normalized loss) to evaluate the success of sparse coding on language model activations. The metrics are tested on synthetic sparse linear data and then applied to several transformer LMs (BERT, TinyStories). The authors report that LM activations can be accurately modelled as sparse linear combinations of feature vectors, and that first and final layers are the sparsest.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly written and well-structured, with a detailed appendix and rigorous mathematical background (all reviewers).  
- The topic (evaluating the sparsity assumption underlying dictionary learning for interpretability) is timely and important (R3).  
- Two baselines are used (non-zero entries and final loss value) and synthetic validation is included (R2).  
- The finding that first and final layers are the sparsest is interesting (R2).  
- **One reviewer (score 5, R1)** found the metrics to be more rigorous and quantitative than prior work, and praised the thorough analysis across model types and layers. This reviewer also highlighted the demonstration of metric robustness on synthetic activations.

### Weaknesses
1. **Metric validity in realistic settings**  
   - The metrics are not convincingly accurate for underparameterized settings, which is exactly the regime that motivates the superposition hypothesis. In Fig. 7, for embedding size 64 the metrics become highly inaccurate after 32 features, and for size 512 they fall off before 128 features. This suggests the metrics are unreliable when there are more features than parameters (R3).  
   - The synthetic experiments assume uncorrelated features, but in practice many features are likely correlated, creating a large gap between synthetic and real models. This undermines confidence that the natural-language results can be trusted (R3).  
   - The paper focuses primarily on synthetic data; more extensive validation on real-world LMs is needed, and limitations (sensitivity to input variation, generalizability to other LMs) are not discussed (R1).  
   - The “ground truth” definition in the appendix (expected weighted number of active features = a/2) conflicts with the standard interpretation of sparse activity and contradicts the paper’s own criterion that a sparsity metric should be invariant under uniform scaling (scaling the “correct” value by 1/2 violates this). If the ground truth line were adjusted to a, the Average Coefficient metric could appear closer to ground truth than the proposed metrics; this unconventional definition should be explicitly noted in the main text or figure captions, not only in the appendix (R4).

2. **Insufficient empirical support for core claim**  
   - The claim that “language model activations can be accurately modelled by sparse linear combinations of features” is not backed by a downstream experiment: if true, pruning activations should preserve model performance; no such experiment is provided (R2).  
   - Experiments rely on a limited set of datasets (only Wikipedia abstracts) and lack diversity; comparisons are primarily to Gaussian models, limiting generalizability. Broader benchmarks and control comparisons (e.g., other corpora) are needed to make the conclusions meaningful (R4).  
   - The comparison of metrics in Fig. 1 requires eyeballing to judge which is best. This is not accurate; adding a 0.5 factor to the Non-zero entries baseline could change the relative ranking (R2).  
   - The paper does not compare its proposed sparsity metrics to other existing methods for measuring sparsity in language models (R1).

3. **Literature gaps**  
   - The interpretability literature review is almost entirely image-classification work prior to 2020; there is very little reference to existing work on sparsity in language models. An overview of NLP interpretability (e.g., arXiv:1812.08951) is strongly recommended (R3).  
   - The paper assumes that sparse representations are more interpretable, but this has been debated; relevant citations include ACL 2021-short.17 and begab.github.io/interpretability-of-sparse-reps (R3).  
   - Section 4.2 seems to describe the information bottleneck hypothesis (arXiv:1703.00810) but does not cite it; closely related BERT analysis (lena-voita.github.io/posts/emnlp19_evolution.html) is also missing (R3).

4. **Missing explanations**  
   - The justification for why the two new metrics are needed and why they are better than existing ones is inadequate (R4).  
   - The reasons for the sparsest-first-and-last-layers pattern are not explored, and the connection to the information bottleneck hypothesis is unclear (R3).
