## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes MeDe, a framework to learn a reusable meta denoiser for identifying mislabeled data across multiple target domains using meta features (e.g., training loss dynamics and confidence of trained models). Two feature extraction methods are introduced: MeDe-L (losses) and MeDe-C (confidence). A binary classifier is trained on a clean source domain with simulated label noise and applied to target domains with different input spaces and label sets. Empirical evaluations are conducted on several datasets.

### Strengths

- The motivation of a reusable meta denoiser that transfers across datasets is interesting and makes sense (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The solution is described as unique and intriguing, with clear motivation and evaluation (R2).
- The paper proposes two concrete methods for extracting meta features (MeDe-L and MeDe-C) (R4).
- The overall framework is simple and easy to follow (R4).
- Theoretical analysis (Rademacher complexity) attempts to show conditions under which source and target domain labels and patterns need to be related for the solution to work (R1, R2). R2 found the theoretical analysis demonstrates how domains need to be related.
- Empirical analysis shows good performance on domain transferability and generalizability of the meta features (R2).
- The setting (LNL with multiple target domains) is interesting and the use of meta-learning is reasonable, potentially inspiring related studies (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient experimentation**:
  - Several recent state-of-the-art baselines are missing: AUM (Pleiss et al. 2020), Confident learning (Northcutt et al. 2021), SimiFeat (Zhu et al. 2022) (R1). UniCon, though closest to the proposed solution and best performing in Table 2, is not mentioned in Table 3 (R2).
  - Limited datasets: experiments are conducted on few datasets; widely-used noisy learning benchmarks such as Clothing1M (or Clothing100K) and NoisyCXR are omitted (R1). More challenging cross-domain transfers (e.g., CIFAR to NoisyCXR or Clothing100K) would be informative (R1).
  - All results are reported over a single run; multiple runs with standard deviation are not provided, so statistical significance of differences cannot be assessed (R1, R2, R4). In Tables 1 and 2, several comparisons show very close accuracy scores; the difference may not be statistically significant (R2, R4).
  - The paper tests only one base classification architecture and one optimization method (SGD); other architectures and optimizers (Adam, AdamW) should be explored (R1 Q4).
  - Synthetic noise generation is limited: only symmetric and asymmetric flipping; class-dependent noise and other realistic noise types are not considered (R1). The noise rate used (40%) is considered unrealistically high for real-world datasets (R1).
- **Premise and transferability not convincingly validated**:
  - It is not clear that training loss dynamics and confidence are indeed shared across datasets; more experiments on diverse datasets are needed to demonstrate this (R1). The method lacks explicit domain adaptation mechanisms and makes no assumptions about label noise in the target domain (R3). It also seems questionable to extract features from two independent neural networks but apply only one denoiser (R3).
  - The effectiveness relies on the existence of learnable connections between source and target data and the ability to artificially generate similar noise on the source domain, but these aspects are not addressed (R3).
- **Missing algorithm details and ablations**:
  - The threshold τ (top T instances selected from target probability list) is not specified; an ablation study on this threshold and its dependence on binary classifier performance is missing (R2).
  - The choice of binary classifier among multiple tested (SVM, neural network, etc.) is not disclosed; results in Tables 2, 3, 4 do not state which classifier was used, nor its performance on the source domain (R2).
  - It is unclear how corrupted source data are generated in the proposed method (R1, R3). The meta-labels (whether a class is noisy) require additional labeling effort and are assumed noise-free; the impact of noisy meta-labels is not discussed (R2).
  - Necessity of both MeDe-L and MeDe-C: their performance is very close in most cases, so why propose both? (R4).
- **Clarity and presentation issues**:
  - The contributions are not clearly stated in the Introduction (R4). The paper is vague in Section 3, providing only a rough framework without discussing algorithm details or potential issues (R3).
- **Theoretical analysis**:
  - The theorem (derived from Rademacher complexity) does not provide new insight; it is directly derived from classical generalization analysis and is not clearly related to the noisy label problem studied (R1, R3). How it helps understand the proposed algorithm is unclear (R3).
- **Missing baseline comparison**:
  - How would the method compare with using foundation models to detect label errors (e.g., Chong et al. 2022)? This is not discussed (R1 Q6). Performance of models without label cleaning is not reported, even though it is a natural baseline (R1 Q3).
  - The paper assumes the source dataset is clean; if not, the approach may suffer (R1 Q1).
- **Minor**:
  - The difference between the proposed methods and baselines on real-world datasets (Table 3) is very small and likely not statistically significant (R4).
