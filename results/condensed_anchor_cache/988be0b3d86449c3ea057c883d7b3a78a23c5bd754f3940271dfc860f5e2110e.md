## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces PRIVQA, a multimodal benchmark (augmenting five existing datasets) designed to evaluate the privacy/utility trade-off when language and vision models are instructed to protect specific categories of personal information. Models are tested on textual and visual QA tasks where they should abstain from answering sensitive questions but answer utility-related ones. The paper evaluates state-of-the-art models (GPT-4, Llama-2) and finds they leak private information even when instructed otherwise. A proposed mitigation, self-moderation (iteratively giving the model its own response and asking it to improve), significantly improves privacy. However, red-teaming experiments using simple jailbreaking methods (textual and image inputs) show that protections can be easily circumvented. One reviewer (score 3) found the technical novelty limited, while others (scores 5, 6, 6) considered the benchmark valuable but had concerns about threat modeling and reproducibility.

### Strengths
1. **Interest and importance of the problem**: The paper tackles the critical and timely issue of privacy protection in LLMs, especially as prompting and instruction tuning become ubiquitous (Reviewer 2).  
2. **Valuable benchmark**: PRIVQA is a novel, open benchmark for evaluating access control in language and vision models, an understudied area with few existing datasets (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4).  
3. **Multimodal focus**: The inclusion of visual tasks and multimodal models (e.g., GPT-4V) is a strength, as few existing works focus on these models (Reviewer 2).  
4. **Use of state-of-the-art models**: Evaluation on GPT-4 and Llama-2 makes results relevant and convincing (Reviewer 3).  
5. **Self-moderation insights**: The self-moderation method improves instruction compliance, and the study reveals persistent biases in protection across different groups (Reviewer 1).  
6. **Red-teaming demonstration**: Detailed red-teaming experiments highlight that even advanced models are easily bypassed by adversarial methods (Reviewer 1).  
7. **Well-defined research question and extensive case studies**: The paper clearly formulates the privacy/utility trade-off and provides illustrative examples (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Limited technical novelty**: The paper primarily tests whether conventional instruction-tuned LLMs can follow privacy instructions. The self-moderation approach is a minor modification of previous “reflection” techniques (a survey [1] is cited). No new red-teaming method is proposed; only existing methods are applied (Reviewer 1).  
2. **Misleading title**: The title “Can Language Models be Instructed to Protect Personal Information?” implies instruction tuning, but the paper only evaluates existing instruction-tuned models; a more accurate title would be “Can Instruction-tuned Language Models Protect Personal Information?” (Reviewer 1).  
3. **Unclear threat model and positioning**: The privacy definition and the real-world use case are not clearly stated. It is ambiguous whether the paper targets training data protection (e.g., extraction attacks) or inference data protection. Protecting training data via instructions is unrealistic (existing extraction attacks like Carlini et al. require many repetitions). The visual part may target inference data, but the scenario is not justified. The paper’s relation to membership inference, extraction attacks, and differential privacy is not discussed (Reviewer 2).  
4. **Dataset construction issues**: The dataset is not curated in a principled way; it is described as too artificial, with attributes artificially inserted and no levels of protection or severity (Reviewer 2). Also, the benchmark could include more sensitive attributes (race, occupation, address, bank account) (Reviewer 4, Q3).  
5. **Lack of code and data sharing**: The paper does not provide code or data for reproduction, even though anonymous sharing is possible (e.g., anonymous.4open.science) (Reviewer 3).  
6. **Inadequate privacy metrics**: The evaluation metrics do not capture worst-case privacy breaches; even a single leak is unacceptable. Metrics should reflect this (e.g., DP with small δ, or TPR at very low FPR). Self-moderation shows trends but no method provides adequate protection for realistic settings (Reviewer 3).  
7. **Unclear connection between Section 4 (privacy experiments) and Section 5 (red-teaming)**: Both sections involve red-teaming as defined in the paper; the separation and redefinition are confusing (Reviewer 1).  
8. **Need for more baselines**: Comparisons with existing privacy protection techniques (e.g., machine unlearning, differential privacy) are missing. The proposed self-moderation is not convincingly shown to be superior (Reviewer 4, Q2; also Reviewer 1).  
9. **Technical depth and convincingness**: The experimental results need stronger validation. For instance, the claim that larger models have more privacy violations (Figure 2) requires explanation, as one would expect larger models to follow instructions better (Reviewer 3).  
10. **Missing ablation and clarifications**:  
   - In Section 3.1, is \(\mathbb{P}\mathbb{G} \cup \mathbb{C}\mathbb{G} = \mathcal{X}\)? Clarify (Reviewer 3).  
   - What are “head entities” in the last paragraph of page 7? (Reviewer 3).  
   - Would GPT-4 also disclose location for unfamiliar landmarks, especially given geotagged metadata? This would strengthen the claim (Reviewer 4, Q1).  
   - Would any of the presented methods be adequate for a realistic setting? (Reviewer 3).  
11. **Scope alignment**: One reviewer questioned whether the benchmark aligns with the ICLR community scope, suggesting the NeurIPS dataset track might be more appropriate (Reviewer 4, Q4).
