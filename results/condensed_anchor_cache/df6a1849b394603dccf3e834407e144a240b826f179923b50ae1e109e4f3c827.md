## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes SHARP, a predict-and-search framework for solving Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) problems. It introduces SKEGAT, a graph neural network that integrates both node and edge features via Sinkhorn-normalized edge attention, and an adaptive Regret-Greedy search algorithm for variable assignment. Experiments on combinatorial auction and item placement problems show improvements over Gurobi, SCIP, and one ML-based method (PaS) in primal gap, survival rate, and primal integral. The four reviewers are split: two rate the paper 3 (reject) and two rate it 5 (accept), disagreeing on novelty, comparative scope, and evaluation completeness.

### Strengths
- **Use of edge features in MILP**: The paper addresses the underutilization of edge information in graph-based MILP representations, an often-overlooked aspect, by proposing SKEGAT (Reviewers 1, 3, 4; Reviewer 2 disputes fundamental novelty).
- **Sinkhorn normalization for EGAT**: Adapting Sinkhorn’s doubly stochastic normalization to edge-enhanced attention is a novel integration that improves training stability and expressiveness (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Adaptive Regret-Greedy search**: The confidence‑threshold‑based Regret‑Greedy algorithm effectively reduces problem size and enhances solution feasibility, addressing scalability and hand‑tuned search barriers (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Empirical improvements**: Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that SHARP outperforms state‑of‑the‑art solvers (Gurobi, SCIP) and a prior ML‑based method (PaS) on multiple benchmarks, with gains in solution accuracy and efficiency (Reviewers 2, 3, 4; Reviewer 1 notes improvements but questions attribution).
- **Clarity and organization**: The paper is well‑structured, clearly written, and explains complex concepts (e.g., Sinkhorn normalization, graph attention) in a mostly accessible manner (Reviewers 1, 3, 4; Reviewer 4 suggests additional visual aids).
- **Practical significance**: The framework is applicable to real‑world combinatorial problems and demonstrates potential for industrial use (Reviewers 3, 4).

**Reviewer disagreement on novelty**: Two reviewers (scoring 3) consider the contributions incremental—SKEGAT is nearly identical to Light‑MILPopt’s EGAT with doubly stochastic normalization, and the search method largely derives from [2]—while the other two (scoring 5) view the integration of edge features and Sinkhorn normalization as novel and significant.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient evaluation of prediction quality**: The reported metrics (primal gap, survival rate, primal integral) do not isolate the contribution of SKEGAT’s predictions from the search module. An “accuracy” metric for node/edge predictions should be added to demonstrate that SKEGAT yields better variable‑value predictions (Reviewer 1).  
   *Reviewers 3 and 4 also note lack of concrete runtime and memory comparisons (only O‑notation) and absence of training/inference time measurements (Reviewers 3, 4).*

2. **Limited comparison with prior and alternative methods**:  
   - Light‑MILPopt (the closest prior work) is not included as a baseline, nor are the essential distinctions of SHARP thoroughly discussed (Reviewer 2).  
   - Only Gurobi, SCIP, and PaS are compared; more recent techniques such as reinforcement‑learning‑based solvers or hybrid deep‑learning‑heuristic models are missing (Reviewer 3).  
   - Ablation comparisons restricted to GAT and GCN; other strong GNN architectures (Graph Isomorphism Networks, Graph Transformers) are omitted (Reviewer 4).

3. **Novelty concerns**:  
   - The proposed Sinkhorn‑normalized EGAT is fundamentally the same as Light‑MILPopt’s approach—both use EGAT with doubly stochastic normalization (Reviewer 2).  
   - The constraint modification ($\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_0} x + \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_1} (1-x) \leq \lambda|\mathcal{X}_0\cup\mathcal{X}_1|$) is a straightforward normalization with insufficient justification (Reviewer 1).  
   - The core contributions (edge‑feature use and post‑hoc processing) are already addressed in prior work; this paper appears to combine existing solutions (Reviewer 2).

4. **Narrow problem scope**: Experiments are limited to two problem families (Combinatorial Auctions, Item Placement) and a few sizes/complexities. Generalization to other MILP classes (e.g., Maximum Independent Set, cut selection) is not tested (Reviewer 3; Reviewer 4 notes lack of non‑bipartite graph structures).

5. **Missing hyperparameter sensitivity analysis**: Hyperparameters $\beta$ (confidence threshold) and $\lambda$ (regret coefficient) are used with fixed settings; no sensitivity analysis or guidelines for tuning are provided. Reviewer 1 also asks how hyperparameter tuning is conducted in detail, as it significantly affects performance (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).

6. **Computational overhead and scalability not adequately addressed**: The added complexity of SKEGAT’s attention mechanisms and Sinkhorn normalization is not quantified; training time, inference time, and memory footprint versus baseline methods are missing. Scalability to large‑scale industrial datasets (beyond the benchmarks) and the practical trade‑offs are unclear (Reviewers 3, 4).  
   *Reviewer 4 specifically notes that the paper only gives O‑notation complexity without concrete measurements.*

7. **Writing and technical inaccuracies**:  
   - **Algorithm 1**: Matrix multiplication dimensions in lines 3–4 do not match (Reviewer 1).  
   - **Missing definitions**: Dimension of $\hat{\textbf{E}}$ in Section 2.3, properties of $\textbf{E}$ in line 144, $W^l$ in line 151, $a$ in Eq. (4) are unspecified (Reviewer 1).  
   - **Equation errors**: Sinkhorn($\alpha^l$) should be Sinkhorn($\hat{\alpha}^l$) in Eq. (5); $x^*$ should be $x\^*$ in lines 310/312, and its first use should be defined (Reviewer 1).  
   - **Inconsistency**: $W^l$ is explained in line 151 but does not appear in Eq. (3); $a^T$ in Eq. (4) is not defined (Reviewer 2).  
   - **Algorithm placement**: Algorithm 1 appears to be an existing method; it should be in the preliminaries, not the methodology section (Reviewer 2).  
   - **Loss function**: The loss in line 268 only works for pure binary variables; additional explanation for continuous variables is needed (Reviewer 1).

8. **Lack of robustness analysis**: The framework’s stability under varying problem sizes, constraint tightness, or noisy input data is not evaluated (Reviewer 4).

9. **Intuitive explanations missing**: Key concepts (Sinkhorn normalization, regret‑greedy search) are presented in a technically dense way without intuitive examples, reducing accessibility for non‑expert readers (Reviewer 4; overlaps with missing details noted by Reviewer 1).

10. **Generalizability limitations**: The bipartite graph assumption limits applicability to non‑bipartite or more complex MILP structures; no discussion on extending the framework to such cases (Reviewer 4).
