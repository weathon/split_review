## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper introduces ClinicalBench, a benchmark comparing 14 general-purpose LLMs, 8 medical LLMs, and 11 traditional ML models (e.g., XGBoost, Logistic Regression, SVM) on three clinical prediction tasks (Length-of-Stay prediction – three-class, Mortality prediction – binary, Readmission prediction – binary) using the MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV databases. Code-based data is converted to text to help LLMs understand semantics. The central finding is that both general-purpose and medical LLMs, across varying scales, prompting strategies (direct, Chain-of-Thought, Self-Reflection, Role-Playing, In-Context Learning), and supervised fine-tuning, fail to outperform traditional ML models by a large margin. For example, traditional ML models achieve Macro F1 scores of 65–70% on Length-of-Stay prediction while LLMs struggle to exceed 30%; similar gaps exist for mortality and readmission. The paper calls for caution in adopting LLMs for clinical prediction. 

There is substantial disagreement among reviewers: two (scores 3, 3) find the work has low novelty and the results predictable, questioning the scientific value; two (scores 8, 8) find the evaluation thorough and the contributions valuable for clarifying LLM limitations in this domain.

### Strengths

- Thorough experiments across many models (14 general-purpose LLMs, 8 medical LLMs, 11 traditional ML models) and three tasks on two datasets (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The three ways of enabling LLMs for prediction (direct prompting, engineered prompting including CoT/Self-Reflection/Role-Playing/ICL, and supervised fine-tuning) reflect the primary application paradigms, making the insights broadly applicable (Reviewer 3).
- Clear writing and organization with informative figures (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Addresses a key problem in the LLM era by systematically comparing LLMs with traditional ML on real clinical prediction tasks (Reviewer 2).
- Valuable insights from the head-to-head comparison, including that specialized medical LLMs do not always outperform general-purpose ones and that only fine-tuning significantly improves LLM performance (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses

- **Predictable results and limited novelty**: Two reviewers (scores 3) argue that the paper essentially confirms the expectation that specialized ML models trained directly on structured data outperform general language models on specific prediction tasks; one states it is “unclear what meaningful insights this work provides beyond demonstrating that LLMs are currently inappropriate for clinical prediction tasks” (Reviewer 1). The other notes that the findings are “straightforward and predictable” and that simple ML models (e.g., logistic regression) are already widely used for their interpretability (Reviewer 2). In contrast, the two positive reviewers (scores 8) consider the evaluation valuable and not obvious; this disagreement is preserved.
- **No new datasets or clinical tasks**: The benchmark does not introduce new datasets or novel processing/tasks from MIMIC-III/IV; its contribution is primarily the evaluation framework (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 adds that the work does not predict the future practical use of LLMs; findings may change with more advanced models.
- **Larger models not tested for engineered prompting**: Only smaller LLMs (7B–9B parameters) are studied under engineered prompting (CoT, ICL, etc.); larger models (34B, 70B) with better instruction-following capabilities are omitted. Concluding that “The effectiveness of typical prompting engineering techniques is generally limited” may be premature without testing larger models (Reviewers 2, 3). 
- **Missing variance and sensitivity analysis**: No variance is reported in the benchmarking tables, even though LLM outputs can be sensitive to input prompts, temperature settings, and the order of input instructions (Reviewer 2).
- **Fine-tuning details unclear**: The main paper does not clarify whether fine-tuning adds a classification head or uses next-token prediction on target outputs. The appendix suggests the latter, but this should be stated explicitly in Section 5 (Reviewer 3).
- **In-Context Learning example selection**: The prompt uses 3 examples; it is not stated whether they are chosen randomly or to be representative. An analysis of how the number and diversity of ICL examples (e.g., different disease types, age groups) affect LLM performance would be helpful, as LLMs can be sensitive to these choices (Reviewer 3).
- **Insufficient discussion of related LLM-for-prediction works**: The paper could more deeply discuss prior work using LLMs for clinical prediction and how this benchmark differs (Reviewer 4).
- **Reliance on MIMIC and potential data leakage**: The current underperformance of LLMs may partly reflect the limitation of training strategies that benefit from data leakage; this limitation is noted (Reviewer 4).
- **Magnitude of performance gap**: Reviewer 1 observes that the huge gap (e.g., 65–70% Macro F1 for traditional models vs. <30% for LLMs on LoS) suggests the comparison may be unnecessary, although positive reviewers disagree and view the gap as a key result.
