## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper analyzes in-context learning (ICL) in reinforcement learning (RL) using a GPT-2 transformer trained to autoregressively predict the next action. It introduces an Omniglot-based benchmark to provide high task diversity and studies how the number of pretraining tasks, model capacity, regularization, and augmentation affect ICL abilities. A theoretical regret analysis decomposes pretraining data into two task-coverage classes and bounds performance. The reviews are strongly split: one reviewer rated the paper highly (8) and found it valuable, while two reviewers rated it poorly (3) citing major conceptual and experimental issues, and one reviewer was mixed (5) acknowledging merits but raising several concerns.

### Strengths
- The problem of in-context reinforcement learning is important and the motivation is clear (Reviewer 2).
- Theoretical contribution: decomposition of pretraining data into two task coverage classes with performance bounds on regret (Reviewers 3, 4).
- First exploration of scaling in-context *reinforcement* learning models; valuable engineering contribution (Reviewer 3).
- Analysis of how the number of tasks in the pretraining dataset and model capacity impact ICL abilities provides useful insights (Reviewer 1).
- Ablation studies on task diversity, regularization, and augmentation are comprehensive (Reviewer 2).
- Introduction of a new Omniglot-based benchmark aimed at offering high task diversity (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Paper is generally well-written and the theoretical part is clearly explained (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Goal‑conditioned setup bypasses core ICRL challenge.** During evaluation the transformer is conditioned on the task description / final goal state (line 328), unlike ICRL methods such as Algorithm Distillation [1] and Decision‑Pretrained Transformer [2] which do not precondition on the task. Conditioning on the final state leaves no uncertainty to adapt to, turning the problem into goal‑conditioned RL rather than true ICRL or meta‑RL (Reviewer 2).
- **Primary metric (L2 loss) is inconclusive.** The main evaluation uses L2 loss on pixel space, which is not directly indicative of task success or policy quality. A small L2 loss can correspond to a visually similar but semantically incorrect trajectory. When “Reward” (return) is reported, it is still hard to estimate success without a clear threshold. Standard ICRL practice uses mean success rate (e.g., AD, DPT) or nth‑percentile success rate (e.g., XLand). The lack of a success metric for the Omniglot benchmark makes comparison with existing approaches difficult (Reviewer 2).
- **Claim of “unprecedented task diversity” is not substantiated.** Existing benchmarks like XLand (and its open‑source analogue XLand‑Minigrid) offer a tree‑like structure of tasks that may provide comparable or greater diversity. No rigorous comparison with such benchmarks is provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Interpretation about Bayesian inference transition is not well‑supported.** The argument in Appendix C that increasing task diversity causes the transformer to move away from Bayesian inference toward in‑context reasoning is unconvincing. The observed behavior can be explained by overfitting or memorization; the evidence does not demonstrate a fundamental reasoning shift (Reviewer 2).
- **Incorrect claim about Meta‑RL adaptation speed.** The statement on line 85 that Meta‑RL methods adapt more slowly than ICRL is contradicted by existing results: VariBAD [7] requires fewer episodes than AD [1] to converge in the Dark Room environment (Reviewer 2).
- **Disconnect between theoretical framework (Section 3) and experiments (Section 4).** The action predictors \(M_{\theta}^{E\text{-}PS}\) and \(M_{\theta}^{F\text{-}PS}\) introduced in §3.1.2 and their regret analysis in §3.2 are never connected to the empirical work in §4. There is no mention of which predictor is implemented or how the theoretical findings inform the experiments. An algorithm sketch and a clear link would help (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Omniglot benchmark description is hard to follow.** The environment in §4.1 is difficult to understand without a visualization. A figure showing 3–5 example trajectories (states, actions, rewards) independent of the transformer approach is needed (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Insufficient baselines.** The only comparison is to MAML, which is not a memory‑based meta‑RL method (MAML sees only the last five strokes, while the transformer sees two episodes). Missing comparisons with state‑of‑the‑art ICRL methods (AD, DPT) and meta‑RL methods (Amago [8]) limit the evaluation. The main lessons (higher diversity, larger models, more regularization help) are not surprising without demonstrating that such factors affect baselines differently (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Missing ablation on sequence length.** The impact of context sequence length on ICL abilities is not studied. Reviewer 1 requested this ablation, and Reviewer 3 noted that related work (AD) reports benefits from larger sequence lengths; comparing tradeoffs between sequence size and task diversity would be interesting (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Main plots (Fig. 3, Fig. 5) lack multi‑seed averaging.** Results are not averaged across seeds, making it impossible to assess the stability of in‑context models (Reviewer 3).
- **Hyperparameter details incomplete.** A single table of training hyperparameters for the best‑performing models is not provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Accessibility issue.** Reviewer 3 noted colorblind‑unfriendly plot colors and requested a style change in revision.
- **Minor missing details.** The specific sequence used as input to the transformer is not clearly specified (Reviewer 1). Assumptions about latent task identity in §3 require justification, and it is unclear whether out‑of‑distribution tasks are considered in §3.1.1 (Reviewer 4). Definitions of \(\mathbfcal{T}\) and \(\hat{g}(\cdot|\cdot)\) in Eq. 4 are ambiguous (Reviewer 4).
