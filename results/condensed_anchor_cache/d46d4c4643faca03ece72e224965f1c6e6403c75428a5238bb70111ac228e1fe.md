## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies learning the degree-2 parity (XOR) function on the Boolean hypercube with a two-layer ReLU neural network trained by standard minibatch SGD on the logistic loss. Both layers are simultaneously updated. The authors prove that with \(\tilde{O}(d)\) samples the network achieves \(o(1)\) population error, which is near-optimal among rotationally invariant algorithms. The proof identifies two phases: a signal-finding phase where many neurons independently align with the XOR features, and a signal-heavy phase where those features are amplified and balanced by second-layer growth. The analysis carefully tracks the evolution of the weights and exploits the symmetries of the problem.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important problem: learning a target that depends on two relevant directions (multi-index) with two-layer neural networks in the feature-learning regime.
- Joint training of both layers is analyzed, which is more realistic than the layerwise training common in prior multi-index learning analyses.
- No unnatural modifications are made to the algorithm (no projections, no layerwise schedules, no random biases); the algorithm is standard vanilla online minibatch SGD.
- The paper includes a thorough literature review and clearly states limitations and future work.
- An interesting observation (Remark 4.3) suggests that joint training may have a regularizing effect that enables feature learning.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a proof sketch that conveys the main ideas.
- It provides what appears to be the first efficient \(\tilde{O}(d)\) sample complexity for learning XOR on isotropic data with a standard neural network and standard training.
- The proof techniques are novel, particularly the observation that the variance of the gradient on signal entries is much smaller than the signal itself (eq. (4.5)).
- The analysis offers intuition on the two-phase mechanism, which may be applicable to other problems.
- The sample complexity is nearly optimal (matching lower bounds for rotationally invariant algorithms).

### Weaknesses
- **Clarity and relation to prior work**: The exposition could be clearer, and the relationship with several relevant works is insufficiently discussed.
  - Ben Arous et al. [2021] is listed in the references but not cited in the text; a comparison with their sample complexity for \(k=2\) (information exponent) single-index targets is needed.
  - The comparison with Ben Arous et al. [2022] is only brief and should be expanded.
  - Relevant references that should be discussed include: Ba et al. [2022] (one GD step beats kernel methods but learns only a single index), Dandi et al. [2023] (multi-index learning with staircase condition), Damian et al. [2023] (smoothed SGD matches SQ lower bound; information exponent \(k\) yields \(d^{k/2}\) sample complexity), and Jacot et al. [2021] (saddle-to-saddle dynamics).
  - The paper would benefit from further discussion of how the proof techniques compare with recent work.
- **Generality and extensions**: The problem setting is restrictive; generalizing to \(k\)-sparse parities is a natural next step but is not addressed.
  - It is unclear what modifications would be needed to extend the analysis to higher-degree parities.
  - The challenges of using \(\ell_2\) loss (rather than logistic loss) for the Boolean parity setting are not discussed.
  - The techniques may be difficult to generalize to other settings, as acknowledged in Appendix A.
- **Dependence on initialization and hyperparameters**:
  - The result relies on small initialization to learn features in the first phase, which is similar to many previous works.
  - The step size \(\eta\) is required to be not very small (\(\eta > 1/\mathrm{poly}(d)\)). It is not clear whether this is a fundamental barrier or an artifact of the proof technique.
  - The batch size is relatively large compared to practical settings; whether a smaller batch size would also achieve near-optimal sample complexity is an open question.
- **Precision of rates**:
  - The order of the \(o(1)\) test loss in Theorem 3.1 is not explicitly stated in the main text. The dependency of sample complexity and running time on the target error \(\varepsilon\) is unclear, making it hard to assess practical implications.
- **Transfer learning**: It would be interesting to know whether the first layer has learned the relevant feature subspace so that other tasks dependent on that subspace could be learned.
- **Phase durations**: The algorithm spends \(\Theta(\log(d)/\eta)\) time in the first phase versus \(\Theta(\log\log(d)/\eta)\) in the second phase; this asymmetry and its connection to observations in Ben Arous et al. [2021] (weak recovery phase) could be highlighted.
- **Minor issues**:
  - Above eq. (4.1): "ie." should be "i.e.,".
  - Definition 4.2: "\(w_{sig}^T\)" should be "\(w_{sig}^T x\)".
  - Lemma 4.1: the scale of \(\zeta\) should be stated to make the lemma more self-contained.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 6, confidence 2) noted significant concerns about exposition and comparisons with prior work; the other three reviewers were more positive (scores 8, confidence 3–4).
