## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers rated the paper 6, 6, 8, 8 (two lower, two higher), with confidence ranging from 3 to 4. They generally agree that the proposed RAPTOR method—recursive embedding, clustering, and summarization to construct a hierarchical tree for retrieval-augmented language models—is a well-motivated and interesting approach that achieves strong results on NarrativeQA, Qasper, and QuALITY. Reviewers 3 and 4 highlight its elegance and potential as a new paradigm baseline for RAG, while Reviewers 1 and 2 are more cautious, raising concerns about the accuracy of state-of-the-art claims, fairness of comparisons, insufficient analysis of summaries, and missing ablations on clustering. There is disagreement on novelty: Reviewer 3 finds it novel; Reviewer 4 considers the novelty low but the method effective.

### Strengths
- Strong performance improvements on multiple QA datasets (NarrativeQA, Qasper, QuALITY) using both open-source and closed-source LMs (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). RAPTOR with GPT-4 improves best performance on QuALITY by 20% absolute accuracy (though Reviewer 1 questions this claim’s accuracy relative to prior works).
- The recursive embedding, clustering, and summarization to construct a tree is an elegant and well-motivated approach that sets up a new potential paradigm for retrieval-augmented generation (Reviewer 3). The hierarchy enables retrieval at different levels of granularity (Reviewer 4).
- Clustering via Gaussian Mixture Models with Bayesian Information Criterion automatically determines the number of clusters, avoiding manual tuning (Reviewer 2).
- RAPTOR achieves better performance compared to commonly used retrievers such as DPR (Reviewers 2, 3).
- Although Reviewer 4 notes the novelty is low, the simple idea of clustering by similarity rather than contiguous segments improves results on three datasets.
- Results reported on three datasets of varying domains using both closed and open source LMs (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Inaccurate state-of-the-art claims**: Reviewer 1 states that multiple SOTA claims are not correct. For example, in ZeroSCROLLS, Flan-UL2 is reported to get 56.9 F1 on Qasper, and GPT-4 gets 89.2 on QuALITY in the same paper. The baselines BM25 and DPR are not well justified, and SBERT can be applied without the tree structure (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 adds that the claim of a 20% accuracy boost is misleading because prior works do not use GPT-4, so most of the boost likely comes from GPT-4 rather than RAPTOR.
- **Unfair model comparisons**: Reviewer 2 finds it problematic that RAPTOR with GPT-4 is compared to LongT5 (3B parameters) and ColtT5, given GPT-4’s larger undisclosed parameter size; RAPTOR was not applied to models of similar size. Reviewer 1 also notes that use of dev data instead of test data for SOTA claims is unclear.
- **Insufficient analysis of generated summaries**: Reviewer 1 finds the analysis in Table 6 difficult to understand and not revealing about summary content; the inline example suggests abstractive benefit but need verification. Reviewer 4 requests examples of generated summaries.
- **Missing ablations on clustering**: Reviewer 3 suggests an ablation of balanced tree-style recursive encoding of contiguous chunks instead of clustered chunks. Reviewer 4 asks about the impact of UMAP and neighbor choice, and how the clustering algorithm (e.g., GMM vs. hierarchical) affects hierarchical structure and retrieval. Reviewer 1 asks why GMM is used and whether passages/summaries belong to multiple clusters.
- **Computational expense**: Recursive summarization with an LLM adds cost; a discussion of this trade-off is needed (Reviewer 3).
- **Information loss vs. enumerating chunk concatenation**: Reviewer 2 questions the benefit of RAPTOR over simply enumerating possible concatenations of chunks, given potential information loss by summarization.
- **Lack of detail on key steps**: Reviewer 2 asks how texts are segmented into chunks (paragraphs or other), what prompt was used for GPT-3.5-turbo summarization, and what embedding is used for retrieval (cluster centroids or summary embeddings, and whether SBERT is used as in clustering). The number of retrieved instances for baseline models is also unspecified, making comparison fairness difficult to judge (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 asks about the hallucination rate and an example in the appendix.
- **Beam search details unclear**: Reviewer 1 finds the description of beam search confusing—whether all visited documents are returned or a single sequence, and how the cumulative cosine score selects the next top-k nodes; the approach does not seem very beam search-like. The claim that RAPTOR scales linearly is deceptive because actual complexity is O(n*k) with small k; the writing should be refined (Reviewer 1).
- **Lack of relevant related work citations**: Reviewer 1 suggests citing Sun et al. 2021 (long-range context), decontextualization (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14772), Gao et al. 2023 (summaries in RAG), and Min et al. 2021 (tree-based retrieval). Reviewer 3 also mentions precedence for hierarchical retrieval.
- **Reproducibility concern**: Reviewer 4 notes that no experiments with open-domain pre-trained models (e.g., LLAMA) are shown, which would improve reproducibility.
- **Chunk segmentation as remaining problem**: Even though clustering is automatic, how to segment texts into chunks is still left as a problem (Reviewer 2).
