## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes **MARS** (**M**ulti-step **A**ction **R**epre**S**entation) to handle fragmentary interaction in real-time reinforcement learning for high-frequency robot control (caused by packet loss or delay). MARS uses a **sc-VAE** to learn a compact latent representation for multi-step action sequences, then trains an RL policy (e.g., TD3) in that latent space; during execution the decoder converts latent actions into action sequences. The method is evaluated in **Mujoco** environments with simulated fragmentary interaction and on a **real-world snake robot**. Results show MARS outperforms baselines (frame-skip, directly generating action sequences) and is comparable to an ideal controller in some tasks. Ablation studies demonstrate the importance of regularization for environmental dynamics.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and significant problem formulation.** Fragmentary interaction is an important real-world issue in high-frequency control (Reviewer 2). The research objectives are clear, with a detailed background introduction (Reviewer 1).
2. **Clear presentation and easy to follow.** The problem is well-illustrated with figures (Reviewer 2). The motivation is clear and the method is clean (Reviewer 4). The paper is easy to understand (Reviewer 3).
3. **Generability of the proposed method.** MARS can work with various RL optimization algorithms (Reviewer 1).
4. **Solid empirical performance.** The algorithm performs well in Mujoco tasks, often matching or exceeding baselines, and ablation studies validate the importance of the modules (Reviewers 2, 3). The method is comparable to an ideal (no-fragmentary) controller in some settings (Reviewer 3).
5. **Real-world robot demonstration.** Experiments on a real snake robot demonstrate the practicality of the method (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
1. **Lack of theoretical justification for the learned representation.** The claim that “action sequences decoded from close latent points have similar environmental influence” is only empirically supported. No formal analysis explains why clustering on environmental impact is better than clustering on value or reward; the assumption that similar environmental changes imply similar rewards/values may not hold, especially in sparse-reward environments (Reviewer 1). Relatedly, the paper claims MARS has “better stationarity” but provides no explanation or proof, and the non-stationarity of trajectories in real-time RL (where executed actions may not match the policy’s output) is not addressed (Reviewer 1, Q3).
2. **Insufficient real-world evaluation for a method motivated by practical problems.** The real-world experiment uses a snake robot with only **54-dimensional proprioceptive observations**—a setting where packet loss is unlikely and stopping is acceptable. This does not demonstrate robustness on platforms that genuinely suffer from fragmentary interaction (e.g., legged robots with multi-modal perception) (Reviewer 3). Details of the real-world experiment are lacking: interaction pattern between executor and agent, sensor suite, actuation system, communication protocol, and challenges encountered (noise, delays, model inaccuracies) are missing (Reviewer 4). No demo video is provided (Reviewer 3).
3. **Incomplete experimental design and analysis in simulation.** The simulation environments (Mujoco) do not inherently exhibit fragmentary interaction; the impact of different communication patterns (variable latency, packet loss rates) is not analyzed (Reviewer 4). Key experimental details are missing: in the random FIMDP tasks, it is unclear whether the number of decision steps in a trial is fixed or randomly decided (Reviewer 1, Q2). There is no discussion of how action sequences are handled when the execution interval is shorter than the sequence length (Reviewer 1, Q2). The paper does not explain why MARS significantly outperforms the “TD3 with advanced decision” baseline (Reviewer 4, Q1) or why its advantage over frame-skips is more pronounced in simpler tasks (Reviewer 1, Q4).
4. **Lack of connection to existing problem formulations.** The proposed **FIMDP** (Fragmentary Interaction MDP) is not clearly distinguished from related formulations such as **POMDPs** and **MDPs with reward delays** (Reviewer 2). The paper does not discuss whether model-based approaches like **MPC** (using a learned world model) could also solve FIMDP, nor how they compare to learning multi-step representations (Reviewer 2, Q2).
5. **Operational details of the method are unclear.** It is not specified whether the decoder runs on the execution device; if so, would the latent representation itself be subject to loss or delay? (Reviewer 1, Q5). There is a typo in Figures 5 and 6, where “Musar” likely should be “MARS” (Reviewer 1, Q6).
6. **Divergent reviewer assessments.** While Reviewer 2 rated the algorithm as solid and the presentation excellent (soundness 3, presentation 4, contribution 3), other reviewers found significant weaknesses in soundness (Reviewer 1: soundness 2; Reviewer 4: soundness 2) and contribution (Reviewers 3,4: contribution 2). The minority positive view contrasts with the broader concerns about theoretical grounding and experimental validation.
