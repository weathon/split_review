## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the PIE dataset of 77K pairs of competitive C++ programs and their performance-improved versions, filtered from CodeNet and annotated via gem5 simulator for reliable runtime measurement. The authors evaluate a broad range of adaptation strategies for LLMs (CodeLlama 7B/13B/34B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) including retrieval-based few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought, fine-tuning with performance-conditioned generation, and self-play synthetic data augmentation. The best result (fine-tuned GPT-3.5) achieves a mean speedup of 6.86× with eight generations, exceeding the average human optimization (3.66×). Reviewers generally acknowledge the importance of the task and the thoroughness of the experimental analysis, but one reviewer (score 5) strongly questions the dataset quality and plausibility of the examples, arguing that the reported speedups may be inflated due to evaluation choices.

### Strengths
- The problem of performance-improving code editing is important and well-motivated; the paper targets a meaningful and challenging task.
- The PIE benchmark is a significant artifact that the community can build upon for training and evaluating LLMs for code optimization.
- The paper covers a comprehensive range of adaptation strategies (prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning, self-play) and provides clear ablations showing how each method improves results.
- Both open-source (CodeLlama) and closed-source (GPT-3.5/4) models are evaluated, revealing the gap between them and how adaptation can narrow it.
- The dataset construction process (using gem5 full system simulator, filtering for correctness, handling performance variability) is reasonable.
- The experiments are thorough, with systematic analysis of prompting, retrieval schemes, and finetuning across multiple models.

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset quality concerns (Reviewer 3, minority view):** The examples in Appendix A.1 appear implausible for competitive C++ programming: programs are functionally different, contain obviously bad code (e.g., missing break statement), and have inconsistent interfaces. These issues call into question the overall quality of the PIE dataset. A random check of supplementary material confirms similar problems. The high correctness percentages in Tables 1 and 2 further suggest low task difficulty.
- **Evaluation metric inflates results:** Speedup is set to 1.0 for incorrect or slower generations, meaning the metric only considers successful optimizations. This makes the reported mean speedup (e.g., 6.86×) potentially misleading, as it averages only over the best cases. A more comprehensive analysis of failure categories is needed (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Correctness degrades with introduced methods:** Performance-conditioned generation (Perf-Cond) and synthetic data augmentation reduce the percentage of correct code. The paper does not adequately explain why (e.g., Perf-Cond’s effect on correctness) or analyze when models fail to produce correct/optimized code (Reviewers 1, 2).
- **Missing experiments and inconsistent model coverage:** GPT-4 is not tested with Few-Shot or chain-of-thought (CoT) in Table 2 (Reviewers 1, 4). The choice of models across methods is inconsistent—e.g., no few-shot or CoT for GPT-4—limiting the comparability of results.
- **Synthetic data saturation and unclear impact:** Self-play synthetic data yields only marginal improvements in Best@8 (and sometimes a minor decrease for HQ+Self-Play, GPT-3.5: 95.42%→95.11%). The paper lacks a convincing explanation for this rapid saturation and why self-play does not consistently improve across metrics (Reviewer 4).
- **gem5 simulator limitations:** Using gem5 with a single Intel Skylake CPU may overfit to that microarchitecture and does not capture big-O algorithmic improvements, which are often a goal in code optimization (Reviewer 3).
- **Underutilization of the PIE dataset:** Only 4K out of 77K pairs are used for fine-tuning (plus 3K synthetic); the rationale for this choice is not clearly justified, and it seems to contradict the quality claim of the dataset (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of code type distribution analysis:** The paper does not report the proportion of different programming constructs or optimization types in training/test data, making it difficult to assess generalizability across programming paradigms (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing details on semantic similarity for synthetic data:** It is mentioned that semantic duplicates are tracked, but no explanation is given for how semantic similarity is computed (e.g., whether it uses CodeBERTScore) (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation issues:** Table 1 is not referenced in text; Table 2 has confusing grouping (dynamic retrieval not separated clearly) and extra horizontal lines (Reviewer 1). The LoRA hypothesis about performance optimization examples not occurring naturally in training data needs clearer explanation (Reviewer 4).
