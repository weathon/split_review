## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies in-context learning (ICL) of variable-order Markov chains (VOMC) by framing language modeling as data compression, using small alphabets and low-order VOMCs. It benchmarks transformers against compression algorithms—context-tree weighting (CTW, Bayesian optimal for CTW priors) and prediction by partial matching (PPM). Key empirical phenomena: (1) transformers learn to compress VOMC in-context, while PPM suffers; (2) performance is not very sensitive to layer count, with even a two-layer transformer doing well; (3) transformers trained on non-CTW priors can significantly outperform CTW. The paper explains these via two theoretical constructions: a D+2-layer transformer approximating CTW for maximum order D, and a 2-layer transformer using feed-forward for probability blending. It also implements synthetic layers and shows a hybrid transformer matches or sometimes outperforms full transformers with fewer parameters.

### Strengths
- **Innovative perspective**: Framing ICL as a data-compression task and connecting it to CTW/PPM is novel and provides fresh insight into transformer sequence modeling (Reviewer 2, 3, 1).
- **Theoretical constructions**: Two well-motivated transformer architectures (one mimicking CTW, one using feed-forward blending) are derived and empirically supported (Reviewer 2, 3, 1). The constructions explain the observed phenomena, including why performance is layer-insensitive (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Empirical evidence and theoretical explanations**: The paper presents clear experiments (e.g., Figures 6&7) and theoretical analysis that show how transformers can mimic and even exceed traditional compression methods (Reviewer 3).
- **Key finding on network depth**: ICL for VOMC does not require deep models—even 2-layer transformers perform well, which is a non-trivial extension from fixed-order MC results (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Practical guided theory**: The experiments lead to a hand-crafted transformer that achieves better performance on VOMC data, demonstrating utility of the theory (Reviewer 1).
- **Readability (positive)**: One reviewer finds the paper math-heavy but highly readable (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of practical motivation and generalizability**: The paper does not convincingly explain why studying VOMC is important for understanding real transformers, beyond previous work on fixed-order MC and HMMs (Edelman 2024, Akyürek 2024). It does not connect findings to real-world ICL or language modeling tasks (Reviewer 1, 4). Multiple reviewers note the exclusive focus on synthetic VOMC data with small alphabets and low orders, and the absence of experiments on standard language modeling benchmarks (e.g., Penn Treebank, WikiText) or on large pre-trained models (GPT, LLaMA). This severely limits the practical relevance of the results (Reviewer 2, 3). One reviewer explicitly states the data differs from real ICL data, and real ICL attention patterns are not compared to Figures 6&7 (Reviewer 3). Another reviewer says no practical implications are discussed (Reviewer 4).
- **Limited novelty and incremental contribution**: The paper’s structure closely mirrors prior ICL analyses (Akyürek 2022/2024, Edelman 2024), with only the class of probabilistic models changed; this hurts novelty (Reviewer 1). Two reviewers (Scores 3, 3) find the core findings (transformers can learn VOMC, outperform CTW) expected given transformer capacity and existing Turing-completeness results, and do not provide strong new mechanistic insights or explanations for the advantage over CTW (Reviewer 4, also echoed by Reviewer 1). One reviewer questions what specific architectural features enable the outperformance, and notes the paper does not analyze structural learning of the context tree or training dynamics (Reviewer 4). Another reviewer asks: what key discoveries beyond fixed-order MC does this paper offer? (Reviewer 4).
- **Insufficient justification for baselines and comparisons**: Why is CTW chosen as the gold standard? Its limitations are not discussed, nor are alternative algorithms considered (Reviewer 4). One reviewer also questions how the compression rate is computed and why it exceeds 1 for PPM in Figure 4 (Reviewer 3).
- **Readability and presentation issues**: The paper is hard to follow without consulting the appendix (e.g., Appendix A/B), which contains essential background and derivations (Reviewer 3). The introduction of transformer attention (Section 2.1) is considered redundant for readers familiar with ICL/LLMs, while insufficient background on CTW and related work forces readers to external sources (Reviewer 3). One reviewer suggests moving the second theoretical construction (Section 4.2) to the appendix and keeping only Section 4.3 (Reviewer 1). Another reviewer states readability could be improved overall (Reviewer 2). (Note: one reviewer dissents, praising the readability; this contrast is preserved.)
- **Missing experimental details and typos**: Several concrete questions are raised: how much training data is used in Section 3.1, is it sufficient, and what are the learning rate and other training details? (Reviewer 3). The term “segment index” in Figures 4 and 5 is undefined (Reviewer 3). There is a typo at Line 356: “Fig 7” should be “Fig 6” (Reviewer 3).
- **Contrast among reviewers**: Two reviewers (Scores 3, 3) find the paper sound but of narrow theoretical interest with insufficient contribution, while two others (Scores 5, 5) see the connection to compression and the theoretical constructions as innovative and well-motivated. The split indicates disagreement on the significance of the work.
