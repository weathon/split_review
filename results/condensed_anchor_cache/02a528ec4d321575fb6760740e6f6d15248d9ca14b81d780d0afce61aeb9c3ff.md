## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Corrective Retrieval Augmented Generation (CRAG) to improve the robustness of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). It introduces a lightweight retrieval evaluator (T5-Large) that assigns a confidence score to each retrieved document and triggers one of three actions: correct, incorrect, or ambiguous. For incorrect retrieval, large-scale web search is used as an extension; a decompose-then-recompose algorithm refines documents. CRAG is plug-and-play and is evaluated on four datasets covering short- and long-form generation tasks. Reviewers generally acknowledge the importance of the problem and the breadth of experiments, but raise significant concerns about technical novelty, baseline selection, methodology clarity, unfair comparison due to web search, evaluator accuracy, latency, and missing analyses.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important research question: mitigating the impact of imperfect retrievers in RAG to improve generation robustness.
- CRAG is a cost-effective method to filter, refine, and improve retrieved documents; it can be applied in various RAG pipelines.
- Comprehensive experiments are conducted across multiple datasets (PopQA, WebQ, etc.) showing effectiveness, robustness, and efficiency.
- The presentation is clear and easy to follow.
- Numerous ablation studies are included.
- One reviewer (score 6) is notably more positive, emphasizing that CRAG shows significant improvements over standard RAG (though only tested on a 7B model), that knowledge correction is necessary to prevent irrelevant results from affecting generation, and that the ablation studies make the experiments solid overall.

### Weaknesses
- The claim that this is the first attempt to design corrective strategies for RAG is inaccurate; prior works such as RARR and DRAGIN have already explored corrective mechanisms.
- Baseline selection is insufficient: comparisons with highly relevant prior corrective RAG works (RARR, DRAGIN) are missing, and state-of-the-art baselines from 2024 are absent.
- The ablation results show minimal impact when removing each component; a deeper analysis of the necessity and role of each component is needed.
- The technical contribution is limited. Many components resemble existing methods (e.g., Self-RAG, RetRobust for retrieval assessment, web search as a fallback). Using large-scale web resources is not a distinct technical contribution, and the paper offers no insights that are clearly distinguishable from existing RAG work.
- The methodology feels disorganized. The motivation focuses on correcting retrieval errors, but web search (if superior) and passage splitting do not directly align with that goal. It is unclear why a better retriever (web search) is not used from the start if it is available in the pipeline.
- The comparison with baselines is potentially unfair because CRAG leverages large-scale web search (commercial search engine) while many baselines (e.g., Self-RAG) are limited to a static corpus, and there is no transparency about whether baselines are also allowed access to web search.
- Accuracy of the T5-based retrieval evaluator is concerning; it is unclear how evaluator accuracy impacts downstream generation. Moreover, using T5-Large (an older, less capable model) seems questionable—an encoder-based classifier or even similarity scores from the retriever might suffice.
- The paper lacks evaluation and analysis of the filtering performance of the evaluator; what happens when the evaluator incorrectly filters out useful information?
- The confidence thresholds are set manually and vary significantly across datasets (shown in Appendix B.3), making them hard to determine in real-world scenarios.
- CRAG cannot handle multi-hop QA because the evaluator assesses each query-document pair in isolation, making it difficult to judge complex inter-document relationships.
- Latency is a core concern: the Knowledge Correction phase introduces significant delays, but the paper only discusses generation time; this could hinder practical deployment.
- The paper lacks a case analysis showing how the three labels (Correct, Incorrect, Ambiguous) affect generation outcomes and how changes in labels alter results.
- It is unclear whether the evaluator’s training on PopQA relevance signals introduces biases and whether it generalizes well.
- The proportion of different actions (correct/incorrect/ambiguous) taken during inference is not reported.
- All experiments are conducted only on LLaMA2 7B; tests on stronger LLMs (LLaMA3, ChatGPT) are needed to assess scalability and whether a sufficiently strong model could internally differentiate accurate vs. inaccurate retrieval without a separate knowledge correction step.
- In Section 5.3, the reported accuracy improvements of CRAG over RAG do not match the numbers in Table 1; this discrepancy (typo or wrong results) should be corrected.
- It is unclear why Table 1 includes results from LMs trained on proprietary data; the main comparison with baselines is only valid for LLaMA2 and Self-RAG LLaMA2.
- There is a minor formatting error: a question mark in a citation on Line 98.
