## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies unconstrained online convex optimization (OCO) with adversarially corrupted gradient feedback. The learner receives potentially corrupted gradients \(\tilde g_t\) instead of true gradients \(g_t\), with no assumptions on how corruptions arise. The goal is low regret against any comparator \(u \in \mathbb{R}^d\). For the case where a bound \(G \ge \max_t \|g_t\|\) on uncorrupted gradients is known, the proposed algorithm achieves regret \(O(\|u\| G (\sqrt{T} + k))\), where \(k\) measures total corruption (number or magnitude). When \(G\) is unknown, a filtering approach gives regret with an extra additive \((\|u\|^2+G^2)k\) penalty. Matching lower bounds are provided for the known-\(G\) case. The results are applied to stochastic convex optimization with corruptions and distributionally robust optimization (DRO).

### Strengths
- The problem is meaningful and practically motivated: handling adversarial corruptions in the unconstrained setting is a natural and previously unstudied challenge (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- The technical difficulty is clearly articulated: existing algorithms suffer extremely high regret even with tiny corruption in the unconstrained case, unlike bounded domains (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The definition of \(k\) is simple yet covers both the number of corruptions and their magnitude simultaneously (Reviewer 3).
- The regret bounds are asymptotically optimal for the known-\(G\) case, with matching lower bounds (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The paper achieves a reduction in space complexity compared to the filtering technique of van Erven et al. (2021) (Reviewer 2).
- The presentation is generally well-written, with clear motivation, an intuitive example (Figure 1), and a clear proof sketch in Section 4 that is accessible to non-experts (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). (Reviewer 3 finds the first algorithm description unclear, creating a contrast noted below.)
- The contributions include both known-\(G\) and unknown-\(G\) regimes, and the results are applied to relevant problems (SCO and DRO) (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: The main techniques are heavily borrowed from previous work. The composite loss function method is directly from Zhang & Cutkosky (2022), and the filtering approach builds on van Erven et al. (2021). The paper does not clearly articulate the key modifications or technical innovations that adapt these methods to the adversarial corruption setting (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). Reviewer 3 specifically notes that the algorithm for known \(G\) may be barely different from Zhang & Cutkosky (2022) for sub-exponential noise, which would be interesting but is not clarified. Reviewer 2 emphasizes that the paper would be much stronger if it elaborated on how the approach differs from or improves upon those works.
- **Presentation clarity issues**:
  - Norm notation is inconsistent: sometimes \(\|\cdot\|\), sometimes \(|\cdot|\) (e.g., compare Equation (5) and (7)). It should be clarified that the 1D case is discussed first but can be extended to \(\mathbb{R}^d\) (Reviewer 1).
  - Line 215: the phrase "(in fact, potentially exponential in t)" is confusing because it appears in a regret decomposition without an algorithm context; the same sentence appears in Zhang & Cutkosky (2022) but makes sense there (Reviewer 1).
  - Equation (5) is confusing: \(\tilde{g}_t^c\) might be scalar or vector; it appears to be written for the 1D case only. It could be simplified as \(\tilde{g}_t^c = \min\{h_t, \|\tilde{g}_t\|\}\) (Reviewers 3, 4).
  - The description of Algorithm 1 (known \(G\)) lacks details: line 6 finds \(w_t\) satisfying an equation, but computational complexity or existence guarantees are not discussed; these are covered in Zhang & Cutkosky (2022) but missing here (Reviewer 3).
  - The unknown \(G\) case (Sections 5, Pages 8–10) is described in a convoluted manner. The proof is presented as a dense sequence of inequalities without clear logical guidance or intermediate explanations. The transition from known to unknown \(G\) lacks clear motivation and justification for the additional terms and modified update rules. Multiple reviewers find this part hard to follow and suggest it should either be simplified, deferred to the appendix, or restructured to highlight the differences from the known-\(G\) case (Reviewers 3, 4).
  - The description of the Filter improvement beyond memory usage is insufficient; it is unclear if a black-box use of Filter would lead to similar bounds or if the improvements are crucial (Reviewer 3).
  - The Epigraph-based regularization reduction: it is unclear if the reduction is the same as that in Cutkosky & Mhammedi (2024) Section 3.3; if not, differences should be stated. The algorithm could possibly be described as a two-step reduction to simplify presentation (Reviewer 3).
- **Lower bound explanation**: Section 4.2 lacks sufficient explanation of how the lower bound is proved; more details would help (Reviewer 1).
- **Assumption on \(k\)**: Knowing \(k\) (which bounds both the number and magnitude of corruptions) is a strong assumption. The paper should discuss its strength and whether it can be relaxed or estimated (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing related work**: The paper "Contaminated Online Convex Optimization" considers corruption of strong convexity and is related; it should be cited in the related work (Reviewer 4).
- **Applications**:
  - The first example (reduction from stochastic convex optimization) is not very interesting; it amounts to taking expectations of OCO guarantees (Reviewer 3).
  - The DRO application is not well-placed in the literature. The connection to Namkoong and Duchi (2016) is not clearly established, and there is no comparison with existing results in the DRO literature to assess relevance (Reviewer 3).
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: Reviewer 3 (score 5, confidence 3) is notably more critical of the presentation and novelty compared to Reviewers 1, 2, and 4 (all score 6). Reviewer 3 rates presentation as 1 and contribution as 2, and explicitly states that the paper "does not do a great job of describing the main modifications" and that the presentation is "convoluted." This negative assessment should be weighed against the more positive evaluations from other reviewers (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). Reviewer 2 also raises novelty concerns despite the high score.
