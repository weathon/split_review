## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces Lyra, an LLM-augmented theorem proving framework with two correction mechanisms: Tool Correction (TC), which post-processes formal proofs by trying a set of predefined tactics (e.g., sledgehammer, auto, arith) when the LLM-generated tactic fails, and Conjecture Correction (CC), which incorporates prover error messages into the prompt to regenerate or refine proof steps. On miniF2F, Lyra achieves SOTA: validation 48.0%→55.3%, test 45.5%→51.2%, and solves 3 IMO problems. All four reviewers gave a rating of 6, though with varying confidence and some skepticism about technical novelty. The paper is well-written and the ablation studies are convincing, but the novelty of both TC and CC is limited as they build on prior ideas from DSP, Baldur, and code generation self-debugging.

### Strengths

- TC and CC are technically sound and the performance gain on miniF2F is impressive (Reviewers 1,2,3,4). Reviewer 4 highlights "the performance improvement is impressive" and the ablation studies are "well-presented and convincing."
- The insight behind TC — that LLMs are better at generating the next conjecture to prove than closing the conjecture — is noted as a smart observation (Reviewer 1). Extensive search via heuristic tactics helps close the conjecture.
- The paper is well written with clear illustration of its contributions (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 rates presentation 4/4, Reviewer 3 and 4 rate 3/3.
- The ablation study downgrading Lyra to DSP without TC and CC is appreciated (Reviewer 2). It clearly isolates the effect of each component.
- All reviewers acknowledge that the techniques lead to an improvement over the state-of-the-art (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 also finds it surprising that models like GPT-4 and Codex can interpret Isabelle error messages, calling it an interesting empirical phenomenon, though the paper does not explore this observation in depth.
- Reviewer 3 ultimately upgraded rating to 6 after authors presented evidence that TC improves performance on another dataset (LISA), suggesting some general applicability.

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty** is a recurring criticism across reviewers. Reviewer 1 states "the paper doesn't have much novelty in terms of the approach." Reviewer 3 finds "the techniques do not involve any significant technical or empirical insights." Reviewer 4 notes "there needs to be more contextualisation of the prior works" and points to direct prior works doing similar or identical things.
  - **Tool Correction (TC)**: The set of 11 tactics in TC have already been proposed in DSP (Reviewer 1). DSP already used sledgehammer + heuristics to close conjectures (Reviewer 4). TC appears to be largely similar, just trying a static list of heuristics; it is unclear if Lyra's tool selection is a reimplementation or introduces novel strategies (Reviewer 4). TC does not involve much interaction with the LLM — it is an exhaustive attempt on a heuristically chosen set of proof methods (Reviewer 2). TC focuses on 'by'-style single-step proofs, which limits deeper interaction between the LLM and the ITP (Reviewer 2). The reliance on a predefined static set of tactics misses opportunities for adaptive, context-aware proof generation (Reviewer 2).
  - **Conjecture Correction (CC)**: Appending error messages for self-debugging and generating multiple candidates has been commonly used for code generation (Reviewer 1). Reviewer 3 states "On a technical level, this is an obvious idea that has been tried before for proof generation and code generation." The Baldur work (April 2023) already proposed using proof assistant error messages to repair proofs (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 4). It is unclear if Lyra's method provides a novel approach to error message analysis (Reviewer 4).
- **Concerns about overfitting and transferability**: Reviewer 3 questions whether the heuristics in TC are overfit to miniF2F and Isabelle, and whether they transfer to other theorem proving tools and datasets. The authors later provided evidence on LISA, but this concern remains for other ITPs (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 2 similarly questions if the approach is too specific.
- **CC’s reliance on error message quality**: Reviewer 3 notes that it would be useful to analyze the nature of error messages from Isabelle and evaluate how much CC depends on their quality. Since models like Codex and GPT-4 are not explicitly trained on ITP error messages, the general effectiveness of error feedback without fine-tuning is not guaranteed (Reviewer 3). Would CC work with error messages from a different ITP? (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**: 
  - No comparison with Baldur (Reviewer 2).
  - A baseline replacing Minerva and Codex in DSP with GPT-4 (i.e., using GPT-4 for proof sketch with 11 tactics + sledgehammer) would be informative (Reviewer 1).
  - No analysis of which tactics fix how many wrong steps in TC (Reviewer 1).
  - Computational cost and time limits: how much time is taken by GPT-4, TC, and CC? If a time limit of 10 or 30 minutes per problem is set, how does the performance compare? (Reviewer 1).
- **Paper details**: There are minor typos (e.g., 'conducted on LLLMs', spelling errors in Algorithm 2) (Reviewers 2, 3). Reviewer 2 notes that the claim "all formal proof begins with proof -" is not strictly true. The discrepancy between informal proofs and formal sketches in Figure 3 is not explained (Reviewer 2). The paper does not specify the exact prompt used for error feedback, leaving reproducibility concerns (implied by reviewer 3's request for analysis).
- **Disagreement on contribution**: Reviewer 1 rates contribution 2 (fair) and Reviewer 3 rates contribution 2 (fair), while Reviewer 2 and 4 rate contribution 3 (good). Reviewer 1, despite upgrading score to 6, still thinks technical novelty is weak. Reviewer 3 had a rating fluctuation (6→5→6) indicating persistent doubts about the technical contribution of TC, but was convinced by additional LISA results. However, the overall assessment is positive with reservations.
