## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers evaluate a paper that quantifies vision understanding of multimodal foundation models (GPT-4o, Gemini Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Qwen2-VL) on standard CV tasks (semantic segmentation, object detection, image classification, depth, surface normals) using prompt chaining. They find the topic meaningful and the presentation clear, but express varied opinions on novelty, practical usefulness, and technical soundness. The paper is seen as a useful benchmark with extensive experiments, but also as an engineering study with limited methodological contribution. Observers agree that current MFMs lag behind state-of-the-art vision models, especially on geometric tasks, and that GPT-4o leads.

### Strengths
- First systematic evaluation of multimodal foundation models on well‑established vision tasks via prompt chaining, enabling API‑accessible models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini) to be tested on dense‑prediction benchmarks.
- Experiments are adequate and span multiple tasks and datasets (COCO, ImageNet, etc.), with detailed prompt‑sensitivity analyses that reveal better models are less sensitive to prompt variation.
- Prompt chaining outperforms naive prompting for GPT‑4V, hinting that inference‑time scaling laws apply to vision tasks as well.
- The work provides a clear, reproducible evaluation benchmark; implementation details and algorithm descriptions are sufficient for replication.
- Results show that while MFMs are respectable generalists, they are still far from state‑of‑the‑art in all tested tasks, confirming the gap between generalist MFMs and specialist models like SAM.
- The paper is well‑written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Engineering vs. research contribution.** Several reviewers view the paper as more of an engineering study than a traditional research contribution. Prompt chaining is described as naive, commonly used, and yields unsatisfactory absolute metrics; it is not proposed as an effective method for the tasks. The observations (e.g., better on semantic than geometric tasks, below SOTA) are considered consistent with common sense, limiting novel insight.  
  *Disagreement:* One reviewer (R1) calls this a “first time” investigation, while another (R3) finds the novelty minimal.

- **Inappropriate task designs.** The object‑detection solution works only for easy cases (single‑instance images; nearby same‑category objects are likely confused), making some results unreliable. For detection, the paper selects images with only one instance of each present class, which is not representative of real detection benchmarks. For semantic segmentation, the use of superpixels is questioned—SAM‑based grouping could improve performance and reduce API cost.

- **Missing comparisons.** Despite citing related works (e.g., DetToolChain for object detection), no direct comparisons with those methods are provided in the object‑detection or segmentation sections. Comparisons with other prompting‑based adaptation approaches are also lacking.

- **Cost and efficiency not analyzed.** The prompt‑chaining strategies are costly and time‑consuming (multiple API calls per image), but no time‑cost comparison is given against direct prompts or specialist models. A detailed cost analysis, especially in comparison with similar prompting methods, is needed.

- **Insufficient ablations and hyperparameter details.** Ablation experiments for detailed design choices in each task are missing. For detection, hyperparameters such as `batchSize`, `numPairs`, and weight objectives are not justified—how are they set and how do they affect performance? Similar details are missing for other tasks. For naive prompting baselines (e.g., Table 8), the exact prompt used is not reported, and it is unclear if naive prompting always lags behind prompt chaining.

- **Genemini 1.5 Pro gap.** In Table 2, Gemini 1.5 Pro with prompt chaining achieves roughly half the metric of its direct prompting counterpart. The authors do not address whether future improvements to prompt chaining could close or explain this gap.

- **Practicality of MFMs for pure vision tasks.** One reviewer (R1) argues that applying MFMs, designed for multimodal text generation, to purely vision tasks is inherently impractical because dedicated vision models (e.g., SAM) already exist, undercutting the motivation for this line of work.
