## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes feature responsiveness scores to measure the probability that changing a feature leads to a desired outcome, addressing the problem that standard feature attribution methods can highlight features that are not actionable for recourse. The authors provide efficient computation methods under complex actionability constraints and present empirical studies in consumer lending. Reviewers agree the problem is important, but diverge on novelty and the scope of the contribution.

### Strengths
- **Critical shortcoming identified**: The paper effectively demonstrates that standard feature attribution methods (e.g., LIME, SHAP) often give “reasons without recourse,” undermining the goals of explainability and consumer protection (R1, R3).
- **Novel actionability measure**: The concept of responsiveness scores offers a novel, nuanced approach to quantifying whether features can actually be changed to achieve a desired outcome (R1, R4). The measure is simple yet powerful and presented in a rigorous, pedagogical manner (R2).
- **Sound methodology**: The action and reachable set framework is well-defined theoretically, and the proposed definition (Definition 4) is clear (R2, R3). The authors provide efficient algorithms to compute responsiveness scores for any model and dataset.
- **Comprehensive experiments**: Extensive empirical studies are conducted using three datasets and multiple explainability methods; code and a Python library are released (R3, R4).
- **General applicability (minority positive view)**: While the paper focuses on lending, Reviewer 2 believes the approach has broad applicability beyond this domain and that it is a valuable step away from over-reliance on Shapley values.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns (majority critical, minority positive)**: Reviewers 1 and 3 find the contribution marginal relative to prior work on recourse verification and counterfactual feature attribution. Reviewer 1 points to work [1] (reachable sets/action sets with binary recourse feasibility) and notes that Table 3 is similar to Table 2 in [1]. Reviewer 3 cites Dutta et al. (2022, PMLR v162), arXiv 2409.20427, and arXiv 1912.03277, which propose feature importance based on changes that flip predictions, and ask how responsiveness scores differ. Reviewer 2, in contrast, considers the contribution original and valuable.
- **Actionability constraints and feature dependencies**: The definition of actionable sets is non-trivial and may not capture real-world feature relationships. Examples: (i) changing education from 1 to 3 can also change age, but age is treated as immutable (R4); (ii) immutable features like age are important for prediction (e.g., K1 admission, Paralympics eligibility) and should not be penalized for being non-actionable (R4). Reviewers also note that the assumption “number of features changed = number of actions” rarely holds (R4). The reachable set is limited by the training data manifold and may break causality (R4). How to identify the reachable set in a distributionally valid way is unclear (R3).
- **Continuous features and cost/effort**: The responsiveness score does not account for the ease or difficulty of actions (e.g., increasing credit score by 2 vs. 20 points) (R1). For continuous unbounded features (e.g., income), sampling may introduce bias that propagates into the explanation (R4). A weighted or cost-sensitive version would be more user-centric (R1).
- **Scope of attribution vs. recourse**: The paper challenges the assumption that feature attribution methods should provide actionable recourse, but Reviewer 1 argues that attribution and recourse are fundamentally different explanations with distinct purposes; the premise that attribution methods must satisfy an “actionability axiom” may be misaligned. Reviewer 4 adds that for LIME/SHAP, defining action sets or individualized recourse may be out of scope.
- **Definitional precision**: The responsive score is defined as a probability without a clear sample space or probability measure; it would be more precise to call it a fraction or proportion (R1). Additionally, the paper does not consider cases where two features are individually irresponsive but jointly responsive (R1).
- **Lack of broader applicability (minority critical)**: Reviewer 2 notes the paper only concentrates on lending; discussing general applicability would strengthen the work. Reviewer 3 also suspects the method only works well for simple discrete features where reachable sets are clear.
- **Missing investigations and extensions**: 
  - Extending responsiveness scores beyond top-k features from attribution methods to recourse generation, counterfactual explanations, and other explainability methods is needed (R1, R3).
  - No investigation of responsiveness at the individual level with varied user costs or multiple alternative recourse sets (R4).
  - Scalability to settings with diverse or sufficient alternative recourse is not addressed (R4).
  - The effect of training data distribution changes on responsiveness scores is not examined (R4).
  - The reachable set and responsiveness scores may leak more model information than local explanations like SHAP/LIME (R4).
- **Presentation issues**: Figures and tables (especially Figure 1) have very small text font, hindering readability (R1, R4). A few more qualitative results beyond numbers (e.g., page 9 lines 467–468) would help illustration (R4).
- **Clarification needed**: Line 96 (“By highlighting features that can be changed to consumers who could not attain a different outcome”) is confusing; it seems to contradict the paper’s claim that current methods fail to identify actionable features (R3).
