## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents FARM, a foundation model that integrates functional group (FG) information into SMILES and graph-based molecular representations via FG-aware tokenization, knowledge graph embeddings, and contrastive learning between sequence and graph views. The method is evaluated on MoleculeNet, reporting state-of-the-art on 10/12 tasks. Reviewers found the idea of incorporating FG inductive bias intriguing and the writing clear, but raised major concerns about dataset split inconsistencies (especially scaffold vs. random splits leading to inflated results), potential data leakage from the FG knowledge graph, overwhelming reliance on ring systems rather than true functional groups, lack of novelty relative to prior motif/fragment-based methods, insufficient ablation/complexity analysis, and missing details on the FG detection algorithm and augmentation strategies.

### Strengths
- The model achieves strong performance on diverse MoleculeNet tasks (10/12) with clear tabular improvements over existing baselines (R1, R2).
- The explanation of FG detection, tokenization, and contrastive integration is clear; the functional group knowledge graph adds structural depth (R1, R3).
- The idea of incorporating inductive bias via functional groups is chemically reasonable and intriguing, with comparisons to multiple FG-based baselines (R2, R4).
- Figures are neat and the writing is comprehensible; analysis of the KG embedding space, functional group substitution, and attention maps enriches understanding (R3).
- The paper highlights the importance of functional groups, which are often overlooked in molecular foundation models (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Tokenization granularity**: The increase from 93 to 14,741 tokens is excessive; the trade-offs and potential mitigations (e.g., pre-training optimizations) are not sufficiently discussed (R1).
- **Missing 3D information**: The model cannot handle stereochemistry or spatial effects, which are critical in many chemical tasks. The claim at L71 that the method overcomes limitations of 3D coordinates and long-range dependencies is unsupported (R1, R4).
- **Limited exploration of contrastive augmentations**: The negative-sample augmentations (node deletion, swapping) are briefly mentioned; a thorough analysis of their impact is missing (R1). Furthermore, if a functional group graph forms a triangle, node perturbation may produce the same graph, generating incorrect negative samples – the handling of non‑linear FG graphs is unclear (R3).
- **Computational requirements**: The added complexity from FG-aware tokenization and knowledge graph embeddings is not addressed (R1).
- **Novelty concerns**: Several reviewers note that incorporating functional groups into molecular representations has been extensively explored in prior work [1–6]; the approach is similar to existing motif‑based tokenization and fragmentation methods. The overall framework (MLM, link prediction, contrastive loss) combines widely used components without new architectural or algorithmic contributions (R1, R3, R4).
- **Insufficient experimental analysis**: The experiment section is too short, lacks in-depth discussion, and does not include adequate ablations to isolate the effect of each component (R1, R4).
- **MAJOR: Dataset split inconsistencies (R2)**:
  - The splits used on MoleculeNet are inconsistent with original recommendations (e.g., random splits for ESOL, Lipophilicity, FreeSolv). The appendix says scaffold splits, but Table/Figure captions indicate random splits. For BBBP, a scaffold split typically yields ~0.75, whereas FARM reports 0.93, suggesting a split methodology issue.
  - GROVER results reported are also higher than in other works, raising concerns about custom splits or dataset variants (e.g., modifications to ESOL/FreeSolv).
  - The authors should provide consistent results using standard MoleculeNet splits and ideally run additional experiments on TDC ADMET benchmarks, which offer fixed splits and leaderboards.
- **MAJOR: Limited FG rule set and generalizability (R2, R3)**:
  - The rules for identifying functional groups are not described in detail; comparisons to frequent subgraph mining are missing. The priority algorithm for assigning atoms belonging to multiple overlapping FGs is not specified (R3).
  - The method relies heavily on fused ring systems (>99% of identified FGs), yet ring systems are generally not considered functional groups. Ablation excluding ring systems is needed to verify whether the claimed benefits stem from true functional groups (R3).
- **MAJOR: Potential data leakage from FG knowledge graph (R2)**:
  - The knowledge graph includes relationship types such as water solubility and lipophilicity (logP). Including these properties may give an unfair advantage on downstream tasks related to those same properties, effectively constituting data leakage. The authors should re‑evaluate after removing such property‑related edges.
- **Missing details in FG detection and fragmentation (R3)**:
  - The algorithm that traverses the graph to detect functional groups is not described; e.g., how are intersected atoms assigned when multiple FGs overlap? What is the priority order?
  - Naming for fused ring systems only considers ring indices and sizes, but bond types (single, aromatic) are essential – how are bond types accounted for?
- **Knowledge graph discretization (R3)**: Continuous values like logP are discretized without explanation; discretization may lose correlation information.
- **Figure 5 unclear (R3)**: It is not explained why removal of multiple functional groups yields parallel results to removal of a single group (orange vs. green molecule), nor why Figure 5(b) shows link prediction performance.
- **Generation tasks**: The method’s effectiveness for molecular generation is not tested; a simple comparison with SMILESLSTM would strengthen the contribution (R3).
- **Imprecise motivation for contrastive learning (R4)**: SMILES and graphs contain the same molecular information; the chemical justification for learning both views via contrastive loss is vague. The choice is not chemically motivated.
- **Complexity and hyper‑parameter sensitivity (R4)**: Combining MLM, link prediction, and contrastive loss introduces multiple objectives and hyper‑parameters; the impact of each component is not thoroughly investigated, and training stability is a concern.
- **Fine‑tuning model choice and seed variance (R4)**: It is unclear whether the language model or the graph model is used during fine‑tuning. The results are reported with only 3 seeds, which is insufficient given the high variance in MoleculeNet tasks; at least 10 seeds should be used.
- **Exclusion of ToxCast (R4)**: Why is ToxCast, a standard MoleculeNet benchmark, omitted from the experiments?
