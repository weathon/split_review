## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes MMD, a method for collision-free multi-robot motion planning that combines single-robot diffusion models with classical MAPF algorithms. MMD uses conditional diffusion to generate trajectories for each robot, while MAPF constraints (e.g., Prioritized Planning, Conflict-Based Search) impose spatiotemporal collision avoidance during the diffusion process. The method scales to many robots by composing local diffusion models. Experiments in simulated logistics-like environments with varying difficulty (e.g., Circle, Weave, 2x2, 3x3 maps) compare MMD variants (MMD-PP, MMD-CBS, MMD-ECBS) against A* search and multi-robot diffusion baselines (MPD). Metrics include success rate, data adherence, and computation time.

### Strengths
* **Novel and meaningful integration**: Combining single-robot diffusion models (MPD [1]) with classical MAPF algorithms is an interesting and direct approach. It sidesteps the need for multi-robot training data and amortizes low-level planning (forward pass instead of RRT/PRM subroutines), which is crucial for handling many constraints from high-level negotiation (R1, R2, R3, R4).
* **Scalability via ensemble**: Using an ensemble of local diffusion models for each robot allows scaling to large environments without retraining, making the method adaptable to real-world applications like automated warehouses (R3).
* **Thorough experimental evaluation**: Evaluation covers diverse scenarios, multiple MMD variants, and ablation studies on planning time and constraint strategies. Success rates and data adherence scores are well presented (R1, R2, R3). Comparisons against MAPF baselines and multi-robot diffusion models are included (R4).
* **Clear exposition**: Motivation, method, and experiments are presented clearly and structured well (R1, R4).
* **Detailed discussion of MMD variants**: The paper compares multiple MMD variants (PP, CBS, ECBS, Guidance, ensemble), helping assess strengths/weaknesses of each constraint strategy (R3).

### Weaknesses
* **Limited novelty according to minority reviewer**: One reviewer (score 6) notes the method seems to be a simple combination of existing single-robot diffusion planning and classical MAPF algorithms, making it hard to distinguish which parts are novel contributions vs. adaptation (R2). This contrasts with other reviewers who consider it novel (R3, R4).
* **Lack of comparison to alternative multi-robot planning methods**: No comparison is made with Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP) [3] for multi-robot planning, which could seamlessly integrate into the MMD framework via factor-graph constraints. Such a comparison (e.g., on Circle/Weave maps with up to 50 agents) would strengthen the paper and benefit the MAPF community (R4).
* **Insufficient explanation of key concepts**: The definitions of “motion pattern”, “task-relevant local diffusion model”, and “data adherence” are unclear. Section 3.3 suffers from this ambiguity, making it hard to understand how the local diffusion model works and what data adherence means (R1).
* **Missing experimental details**: It is unclear how the single-robot diffusion model is trained (which dataset? single-robot or multi-robot?). This limits reproducibility (R2). Another reviewer questions whether the MPD method (trained on multi-robot data) is fairly compared to search-based A* on data adherence, since A* is not learned (R2). Also, MPD’s low performance even with <5 agents raises concern (R2).
* **Computational complexity and efficiency not fully addressed**: The paper should discuss computational complexity between A* and MMD (R1). The methods are noted to be inefficient as the number of agents increases (R2). A more detailed discussion of computational overhead, especially for MAPF constraint strategies and the denoising process, is needed (R3).
* **Trade-offs between MMD variants unclear**: It would help to know if certain strategies (PP, CBS, ECBS, Guidance, ensemble) are more suitable for specific environments or robot counts (R3).
* **Generalization concerns**: Relying on single-robot data may not be sufficient for complex, dynamic environments where multi-robot interactions are essential. The paper would benefit from exploring how single-agent data can benefit cooperative planning in such settings (R3). It is also unclear how well the approach generalizes beyond warehouse-like environments to less structured or highly dynamic settings (R3).
* **Missing discussion of alternative search algorithms**: Only A* is considered as a baseline; other search algorithms in multi-agent motion planning should be discussed or at least justified (R1).
* **Fig. 3 intuition**: The paper does not explain why A* maintains a good success rate for Circle and Weave scenarios (Fig. 3) even as the number of agents increases, and whether there is a trade-off with computational efficiency compared to MMD (R1).
