## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes NISQ-TDA, a new end-to-end quantum algorithm for topological data analysis (TDA) that is designed for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices. The algorithm estimates Betti numbers of a simplicial complex using a shallow quantum circuit, overcoming the data-loading problem by constructing an explicit circuit for data representation via a Fermionic boundary operator and projecting onto problem-specific simplices using quantum rejection sampling with mid-circuit measurements. Betti numbers are estimated via stochastic rank estimation recast as trace estimation with a truncated Chebyshev series and Hutchinson’s method. The algorithm has been fully implemented on a 12-qubit trapped-ion quantum computer (Quantinuum) and on noisy simulators, showing good agreement with noiseless simulations and robustness to noise. Theoretical analysis provides error guarantees and potential asymptotic speedup over classical algorithms for certain problem classes, e.g., when the number of holes grows exponentially with the number of vertices.

### Strengths

*   Addresses the important problem of finding end-to-end NISQ applications: the first short-depth quantum ML algorithm with a rigorous performance guarantee.
*   Topological data analysis (especially high-order Betti numbers) is classically hard under widely believed complexity-theoretic conjectures, making it a strong candidate for quantum advantage.
*   Existing quantum TDA algorithms (e.g., Lloyd et al.’s QTDA) require deep circuits and fault-tolerance; NISQ-TDA uses significantly shallower circuits (circuit depth O(n), but improved from alternatives).
*   Explicit circuit construction avoids the unrealistic data-loading problem common in many quantum ML proposals; the data is not stored on the quantum device.
*   The algorithm is fully implemented on real quantum hardware (12-qubit trapped-ion device) and on noisy simulators, with numerical results (Figs. 1A–D) supporting noise robustness.
*   Uses a clever combination of Fermionic boundary operators for efficient Pauli decomposition and quantum rejection sampling for projection; the mid-circuit measurement technique may inspire other NISQ algorithms.
*   Resource analysis and real-machine results (Fig. 1A–D) are presented; plots are clear and easy to follow.
*   The paper is well-structured and discusses potential applications to ML, AI, neuroscience, and cosmology.
*   End-to-end implementation and close match with noiseless simulation are remarkable.

### Weaknesses

*   **NISQ applicability:** The algorithm is not strictly NISQ because the circuit depth scales as O(n) (number of qubits), whereas true NISQ devices typically allow only O(log n) depth before noise dominates. Practical utility on larger systems remains uncertain.
*   **Noise tolerance analysis missing:** There is no theorem analyzing the amount of local depolarizing noise per gate that NISQ-TDA can tolerate. A fixed noise level per qubit will likely cause overall error that grows rapidly with system size, and the current analysis does not clarify scaling behavior.
*   **Insufficient hardware detail:** The hardware implementation lacks specifics: no explicit gate sequences for the Pauli operators, no information about qubit connectivity, gate fidelities, or shot counts used for the probabilities in Fig. 1B–D. The noise model in simulations is not specified.
*   **Lack of clarity in technical description:** Section 3’s discussion of projection operators \(P_{\Gamma}\) and \(P_k\) and quantum rejection sampling is hard to follow; a concrete example (e.g., for a 2-simplex) is needed to show explicit circuit construction.
*   **Missing empirical rigor:** Experiments are preliminary (small datasets, no statistical significance analysis, no comparison with classical algorithms or other QTDA methods). Results are presented without baselines, making it hard to isolate the algorithm’s contribution or verify noise resiliency beyond simple agreement.
*   **Practicality of quantum advantage conditions:** The speedup claim relies on the input simplicial complex having a number of holes that grows exponentially with the number of vertices. This is a strong constraint; it is unclear whether such complexes arise in real-world TDA applications. Even if they do, verifying the condition might itself be computationally hard, potentially negating any quantum advantage. The paper should discuss this more concretely.
*   **Missing background:** For readers unfamiliar with TDA, the paper does not adequately explain simplicial complex construction from raw data or provide sufficient comparison with existing quantum TDA approaches (e.g., quantum walks, adiabatic computation).
*   **Missing complexity comparison:** Theorem 1 gives time complexity \(O(\frac{n\log(1/\epsilon)}{\sqrt{\delta} \epsilon^2 \zeta_k^{2\log(1/\epsilon)/\sqrt{\delta}}})\); it would be useful to compare this directly with the fault-tolerant QTDA complexity (\(O(n^5/(\delta_k \sqrt{\zeta_k}))\)) to see if NISQ-TDA can outperform it in any parameter regime.
*   **Chebyshev truncation dependence unclear:** The resource analysis in Fig. 1A treats the Chebyshev truncation number as a parameter; its exact dependence on input data size (or on the projection operator) is not stated.
*   **Large noise-induced errors:** In Fig. 2A, errors are large for intermediate-size problems even with moderate noise (e.g., 0.001/0.01 gate error). No discussion of further error suppression or mitigation techniques is provided.
