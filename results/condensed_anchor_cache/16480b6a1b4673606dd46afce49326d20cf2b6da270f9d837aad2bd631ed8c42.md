## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces DETER, a dataset for regional deepfake detection containing 300,000 edited images (reviewer 1 incorrectly states 30,000) from four state‑of‑the‑art generators (two GANs, two diffusion models) and three manipulation types: face swapping, attribute editing (both localized to facial regions), and inpainting on random image regions. It includes masks, post‑processing to improve realism, and human studies showing a 20.4 % lower human detection rate compared to other fake datasets. The paper also proposes improved evaluation protocols with region‑based accuracy and extra unaltered images to mitigate spurious correlations. Benchmarking is done with several detection methods (Faster R‑CNN to DINO). Reviewers were split: one gave a high score (8) praising the contributions as “interesting and important”, while another gave a low score (3) with multiple criticisms; two others gave 5, rating the problem as useful but noting limitations in scale, recency, and ethics.

### Strengths
- New large‑scale dataset incorporating state‑of‑the‑art generative models (GANs and diffusion models) and three manipulation types, notably image inpainting which is novel for deepfake datasets (all reviewers).
- Includes pixel‑level masks for regional evaluation and addresses spurious correlations by adding unedited images (reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- Comprehensive benchmarking using multiple detection methods from 2015 Faster R‑CNN to DINO, with extensive experiments and cross‑domain analysis (reviewers 1, 3).
- Well‑documented human evaluation studies and GPT‑4 evaluation showing the dataset’s difficulty (reviewers 1, 3).
- Proposes region‑based image‑level classification accuracy as an additional assessment criterion (reviewer 2).
- Clear methodology presentation and well‑structured experiments (reviewer 3).
- The research problem (regional deepfake detection) is useful and the dataset is valuable for the community (reviewer 4).
- Good organization of the paper (reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset size and diversity concerns** – Reviewer 1 states the dataset contains 30,000 images (likely a misreading; abstract says 300,000). Reviewer 4 questions whether 300,000 is truly “large‑scale” given limited diversity, resolution, and number of unique identities; this may limit training of robust models. One reviewer (score 8) did not raise this as an issue.
- **Model coverage** – Only four generators are included (two GANs, two DMs), which may be insufficient for a comprehensive dataset given the rapid development of generative AI (reviewer 1). No instruction‑based editing from commercial platforms (e.g., the most used deepfake threat) is included (reviewer 1).
- **Evaluation and insights shallow** – The IoU cutoff of 0.5 is not informative; average IoU should be reported instead (reviewer 1). Insights from Section 4.3 are observational and lack explanation (e.g., why GAN‑edited images are harder to detect, why eyes/eyebrows cause more errors, why DETER transfers better to OpenForensics when both are face‑swapping). No granularity analysis in the human study (e.g., which manipulation type – FaceSwap, Attribute, Inpaint – is hardest) (reviewer 1). The novel “multiple faces” aspect is not explored in either human studies or benchmarking (reviewer 1).
- **Human study design flaw** – Including an “I’m not sure” option harms data quality; a forced binary choice with confidence scores would be better (reviewer 1). The same remark applies to the GPT‑4 evaluation.
- **Lack of open source** – The dataset is not yet open‑sourced, nor does the paper mention a plan to release it (reviewer 1).
- **Ethical and legal issues** – No discussion of rights to modify/redistribute CelebA and WiderFace images (e.g., CelebA’s license prohibits further distribution). No discussion of consent from individuals in the images or rights clearance for redistributing modified faces (reviewer 3).
- **Recency of detection methods** – In Table 4, the methods are not up‑to‑date; the selection seems arbitrary and lacks recent state‑of‑the‑art regional deepfake detection approaches (reviewer 4). Also, DINO (Zhang et al., 2023) is mis‑referenced as DINO ’22 (reviewer 4).
- **Unsolved questions left as weaknesses** – Why not use original unaltered fake images to address the “assume target regions exist” problem? (reviewer 1). Will the 140K unseen unmanipulated images be released? (reviewer 1). How do you ensure the dataset remains relevant as new generative models emerge? (reviewer 3). How does the post‑processing pipeline affect natural artifacts that aid detection? (reviewer 3). Why not include more diverse manipulation techniques beyond facial modifications? (reviewer 3). What instructions were fed to GPT‑4 in Section 3.3? (reviewer 1). Why does model capacity not influence performance (Faster R‑CNN vs. DINO give similar results)? (reviewer 1). It would be interesting to benchmark AIGC detectors pre‑trained on previous SOTA datasets against DETER (reviewer 1).
- **Grammar errors** – e.g., “GANs‑based” and overlong sentences (reviewer 4).
