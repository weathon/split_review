## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper converts EquiformerV2 into a Deep Equilibrium Model (DEQuiformer) by adapting techniques from TorchDEQ, enabling fixed-point reuse across consecutive MD frames. It reports 10%–20% improvements in accuracy and inference speed on MD17, MD22, and OC20 200k (1000-point test subsets) over EquiformerV2, along with reduced memory overhead. Three reviewers (R1, R2, R3) view the application of DEQ to ML force fields as novel and promising; one reviewer (R4, score 3) considers it a mere change of domain without methodological innovation.

### Strengths
- **Novel application of DEQ to MLFF**: The paper is the first to combine DEQ with equivariant force-field architectures. (R1, R2, R3) This contrasts with typical message‑passing improvements. (R3) R4 disagrees on novelty.
- **Practical improvements over EquiformerV2**: DEQuiformer outperforms EqV2 on MD17, MD22, and OC20 200k in both force accuracy and inference time, and reduces training time. (R1, R2, R3) The approach works for both molecules (MD17, MD22) and catalysts (OC20). (R3)
- **Post‑training accuracy–speed trade‑off**: The fixed-point error threshold can be modulated after training. (R1)
- **Memory efficiency**: DEQ’s implicit depth reduces GPU memory requirements during training. (R2)
- **Stable fixed‑point convergence**: The paper empirically shows that DEQuiformer reliably reaches a fixed point during inference. (R1)
- **Considers edge cases**: The impact of fixed-point convergence settings on final performance is examined. (R3)
- **Opens future directions**: Analogies with self‑consistent electronic‑structure solvers and potential for predicting other equilibrium properties (e.g., charge distributions) are noted. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited to direct‑force prediction**: The method only applies to models that predict forces directly, not forces derived from energy gradients. This is a significant limitation for long MD simulations requiring energy conservation (R1, R3). Non‑conservative forces can cause non‑physical behavior over many steps; relaxations do not need conservation but also do not require millions of steps. (R1) R4 questions the logic: DEQ operates on hidden representations, so it should work with any output head (energy or force autograd). The paper does not clarify this contradiction.
- **Insufficient evidence for temporal fixed‑point reuse**: (R4) Experiments lack comparison of solver iterations with and without reuse, and no actual MD simulations are performed to demonstrate speedups in realistic settings. (R1) questions shock simulations where consecutive frames are very different, and whether reuse guarantees fewer solver steps. (R3) Fixed-point reuse could not be tested on OC20; the OC20 MD subset exists and a short MD simulation in ASE could have been used, making this a key oversight.
- **Temporal ordering requirement limits applicability**: The reuse strategy relies on sequentially ordered data, which breaks down during phase transitions (e.g., water melting) and is inapplicable to non‑sequential datasets (rMD17, OC20). (R2) This restricts the method to datasets where temporal order is preserved. (R2) The paper should discuss how to adapt to non‑sequential data. (R2)
- **Baseline comparisons are incomplete and potentially biased**:
  - EqV2 baselines were not thoroughly tuned for every experiment; results may reflect more favorable hyperparameter tuning for DEQ rather than inherent advantage. (R3)
  - Comparisons are made only on smaller dataset versions (MD17, MD22, OC20 200k) rather than the original larger ones (OC20‑2M). (R3)
  - MD17 test set uses only 1000 points, whereas the conventional split uses all remaining data. (R4)
  - MD22 data split is inconsistent with the original MD22 paper and common MLFF splits. (R4)
  - Energy and force tables for MD17 and MD22 are separate instead of combined, and the EqV2 energy errors appear exceedingly poor, suggesting the training may not have fully converged, potentially creating an unfair baseline. (R4)
- **Large variance in Table 1 (MD22) results**: Force MAEs show high variability across molecules and configurations (e.g., Stachyose: all EqV2 ~11, DEQ 2‑layer ~0.31; Ac‑Ala3‑NHME: EqV2 1‑layer 10× larger). This raises concerns about training stability and reduces confidence in the conclusions. (R3, R4)
- **Missing comparisons with other ML force field architectures** (e.g., SchNet, DimeNet, MACE, etc.). (R1, R4) This limits assessment of DEQuiformer’s relative performance in the broader landscape. (R1)
- **Lack of ablation for temporal correlation effect**: It is unclear whether performance gains stem from fewer parameters in DEQ or from the temporal initialization. A comparison of DEQ with and without reuse, holding parameter count constant, is needed. (R2, R4)
- **Stochasticity of the fixed‑point solver**: The possibility of multiple solutions for the same input could lead to history‑dependent artifacts in MD; this is not discussed. (R3)
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Fig. 4b is confusing: the meaning of percentage values and why bars of the same color do not sum to 100% is unclear. (R4)
  - The validation‑set construction (50 samples, extra 1000 consecutive samples) needs clarification—especially whether those 1000 samples are truly separate from the validation set. (R4)
  - The paper lacks a pseudocode comparison to clarify which parts are adapted from TorchDEQ and which are new. (R2)
  - A table with parameter counts across all models should be added. (R2)
  - More introductory material on equivariance and Equiformer is needed for readers unfamiliar with the field. (R4)
- **Missing related work**: The introduction omits several relevant MLFF architectures (e.g., SchNet, DimeNet, SphereNet, Equivariant Vector‑Scalar models). (R4)
