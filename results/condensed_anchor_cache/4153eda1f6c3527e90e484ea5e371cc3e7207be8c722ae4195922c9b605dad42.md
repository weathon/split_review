## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper addresses the problem of maintaining recursive-abstractive indexing trees in dynamic datasets for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). It proposes two algorithms: adRAP, which updates the RAPTOR tree incrementally when documents are added or removed, and postQFRAP, a post-retrieval method that builds an online query-focused recursive-abstractive tree over retrieved chunks to refine context. The methods are evaluated on MultiHop, QASPER, and QuALITY using LLM-judged metrics (answered question, context relevance, human coherence, and win‑rate on comprehensiveness, diversity, empowerment, directness) and efficiency measurements (Table 10). Experimental results show the proposed methods achieve performance close to a fully recomputed RAPTOR baseline.

### Strengths

- Document updating is a common and practical problem; developing online indexing algorithms that handle dynamic datasets is useful (Reviewer 1).
- Online clustering is a reasonable approach to this problem (Reviewer 1).
- The method (especially equation 3) is mathematically interesting and sound (Reviewer 2).
- The pseudo‑code improves paper clarity (Reviewer 2).
- adRAP addresses the most important limitation of RAPTOR – the need for full recomputation in dynamic settings – and is computationally efficient (Reviewer 3). Table 10 shows adRAP is faster and requires fewer summarization calls than recomputing the entire tree.
- Both adRAP and postQFRAP have performance close to RAPTOR (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses

- The contribution is incremental; the proposed method is an extension of previous recursive abstractive indexing algorithms (Reviewer 1).
- The two contributions (adRAP and postQFRAP) are separate and address different problems; the paper should focus on one main contribution (Reviewer 1).
- adRAP is essentially an online clustering algorithm with a tree‑structure updating strategy. The authors should explain and compare different online clustering algorithms (e.g., the theory‑guided Chinese restaurant process) rather than the current ad hoc clustering (Reviewer 1).
- Baselines are too weak. Comparisons are needed with:
  - Different online clustering algorithms and online search‑result organization algorithms from current RAG studies (Reviewer 1).
  - For adRAP: (1) no update at all (retrieve from the old RAPTOR tree plus new documents separately) and (2) construct a new RAPTOR tree only on new documents and retrieve from both the new and old trees (Reviewer 3).
  - For postQFRAP: simpler summarization techniques without explicit clustering, such as Tree Summarize, Create and Refine, or Accumulate response synthesizers in LlamaIndex (Reviewer 3).
  - Other state‑of‑the‑art baselines like GraphRAG; the paper’s claimed black‑box compatibility may limit its impact (Reviewer 2).
- Evaluation metrics are indirect. The use of an LLM Judge introduces subjectivity and potential bias; direct metrics (e.g., accuracy on QuALITY, F1 on QASPER) should be used for more reliable and comparable results (Reviewer 3). This also makes it hard to compare with existing literature that uses standard evaluation protocols (Reviewer 3).
- The presentation of experimental results is hard to follow and analyze (Reviewer 1).
- The chosen datasets (QuALITY and QASPER) include many questions answerable by a single document. Original RAPTOR built one tree per document, so the benefit of the proposed methods (which assume a single tree over all documents) is unclear. Evaluation on complex multi‑document reasoning questions is needed (Reviewer 3).
- The impact of dynamic data for RAG is questionable. Building a tree is a one‑shot process; the real efficiency bottleneck is inference speed. For small additions, documents can simply be assigned to existing clusters; for large additions, a full recomputation is still required (Reviewer 2).
- postQFRAP builds a tree for all retrieved chunks at query time, which is inefficient. It requires multiple summarization calls per query, negating any efficiency gains from adRAP and introducing significant overhead compared to pre‑processing methods like SBERT or DPR (Reviewer 2).
- Performance is weak compared to the baseline (RAPTOR). The most salient improvement (comprehensiveness, diversity, empowerment on QuALITY, line 443) is not clearly attributed to adRAP or postQFRAP (Reviewer 2).
- No ablation study evaluates the effectiveness and efficiency of adRAP and postQFRAP separately (Reviewer 2).
- The writing is verbose. Section 3 is too long with unnecessary details (e.g., basic RAG background, EM algorithm) that could be shortened or moved to an appendix. The last paragraph of Section 1 should be expanded to clarify claims (e.g., “postQFRAP effectively enhances retrieval quality”, real‑world datasets, baselines) (Reviewer 2).
- Reviewer 2 raises a specific unanswered question: Have the authors performed a detailed analysis of the overall efficiency of “adRAP + postQFRAP” vs. RAPTOR when only a small number of documents is added? This concern overlaps with the criticism that the dynamic‑data contribution may be marginal and should be explicitly addressed.
