## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Question-Aware Knowledge Graph Prompting (QAP) for multiple-choice QA. QAP integrates a frozen LLM with a GNN-based soft prompt. Two novel modules are introduced: Question-Aware Neighborhood Aggregation (QNA), which injects question embeddings into GNN aggregation, and Inter-Option Attention (IPT), which models relationships between answer options using multiple attention heads. Experiments on several MCQA benchmarks with Flan-T5 (3B and 11B) show performance gains over existing methods. All reviewers rate the paper 3/5, acknowledging the contributions but raising significant concerns about comparisons, experimental scope, and analysis depth.

### Strengths

*   The deep interaction between GNNs and LLMs via the QAP architecture (QNA + IPT) is a well-motivated contribution. The method keeps the LLM frozen and trains only the prompt modules.
*   QAP outperforms other prompting methods on multiple MCQA datasets, and ablation studies confirm that each design choice (QNA, IPT, multiple heads) is reasonable and contributes to the overall improvement.
*   The approach is relatively simple yet yields significant performance gains, which facilitates constructive discussion on prompt engineering for MCQA.
*   The analyses of each component (QNA, IPT, multi-head) are extensive.

### Weaknesses

*   **Insufficient comparison with related deep-interaction methods.** The paper does not compare with GreaseLM (Zhang et al., 2022), QAT (Park et al., 2023), QA-GNN (Yasunaga et al., 2021), or GNP (Tian et al., 2024). For QA-GNN and GNP specifically, the paper fails to provide any conceptual or architectural comparison to explain why QAP is better, despite those works also incorporating question context into graph representations (e.g., QA-GNN’s Query Node, GNP’s cross-modality pooling).
*   **Unclear source of improvement over small LM baselines.** On OBQA and MedQA with T5-3B, QAP does not show significant gains over traditional MCQA models (QA-GNN, GreaseLM) that use small LMs (AristoRoBERTa, SapBERT). This makes it difficult to determine whether QAP’s superiority stems from the proposed method or simply from the larger LLM backbone.
*   **Limited LLM backbone evaluation.** Only Flan-T5 (3B and 11B) is tested, an encoder-decoder architecture. The paper does not demonstrate that QAP works with decoder-only LLMs (e.g., LLaMA), which are now predominant. The authors should explain why decoder-only backbones were omitted and provide experimental verification.
*   **Missing comparison with ToG.** For multi-choice QA, ToG (which performs multi-step reasoning on KGs with beam-search pruning) may be more appropriate. Retrieving an N-hop subgraph for the topic entity is more costly than ToG’s alternating beam search and single-hop retrieval. ToG also removes the dependence on pre-trained entity embeddings. The paper should report FlanT5-11B + ToG performance and compare training/inference costs.
*   **Incompleteness of case study.** Only four examples are provided, which is not a solid basis for a case study. The method could easily be applied to all test datasets for visualization.
*   **Experimental setting issue in ablation study.** During training, the model is forced to generate the ground-truth text of the correct option. But during evaluation, next-token logits appear to be used to predict the option. This overlooks LLM reasoning processes (Wang et al., 2024, “My Answer is C”) and may disadvantage one ablation (Prompt-Enhanced LLM), making the comparisons unfair. More details on the evaluation protocol are needed.
*   **Lack of discussion on computational cost.** Both training and inference costs (e.g., the cost of retrieving N-hop subgraphs) are not discussed, making it hard to judge practicality.
*   **Typos.** Line 73 has a double “the”; Line 376 writes “GNA” instead of “QNA”.
*   **Generality to other QA formats.** The method is specialized for MCQA, but it is unclear whether it can be extended to general QA (e.g., open-ended answering).
