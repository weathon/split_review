## Merged Review

### Summary
UniMatch combines explicit hierarchical molecular matching (at atom, substructure, molecule levels) with implicit task-level matching via meta-learning for few-shot drug discovery. It outperforms state-of-the-art methods on MoleculeNet, FS-Mol, and Meta-MolNet. However, concerns are raised about novelty, timeliness of baselines, technical clarity, and chemical accuracy of an example.

### Strengths
- First method integrating multiple levels of molecular structures; significant contribution to drug discovery (R1).
- Concept of modeling molecules across multiple levels is promising (R2).
- Tackles a valuable domain-specific problem with high societal value; method reasonably adaptable to other domains (R3).
- Extensive experiments across MoleculeNet, FS-Mol, and Meta-MolNet benchmarks consistently outperform baselines (R2, R3, R4). Ablation study clearly demonstrates contribution of every component (R3).
- Writing is clear with well-crafted figures; includes discussion of limitations and detailed supplementary explanations (R1, R2, R4).
- Universality of method examined across different network structures (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Timeliness**: Baselines and related work lack works from the last 1–2 years. Pre-GNN (Hu et al., 2020) is outdated; more powerful recent pretraining models should be considered, e.g., UniCorn (ICML 2024) and “Adapting Differential Molecular Representation with Hierarchical Prompts” (Briefings in Bioinformatics, 2024) (R4).
- **Novelty**: Hierarchical molecular representation extraction is commonly seen; the paper uses standard attention and meta-learning. This may not meet novelty standards of top conferences (R4). (Minority reviewer 4 is more critical; others scored 8.)
- **Technical clarity**: The description of the dual matching mechanism is hard to follow, which detracts from an otherwise solid work (R3, major).
- **Related work length**: The related work section is lengthy; consider condensing or moving it (R2).
- **Mean pooling concern**: Using mean pooling may lead to a predominantly molecule-level representation, potentially losing crucial atomic and substructural details (R2, question).
- **Chemical inaccuracy**: Example in lines 075–077 and Figure 1 refers to “hydrogen ions” within a molecule; should be “hydrogen atoms.” Acidity determination is not due to hydrogen atoms alone but to electronegativity of attached atoms (chlorine, oxygen) and substructure (e.g., H-O-S) (R3, minor).
- **FS-Mol description error**: Line 409 describes FS-Mol as a benchmark “for macromolecules (i.e., proteins).” This is inaccurate; tasks measure whether small molecules bind to specific proteins. Due to size, modeling proteins with this approach would be computationally infeasible (R3, minor).
- **Interpretability**: Suggest investigating interpretability of multi-level representations with gradient-based methods like DeepLIFT (R1, weakness).
- **Abbreviation consistency**: Clarify whether AUCPR and AUPRC refer to the same metric; use consistent abbreviation throughout (R1, question).
- **Missing QM tasks**: Why were QM tasks not considered in MoleculeNet evaluation? Only biophysical properties are included (R3, question).
- **Inductive bias for attention**: Would explicitly introducing an inductive bias to different levels of attention be helpful? (R3, question).
- **Generalization to diverse structures**: How well does the method generalize under a scaffold split for support and query sets? The failure on MUV is attributed to “severe distribution imbalance” without clear explanation; why are other baselines able to overcome this issue? (R4, question).
- **Figure 2 overlap**: In Figure 2, the query molecule appears identical to one in the support set. Is overlap between support and query allowed in implementation? (R4, question).
- **Table 1 notation**: The numbers in parentheses after dataset names (e.g., seeming number of tasks) are not explained in the main text (R4, question).
