## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes NAISR, a 3D neural additive model for interpretable shape representation. It combines deep implicit functions with an atlas that deforms according to disentangled covariates, enabling shape reconstruction, transfer, evolution, and disentanglement. Evaluations are conducted on Starman (simulated 2D), ADNI hippocampus (3D), and pediatric airway (3D) datasets, showing strong reconstruction while retaining interpretability. All reviewers found the paper well-written and easy to follow, with sufficient experiments and clear visualizations. Scores were 6, 6, 6, and 8; one reviewer (score 8, high confidence) was particularly positive about the contribution and potential, while the others (score 6) were supportive but raised specific technical concerns.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a significant gap: quantitatively capturing shape changes with respect to problem-specific covariates in an interpretable manner.
- The method is reasonable, and claims are backed by both quantitative and qualitative evidence using standard metrics (Chamfer distance, Earth mover’s distance, Hausdorff distance).
- The approach leverages neural additive deep implicit representations, offering interpretability and resolution-agnostic shape reconstructions.
- Comparisons with state-of-the-art implicit shape representation methods (e.g., A-SDF, Mu 2021) demonstrate competitive or superior performance.
- Experiments are thorough, covering simulated and real medical datasets, with extensive supplementary details.
- Visualizations are clear, and code is provided in the supplementary material.
- The paper is well-structured, logical, and easy to follow; related works and their limitations are comprehensively mentioned (noted by two reviewers).

### Weaknesses
- **Research survey and related work limitations:** The survey of related models is relatively limited, focusing primarily on implicit function representations. For instance, parameterized shape editing work such as Wei (3DV 2020) is missing. The relationship to A-SDF (Mu 2021) is not clearly described; the paper should explicitly state the methodological advantages of NAISR over A-SDF and what specific limitations of A-SDF are addressed by the additive deformation model.
- **Algorithm and training details insufficient:**  
  - More details on training the additive model \( g_i \) are needed.  
  - The loss function comprises six components, each with a scalar coefficient. Ablation is only in supplementary (Tables S.12, S.13) and does not clearly explain the interplay between loss terms, effects of coefficient values on reconstruction vs. disentanglement vs. transfer, or sensitivity to these hyperparameters.  
  - SIREN backbone details are missing: specific architecture (number of layers, neurons per layer), weight/bias initialization strategy crucial for convergence, and how these choices impact learning.  
  - The atlas initialization is not described; showing the learned atlas across epochs would be helpful.  
  - The paper assumes pre-aligned and centered shapes; this requirement should be explicitly stated in the problem description (Section 3.1).
- **Assumptions about covariates and shape distribution not thoroughly examined:**  
  - The model assumes a unimodal population representable by a single learned atlas deforming according to covariates. Its ability to handle multimodal distributions or topological variations is unclear (e.g., both healthy and diseased shapes may not be captured by a single template).  
  - It is unclear what happens when a key covariate is unknown (e.g., not provided in metadata) or an irrelevant covariate is included. Does the unknown variation get absorbed into the latent code \( z \), potentially confounding interpretation? Does the model effectively ignore irrelevant covariates?  
  - The use of a non-amortized latent code (optimized during training and inference) is not adequately justified compared to using a neural network to estimate latent codes.
- **Evaluation gaps:**  
  - The model's performance under limited training sample sizes (common in medical imaging) is not evaluated.  
  - Statistical aspects of the learned model (e.g., dimensionality and distribution of the latent space) are not analyzed.  
  - Computational complexity and runtime for training, inference, shape transfer, evolution, and reconstruction are not discussed.  
  - Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is missing, making it difficult to assess robustness and reproducibility.
- **Practical utility for medical imaging not convincingly demonstrated:** The reconstructions smooth out fine details compared to the gold standard (e.g., Figure 2), yet these details may be clinically significant (e.g., bronchial variations in airways). The paper does not clarify how the trade-off between smoothing and detail preservation is addressed or why the level of smoothing is appropriate for medical applications.
- **Minor:** Limitations (e.g., invertibility problem) are only discussed in the supplementary material; they should be in the main manuscript. The conclusion ends with a reference to the supplementary, which is undesirable.

**Note:** One reviewer (score 8, high confidence) was especially positive about the contributions and potential impact, while the other three reviewers (score 6) supported the work but raised more substantive technical concerns.
