## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a randomized smoothing framework to provide certified robustness guarantees for combinatorial optimization (CO) solvers, along with an enhancement method that leverages “easy instances.” Experiments are conducted on DAG scheduling and TSP tasks. While one reviewer (R2, score 1) finds the paper severely lacking in methodological clarity, proper definitions, and appropriate experimental validation, others (R1 score 5, R4 score 6) see novelty and potential in the approach despite noting substantial limitations. A third reviewer (R3, score 3) acknowledges the theoretical ambition but points out numerous technical and notational problems.

### Strengths
- Novel exploration of certified robustness for CO solvers, which is an understudied area. (R1, R3, R4)
- Adaptation of randomized smoothing to combinatorial problems, respecting constraints and non-categorical outputs. (R1)
- The method is theoretically grounded and could apply to dynamic optimization tasks. (R1)
- The enhancement method using “easy instances” suggests a way to improve robustness without retraining. (R1)
- Provides a theoretically sound robustness certification guarantee, useful when a priori guarantees are required. (R3)
- Describes how to use the guarantee to produce provably robust solvers via randomized smoothing; this is arguable the main contribution. (R3)
- Paper is generally well-written with clear explanations. (R4)
- Focuses on a foundational framework study. (R4)
- Experiments are conducted on representative problems (DAG scheduling, TSP). (R4)
- The direction of formal guarantees for trained ML models in CO is relevant. (R2, despite many criticisms)

### Weaknesses
- **Generalizability**: The method is primarily evaluated on DAG scheduling and TSP; it is unclear how it extends to structurally richer CO problems or multi-objective cost functions. (R1, R4)  
  - *R4 specifically asks why the MC problem from ROCO was not included.*
- **Robustness metric limitations**: The use of Wasserstein distance may be too restrictive for CO tasks where cost functions or constraints differ significantly. (R1)  
  - The integral in Theorem 4.1 (an integral of a CDF) may be infinite, raising a theoretical concern. (R2)  
  - Absolute values taken of CDFs are unnecessary since CDFs are non-negative. (R3)
- **Empirical evaluation weaknesses**:  
  - Only specific perturbation strategies (random search, simulated annealing) are tested; adaptive or adversarial perturbations are missing. (R1)  
  - Experiments are incompletely stated and not extensive; details are relegated to the appendix. (R2, R3)  
  - TSP instances with 100 cities are very small; the authors should consider much larger instances from TSPLib (tens of thousands of cities) that are hard to solve. (R2)  
  - It is extremely hard to understand how TSP instances are generated, and no mathematical formulation of the problems or data distribution is given. (R2)  
  - The solver selection for DAG scheduling does not include state-of-the-art or learning-based solvers. (R4)  
  - No guidance is provided on how to choose parameters *m* and *β* in practice. (R3)  
  - The denominator in the ratios of Figure 1 is unclear, and “SFT” (used as baseline in line 431) is not defined. (R3)
- **Definitions and theoretical framework clarity**:  
  - The paper lacks a principled, self‑contained definition of robustness for CO solvers. (R2, R3)  
  - The “certified radius” is not properly defined; it is merely stated as an inequality. (R3)  
  - The statement in line 186 that “finding the optimal solution for a given problem instance is often NP-hard” is incorrect; the problem that is NP-hard is to solve *any* instance. (R3)  
  - The claim (line 187) that “no polynomial-time algorithm exists” relies on the P≠NP assumption. (R3)  
  - Many notations are ill‑defined or never defined: the “strictness” of a constraint; the underlying probability distribution for the random variable in Eq. (4); variable *v* in Eq. (5) (should it be *u*?); the construction of matrix *H_b* from general constraints; *g_θ* in Eq. (6). (R2, R3)  
  - Algorithm 1 is called “Randomized Smoothing…” but outputs a distance; it is unclear what has been smoothed. (R3)  
  - The Robustness Enhancement Method (Algorithm 2, Section 4.2) is not explained: why searching for an easy instance enhances robustness? (R3)  
  - Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 define *F(r)* but never use it afterward. (R3)
- **Missing literature connections**: The paper does not discuss the large body of work on robust optimization, nor does it differentiate itself from it. (R2)  
  - The authors are encouraged to review *Compact Optimality Verification for Optimization Proxies* (Chen et al., 2024) and related work. (R2)
- **Specific technical issues**:  
  - The CDF in Eq. (4) appears to be defined w.r.t. a distribution over *Q*, but that distribution is never specified; the source of randomness is unclear. (R2, R3)  
  - In Algorithm 1, “F_{H_b + z^{(j)}}” should likely use ⊕ instead of +. (R3)  
  - Theorem 4.1 bounds the distance between smoothed distributions *G* but never refers to the original cost CDF *F*, causing confusion. (R3)  
  - Citation style is incorrect in places (R3 minor, but indicative of presentation issues).
- **Reviewer disagreement on clarity and methodology**: One reviewer (R2) finds the paper’s logic hard to understand and the overall methodology without a principled framework, while another reviewer (R4) calls the paper well‑written and clear. (R2 vs R4)
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms** (to be treated as weaknesses):  
  - Does the model handle adversarial perturbations, or only random perturbations? (R1)  
  - How does the enhancement method scale with increased constraint complexity? (R1)  
  - Can the robustness guarantees extend to multi-objective cost functions? (R1)  
  - How does this work differ from the robust optimization literature? (R2)  
  - Why was the MC problem from ROCO not selected? (R4)
