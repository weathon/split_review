## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an information-theoretic framework to evaluate text explanations: a relevance score (I(X;E)) and an informativeness score (I(Y;E)). It applies these to rationale and natural language explanations (NLE) on the e-SNLI dataset using multiple embedding models (RoBERTa, OpenAI, Cohere). The reviews are split: two reviewers (8,8) find the approach novel and promising; two (5,5) question its clarity, human validation, and practical novelty.

### Strengths
- Novel attempt to apply information-theoretic measures (mutual information approximations) to evaluate text explanations; only one prior paper with similar concepts in a different context was found (Reviewer 2).
- The framework considers the post-hoc text pipeline as a series of communication channels and quantifies two key aspects: relevance (input–explanan) and predictive informativeness (explanan–target) (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- The paper demonstrates feasibility of using information-theoretic tools in this nascent field, which is a worthwhile contribution (Reviewer 1).
- The framework contemplates a high degree of automation, making it feasible to deploy in production settings (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments include three embedding models (RoBERTa, OpenAI, Cohere) to test generalizability of the scores, which depend on embedding quality (Reviewer 3).
- Two common explanation types are considered: tokenwise rationale and natural language explanations (NLE) (Reviewer 3).
- Good overview of related work, claims supported with experimental results, limitations acknowledged, and presentation clear (Reviewer 2). [Note: Reviewer 3 and 4 disagree on clarity and support; see weaknesses.]

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear experimental design and reporting** (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). The structure of Sections 4 and 5 is confusing: the experimental design (rationale, procedure, aims, metrics) is not clearly described; it reads more as “Results and Evaluation” than a description of experiments. It is unclear how the dataset, silver labels, and language models come together in the evaluation pipeline (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). The goal of the paper is not clear until the end (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of human validation** (Reviewer 3). The paper claims to unify evaluation of rationale and NLE, but the results show the proposed metrics do not have consistent meaning for human interpretation. No user study is conducted to validate alignment with human judgments of explanation quality. This is a significant gap, especially for natural language explanations where human understanding is paramount.
- **Limited novelty and utility of the proposed scores** (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4). The relevance score I(X;E) correlates highly with lexical overlap and embedding similarity, raising questions about whether it captures deeper semantic relevance or only surface similarity. The paper does not explore limitations of using embedding similarity as a proxy for relevance (e.g., semantically relevant words may not be close in embedding space). The informativeness score also does not clearly outperform existing metrics (e.g., ROUGE for NLE) (Reviewer 4). Overall, the paper has limited innovation and usefulness (Reviewer 4).
- **Methodological questions about mutual information estimation** (Reviewer 4). (a) Estimating MI via InfoNCE requires training—what data and what training process? (b) The appendix compares estimators (CLUB, SMILE, InfoNCE). According to the CLUB paper, its estimate should be higher than true MI, so how do the authors judge that InfoNCE is more accurate? (c) Why use V-information instead of standard mutual information? (d) How does the method depend on accuracy of the MI estimator, and what are its advantages over traditional evaluation methods?
- **Presentation and naming issues** (Reviewer 3). The term “informativeness” is used both for the proposed I(Y;E) and for a GPTScore evaluation item, causing confusion. “Informativeness” is missing from Table1’s x-axis in Figure3. The x-axis order in Figure3 should match Table1. Figures 5 and 6 should be moved to main text; prompt templates and multimodal discussion could go to appendix. Proper references for statistics in Sections 4.3, 5.1, and 5.2 are missing (e.g., “16% and 21% of the variance” claims without clear citation to figures).
- **Modest conclusions and limited insight** (Reviewer 1). The findings are preliminary; the paper shows feasibility but gives limited actionable insight.
- **Minor technical issues** (Reviewer 2). Why use Spearman instead of Kendall correlation? Were Spearman assumptions checked? Figure 4: two plots with identical descriptions but captions refer to different concepts (informativeness vs. rationale).
