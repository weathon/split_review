## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper curates 75 misinformation datasets, evaluates 35 claim-based datasets for label quality, spurious correlations, and political bias, provides LLM-based baselines, and argues that categorical labels are suboptimal, proposing an entailment-based metric using fact-check articles. Reviewers noted the comprehensive collection and useful insights but raised concerns about novelty, narrow scope, methodological flaws, and conference fit. One reviewer was more positive (score 5), the other three were critical (scores 3).

### Strengths
- Large-scale curation and analysis: Provides the largest collection (75 datasets) and thoroughly evaluates 35 claim-based datasets for label quality, spurious keyword/temporal correlations, and political bias.
- Useful insights in the Data Quality Section, especially on spurious correlations, which future research should consider.
- Attempts to standardize a labeling schema across datasets, potentially facilitating cross-dataset comparisons.
- Novel proposal in Section 5.2: moves beyond binary categorical labels to an entailment-based evaluation using fact-check articles, addressing fundamental labeling ambiguity. One reviewer found this the most significant and original contribution.
- Critical examination of label quality and discussion of alternative evaluation methods.

### Weaknesses
- Narrow focus on claim-based datasets limits generalizability to broader misinformation detection (e.g., narrative manipulation, misleading framing); title and scope may misrepresent the work as covering all misinformation detection when it primarily addresses fact-checking.
- Insufficient exploration of foundational labeling concepts (credibility, reliability, source accuracy); no discussion of definitions of misinformation vs. disinformation vs. fake news, causing conflation (e.g., line 214 switches to "disinformation"). Label simplification across datasets likely oversimplifies differences in what is being labeled.
- Lack of in-depth dataset comparison; does not clearly indicate which datasets are suitable for specific research questions.
- Limited discussion on modality and data evolution: overlooks the shift towards multi-modal (video/image) misinformation and challenges from generative AI, crucial for future research.
- Political bias analysis (Section 4.4) is itself potentially biased due to using LLMs that lean liberal (e.g., ChatGPT); reliability and robustness are questionable.
- LLM-based baseline (Table 6) is not competitive; using web search may allow LLMs to cheat by finding answers online. The authors acknowledge this is only a lower bound, but a standard misinformation detection baseline (e.g., Shu et al. 2017) should be included.
- Lists labeling approach limitations (Section 4.1) but then uses original labels in evaluations without proposing solutions to the labeling problem.
- No consideration of temporal drift: claims initially considered misinformation may become true over time (e.g., COVID lab leak hypothesis) but is not controlled for.
- Structure and flow: some sections repetitive, especially in labeling and quality assessment discussions.
- Conference fit: two reviewers explicitly state the paper is outside ICLR's scope (a dataset survey, not methodological contribution); recommended submission to a workshop or journal; authors did not justify suitability.
- Novelty: prior surveys (D'Ulizia et al., Sharm et al.) already exist; collecting more datasets is not sufficiently novel. The novel elements (e.g., binary labeling flaw) are confined to a small subsection and not the main focus.
- Contrast: One reviewer (score 5, confidence 4) praised the comprehensive analysis and discussion on quality assessment; the other three (scores 3) found the paper lacking in novelty, scope, methodology, and conference fit.
