## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a hybrid fine-tuning approach that jointly updates both full LLM parameters (via zeroth-order optimization) and PEFT modules (via first-order optimization). It introduces a "hybrid generalized smoothness" concept for convergence analysis and provides empirical results on three small downsampled datasets. There is significant disagreement among reviewers: two reviewers (R2 and R3) gave a score of 5, while two (R1 and R4) gave a score of 3, reflecting conflicting views on novelty, theoretical depth, and experimental strength.

### Strengths

- Combining a zeroth-order method with a PEFT method for LLM fine-tuning is a feasible approach (R1).
- The paper discusses convergence of the proposed method under a novel "hybrid generalized smoothness" assumption (R1, R4). Reviewer 3 specifically highlights this concept as a novel expansion of classical optimization theory that accounts for heterogeneous dynamics of joint LLM and PEFT training and is versatile to other settings (e.g., layer-wise fine-tuning or models with trainable external modules).
- The observation on learning rate size (different learning rates for different parameter parts) is interesting (R4).
- Empirical results are provided on small datasets (R1, R4).
- The paper is well written and easy to follow (R4); however, R1 finds it not well written (see Weaknesses).
- Convergence analysis and empirical results are provided (R4).

### Weaknesses

- **Lack of technical novelty.** The hybrid approach is described as a direct, trivial combination of zeroth-order optimization for full parameters and first-order optimization for PEFT modules, with the only distinction being different learning rates—a commonly known and well-accepted practice (R1, R2, R4). R2 calls it a “trivial trick.” No specific details are given on how weight updates from both methods are integrated (e.g., obtaining ΔW₁ from zeroth-order and ΔW₂ from PEFT, then updating W) or how conflicts are resolved (R1).  
- **Theoretical contribution not significant.** Most lemmas and theorems are derivable from Li et al. (2024) (R1). The “hybrid generalized smoothness” is a fine-grained version of regular smoothness that treats coordinates separately but does not introduce a paradigm shift (R4). The convergence guarantee is essentially a variant of SGD with zeroth-order noise and coordinate-dependent learning rates, which is well studied in zeroth-order optimization literature (R4). Reviewer 3 disagrees and finds the concept novel, but that view is not shared by R1 or R4.  
- **Insufficient experiments.** Experiments are conducted on only three small downsampled datasets, which are too easy for billion-parameter models and limited in size and quantity (R1, R4). The proposed method does not significantly surpass baseline methods (R3). Comparisons with advanced zeroth-order methods (e.g., [1]) and first-order methods like SGD or Adam as references are missing (R1, R3). The experimental setup, comparison methods, and result discussion indicate inadequate training; the paper largely repeats MeZO experiments (R1).  
- **Missing critical comparisons.** No memory or wall-clock time comparison is provided, even though the hybrid approach’s efficiency is a key motivation (R1, R4). The zero-order optimizer and first-order optimizer used are not explained (R1). Table 1 methods do not align with those discussed in the experimental details; proper citations are needed (R1).  
- **Technical errors and unclear presentation.** The statement above Eq.(2) is incorrect: one-sided and two-sided gradient estimators have different forms and cannot be expressed by a single equation (R1). The paper is not well written, lacks conciseness, and needs necessary experiments (R1).  
- **Unanswered questions that reflect criticisms.** (R3) Why not train each module sequentially with distinct learning rates? What are the benefits of mixing them within the same training phase? (R3) In Figure 3 of Appendix D.2, why does the accuracy rate of hybrid tuning show a decreasing trend over time steps? (R4) What is the point of random shuffling in Algorithm 1? (R1) Are memory and computation costs recorded beyond accuracy?
