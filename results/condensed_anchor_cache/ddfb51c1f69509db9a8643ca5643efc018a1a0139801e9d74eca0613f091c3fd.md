## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes DISK, an end-to-end method for inferring spurious (domain) labels using KL-divergence between a training set (with spurious correlation) and a validation set (without that correlation). The inferred labels are then used for subsampling and high-confidence selection (HCS) to mitigate subpopulation shift. The paper evaluates on synthetic 2D data, MNIST, F-MNIST, CIFAR, and CelebA. Reviewers generally agree the problem is important. However, three reviewers (scores 3,3,3) express significant concerns about assumptions, methodology clarity, missing baselines, and experimental completeness. One reviewer (score 6) was notably more positive, praising the solid proofs and well-designed experiments, though still raising several issues.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles an important and extremely challenging problem: inferring spurious correlations without domain labels (R1, R2, R4).
- The method is intuitive and the paper is well-written and easy to understand (R2).
- The work is relatively solid, with detailed proofs and necessary experiments attached (R3).
- Experiments include synthetic 2D data and real-world datasets; the in-depth analysis of DISK’s ability to infer spurious labels and its consistency with human judgment is appreciated (R3, R4).
- The comparison of neural-network-based DISK with human decisions on complex spurious features is a contribution; the discussion of potential integration with human decisions when domain information is available is insightful (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Assumption of a validation set with different spurious correlations** – The method assumes a validation set where the training set’s spurious correlation is absent or different. Constructing such a validation set without knowing the spurious correlation is non-trivial (R1). The premise requires knowledge of subgroups during data curation, contradicting the motivation to eliminate group labels (R4). The method also implicitly assumes prior knowledge of the number of spurious values (to learn *w*), unlike methods like JTT (R2). The sensitivity to how different the validation set must be is not theoretically or empirically characterized (R2). The discrepancy between training and validation correlations could lead to data leakage (R3).

2. **Motivation and soundness of the KL-divergence objective** – The KL maximization objective (Equation 2) aims to maximize label distribution difference between inferred domains, but spurious correlations do not necessarily affect the label distribution (e.g., two domains with identical label distributions but different spurious correlations, R1). The choice of KL divergence over other divergences (e.g., Wasserstein) is not justified (R1). The theoretical basis of the High Confidence Selection (HCS) module and how the confidence threshold is defined are not provided (R3).

3. **Definition and inference of spurious labels** – Definition 1 states a spurious label indicates the category of an instance, but the abstract says it is also the domain label, which does not indicate category (R1). The technical steps of how to obtain the spurious label \(y_s\) from \(f_{DISK}\) are not described (R1, R3). Why both spurious and invariant representations are considered, and the advantage over simpler methods like clustering, is not explained (R3). The calculation of cross-entropy H and conditional densities in Equations (4)-(6) is unclear, and the notation for \(y^{tr/val}\) and \(y_{s,w}^{tr/val}\) is not well-defined (R4).

4. **Insufficient comparison with relevant baselines** – The experiments focus on baselines that require actual spurious labels (e.g., DFR). Many existing methods for estimating spurious labels without group labels (e.g., clustering-based: No Subclass Left Behind; loss-based: JTT, Learning from Failure, Spread Spurious Attribute; and EIIL for group discovery) should be primary baselines (R2, R4). Missing comparisons include JTT, CnC, and EIIL (R2). It is unclear whether IRM is applicable to the MNIST/CIFAR experiments (R1). The method should also be evaluated on NLP datasets (MultiNLI, CivilComments) to test generalization (R2).

5. **Incomplete experimental evaluation and analysis** – Ablation studies are needed on the strategy for determining spurious labels and the necessity of KL-divergence (R3). The performance advantage over baselines is not evident; on CelebA the analysis is unconvincing and appears speculative, suggesting lack of universality (R3). The proposed metric (Definition 2) is flawed because it can be inflated by inferring very few minority samples; intersection-over-union and inference accuracy should be reported (R2). Subsampling combined with HCS may severely limit performance when subgroups are substantially underrepresented, making imbalance worse; the limit of subgroup imbalance ratio DISK can handle should be discussed (R4). Sensitivity to hyperparameters (e.g., \(\gamma\)) is not shown; a sweep over \(\gamma\) with WGA should be provided (R2). The validation sets used for each dataset and their degree of difference from the training set should be described (R2). The method for hyperparameter selection in Table 3 likely requires a labeled validation set and should be clarified (R2). Other second-stage methods beyond subsampling (e.g., GroupDRO) should be considered (R2). In Figure 3, reporting the accuracy of the classifier in distinguishing the spurious correlation would be clearer (R2). Estimated spurious labels should be visualized on the toy data (R4).

6. **Presentation and clarity issues** – The problem description mixes subpopulation shift with domain adaptation/generalization, missing the important distinction that in subpopulation shift domain and core labels are highly correlated (R4). The related work section could be better organized to highlight motivation and advantages over prior work (R3). A figure summarizing the DISK framework is lacking (R3). Symbols should be gathered in a table (R3).

7. **Additional requested analyses** – Theoretical and empirical characterization of how different the validation set must be for the method to be effective (R2). Behavior under multiple spurious correlations needs analysis; the method might latch onto a single spurious feature while ignoring others (R2). Clarification on whether the method can handle very large or unknown numbers of spurious labels (R2). Performance of baselines when using the training and validation sets as two domains (no exact domain info) should be reported (R1). How DISK works when data are labeled with domain information, partially carry it, or have none at all (R3). Discussion of data leakage due to discrepancy between training and validation sets (R3).
