- Scores: 3, 8, 8, 8

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper presents OMNI-EPIC, a framework that uses foundation models (LLMs and VLMs) to generate environment code and reward functions for reinforcement learning, guided by model of interestingness filters. Reviewers are highly polarized: three reviewers (R2, R3, R4) rate the paper 8 and see it as an original, ambitious step toward open-ended learning, while one reviewer (R1) rates it 3, citing major concerns in scholarship, evaluation, and claims. All reviewers agree that the idea is promising but that the experimental results do not substantiate the strong claims made.

### Strengths
- The idea of using code as an environment representation is more expressive than prior work that changes hand-designed features within constrained environments (R2).
- The full pipeline can theoretically run for a long time to continuously generate novel tasks, which is a valuable direction for the open-endedness community (R2, R3).
- OMNI-EPIC successfully employs foundation models to leverage notions of human interestingness, a key challenge in unsupervised environment design. It provides an excellent example of using foundation models for this purpose (R3).
- The paper is generally well written, well motivated, and the methods are described in detail with a clean appendix (R3, R4). Generated tasks are fun and the accompanying website is appreciated (R4).
- The paper tackles an ambitious problem—automatically generating diverse tasks for RL agents—and the use of foundation models is a promising approach (R1). (Note: R1’s positive assessment is limited; contrast with his overall negative view.)

### Weaknesses
**1. Claims vs. Experimental Support**
- The paper repeatedly claims that OMNI-EPIC can generate an “endless”/“never-ending” stream of novel and learnable environments (e.g., L082, L151, L424). However, experiments only show generation of 200 environments (with assumed solvability) and only 22 tasks when RL agents are actually trained. No evidence is provided that this process continues indefinitely or maintains novelty and learnability over longer runs (R3, R4).
- Claims about “never-ending curriculum learning” are unsupported because the paper does not train a single generalist agent, does not measure transfer learning (agents are initialized from nearest solved task but this is not tested), does not show difficulty increase over time, and does not verify that the task generator actually optimizes for learning progress (R4).
- The paper states that training a generalist agent on OMNI-EPIC-generated tasks “would be straightforward” but provides no evidence for this; training RL agents is known to be tricky (catastrophic forgetting, saturated networks) (R1).
- One reviewer (R1) notes that the experiments are constrained to one specific simulator and discrete action space, making it unclear how this contributes to the grand goals of open-ended learning.

**2. Scholarship and Presentation Issues**
- The writing is sloppy and uses imprecise language. Examples:
  - “Because the model has distilled a sense of interesting from reading the internet” – models do not read; they are trained on internet data (R1, R2 similarly).
  - “Since code is Turing complete” – many programming languages exist that are not Turing complete; Python being Turing complete does not guarantee that the foundation model can generate any computable environment (R1, R3).
- The paper lacks comparison with the large body of work on procedural content generation (PCG) (R1). It also fails to acknowledge the older and wider field of intrinsic motivations (Schmidhuber 1991, Oudeyer & Kaplan 2007, competence progress, etc.) which is foundational for the presented work (R4).
- Figure 2 is very hard to understand and parse (R2).
- The “cell coverage of archive diversity” metric is not explained in the main text (R3).
- Some references have inconsistent formatting (R3). Typos: “narrow distributions of environment” → +s, “OMNI-EPIC continuously generate” → generates (R4).

**3. Evaluation Deficiencies**
- The evaluation focuses on proxy metrics of environment diversity instead of directly evaluating whether generated environments actually improve RL agent learning (R1). The paper justifies this by citing limited computational resources, but acceptance of a paper that does not evaluate its core claims is not justified (R1).
- Success is measured by a VLM, but this VLM is not validated against human judgments. If the signal is unreliable (as the paper itself suggests when using VLMs elsewhere), the final metric of number of solved tasks is also unreliable (R4). The authors state that current VLMs are not accurate enough for success detection, yet use them anyway – why? (R3, R4)
- The long run without learning experiment (200 environments) does not measure how many tasks are actually learnable. There is a high chance that executable code produces impossible tasks. The paper should report the proportion of tasks that compile, are accepted by MoI, and are solved (R4).
- The diversity metric in Figure 4 is computed on all generated tasks, not filtered for solvability. The community cares more about diversity of learned behaviors (R4). The PCA discretization size may affect results (R4).
- The MoI (model of interestingness) is not validated against human judgments. For example, in Figure 3, changing the surface form of the goal (e.g., “jumping across platform” above water vs. above lava) is considered novel, but these changes do not affect the task, only background color (R4).

**4. Open-endedness and Novelty Concerns**
- The system may simply be retrieving and recombining environments that exist in the LLM’s training data (e.g., from GitHub). This would make it a retrieval system bounded by human-created content, contrary to the open-ended goal (R1). The paper does not consider this possibility (R1).
- Relying solely on LLMs raises questions about novelty generation: will the LLM ever generate truly novel environments or will it be limited by the biases in its training data? Can it generate environments that are very different (e.g., switching from physics-based R2D2 to a game like Pong)? (R2, R3)
- The similarity-based conditioning on nearby tasks could lead to cyclic behavior: if the archive contains two clusters, the generator may oscillate between them and never generate anything new (R2). Does the method avoid this? (R2)
- The open-endedness claim is supported only by Figure 4, which shows 13 environments generated before plateau; this is insufficient and the decision mechanism may be unstable (R4).

**5. Technical Robustness and Missing Ablations**
- OMNI-EPIC relies on multiple LLMs working together, which can be brittle. No experiments report the ratio of successful vs. failed attempts (e.g., code that compiles, passes MoI, is solvable) (R3, R4).
- The paper should conduct ablations of the task generator:
  - Does the LP (learning progress) prompt actually generate tasks more tailored to the agent?
  - Does combining it with success/failure feedback help generate more solvable tasks?
  - Does conditioning on nearby tasks improve performance over uniform sampling? (R4)
- It is unclear what happens to tasks that fail to compile: are they added to the archive as failed? If not, how is this negative feedback routed back to the generator? Should “failed because of compilation” and “failed because agent couldn’t solve” be distinguished? (R4)
- Is the code open source? Reproducibility requires releasing the code (R2, R4).
- The paper does not explain why previous attempts at optimizing against definitions of interestingness have resulted in pathologies and how OMNI-EPIC overcomes these (R4).

**6. Specific Weaknesses from Minority Reviewer (R1)**
- R1 rates the paper 3 (while the other three rate 8) and emphasizes that the paper reads like an informal report, making it difficult to pinpoint the problem and claims. The scholarship is insufficient for publication in its current form. R1 also questions the definition of “Darwin-completeness” and how it relates to evolution and open-endedness.
