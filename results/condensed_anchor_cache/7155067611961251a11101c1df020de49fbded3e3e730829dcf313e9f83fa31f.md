- Decision: Accept
- Scores: 8, 6, 8, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a subquadratic-time algorithm for computing Gaussian kernel matrix-vector products under a new “heaviness” assumption (sum of entries scales linearly with n). The algorithm combines hashing to find heavy keys and random sampling for light keys, achieving time Õ(d n^{1.89}/ε²) with success probability 0.99. It is the first subquadratic algorithm for unrestricted vectors (allowing negative entries) and separates additive‑error from relative‑error models. The assumption is validated on a BERT model. Scores were 8, 6, 8, 6: two reviewers found the contribution strong and novel, while two others rated it lower, citing overclaimed contributions, insufficient empirical validation, and limited comparison with prior work.

### Strengths
- **First subquadratic for unrestricted vectors.** The algorithm is the first to run in subquadratic time for kernel matrix‑vector multiplication with general (unrestricted) vectors, unlike Backurs et al. (2021) which required non‑negative x. (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Novel subclass and separation of error models.** Points out an important subclass (weight distribution) that admits faster algorithms while covering many real datasets. It also formally separates additive‑error (allowing negative entries) from relative‑error settings, providing the first such algorithm for the former. (Reviewer 1)
- **Strong practical motivation and assumption validation.** The work is motivated by fast attention in LLMs and validates the core assumption on BERT, which supports real‑world applicability. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Technical contribution to hashing‑based kernel algorithms.** Builds on and improves Backurs et al. (2021) using LSH‑based heavy‑key finding and random sampling, contributing to the growing literature on hashing for high‑dimensional kernel computation. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Efficiency improvement for applicable instances.** When both Backurs et al. (2021) and the new algorithm apply, the latter is more efficient. (Reviewer 1)

### Weaknesses
- **Overclaimed contributions.** Despite the split (two reviewers gave 8, two gave 6), two reviewers (3, 4) noted that the title and text (e.g., “first algorithm for high dimensional approximate KMV that runs in subquadratic time for general vectors”) overstate the novelty. Low‑rank decompositions via random Fourier features achieve similar ends under different assumptions/guarantees; the paper should emphasize its conditional assumption and position itself more carefully. (Reviewers 3, 4)
- **Insufficient empirical validation.** 
  - Assumption A is only tested on BERT, not on other architectures (RoBERTa, GPT), so claims of broad applicability to modern transformer‑based models are not fully substantiated. (Reviewer 2)
  - The paper reports only the maximum of the heavy‑entries ratio over all sequence lengths, but does not show how this ratio varies with n, which is needed to verify that the assumption is independent of n. (Reviewer 3)
  - No wall‑clock time experiments are provided; subquadratic complexity does not guarantee a fast algorithm in practice (especially for moderate n). Practical speedups remain unvalidated. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4)
- **Missing comparisons and clarifications with prior work.**
  - Alman & Song (2023, published at NeurIPS 2023; cited as 2024) achieve nearly linear O(n^{1+o(1)}) for attention approximation under d=O(log n), while the paper’s approach allows d=o(log² n) but has a worse exponent. The practical trade‑off is not discussed; one reviewer questions why a KDE approach is needed if Alman & Song already give nearly linear time. (Reviewer 2)
  - Core techniques (heavy keys via LSH, random sampling) are slight adaptations of Charikar et al. (FOCS20); the paper could better highlight what is genuinely new. (Reviewer 1)
  - The relationship to other easy subclasses (e.g., stable‑rank constraints) and to known fine‑grained lower bounds (Backurs et al. 2017, Alman‑Song 2024, Alman‑Guan 2024) is not addressed; doing so would clarify the power of the new subclass. (Reviewer 1)
- **Theoretical gaps and missing details.**
  - The running time’s dependence on the constant c in Assumption A is not given; it may be exp(poly(c)), which could be large if c is moderately larger (e.g., 10, 50). (Reviewer 4)
  - Theorem 1.1 gives only constant probability; can it be boosted with logarithmic dependence on the failure probability? This is important for many‑query settings (training, serving). (Reviewer 4)
  - Space complexity is not discussed; it may be O(n²), which is a practical concern. (Reviewer 3)
  - Do the results imply improved results for KDE or the power method? (Reviewer 4)
  - Can one trade off time complexity and success probability, e.g., by changing the exponent on n? (Reviewer 3)
- **Presentation issues.**
  - Algorithm 3 appears only on page 9; a schematic in Section 1.3 would be helpful. (Reviewer 3)
  - Sections 3 and 4 could be swapped for better flow. (Reviewer 3)
  - Line 151: should be ∥·∥₂², not ∥·∥₂. (Reviewer 4)
  - Alman & Song (2024) should be cited as 2023 (NeurIPS 2023). (Reviewer 2)
- **Limited impact on attention approximation.** 
  - The paper does not actually demonstrate how the algorithm improves attention computation or evaluate it on transformer tasks. Zandieh et al. (2023) already handle arbitrary Q,K,V without non‑negativity constraints; one reviewer questions what new benefit this algorithm provides for attention approximation. (Reviewer 2)
- **Lack of ablation and experimental comparisons.** No running‑time comparisons with Backurs et al., Alman & Song, or random Fourier features are provided. (Reviewer 2) Also, an alternative formulation for Lemma 3.1 using diagonal matrices (K_SM = diag(exp(q_i²/2)) K_G diag(exp(k_j²/2)) would be simpler; the authors should justify their choice. (Reviewer 3)
