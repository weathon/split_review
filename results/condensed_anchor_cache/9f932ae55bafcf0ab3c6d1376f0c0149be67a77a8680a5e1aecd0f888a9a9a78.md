## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies local label differential privacy (LDP) in multi-class learning. It proposes a vector approximation mechanism that converts each label into a K-dimensional vector by applying binary randomized response to each bit of the one-hot encoding. The method is simple to implement, introduces minimal computational overhead, and is shown to outperform prior methods (LP-MST, ALIBI, RRWithPrior) on synthetic data (mixture of Gaussians) and real datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100). The authors provide a theoretical analysis for k-NN showing that excess risk scales roughly as \(\sqrt{\ln K}\) with the number of classes. Three reviewers rated the paper 8, one rated it 6, with the lower rating reflecting concerns about novelty, practical applicability, and theoretical justification.

### Strengths
- The paper studies an important and well-defined problem: label DP for large multi-class datasets, which has practical relevance for applications with long-tailed data distributions.
- The proposed vector approximation method is simple, easy to implement, and introduces little additional computational overhead relative to prior methods (e.g., ALIBI requires Bayesian inference and softmax normalization).
- The method clearly outperforms existing baselines (LP-MST, ALIBI) on standard benchmarks, especially when the number of classes \(K\) is large and the privacy budget \(\varepsilon\) is small.
- The evaluation protocol is solid and follows standard practices; experiments include synthetic data, MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100.
- The paper is well-organized and easy to follow; it provides necessary proofs of privacy guarantees and general intuition.
- The theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) shows that performance degrades only slightly with \(K\) (roughly \(\sqrt{\ln K}\)), which is well-suited to the multi-class setting.

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical analysis is incomplete.** All reviewers call for stronger theoretical justification:
  - The k-NN analysis (Appendix B.1) does not show how excess risk scales with the sample size \(N\) (e.g., as \(N\to\infty\)); the scaling with \(k\) (neighborhood size) may bring in dimensionality factors. A more complete picture, similar to the \(K/\sqrt{N}\) and \(\sqrt{K/N}\) bounds from prior work, would be valuable. (Reviewer 1)
  - The analysis focuses on dependence on \(K\) but fails to compare error bounds with ALIBI, which also uses a vector representation. An explicit comparison of how the proposed method’s bounds relate to those of ALIBI is missing and would provide a more direct and relevant comparison. (Reviewer 2)
  - The claim that vector encoding is superior to scalar encoding lacks rigorous justification. A theoretical result analogous to Theorem 2 applied to randomized response over the simplex would support a comparative analysis of the privacy-utility tradeoff. (Reviewer 3)
  - Only an upper bound is given; lower bounds for previous methods that use scalar labels alone are not provided, which would clearly demonstrate the necessity of the proposed method and potentially show a phase transition between \(\log K\) and \(K\) dependence. (Reviewer 4)
- **Novelty is questioned by one reviewer (Reviewer 3).** The method combines RAPPOR (Erlingsson et al., 2014) with the “soft label” approach of ALIBI (Malek Esmaeili et al., 2021). While two reviewers (1 and 2) consider this combination a legitimate contribution, Reviewer 3 notes that the authors themselves acknowledge prior use (lines 232–233) and finds the core mechanism not new, raising concern about the contribution.
- **Practical applicability concerns (Reviewer 3).** The method is described as requiring training a separate discriminant for each class (K separate classifiers), which introduces significant computational overhead when K is large and is less scalable than methods that learn a single classifier. Another reviewer (Reviewer 2) notes that the paper’s claim of \(O(K^2)\) vs \(O(K)\) computational cost for label transformation is misplaced because training time dominates in any practical ML application.
- **Experimental limitations.** 
  - The experiments use standard balanced datasets; there are no experiments with non-uniform or long-tailed label distributions, which would better demonstrate the method’s practical relevance and test how it compares to prior methods that use prior information (e.g., RRWithPrior, LP-MST). (Reviewers 1 and 4)
  - The authors do not investigate how long-tail distribution or class imbalance might affect theoretical guarantees. (Reviewer 4)
- **Presentation issues.** 
  - The focus on local differential privacy is not clear from the title and abstract; these sections should be revised. (Reviewer 3)
  - The “Others” section of the related work could discuss prior techniques in more detail. (Reviewer 3)
  - The paper is somewhat repetitive and could be more concise. (Reviewer 4)
- **Specific reviewer questions that overlap with criticisms:**
  - Reviewer 2 asks for elaboration on the claim that ALIBI’s softmax normalization causes bias (line 107). This is a criticism of ALIBI and needs justification.
  - Reviewer 2 asks how changing the activation function of the last layer affects model performance (Section 4.3) and whether the authors modify the training process in experiments; this is a request for clarification on implementation details that may affect reproducibility.
  - Reviewer 3 questions the theoretical basis of the claimed improvement and the novelty (already covered above).
  - Reviewer 4 asks about the purpose of a footnote in Table 3; while minor, it indicates a presentation clarity issue.
