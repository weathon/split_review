## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Ada (Action Domain Acquisition), a framework that leverages large language models (LLMs) to automatically construct a library of symbolic action abstractions (high-level operators) and learn corresponding low-level policies for hierarchical planning. Given a natural language task and symbolic state, the LLM proposes high-level action sequences and defines operators (preconditions/effects in PDDL) for those not yet in the library. Operators are scored based on their executability and success in solving tasks; only high-scoring ones are retained. Low-level policies are acquired via backward search (BFS) and refined through environmental interaction. The method is evaluated on Mini Minecraft and ALFRED household tasks, outperforming several LLM-based baselines (e.g., code policy prediction, subgoal planning) by noticeable margins. Reviewers note that the method assumes a perfect symbolic state representation and requires substantial environment interaction for low-level policy acquisition.

### Strengths

- **Innovative and well-motivated approach:** Several reviewers highlight the novelty of using LLMs to derive symbolic action abstractions and verify them in a hierarchical planning framework, bridging LLMs with classical planning. One reviewer (R4, score 8) finds the work “very well written and easy to follow,” and another (R3) states the idea is “intuitive and effective” and “would be of interest to the planning and decision-making community.”
- **Strong empirical performance:** The method achieves noticeably higher success rates than baselines on both Mini Minecraft and ALFRED, as shown in Tables 1 and 2. R5 notes “strong performance over the baselines by noticeable margins.” R1 and R3 also highlight the empirical evidence.
- **Two-stage pipeline is well-motivated:** The use of an LLM to first decompose a task into high-level actions and then define operators for unknown actions is considered sound (R5). R3 agrees, calling the approach “clearly written and supported by strong empirical evidence.”
- **Tackles an important problem:** The work addresses action grounding issues common when LLMs are used directly for planning (R5). R1 emphasizes the “unique approach to decomposing complex tasks.”
- **Baselines cover diverse LLM uses:** R4 appreciates that the baselines evaluate different ways of incorporating LLMs into planning and action selection (code policies, subgoal prediction, full action sequences). R5 also finds the baselines informative.
- **Task environments assess different complexities:** Mini Minecraft tests composability of learned abstractions; ALFRED tests grounding to real-world-like household tasks (R4). R1 notes the method generalizes from simpler to complex tasks.

### Weaknesses

- **Goal misspecification:** On ALFRED, LLM often fails to recover the correct formal goal predicate from ambiguous language (e.g., “slice a tomato and put it on a table” could refer to a dining, side, or coffee table). This leads to planning failures when the chosen interpretation mismatches the ground truth (R1).
- **Policy inaccuracy and low-level failures:** Learned low-level policies sometimes miss fine-grained geometric details, possibly due to insufficient training data or limitations in the learning algorithm (R1).
- **Operator overspecification:** Learned operators can be too specific—e.g., a *SliceObject* operator specifying a particular knife type (R1, R4). R4 asks whether multiple instances (e.g., butter knife vs. steak knife) were observed and whether overspecification was data-driven or arbitrary. R4 suggests more diverse proposals could help, but that was not attempted.
- **Assumption of perfect symbolic representation:** The method assumes a perfect mapping Φ from environment state to object classes and predicates, which is unrealistic for real-world robotics (R2, R5). R5 notes this is a significant limitation for practical applications—no noisy or incomplete state is considered.
- **Extensive environment interaction:** Acquiring low-level action sequences relies on brute-force backward search (BFS) for each subgoal, which is computationally expensive and sample-inefficient. R5 questions scalability to more complex environments and whether offline or more efficient alternatives (e.g., imitation learning, shaped RL) could be used. R1 also notes that the paper does not address motor-level planning.
- **Lack of novelty and missing comparison to prior work:** R2 (score 5) finds the pipeline “reminiscent of the Voyager model” (dynamic skill library) and suggests the paper better highlight differences. R2 also notes missing citations [1,2] for other LLM-based task decomposition methods. R1, R3, R5 on the other hand consider the contribution novel (minority contrast: R2 is more negative on novelty).
- **Benchmark simplicity:** R2 argues that Mini Minecraft is significantly less complex than the full Minecraft, leading to a 100% success rate on some tasks and potentially masking limitations. R4 asks whether learning on simpler tasks biased the abstraction library toward primitive actions rather than forcing composition.
- **No multi-LLM comparison and limited noise analysis:** The paper uses only one LLM (likely GPT-3/3.5, not GPT-4). R4 and R1 suggest experiments with multiple LLMs are needed to understand required LLM characteristics and to assess robustness. R4 also notes “it is not clear how much noise the system can handle.”
- **No comparison to hand-coded abstraction learning:** R4 points out the absence of baselines using hand-crafted action abstractions or other methods for learning abstractions (e.g., STRIPS learning).
- **Redundant or semantically similar operators:** The LLM generation is not conditioned on existing operators, risking redundant additions (e.g., a *place* operator alongside a *put* operator, a *clean* operator alongside a *clean_and_cool* operator). Without a filtering mechanism, the operator library may grow unnecessarily, harming planner efficiency (R3).
- **Operator scoring ignores goal usefulness:** In Sec 3.4, operators are scored based only on executability at the low level, not on whether they contribute to the overall task goal. This could retain operators that are feasible but not useful for achieving the high-level plan (R3).
- **Missing statistics on plan length and operator count:** R3 requests details on the number of operators learned and the length of high- and low-level plans to better judge task complexity, especially for compositionality experiments.
- **Baseline details unclear:** R5 notes that the Code Policy Prediction baseline (Table 1) is insufficiently described—how the LLM generates imperative code policies and how function call sequences are produced and grounded to the environment is not specified, making reproducibility difficult.
- **Sensitivity to task ordering not evaluated:** R4 asks how the ordering of tasks (randomly shuffled) affects the resulting operator library and whether subpar plans (e.g., unnecessarily long) were common. R4 also questions the choice of ALFRED over Habitat and how often found plans are suboptimal.
- **LLM output accuracy:** R4 questions how accurate the LLM’s mappings from language to goal specification and operator definitions are. No ablation quantifies this.
- **Scalability concerns:** R1 asks how performance degrades with increasing number of tasks or environment complexity; R5 also raises scalability concerns for low-level policy learning.
- **Minor issues:** R3 notes a typo in Sec 3.4: “s/b > τ_r” (likely should be “> τ_r”); R2 mentions lack of detail on generalization ability of symbolic representations to real-world environments.
