## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a Task Codebook mechanism for generative Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to handle sequential task adaptation while mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The codebook comprises task-specific keys (learned from outputs of a chosen layer \(l\)) and task values (MLP adapters). During inference, the model performs nearest-neighbor lookup to select the correct task adapter. The authors also propose a benchmark of 36 datasets covering 8 applications and 4 incremental learning setups. Experiments show the method outperforms prior approaches such as prompt/prefix tuning, though multitask models often score higher.

### Strengths
- The idea of encoding tasks via a codebook to handle different tasks and prevent forgetting is reasonable, intuitive, and interesting (R1, R2, R3).
- The method maps layer outputs (task keys) to task-specific MLPs (values) in a clear, easy-to-follow manner; the diagram is easy to understand (R1, R3).
- The paper introduces a comprehensive new multi-task benchmark that includes diverse cross-modal tasks and multiple incremental settings, exposing forgetting in existing models and completing the contribution (R1, R2, R3).
- Extensive experiments demonstrate effectiveness, and the method outperforms other anti-forgetting techniques including prompt/prefix tuning (R1, R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The task codebook is essentially a collection of adapters, and PEFT is standard. The task key could be replaced by the inherent reasoning ability of LLMs to classify tasks from input text, making the key mechanism potentially unnecessary (R3). The idea strongly resembles Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), but this connection is neither mentioned nor compared; a proper comparison to MoE is missing (R1).
- **Concerns about the incremental learning setting**:  
  - The fundamental challenge of sequential learning is unclear when current VLMs already collect vast multi-task data (R3).  
  - Multitask models (trained with all data randomized) mostly outperform this method, raising the question of why one would prefer sequential (one-by-one) workloads over joint training or multi-task fine-tuning, which seems more feasible (R1, R3).  
  - The paper shows better performance only compared to TCIA (R3, detail unclear), not against straightforward multi-task fine-tuning.
- **Missing comparisons to multimodal large language models (MLLMs)**: The paper does not differentiate from MLLMs such as LLaVA (strong instruction-following, zero-shot captioning/VQA/OCR) or Ferret (localization). A dedicated comparison section and experiments evaluating the method against these MLLMs on the proposed benchmark are needed, especially to highlight advantages in incremental learning (R2).
- **Data quality and pretraining**: The models are pretrained on closed-source WebLI-100M, a carefully curated high-quality dataset that may artificially boost performance over models pretrained on less curated data. The authors should justify the choice of WebLI and address whether better data explains the gains (R1).
- **Inference time overhead**: Nearest-neighbor lookup time grows with the number of tasks, yet only lookup accuracy is reported. A study of time costs during training and inference, along with the trade-off, is missing (R1).
- **Presentation issues**: Citation formatting incorrectly uses \cite instead of \citep, making references hard to read. Typo on line 148: “processing” should be “processor” (R1).
- **Minority conflict in assessment**: Reviewer 2 (score 6, confidence 5) finds the approach reasonable and the experiments extensive, while Reviewer 3 (score 3, confidence 4) strongly questions the necessity of task keys and the overall novelty, and Reviewer 1 (score 5, confidence 3) raises practical concerns about overhead and data quality.
