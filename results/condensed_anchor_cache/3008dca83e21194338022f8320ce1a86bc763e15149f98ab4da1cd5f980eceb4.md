## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers found the paper to introduce a novel and useful generalization of calibration error (beta-calibration) that ensures calibration holds across question-answer subgroups, with well-motivated post-hoc methods and theoretical guarantees. The exposition is clear and experiments span multiple QA datasets and models. However, there were significant concerns about limited evaluation of partitioning strategies, reliance on a single calibration method, evaluating using the same metric being optimized, insufficient practical demonstration, and questions about technical novelty and applicability.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem and perspective**: The concept of beta-calibration is novel and addresses a significant real-world need—calibration that is sensitive to subgroups of interest (e.g., user-defined topic groups). This is the first work to identify and directly tackle this issue (R1, R4). R4 called it “a solid contribution that offers a novel and useful new perspective on post-hoc calibration.”
- **Clear exposition and rigor**: The paper is well-structured with clearly defined notations and concepts, making it easy to follow (R1, R4). Theoretical results (e.g., bounds on calibration error) are helpful and rigorously proven (R2, R4).
- **Comprehensive experimental design**: Evaluated across five different QA datasets and multiple models, including selective answering as a practical downstream task to demonstrate utility beyond calibration error alone (R1, R3).
- **Theoretical guarantees**: The methods come with distribution-free guarantees, which is a valuable contribution (R1, R2).
- **Method motivation**: The post-hoc calibration methods sensibly extend prior work (uniform-mass double-dipping histogram binning, scaling) and are well motivated (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited generalizability of the partition strategy (beta function)**: Only one type of partition was tested (based on DistilBERT embeddings). It is unclear how the methods would perform with other meaningful partitions (e.g., by question difficulty, topic) or whether the specific embedding-based grouping is relevant to user interests (R1, R3). R3 noted reliance on a smaller/older model (DistilBERT) as a key limitation.
- **Overlapping group issue not addressed**: The framework assumes each question belongs to exactly one group, but in practice a question may span multiple user interests. The paper does not discuss this limitation or propose a solution (R1).
- **Only one base calibration method tested**: Only binning (UMD histogram) is evaluated as the post-hoc calibrator. Missing comparisons with other standard methods, particularly isotonic regression and Platt scaling, make it unclear whether the framework generalizes across calibrators. This is a notable gap (R1, R2). R2 added that using only verbalized confidence as the base score is questionable; the paper lacks core modeling details (e.g., model size, whether instruction-tuned variants were used).
- **Evaluation using the same metric being optimized**: The primary evaluation metric (beta-calibration error) is exactly the one the method optimizes. Reviewers strongly recommended reporting additional established metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score to avoid circularity and strengthen claims (R3, also R2). R2 also noted that standard ECE (with binning) already captures some subgroup calibration, questioning the distinct advantage of beta-calibration.
- **Potential mischaracterization of ECE**: In the toy example (Table 1/Figure 1), standard ECE with binning is equivalent to beta-calibration; the paper does not clearly motivate why its variant is inherently better or more useful (R2).
- **Assumption of an oracle for semantic equivalence**: The approach uses Llama-3.1 to determine answer correctness (semantic equivalence), raising concerns about whether the model could have memorized the QA datasets, and whether it is a reliable oracle (R3).
- **Dataset-specific calibration sets**: The method requires a calibration set from the target domain, which may not be available for novel problems. This puts baselines (e.g., out-of-the-box prompts) at an unfair disadvantage and limits practical applicability (R3).
- **Limited technical novelty**: The adaptation to LLMs largely applies existing calibration methods (UMD binning, scaling) without significant technical innovation (R3). R2 noted that methods optimize for the specific binning function used in evaluation, which weakens the demonstration.
- **Insufficient result analysis**: Calibration errors are reported only as averages across partitions. Per-partition results, variance, and discussion of which subgroups improve most (or fail) are missing, which would provide deeper insight (R1). R4 asked for more detail on which examples end up in which bins and the relationship between raw and recalibrated scores (e.g., scatterplot).
- **Empirical results may not motivate adoption**: While the method outperforms baselines in Table 2, the selective prediction results (Tables 6, 7) are often worse than or statistically indistinguishable from elicited confidence scores, limiting practical appeal (R4).
- **Utility depends on meaningfulness of groups**: The value of beta-calibration is clearest when groups are user-defined and intuitive (Figure 1), but if the groups are not meaningful, it is unclear why practitioners should pursue these stronger guarantees (R4).
- **Ambiguities in method and evaluation**: Several details are unclear: how fixed vs. random intercepts/slopes are computed in Equation (5) (R1 Q1); what value of α was used for Figure 2 and how it was chosen (R1 Q2); how bins are constructed when computing calibration error (R1 Q3); and how tight the theoretical bound is in practice and whether it informs hyperparameter selection (R1 Q4). The bound appears to follow prior work rather than leveraging the new bound (R1 Q4).
- **Missing related work and metrics**: R2 noted that MCE and other metrics (AUROC, Brier score) should be discussed in related work, as summarized by Tran et al. (Plex).

*Note: While R1 and R4 were generally positive (scores 8, 8), R2 and R3 were more critical (scores 5, 6) regarding technical novelty, evaluation rigor, and practical significance. This contrast is preserved in the weaknesses above.*
