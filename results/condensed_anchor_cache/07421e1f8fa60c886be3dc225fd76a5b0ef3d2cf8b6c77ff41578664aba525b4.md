## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates WiSE‑FT, a weight‑space ensemble method, to understand how ensemble models improve out‑of‑distribution (OOD) performance. It observes a “FalseFalseTrue” phenomenon where the ensemble corrects samples that both individual models (pre‑trained and fine‑tuned) predict incorrectly. A theoretical analysis in a multi‑class linear setting shows that using a diverse set of spurious features reduces the influence of each spurious feature, improving OOD generalization. This contradicts the traditional focus on invariant features. The analysis also explains why weight‑space ensembles outperform output‑space ensembles. The paper identifies that fine‑tuned models’ overconfidence in OOD data hurts ensemble performance and proposes BANG (BAlaNced averaGing), which fine‑tunes with mixup and label smoothing before applying WiSE‑FT, to mitigate overconfidence. Experiments on MultiColorMNIST and ImageNet support the theory and show BANG improves over WiSE‑FT.

### Strengths
- The “FalseFalseTrue” phenomenon is unexpected and interesting. The ratio of such corrections is comparable to the total OOD improvement provided by WiSE‑FT (Figure 2, Table 1).
- The theoretical setup (multi‑class linear model with many spurious features) is solid, well‑organized, and clearly explained. Definitions and assumptions are precise; proofs are sound.
- The analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for how ensemble‑based methods improve OOD performance via diverse spurious features, offering the first explanation for why weight‑space ensembles outperform output‑space ensembles.
- The MultiColorMNIST experiment (Table 1) directly demonstrates that increasing the number of diverse spurious features improves the ensemble’s OOD performance, consistent with the theory.
- BANG yields OOD accuracy improvements over WiSE‑FT on ImageNet (Table 2) and other datasets (Appendices).
- The paper is well‑written with a clear structure and helpful illustrative examples that make the logic easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Connection between theory and WiSE‑FT**: The theoretical analysis assumes a simple linear model where the ensemble operates on the output logits. However, WiSE‑FT interpolates all model weights, not just the linear layer. It is unclear how this weight‑space scaling translates to the paper’s linear‑output model. One reviewer (score 5) notes this as a sizeable gap, as WiSE‑FT specifically ensembles models that are good OOD / bad ID and good ID / bad OOD, which is only loosely addressed (e.g., “low overlap is good for weight‑space ensembles”). The paper itself acknowledges: “this method can not be directly applied to WiSE‑FT since WiSE‑FT ensemble model weights instead of the outputs.”
- **Overemphasis on output ensembles**: The paper extensively discusses output‑space ensembles, even though they are not known to perform as well as WiSE‑FT. No comparison or discussion of boosting is provided, despite boosting having a similar flavor of forcing models to rely on different features.
- **BANG’s mechanism and novelty**: BANG is essentially fine‑tuning with mixup and label smoothing before applying WiSE‑FT. One reviewer questions whether the gains come from better calibration or from mixup/LS simply improving the OOD performance of the fine‑tuned model. Appendix E.6 shows “oracle weights” that scale standard fine‑tuned models, but this does not establish that BANG’s improvement is due to calibration rather than improved fine‑tuned model quality. A more direct test would be an “accuracy‑on‑the‑line” analysis where models fine‑tuned with mixup+LS at the same OOD accuracy as standard fine‑tuned models are compared; it would strengthen the calibration claim if BANG outperforms WiSE‑FT even under this controlled condition. Another reviewer suggests that a more direct confidence correction (e.g., temperature scaling for each checkpoint during fine‑tuning) would be preferable to BANG’s mixup/LS approach.
- **Generalization to more than two models**: The paper only considers ensembling two individual models (pre‑trained and fine‑tuned) in the “FalseFalseTrue” analysis. It is unclear if the findings hold for ensembles of three or more models, and whether further improvements are possible (Table 1 only shows two‑model results).
- **Applicability to datasets with a single spurious feature**: On datasets like Waterbirds, where only one strong spurious feature (background) exists in training, will the ensemble method still be effective? Could one manually add synthetic spurious features (color, rotation, scale) and then apply BANG to improve performance?
- **In‑distribution performance of BANG**: On ImageNet’s in‑distribution test set, fine‑tuned models with mixup+LS outperform standard fine‑tuned models by ~1.5%, yet BANG does not outperform WiSE‑FT. The intuition for this discrepancy is not explained.
- **FalseFalseTrue analysis for BANG**: Appendix E.6 suggests BANG helps in a “TrueFalseTrue” setting (finetuned model incorrect and overconfident). It would be informative to see the “FalseFalseTrue” analysis (like Figure 2 left) for BANG vs WiSE‑FT to understand whether calibration improvements specifically affect the FalseFalseTrue correction rate.

*Minority viewpoint*: One reviewer (score 5) is less convinced of the theory’s relevance to WiSE‑FT and of BANG’s novelty, while the other two reviewers (both score 8) find the theory solid and the empirical results supportive. This contrast is noted; the minority concerns are fully captured above.
