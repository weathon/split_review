## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies reward model (RM) inconsistency in RLHF—whether RMs can recognize semantic changes to different prompts and adjust reward assignments accordingly. The authors propose **Contrast Instruction**, a benchmark evaluating consistency via quadruplets of lexically similar instructions with different ground-truth responses, using two metrics: response consistency (C_res) and instruction consistency (C_ins). The benchmark is automatically constructed from four open-source human preference datasets using SimCSE embeddings with cosine similarity in [0.75, 0.9]. Experiments show that standard RMs (trained with ranking objective on Llama-7B) perform near random (C_res ≈ 53.6%) while humans achieve ~80%. Two simple techniques are proposed: **ConvexDA** (data augmentation with WordNet synonyms during training) and **RewardFusion** (weighted average of reward scores from similar training examples at inference). The authors also show that using a more consistent RM (fine-tuned on Contrast Instruction data) for RLHF yields more preferred generations in human evaluation.

### Strengths
- The paper introduces a novel, important, and often overlooked aspect of RM evaluation—reward consistency—and provides a clear definition and automated benchmark construction that can be easily applied to existing instruction-tuning datasets.
- The investigation into RM inconsistency is empirically thorough: experiments across multiple datasets clearly demonstrate that current RMs suffer from inconsistency, and the trickle-down effect on downstream RLHF performance is an interesting and intuitive finding.
- The proposed benchmark (Contrast Instruction) has the potential to become a standard evaluation tool for practitioners; the methods to improve consistency (ConvexDA, RewardFusion) are simple and easy to implement.
- Table 7 is particularly useful in showing that reward consistency and test-set accuracy (RMEval) do not necessarily correlate, analogous to the FID vs. human score disconnect in generative models.

### Weaknesses
- **Benchmark construction validation is insufficient.** The cosine similarity range [0.75, 0.9] for selecting lexically similar but semantically different instruction pairs is not thoroughly justified. No analysis of sensitivity to this threshold, distribution of semantic similarity within the range, or impact on benchmark difficulty is provided. The authors did not manually inspect the pairs or use a model-based validation (e.g., GPT-4) to confirm that one response is strictly better; relying solely on SimCSE cosine distance is questionable (Reviewers 1, 3, 5).
- **Evaluation of existing RMs on the benchmark raises concerns.** The near-random C_res score (53.6%) on data from the same datasets used for RM training is surprising, as C_res resembles the RM training objective. It is unclear whether this reflects a fundamental limitation of the model or insufficient training/hyperparameter optimization. The authors should verify if the model can overfit to achieve higher C_res, compare with other RM losses (e.g., margin loss from Llama 2), and provide training convergence details (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Limited model scope.** All main experiments use only Llama-7B. No scaling analysis (smaller/larger models) or comparison across architectures (e.g., Llama2-7B vs. Llama2-7B-chat) is provided, despite the claim that the benchmark should be broadly useful. The authors should also evaluate on closed-source models like GPT-4 via prompting to show general consistency trends (Reviewers 1, 3, 5).
- **Proposed methods (ConvexDA, RewardFusion) are not convincingly demonstrated to improve consistency generally.** It is unclear why synonym augmentation (ConvexDA) and inference-time averaging (RewardFusion) should fix inconsistency beyond lexical similarity—they appear tailored to the specific Contrast Instruction setup. No comparison is made to simply using an LLM to generate semantically similar pairs for augmentation. A more detailed explanation of how these methods address the underlying mechanism of inconsistency is needed (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
- **The RLHF downstream experiment does not use ConvexDA/RewardFusion; instead, a reward model fine-tuned on Contrast Instruction data is used.** The authors need to clarify whether ConvexDA was used for that fine-tuned RM (as implied in Appendix C) and evaluate the proposed methods directly in the RLHF loop. Additionally, the phrase “finetuning with contrast instructions format” requires precise definition (Reviewer 1).
- **Human evaluation is limited and potentially biased.** Only 100 data points were evaluated, with no error bars or variance reported (error bars are only shown in Figure 3 but missing from other results). The annotators were the authors themselves, raising concerns about objectivity. Reviewers 3 and 5 note that small sample size and lack of external annotators undermine confidence in the conclusions.
- **Several missing comparisons and ablations are needed.** (a) Why does the benchmark not see inconsistency in standard RLHF datasets (e.g., Carper AI, hh)? A comparison to current SOTA reward models on standard open-source datasets is required. (b) Would Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) also suffer from this inconsistency? (c) Would reward model ensembles (as in Coste et al.) help mitigate over-optimization? (d) How does choosing different number of similar examples in ConvexDA affect performance? (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5).
- **Clarity and methodological gaps remain.** The notation after Equation 1 is unclear (missing "no"). The term “estimated human performance” in Table 2 needs explanation. The data variances in Figure 3 make comparisons in Sec. 7.2 inconclusive, though the error bars are appreciated (Reviewers 3, 4, 5).
