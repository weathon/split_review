## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces a generative framework for estimating counterfactual outcomes under time-varying treatments. The method combines a conditional variational autoencoder with inverse probability weighting (IPW) to handle high-dimensional outcomes without explicit density estimation. The approach is described as simple, easy to use, and accessible. Experiments on synthetic and real-world data (including COVID-19 data) show good performance against baselines. Reviewers generally agree the problem is important and the approach interesting, but they raise significant concerns about theoretical foundations, clarity, experimental comparisons, and practical interpretability. There is notable disagreement: one reviewer (score 3) finds the theoretical core flawed, while others (scores 5 and 6) find the approach technically sound but lacking in explanation and baselines.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a difficult and relevant problem: estimating counterfactual outcomes under sequential, time-varying treatments with high-dimensional outcomes. [R1, R2, R3, R4]
- The proposed method is simple, easy to use, and generates counterfactual samples without imposing rigid distributional assumptions on the counterfactual outcome. [R1, R2]
- The method handles high-dimensional outcomes, which most existing studies do not address. [R2, R4]
- The approach is technically sound and the main ideas are presented well, though clarity can be improved. [R3]
- Experiments are conducted on multiple datasets including real-world data (e.g., COVID-19), and the results are good compared to baselines. [R1, R4]
- Using a generative model for counterfactual distributions is an interesting idea. [R4]

### Weaknesses

**Theoretical and Foundational Issues**
- **Proposition 1 is problematic**: The derivation mixes a fixed treatment sequence with an expectation over $\bar{a}$; the right-hand side of Eq. (4) would be the same for all $\bar{a}$ while the left-hand side should differ; the sum in Eq. (4) should be an average; the index in Eq. (5) runs $t-d$ instead of $t-d+1$. The proof introduces an unwarranted expectation over $\bar{a}$. This raises concern about whether the formula was actually used correctly in experiments. [R1, strong criticism; other reviewers did not comment on this specific point.]
- **Remark 1 is unsupported**: Claiming doubly robust methods are less robust to model misspecification than IPW methods contradicts standard understanding; the cited Appendix D contains no relevant discussion. [R1]
- The only theoretical guarantee is unbiasedness of the weighted log-likelihood, which is closer to treatment effect estimation than density estimation. The paper is purely applied, so comparisons to harder density methods (e.g., Kennedy et al. 2023) are inappropriate. [R1]
- The absence of a unified DR theory for longitudinal density is not a valid reason to avoid DR estimators when goal is not density estimation; a large literature on IPW/AIPW for dynamic treatment regimes and RL already exists. [R1]
- The assumption that history dependence length $d$ is finite and known makes the IPW extension trivial; variance of IPW can easily blow up as $d$ grows. [R1]
- IPTW values can be very small, especially in sequential treatment settings, leading to instability. [R2]
- For sequential treatments, the counterfactual treatment is not unique (many sequences map to the same final pattern), but the notation does not reflect this. [R2]

**Methodology and Implementation Details**
- If covariates $\mathbf{x}$ are not used in the generator, what is the rationale for computing weights based on $\mathbf{x}$? [R2, question]
- Can the authors provide a proof for Eq. (2)? What would change if the distance were defined using MMD or Wasserstein distance? [R2, question]
- In Algorithm 1, $\epsilon$ is drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ where $I$ denotes the total number of individuals; this would produce very large variance samples, and the choice of normal distribution is not justified. [R3]
- The training objective (Eq. 2) is approximated by Eq. (5) per time step $t$, yielding $T$ approximations. It is unclear which value (sum, average, per-step) should be minimized during training. [R3]
- Algorithm 1 uses $t = d, \dots, T$ while the problem setup states $t=1,\dots,T$; this discrepancy needs explanation, and the method for determining $d$ is not given. [R3]
- It is unclear whether the framework can be extended to individual-level outcome estimation, which would seem natural. [R2, question]

**Experimental Evaluation and Comparisons**
- The paper lacks a detailed comparison of technical innovations with previous approaches such as G-Nets, KDE-based methods, and other baselines. A paragraph discussing differences and advantages would clarify contribution. [R4]
- Two relevant baseline papers are missing: "Disentangled Counterfactual Recurrent Networks for Treatment Effect Inference Over Time" and "Estimating Counterfactual Treatment Outcomes Over Time Through Adversarially Balanced Representations". [R2]
- Treatment variables in the datasets are described vaguely; e.g., for the COVID-19 datasets, specific interventions (mask mandates, lockdowns, etc.) are not specified. [R4]
- The paper does not explain how the generative model's output can be used for causal tasks such as ITE estimation, or whether the method is designed only for population-level inference. [R4]

**Presentation and Organization**
- The paper is not well organized; standard causal assumptions are deferred to the appendix without even naming them in the main text. [R3]
- Explanation focuses on what was done rather than why; readers may not understand the reasoning behind design choices. [R4]
- Notations are occasionally confusing and assumptions are vague. [R1, R3, R4]
- The paper claims that DR methods are less robust than IPW, which is not standard and lacks support. [R1]
