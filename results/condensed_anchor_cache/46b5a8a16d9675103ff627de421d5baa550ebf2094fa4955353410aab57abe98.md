- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 3, 3, 3

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes two projection-based distributed constrained optimal consensus algorithms for multi-agent systems under a directed graph, addressing set constraints and general constraints separately. The algorithms only exchange relative states among agents, and the analysis includes a transformation using a sliding mode approach. The paper claims an ergodic convergence rate of O(1/k). Three of four reviewers assigned score 3, one gave 5. There is general agreement that the theoretical analysis is mathematically rigorous, but the presentation, experimental validation, and justification of novelty are inadequate. The paper requires major revisions in writing, experimental scope, and clarification of its contributions.

### Strengths
- **Clear theoretical structure**: The paper is well organized, assumptions and lemmas are stated, and the proofs are mathematically correct (R1; but see weaknesses on presentation by R2,R3,R4).
- **Use of constant step-sizes**: Both algorithms employ constant step-sizes, which can provide faster convergence compared to diminishing step-size methods (R2, R3, R4).
- **Connection to sliding mode**: The approach includes a link to the sliding mode technique, which may provide a different perspective (R4).
- **Validation of faster convergence**: Experiments show that the proposed method for set constraints converges faster than a baseline (the DSP algorithm (6)) (R3).
- **Privacy aspects**: Exchanging only relative states can help preserve privacy (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Very poor presentation and writing**:
  - The paper is hard to follow; mathematical results (e.g., Assumption 2.1, content of Sections 2.2 and 2.4, equation (18)) are given without context or justification (R3).
  - Proposed algorithms (equations (5), (12), (13)) lack explanation of terms and novel parts; key symbols such as \(T\) and \(\alpha\) are not defined before their first use (R2, R3, R4).
  - Extensive typos and grammatical errors: “construct” should be “constructing” (line 29); “spinning” should be “spanning” (line 137); “DPS” appears instead of “DSP” (line 484); missing punctuation (line 403); undefined symbols \(\gamma_{ik}(k)\) used before definition; Laplacian mentioned before its definition (beginning of page 3); superscript ^2 in Lipschitz definition (line 151); normal cone definition in Lemma 2.2 missing \(x\in\Omega\); poor phrasing throughout (R1,R2,R3,R4).  Reviewer 4 provides a detailed list of over 30 specific errors.
  - The legend of Figure 1(b) refers to equation (24), which is not in the main text (appendix only) (R3).

- **Inadequate experiments**:
  - Only one synthetic dataset is used; the cost function \(f_i(x_i)\) in Section 5 does not correspond to a quadratic as claimed (R2, R3, R4).
  - The only baseline is the DSP algorithm (6) – the paper alternately calls it DPS, and its full name and exact difference from the proposed methods are never clarified (R1,R2,R3,R4).
  - Many optimization methods with constraints reviewed in the introduction are not used as benchmarks (R2, R4).
  - Step-size parameters were manually tuned for best performance, which is not practical and not explained how to select them in a distributed manner (R4).
  - Results appear to be from a single run (not multiple independent runs) (R3).
  - The problem is artificial; no real-world application (e.g., distributed model predictive control, resource allocation) is considered (R4).

- **Restrictive or problematic assumptions**:
  - Assumption 2.1 requires the graph to be strongly connected and balanced – this is restrictive and not sufficiently motivated (R4).
  - Assumption 4.3 assumes boundedness of gains \(\gamma_{ik}\), but these gains are updated online; boundedness should be proven, not assumed (R4).
  - The paper does not discuss how to relax the balanced graph requirement or compare with related techniques like DIAG, push-sum, or push-pull (R4).

- **Insufficient validation of theoretical contribution**:
  - The technical proof is described as conventional; no application to analyze other DSP algorithms is provided (R1).
  - The sliding mode approach is claimed but not elaborated enough for the reviewer to assess novelty or contribution (R1).
  - The motivation for the proposed approaches is limited: e.g., “diminishing step-sizes cause slower convergence” is not strongly supported, and existing complexities from inequality constraints are not explained (R3).

- **Missing ablations and comparisons**:
  - No ablation experiments are performed to show the effect of the projection operators (R1).
  - No comparison with the work of Liu et al. (IEEE TAC 2017) on constrained consensus with fixed step size (R3).

- **Technical omissions**:
  - How to realize projection under complicated constraints (nonconvex, high dimensions) is not addressed (R1).
  - The algorithm for general constraints (Section 3.2) uses gain sequences \(\gamma_{ik}(k)\); no distributed step-size rule is given, nor explicit guidelines for selecting \(\alpha_i,\alpha_{v_i},\alpha_{\gamma_{ik}}\) in Theorem 4.4 (R4).
  - Equations (30) and (42) are not justified as computationally distributed (R4).

- **Redundancy and standard results**:
  - Background material occupies almost half the paper; equilibrium equations on pages 6 and 8 are obvious (R4).
  - Theorem 2.2 is a classic result that need not be restated (R4).

- **Terminology**: The term “distributed optimal consensus” is non-standard; “distributed optimization” or “consensus optimization” is more widely used (R4).  Early work by Tsitsiklis (1980s) should be acknowledged (R4).

- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (R1) gave a score of 5 and found the presentation well organized and the mathematics correct, though still noted insufficient comparisons and conventional proofs. The other three reviewers considered the presentation poor and the contributions limited.
