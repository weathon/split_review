## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes that LLMs’ safety alignment is shallow—it primarily adjusts the generative distribution of only the first few output tokens—and that this explains vulnerabilities to adversarial suffixes, prefilling, decoding parameter, and fine-tuning attacks. It provides case studies and KL-divergence evidence supporting this claim. To mitigate the issue, the paper introduces two methods: (1) a data augmentation approach that trains the model to refuse after a few harmful‑answer tokens, and (2) a constrained‑SFT (or per‑token DPO) objective that limits distribution shift on early tokens during fine‑tuning. Experiments show improved robustness against several attack types.

### Strengths
- The key claim is intuitive and well‑articulated: alignment objectives (RLHF/DPO) can be satisfied by merely learning common refusal prefixes, making models vulnerable when attackers can control the first tokens (Reviewers 1, 2, 3).
- The paper provides a comprehensive exposition of the shallow alignment issue and ties together many seemingly different attack types (adversarial, prefilling, sampling, fine‑tuning) under a single explanatory framework (Reviewers 1, 2).
- Experimental results, including KL‑divergence between base and aligned models at different token positions, partially support the claim (Reviewers 1, 3).
- The proposed fine‑tuning objectives are simple, intuitive, and demonstrably effective. The per‑token constrained objective (constrained‑SFT) and the data‑augmentation approach (postponing refusal) are promising baselines for future work (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- The paper includes a good variety of experiments across models, datasets, and attack types, and provides both empirical and theoretical support (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The writing is excellent, well‑organized, and easy to follow (Reviewers 2, 4).
- The contributions lay groundwork for future safety alignment solutions; exposing the shallow alignment issue could inspire many more mitigation approaches (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of baselines:** Neither main experiment includes comparisons to relevant prior defenses. For prefilling/jailbreak attacks, circuit‑breaking (Arditi et al., 2024) and prompt‑level defenses are omitted. For fine‑tuning attacks, Vaccine (Huang et al., 2024) and LDIFS (Mukhoti et al., 2023) are not compared. The paper would be stronger by including these baselines (Reviewers 1, 3, 4). Reviewer 3 explicitly notes this as a weakness; Reviewer 4 also calls for baselines like Vaccine. Reviewer 1 suggests circuit‑breaking as a strong competitor.
- **Incomplete explanation of why methods work and their remaining vulnerability:** The ASR after mitigation is often not close to zero, and the paper does not adequately discuss why. It is unclear whether this is due to other vulnerabilities beyond shallow alignment or incomplete mitigation. For the data‑augmentation method, the true mechanism needs more discussion—e.g., it may train the model to refuse only after a fixed‑length harmful prefix, but adaptive attacks using longer harmful phrases could bypass it. The reason the augmentation method does not protect against fine‑tuning attacks is also not specified (Reviewers 2, 4). Reviewer 4 raises specific concerns about longer GCG targets; Reviewer 2 asks why ASR remains high.
- **Missing experiments / analyses:** (a) No averaging over sampling noise or reporting of greedy‑decoding results; reported results may vary (Reviewer 1). (b) No human evaluation of the LLM‑based safety judge; a small sample would strengthen the paper (Reviewer 1). (c) Experiments measuring reward‑hacking during alignment training (e.g., does the model learn shallow prefixes to game the reward?) are missing (Reviewer 3). (d) System overhead (extra forward pass during constrained‑SFT) is not analyzed (Reviewer 4).
- **Related work and literature review are insufficient:** The related‑work section lacks detail on how the paper differs from prior observations of token‑level asymmetries; it does not mention all existing mitigation strategies (e.g., multiple fine‑tuning defenses listed by Reviewer 4). The paper also does not discuss Mukhoti et al. (2023) (LDIFS) which shares a similar insight of constraining feature shift. The claim of being “the first to consolidate the unified notion” is vague (Reviewers 2, 4).
- **Threat model and real‑world applicability are unclear:** The exposition primarily suits open‑weight models; the scenario where an attacker can control the first few output tokens (e.g., prefilling) could be better motivated with a concrete real‑world example (Reviewers 2, 3). Reviewer 2 specifically requests an example of how a jailbreak might be deployed in practice.
- **Data‑augmentation concerns:** The augmented training texts are incoherent (e.g., partial harmful response followed by refusal). Reviewer 2 asks whether this compromises model learning. Reviewer 4 asks whether the harmful‑answer prefix used in augmentation matches the target phrase of attacks like GCG (e.g., “Sure,”)—clarification is needed. The effect of varying the refusal placement distribution (e.g., position, length) is not explored (Reviewer 3).
- **Contrast in reviewer assessment:** Reviewer 3 rated the paper 8 (vs. 10 from others), with confidence 5, and noted lower presentation quality (2) and contribution (2). This reviewer specifically found the literature review lacking, the threat model unclear, and missing reward‑hacking experiments. Other reviewers were more positive (scores 10, confidence 4–5). The paper would benefit from addressing the concerns of the more critical reviewer.
