## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Reward-RAG, which trains a reward model (via CriticGPT/GPT-4 annotations and MSE) to score query-document relevance, then uses that reward model to generate synthetic datasets for fine-tuning the RAG encoder (e.g., E5-large-unsupervised) with InfoNCE loss. The goal is to align retrieval with human preferences and improve in-domain and out-of-domain performance. Experiments are reported on retrieval (Table 1, NDCG@10) and RAG QA (Tables 2 and 3, Exact Match) across several benchmarks. Reviewers note that the synthetic data generation idea is new and the method is clearly presented, but they raise major concerns about experimental design, fairness of comparisons, missing baselines, lack of OOD improvement, technical novelty, and the reliance on proprietary models.

### Strengths
- The proposed method for synthetic data generation is new (R1).
- Clear representation: the method section includes detailed formulas and clear figures (R2).
- The method is sound, and in-domain experiment results are good: the proposed approach beats many baselines despite using a smaller retriever (R2).
- Clearly written contribution and method of the paper (R3).
- Innovative idea of using RLHF for RAG encoder model training (R3).
- RAG has been bottlenecked by retrieval quality, and innovation on this front is badly needed and critical (R3).
- Extensive choice of strong baselines (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient retrieval evaluation:** Rigorous evaluation of the generated synthetic data’s impact on retrieval is missing. For example, why does the method work better than re-ranking? (R1)
- **Unfair comparisons in Table 1:** Mostly compares with older and smaller encoder models; even then the method only wins on the NQ dataset (R1). No baseline comparable to the RLHF-finetuned “E5-large-unsupervised (ours)” is provided (R3). Table 1 does not report OOD retrieval performance (R2).
- **Unfair comparisons in Table 2:** The base LM for Reward-RAG is GPT-4o/ChatGPT, while baselines like Self-RAG use 7B/13B models; GPT-4o is significantly better than most 13B-scale models (R1). No baseline uses the same underlying LM (e.g., GPT-4o) with different retrievers to isolate retrieval improvements (R1). No baseline built on “E5-large-unsupervised” and then finetuned with common retrieval datasets is compared (R3). The question “What is the LLM in the retrieved RAG baseline?” is unanswered (R2). When combined with GPT-3.5-turbo, performances on TriviaQA and FEVER drop significantly (R4). Table 3 lacks a non-RAG baseline for GPT-3.5 turbo (R4).
- **OOD performance is not demonstrated:** The claim of OOD benefit (line 161) is unconvincing because the reward model and retriever are not trained on OOD data (R2). OOD experiments show the proposed approach performs worse than baselines in all tasks when the inference model is the same (GPT-4-0613); otherwise comparisons are unfair (R2). Table 1 omits OOD retrieval results (R2).
- **Missing critical baseline for encoder finetuning:** The experiment lacks a comparison of RLHF-style finetuning against other finetuning methods on the same encoder (e.g., finetuning E5-large-unsupervised on common retrieval datasets) (R3).
- **No analysis of the reward model:** How well does the reward model align with humans (GPT)? How well does it generalize to OOD domains? (R2)
- **Limited technical novelty:** The claim of an RLHF-like alignment framework is unconvincing—merely training a reward model to generate a synthetic dataset is a common technique (R4). There are no specific considerations for “RAG” aligned with human preference (R4). The approach does not bring much new insight and novelty (R2).
- **Heavy reliance on proprietary GPT-4:** The method uses GPT-4 both as critic and generator, hindering fair comparison with baselines that do not use GPT-4 (R4).
- **Presentation issues:** Table 1’s first column should be “Encoder models” not “Task”; captions describing evaluation benchmarks are confusing (R3). Line 360: “Table 4.2” should be “Table 3” (R2). The term “CriticGPT” may not be general or accepted in the community; critics could be established with other proprietary or open-source models (R4). The term “RLHF” is inaccurate because the reward model is used for relevance, not human feedback; “contrasting feedback” is recommended (R3).
- **Missing experimental details:** Tables 1, 2, and 3 overlook parameter sizes, training datasets, and computational cost (R4). Table 3 needs details on the RRF-4 method (re-ranking algorithm and which retrievers are used) (R2). Does the embedding (Emb_p) contain query information (e.g., if the [SEP] token separates attention)? (R2)
- **Quality concerns:** The synthetic dataset may have critical quality issues given the performance drops with GPT-3.5-turbo in Table 2 (R4).
