## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tackles the orthogonal problem of assessing what data benefits a fixed convex classifier (or convex surrogate) by interpreting feature space, and using influence estimation to improve utility, fairness, and robustness. It proposes influence-based data selection approaches, including a regression tree to predict influence scores, and a trimming scheme. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets (Adult, German, Drugs, CelebA) validate the method in conventional classification, distribution shifts, fairness poisoning, utility evasion, online learning, and active learning. The work is compared against TMC Shapley and random baselines.

### Strengths
1. **Important research problem**: The paper addresses a realistic and often overlooked issue—selecting important training data to improve model performance, rather than focusing solely on model architectures or algorithms (Reviewers 2, 5).
2. **Breadth of properties and applications**: The paper considers many aspects beyond accuracy, especially fairness, and extends to noisy labels, active learning, adversarial robustness, distribution shifts, and fairness/utility attacks (Reviewers 1, 3). The thorough experimentation across these diverse settings is impressive and demonstrates versatility (Reviewers 1, 2).
3. **Thorough experiments and compelling results**: Validation on both synthetic and real-world datasets with clear, sound presentation (Reviewers 1, 3). The influence-based deletion approach remains effective across a range of settings (Figure 2, Reviewer 1). The method achieves best performance in a series of tasks (Reviewer 5). Comparisons to adequate baselines, including TMC Shapley, strengthen the conclusions (Reviewer 1, 4). The combination of influence estimators for sample valuation (for different utilities) and CART as a feature selector is an interesting and useful perspective (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Unclear motivation and technical contribution**: The core idea is not well-justified. The paper essentially studies coreset selection via influence functions, which is not new; the contribution appears to be a straightforward combination of influence estimation and decision trees, and it is unclear why the tree is needed (Reviewer 5). The connection to previous influence function work (Koh & Liang) is not clearly differentiated mathematically (Reviewer 5). The CART procedure is under-motivated: it takes (x_i, y_i) and predicts influence scores, yet the label should not affect the tree quality for measuring feature effects; the actual goal of the tree (feature effect on performance metric vs. predicting influence) is ambiguous, and the trimming procedure—not the CART—is what is actually used in later experiments, weakening the claimed contribution (Reviewer 1).
2. **Insufficient detail and missing retraining discussion**: The trimming procedure lacks clarity about retraining—it is not specified whether the model is retrained after deletion, whether the original model is used to decide which samples to trim, and what x-axis zero in Figure 2 represents (Reviewer 1). The paper does not specify if the same model is used throughout or if the model changes (Reviewer 1).
3. **Scalability and computational complexity concerns**: Tree-based influence estimation may not scale to large datasets; building trees for many subsets or complex feature interactions is costly, and re-training the tree for each influence estimation may be prohibitive (Reviewer 2). The quadratic dependence on training samples typical of tree-based algorithms is a concern (Reviewer 2). Influence functions already have high incremental complexity due to Hessian computation per x_i, and adding CART further increases complexity—the time/space complexity of Algorithm 1 and 2 is not analyzed (Reviewers 3, 4, 5). Code for the computational experiments (especially Section E.1) is not in the shared code base (Reviewer 4).
4. **High-dimensional data and image limitations**: The method is demonstrated mostly on tabular data; extending the feature analysis (e.g., Figure 1) to images or text is tricky (Reviewer 1). Influence function and feature-space interpretations for tabular data may not translate to images—tree models may not capture complex spatial relationships even after dimensionality reduction, and reliance on deep embeddings could obscure interpretability (Reviewer 2).
5. **Model class limitations**: The work assumes convex classifiers or convex surrogates; this may lead to sub-optimal influence estimation when the model is not at a stationary point or has not converged, potentially causing misleading data valuations (Reviewers 3, 4).
6. **Lack of unifying story and missing connections**: The discussion and experiments for utility, fairness, and robustness feel distracted without a unifying narrative (Reviewer 3). The connection of the feature explanation via influence functions to existing XAI literature is lacking (Reviewer 3). Related works on influence-based data relabeling and mitigation (Kong et al., Adebayo et al., Richardson et al., concurrent “Understanding Unfairness via Training Concept Influence”, Sattigerri et al.) are not acknowledged or contextualized (Reviewer 1). No discussion comparing this work with Shuo Yang et al. (ICLR 2023) on dataset pruning (Reviewer 5).
7. **Presentation and clarity issues**: Writing is confusing, especially at abstract and introduction, where it seems the method identifies which features positively/negatively influence utility, but the focus is on training samples, not features; this should be revised (Reviewer 4). The paper is 32 pages instead of the expected 9 (Reviewer 4). Figure 3 and appendix illustrations are not informative and need more description (Reviewer 5). High-level intuitions for regression tree prediction and hierarchical shrinkage regularization are missing (Reviewer 5).
8. **Experimental design concerns**:
   - Figure 2: The nearly constant utility values under random deletion seem unrealistic; the scale for accuracy is not intuitive across datasets (Reviewer 4). Figure 2C is unexplained (Reviewer 4).
   - Figure 10 (appendix): If removing low-value samples, TMC-Shapley accuracy should increase, not remain flat—the graph suggests trimming high-value samples, which would mean influence-based trimming is performing poorly (Reviewer 4).
   - More informative comparisons would include vanilla influence estimation (without CART) and other influence-based methods, instead of only TMC-Shapley and random (Reviewer 4).
   - All datasets are binary; the reason for this design choice (e.g., value computation scaling with classes) is not discussed (Reviewer 4).
   - For fairness experiments (Section 5.1), the choice of fairness intervention baselines is not justified (Reviewer 3). The influence estimator may need to have the same architecture as the classifier (Reviewer 3).
9. **Missing analysis of selected vs. excluded data**: The paper would benefit from showing the difference in features and samples selected/excluded, and the consequential utility change when training only on selected vs. excluded data versus the full dataset (Reviewer 4).
