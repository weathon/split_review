## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes Hot PATE, an extension of the Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles (PATE) framework tailored for generative models and tasks with multiple valid responses. Instead of the noisy argmax in standard PATE, Hot PATE aggregates teacher response distributions to preserve diversity. The method uses a temperature parameter in softmax to control diversity and is demonstrated on discrete prompt learning with OpenAI GPT-3.5. Scores: 3, 5, 3, 3 (one reviewer gave 5, the others 3). Most reviewers found the idea interesting but the execution and evaluation insufficient.

### Strengths

- **Original concept:** Extending PATE to generative models is an innovative and intuitive idea (R1, R2, R3). Increasing the information conveyed beyond the argmax is a valid direction (R3).
- **Problem discussion:** The paper provides a clear discussion of the tension between diversity and privacy in PATE (R1).
- **Simplicity:** The proposed approach is simple and easy to grasp (R1).
- **Theoretical analysis:** A thorough theoretical analysis of the Hot PATE framework is presented (R2).
- **Minority positive view (R2, score 5):** This reviewer considered the extension innovative and the theoretical analysis strong, but still noted significant weaknesses.

### Weaknesses

- **Very limited experimental evaluation.** Only a single experiment with 5 outputs is reported, using only GPT-3.5 top-5 tokens. There is no evaluation of student model performance, no open-ended queries, no diverse tasks (e.g., summarization, image generation), and no ablations over datasets or model architectures. This severely limits conclusions about robustness and generalizability (R1, R2, R3).
- **Insufficient detail and clarity.** The writing is difficult to follow, reads like a draft, and lacks clear definitions. Key aspects are unclear: how \(w_j\) is computed, what constitutes the student model, what is considered private information, and how experimental setup is implemented (R2, R3, R4).
- **Missing related work and baselines.** The paper does not mention or compare to prior PATE extensions for generative or diverse settings. Notably, it incorrectly claims that Duan et al. (2023) “requires a source of unlabeled non-private training examples” (Duan et al. studied both IID and OOD transfer) and asserts that PATE was only used for classification (ignoring G-PATE for image generation). A clear articulation of novelty versus prior distribution-aggregation ideas (original PATE Appendix B.1) is lacking (R3, R4).
- **Scope ambiguity and limited novelty.** The paper presents itself as a general extension but focuses almost exclusively on prompt learning, making it unclear whether the contribution is task-specific. Several reviewers doubted that the extension is sufficiently novel compared to existing work (R3: distribution aggregation was already discussed in original PATE; R4: builds heavily on Duan et al. 2023 with minimal addition).
- **Scalability and complexity not analyzed.** The method requires a number of yield queries that grows quadratically with the number of teachers, yet no analysis of computational resources, memory, or time complexity is provided. This raises feasibility concerns for realistic models and datasets (R1).
- **No guidance on privacy-utility trade-offs.** The effect of the privacy parameter \(\epsilon\) on output quality and diversity is not analyzed, leaving practitioners without a way to choose \(\epsilon\) or understand the trade-off (R1).
- **Notation and writing issues.** Definition 1 has a missing left-bracket and potentially non-rigorous notation (\(j \in V\) vs. index). The text contains confusing sentences (e.g., “Diversity and privacy appear to be conflicting in that DP requires that the output token is supported by sufficiently many teachers…”) and a typo (“utility must deteriorates”) (R3, R4).
- **Questions that highlight gaps.** Several reviewer questions overlap with missing analyses: e.g., “How does the framework perform on fully open-ended queries?” (R1), “What measures ensure output quality in complex scenarios?” (R1), “How does scalability impact privacy?” (R1), “Why only GPT-3.5 and top-5 tokens?” (R2), “What is the student model and how is it validated?” (R2). None of these are addressed.
