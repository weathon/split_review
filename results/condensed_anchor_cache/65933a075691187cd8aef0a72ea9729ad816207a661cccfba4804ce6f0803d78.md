## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper addresses Rehearsal-Free Continual Category Discovery (RF-CCD), proposing a method (“Freeze and Cluster”) that leverages a pre-trained model (DINO) fine-tuned only on known classes, freezes the backbone, estimates the number of novel classes via a minimum distance threshold, and uses k-means clustering on frozen features to generate pseudo-labels. Only the classifier is trained incrementally. The work is motivated by an observation that updating the backbone with unlabeled data degrades representation quality. Experiments on Stanford Cars, CUB, iNat, and Tiny-ImageNet show superior performance over prior RF-CCD methods.

### Strengths
- The problem of RF-CCD is a novel blend of continual learning and novel class discovery, and the paper is the first to integrate advances from both fields for this task.
- The motivation is clear and well-supported by comprehensive preliminary experiments (Fig. 1) that reveal representation degradation when unlabeled data is used to update the backbone.
- Freezing the backbone and training only the classifier is a straightforward yet effective technique to preserve pre-trained knowledge and achieve strong performance on standard benchmarks.
- The paper is generally easy to follow; figures (e.g., Fig. 1) are clear and adequate. The appendix provides additional experimental details that further analyze the approach.
- The method outperforms existing RF-CCD baselines across multiple datasets.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and simplicity:** Multiple reviewers (2, 3, 4) note that the method is essentially a simple combination of existing continual learning and novel class discovery techniques (freeze backbone + k-means clustering + classifier retraining) without significant methodological novelty. The plasticity-stability trade-off is well understood, and the solution is intuitive. Reviewers suggest exploring more sophisticated balancing strategies (e.g., Grow and Merge, feature replay methods such as FRoST, Prototype Augmentation [R1], or Happy [R3]).
- **Insufficient comparisons with recent related works:** Reviewer 2 points out that more GCD works should be compared, specifically [1] Online Continuous Generalized Category Discovery, [2] PromptCCD, [3] Happy. Reviewer 4 recommends comparing with feature-replay methods like FRoST [R2] and Happy [R3] that also adjust the feature space. The paper does not answer what performance can be achieved by combining the best GCD and continual learning methods for this task.
- **Dataset scale:** Evaluation uses only small-scale datasets (Stanford Cars, CUB, iNat, Tiny-ImageNet). Results on larger-scale datasets (e.g., ImageNet-1K) are needed for more convincing evidence.
- **Interpretation of preliminary experiments (Fig. 1) is questioned:** Reviewer 3 argues that comparing a supervised loss (true labels) with an unsupervised loss (SeDist) is not a fair test of representation quality. SeDist may inherently conflict with the pre-training objective, causing degradation. They suggest replacing SeDist with the original pre-training loss [A] and testing in an incremental setting that includes both old and new classes. The conclusion that “learning with unlabeled data accumulates noise” is also questioned by Reviewer 4, who notes that feature-replay methods can prevent such degradation.
- **Class number estimation threshold (\(d_{min}\)) is problematic:** Both Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 4 raise concerns. The threshold is required to be pre-defined yet is intractable to set in practice. The algorithm assumes novel classes share the same granularity as base classes; it is unclear how robust it is when novel classes are more fine-grained. Reviewer 1 asks for further development on finding the optimal number of clusters.
- **Noise in pseudo-labels and clustering choice:** Reviewer 1 asks for an ablation with artificially introduced noise in pseudo-labels and exploration of alternative clustering algorithms beyond k-means. Reviewer 4 questions why the classifier is trained on samples drawn from Gaussian distributions fitted to clusters instead of using clustering results in a classifier-free manner.
- **Overfitting of backbone on limited labeled data:** Reviewer 4 raises concern that the backbone is only tuned using labeled known classes (session t=0), which may cause overfitting due to limited labeled data.
- **Lack of session-by-session results:** Reviewer 4 requests detailed performance per incremental session (at least for one or two datasets) to show behavior over time.
- **When to freeze is insufficiently discussed:** Reviewer 1 notes that the decision to freeze after the first supervised session is critical; the paper does not provide analysis of different freezing strategies or criteria for the optimal freezing point.
- **Gaussian assumption for cluster distributions:** Reviewer 1 asks what happens if the data does not follow a single Gaussian per cluster and suggests testing on datasets with known non-Gaussian distributions.
- **Computational efficiency not discussed:** Reviewer 1 notes the need for a discussion on computational cost.
- **Writing issues:** Reviewer 3 points out that the abstract claim about integration of previous methods is not directly linked to the conclusion about representation degradation. The paper does not clearly define the “unique challenges” of RF-CCD (L58). The introduction lacks an explicit explanation that updating with unlabeled data disrupts the pre-trained backbone. The connection between the conclusion in L300 and the proposed method (Section 4) is not sufficiently clarified.
- **Minor writing errors:** Reviewer 2 notes grammatical issues (e.g., “novel class by” should be “novel classes by”; “known classes data” should be “known-class data”).
- **Unclear whether baselines use same pre-trained model:** Reviewer 3 asks if MetaGCD, Frost, and KTRFR also use DINO for fair comparison in Table 1, and whether representation updates are applied for the combined approaches.
- **Disagreement among reviewers:** Two reviewers (1 and 2) rate the paper 5 and find the method reasonable, while two others (3 and 4) rate it 3 and express stronger concerns about novelty, convincingness of conclusions, and practical issues like threshold tuning. The more critical reviews emphasize that the method is too simplistic and the experiments lack deeper analysis.
