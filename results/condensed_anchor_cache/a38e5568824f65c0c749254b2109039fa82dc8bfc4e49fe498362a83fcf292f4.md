## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces PABBO, a fully amortized approach to Preferential Bayesian Optimization that uses a transformer-based neural process architecture trained end-to-end with reinforcement learning and tailored auxiliary losses. It meta-learns both the surrogate model and acquisition function, achieving orders-of-magnitude speedup over GP-based PBO while often improving optimization quality on synthetic and real-world benchmarks up to moderate dimensionality.

### Strengths
- **Novel contribution**: Fully amortizing PBO via a transformer architecture is new and reduces inference cost; the method enables end-to-end training for preference feedback and incorporates an explicit acquisition head that aligns training with downstream use.
- **Well motivated**: Amortization makes sense when the same or similar optimization problem is solved many times; aligning objectives with downstream use is sensible.
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well written and easy to follow; experiments hit key expected points.
- **Empirical performance**: On tested benchmarks (synthetic functions, HPO-B tasks up to 9 dimensions), PABBO often outperforms GP-based methods in accuracy while being orders of magnitude faster at inference; extensive experimentation and ablation studies are provided.
- **Potential for scaling**: The approach uses neural networks instead of GPs, which can scale to a large number of data points and handle large-scale problems.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited evaluation on harder problems**: All experiments are on very small dimensions (max 9-d from HPO-B); moderate-dimensional tasks are rare. This leaves scalability to higher dimensions in question. The paper does not include large-scale experiments (e.g., thousands of observations) where amortization’s full advantage would be realized. Gains at the 100-observation scale are modest, and BO methods perform comfortably there.
- **Pretraining requirements and sensitivity**: The pretraining requires a large amount of data, which can be costly. There is no ablation on the choice/selection of meta-training data, so it is unclear how robust PABBO is to different pretraining sets.
- **Performance advantage not always significant**: In many cases the advantage over GP baselines is modest; for larger models like PABBO1024 the inference-time benefit over GP-based alternatives may diminish (Fig 3, 5).
- **Missing baselines and evaluation metrics**: The paper treats simple and cumulative regret interchangeably. If the policy is optimized for cumulative regret, cumulative regret should be reported. If simple regret is the target, baselines should include best-arm identification methods (e.g., top-two Thompson sampling, which is optimal in the discrete case); no such comparison is provided. No connection is drawn to preference tuning for language models (RLHF), which addresses the same problem.
- **Architectural and training concerns**: 
  - The choice of Gaussian distribution in Eq. (4) is not mathematically justified; its role in out-of-distribution performance is unclear.
  - The RL setup appears static (reward defined as max of observations so far, line 305); it is unclear how RL differs from supervised learning here and whether rewards are learnable.
  - The construction of the query set when evaluating the acquisition head (line 303) is not explained.
  - PABBO currently only supports fixed input dimensionality and pairwise preference queries, limiting flexibility (e.g., queries with multiple options, as in prior work).
- **Lack of theoretical foundation**: The paper focuses on numerical results without providing a theoretical basis or mathematical intuition for the approach.
- **Missing analyses**: 
  - No study of the breakpoint where amortization pays off over direct search; no warm-starting analysis; no FLOPs comparison.
  - The effect of query budget *T* and hyperparameters (e.g., lambda=1 fixed across all experiments; choice of discount factors gamma=0.5,0.9,0.98,1.00) is not explained or ablated.
  - How PABBO avoids getting stuck in sub-optimal solutions due to insufficient exploration is not addressed.
- **Minor presentation issues**: Equation (4) lacks prior explanation of y_{i,1} and y_{i,2} (line 189); term “the rest of P queries” (line 219) may confuse readers; typo “M=50 For” (line 718).
- **Limited user preference modeling**: The method assumes a single, consistent preference model; it does not handle varying user preferences or allow personalization, which is a key use case for preferential optimization.

*Minority reviewer (score 5) is notably more critical, highlighting the lack of theoretical foundation and insufficient justification for architectural/hyperparameter choices as key shortcomings, while acknowledging the end-to-end training and amortization contributions.*
