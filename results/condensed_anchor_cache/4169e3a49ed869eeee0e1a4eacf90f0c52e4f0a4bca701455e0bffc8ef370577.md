## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a multi-task best arm identification (BAI) problem with risk constraints in the fixed-confidence setting. Each arm has multiple performance metrics; the goal is to optimize one metric while ensuring that quantiles of other metrics remain below specified thresholds for each task. The authors derive a tight instance-dependent lower bound on sample complexity, establish optimality conditions for the static optimal sampling ratio, and discuss how it balances tasks and constraints while addressing optimality vs. feasibility. They develop a Track-and-Stop strategy (Algorithm 1) claimed to achieve asymptotically optimal sample complexity, a computationally efficient strategy (SEC, also called SEQSR) that iteratively solves the optimality conditions, and extend results to linear bandits. Numerical experiments compare against ESR, ASR, and FWSR.

### Strengths
- The formulation of a multi-task BAI problem with risk (quantile) constraints is novel and models a wide range of real-world applications. The risk constraint generalizes traditional expectation-based constrained bandits.
- Derivation of a tight, instance-dependent lower bound on sample complexity (Theorem 1) and optimality conditions for the static sampling ratio (Theorem 2). The analysis provides insights into instance-dependent hardness (Lemma 1, Lemma 2, Proposition 1) and how the lower bound balances difficulty across tasks and constraints.
- The Track-and-Stop strategy (Algorithm 1) is provided with an asymptotic optimality guarantee (Theorem 3). The extension to linear bandits (Section 5) is also considered.
- A computationally efficient strategy (SEQSR) is proposed and shows good performance in simulations relative to benchmarks (ESR, ASR, FWSR).
- Numerical experiments are thorough and use multiple benchmarks.
- The paper is overall well-written and clearly organized (Reviewer 2); Reviewer 4 notes it is well-motivated with solid theoretical foundation and effective experiments.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical innovation**: Algorithm 1 essentially replicates the Track-and-Stop method of Garivier & Kaufmann (2016) [1]. The efficient sampling rule in Section 4.2 closely resembles the BestChallenger method from [1, Section 6], and Equation (17) directly corresponds to the definition of BestChallenger in that setting. The authors should have cited BestChallenger explicitly. (Reviewer 1, supported by Reviewer 3’s comment that the work "follows a standard track-and-stop algorithm development and analysis machinery.")
- **Motivation for problem formulation unclear**: The connection to real-world risk management is not concretely illustrated. At least 1–2 specific application scenarios should be described that link the quantile risk constraints to actual requirements. (Reviewer 2)
- **Sample complexity matching lower bound (no log factor)**: It is surprising that the sample complexity of Algorithm 1 (Theorem 3) perfectly matches the lower bound even for logarithmic factors. Intuition is needed for why a union bound over arms and tasks does not appear. (Reviewer 2)
- **Dense notation and lack of intuition**: The formulas and notation are heavy, with abuse of notation and no declaration in the main body. The paper would benefit from simplification and more intuition behind the algorithm design and theoretical results. (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3)
- **Multi-task formulation may be reducible to independent tasks**: Since tasks appear independent (even in the linear bandit extension), the agent could solve each task separately with confidence δ' and combine results. The lower bound in Theorem 1 equalizes complexity per task, effectively enabling per-task error guarantees. A clear justification for solving all tasks jointly is missing. (Reviewer 3)
- **Track-and-Stop limitations**: Algorithm 1 requires solving a computationally heavy min-max problem at each step, making it impractical. Its provable guarantee (Theorem 3) is asymptotic (δ→0) and does not imply finite-time guarantees (this explains why ESR is worse than SEQSR in simulations). The computationally efficient algorithm (SEQSR) lacks any provable sample complexity guarantee, even in simplified models. (Reviewer 3)
- **Overlap with existing BAI with safety constraints**: The paper does not clearly differentiate its contribution from prior work on BAI with safety constraints (e.g., Wang et al., 2022). The risk constraints appear similar to safety constraints, and the multi-task aspect's novelty relative to single-task constrained BAI is not articulated. A more explicit comparison and rationale for novelty is needed. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing details**: Examples are needed for the setting where all metrics are observed when an arm is pulled (i.e., whether the framework and Theorem 5 still apply). The motivation for requiring the agent to choose a metric at each time step (and only observe its reward) is not justified. (Reviewer 3)
- **Presentation issues**: Missing periods at the end of formulas (e.g., Equations (8), (9), (16)). A typo on line 409: "ESR" should be "USR" (based on figure). (Reviewer 3, Reviewer 4)
- **Questions that overlap with weaknesses**: 
    - Can performance guarantees (asymptotic or finite-time) be derived for the SEQSR algorithm to quantitatively compare with H* or H in Proposition 1? (Reviewer 3)
    - Would the algorithm development framework still work if all metrics are observed at each arm pull? (Reviewer 3)
    - How would the model incorporate additional constraints like fairness? Anticipated challenges? (Reviewer 4)
    - Can the authors provide insights into the robustness of results under varying experimental configurations? (Reviewer 4)

*Note on reviewer disagreement:* Reviewers 1 and 3 assess the contribution as limited (scores 6 and 5, with contribution ratings 2) due to lack of novelty and standard track-and-stop machinery, while Reviewer 4 rates it higher (8, contribution 4) as a significant contribution with a novel formulation. Reviewer 2 (6, contribution 3) is in the middle, acknowledging the theoretical contributions but raising motivation and clarity issues. This contrast should be considered in evaluating the overall merit.
