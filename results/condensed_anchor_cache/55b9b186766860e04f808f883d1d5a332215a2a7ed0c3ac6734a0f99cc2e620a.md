## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes H-GAP, a state-action trajectory generative model for humanoid control. It uses a VQ-VAE to encode state-action clips into discrete codes, a transformer to model future distributions conditioned on past observations, and MPC planning with multiple trajectory rollouts. Trained on the MoCapAct dataset, H-GAP is evaluated on a 56 DoF humanoid for imitation learning and downstream control tasks. It outperforms offline RL policies and traditional MPC baselines (e.g., MPPI) without per-task retraining. Scaling experiments analyze model sizes up to 300M parameters. The reviewers acknowledge the principled design and strong results, but raise concerns about motion quality, evaluation of planning capabilities, scalability for downstream tasks, and missing experimental details.

### Strengths

- Generalist approach that is simpler than offline RL methods: no need for simulator access or training separate high-level policies per task.
- Outperforms offline RL policies and traditional MPC algorithms (e.g., MPPI) in most downstream tasks, despite being a single generalist model.
- Principled combination of VQ-VAE (multi-code per transition) and transformer to jointly learn a motion prior and forward dynamics model, enabling autoregressive querying for MPC planning.
- Strong imitation learning results on a 56 DoF humanoid, validating representation of diverse motor behaviors from MoCapAct.
- Imitation learning performance improves with model and data scale, suggesting potential as a foundation model for humanoid control (though downstream task performance drops – see Weaknesses).
- Analyzes scaling properties up to 300M parameters, revealing that accuracy gains do not always translate to downstream task gains.
- Outperforms MLP-backed behavior cloning in imitation learning by a large margin.

### Weaknesses

- **Motion quality and jitter:** One reviewer (R1) notes severe jitter in the provided website visualizations compared to ground truth, questioning whether the transformer fails to model temporal smoothness or the VQ-VAE poorly represents motion clips. Without supplementary videos (the link in the abstract is broken – reported by R3), it is difficult to assess the naturalness of generated human motion; keyframes or quantitative smoothness metrics are missing. 
- **Coverage of complex motions:** R1 is unconvinced of the model’s ability to generate complex motions (e.g., backflips) – the data scaling experiments only randomly sample from the dataset rather than adding new action categories. R2 suggests analyzing motion skill coverage by randomly sampling initial states and generating rollouts, with a clear description of the sampling methodology (uniform, Gaussian, or from the dataset).
- **Planning vs. trajectory modeling:** The current evaluations (especially Table 1) mainly test memorization/replication of training data rather than genuine planning. R2 recommends conditioning on small segments of reference trajectories (not just initial states) to better evaluate context utilization, and to quantify the Gaussian noise added during action selection to demonstrate that performance is not just replication. Additionally, experiments with out-of-distribution initial states (e.g., perturbed poses that require “catching up” to imitate a reference) would help isolate planning contributions. R2 also requests more complex downstream tasks requiring long-term planning beyond trajectory imitation, such as navigating to specified locations or following heading/orientation commands, which would test the model’s ability to compose behaviors.
- **Downstream task scaling paradox:** R3 observes that while imitation learning scales with model size, downstream task performance drops for larger models. This contradicts the claim of providing a basis for a foundation model. No explanation is offered (overfitting, optimization challenges, or fundamental limitation). This requires investigation and discussion.
- **Efficiency and hyperparameters:** R1 questions the need to generate multiple trajectories for MPC – the efficiency (training and inference time) is not reported. R3 asks for training time, the number of samples N for trajectory length T=M=16, L=4, and inference time for Table 2 results compared to alternatives. The choice of temperature and the parameters Upsilon and rho in Algorithm 1 are not specified.
- **Why action modeling?** R1 questions the use of action trajectories if the whole framework is simulator-free; why not model only state trajectories and follow kinematics-based human motion generation? This design choice needs justification.
- **Missing evaluation metrics:** R3 asks what metrics are used in Tables 1 and 2. R1 notes that Table 1 mainly reveals modeling/memorization abilities; metrics that capture motion diversity, smoothness, and planning success should be clearly defined.
- **Disagreement among reviewers:** While R2 and R3 are positive (ratings 8, 8), R1 is more critical (rating 6), especially regarding motion jitter, efficiency, and the need for action modeling.
