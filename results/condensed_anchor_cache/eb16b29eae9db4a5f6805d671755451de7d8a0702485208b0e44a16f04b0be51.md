## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces two new classes of flow-based variational mutual information (MI) estimators: Joint Variational Flow (JVF) and Neural Variational Flow (NVF). Both extend existing Gaussian-based variational estimators (JVG, NVG) by incorporating normalizing flows while maintaining moment‑matching strategies for computational efficiency. The estimators inherit theoretical guarantees and provide an approximate joint distribution from which samples can be drawn. Experiments on synthetic data show that the proposed methods can handle large MI problems where discriminative estimators (MINE, InfoNCE) struggle, and the method is demonstrated in a Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design (BOED) setting.

### Strengths
1. **Originality and theoretical innovation** – Combining normalising flows with Gaussian variational MI estimators (JVG and NVG) is a creative and theoretically sound extension. The approach enhances expressivity while preserving existing guarantees. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
2. **Stabilised training** – The alternative formulation where the Jacobian of both flows appears in the objective mitigates stability issues (Lemma 3.3). (Reviewers 1, 2)
3. **Additional utility** – The estimators yield an approximate distribution of \(p(x,y)\), enabling sample generation for prediction or simulation; the idea may also extend to general flow‑based density estimation. (Reviewers 1, 2)
4. **Thorough theoretical development** – The paper provides clear proofs, lemmas, and an honest discussion of limitations and failure cases. (Reviewer 2)
5. **Experimental scope (synthetic)** – A diverse set of synthetic benchmarks demonstrates effectiveness on large MI problems where discriminative estimators (MINE, InfoNCE) fail, and shows robustness to model mismatch. (Reviewers 2, 3)
6. **Good literature coverage** – The paper adequately surveys relevant literature. (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 4 noted one missing reference – see Weaknesses)

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient real‑world validation** – All experiments are on synthetic data. This is inadequate to demonstrate robustness on complex real‑world data, and for ICLR more realistic benchmarks are expected. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)  
   * The BOED evaluation is limited to a single location‑finding scenario; prior works (Foster et al. 2021, Ivanova et al. 2021) evaluate on multiple benchmarks. (Reviewer 2)
2. **Missing error bars and ablations** – The experiments lack error bars and ablation studies on key parameters such as dimensionality and the impact of the number of sequential decisions. (Reviewer 2)
3. **Missing empirical comparisons**  
   * No empirical comparison between the two proposed JVF variants. (Reviewer 1)  
   * No evaluation in representation‑learning scenarios where InfoNCE is commonly used; practical value in such settings is not demonstrated. (Reviewer 1)  
   * The paper does not compare against CLUB (Cheng et al. 2020), a neural‑network‑based MI estimator that addresses a similar problem. (Reviewer 4)
4. **Assumptions limitation** – JVF assumes availability of a joint distribution, which may not be practical. NVF relaxes this, but the general applicability under different conditions remains underexplored. (Reviewer 3)
5. **Robustness concerns** – Robustness under model misspecification (e.g., non‑Gaussian noise, heavy‑tailed distributions like Student‑t) is not thoroughly validated; performance may degrade significantly with heavy tails. (Reviewer 3)
6. **Handling lower‑dimensional manifolds** – The paper does not clarify how the methods handle data that resides on a lower‑dimensional manifold. (Reviewer 1’s question)
7. **Imprecise classification of MI estimators** – The paper’s distinction between “critic‑based” and “variational” estimators is imprecise: many critic‑based estimators (DV, NWJ, InfoNCE) are also variational (Poole et al. 2019); the described “variational” estimators should be considered parametric. (Reviewer 1)
8. **Presentation / notation** – The notation for random variables is inconsistent and does not follow information‑theory conventions (e.g., using lowercase \(x\) for random variables; Cover & Thomas recommend uppercase \(X\)). This is distracting for readers with an information‑theory background. (Reviewer 4)
9. **Minority positive view contrasted** – One reviewer (score 8, confidence 5) found the paper sophisticated, well‑grounded, and the experiments impressive. In contrast, the other three reviewers (scores 5‑6, confidence 3) raised significant concerns about limited practical validation, missing comparisons, and insufficient benchmarks for the application domain, and felt the experiments were more suited for AISTATS than ICLR.
