## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Semantic Embedding Uncertainty (SEU), which uses average pairwise cosine similarity of semantic embeddings from multiple LLM responses to quantify uncertainty, and Amortised SEU (ASEU), a single-forward-pass latent variable model that avoids multiple generations. The authors argue that prior bidirectional entailment methods (e.g., Semantic Entropy) are overly sensitive to minor wording variations, and that cosine similarity provides a continuous, robust measure. Experiments on three short-answer QA datasets (across three LLMs) show SEU outperforms prior methods, and ASEU outperforms length-normalized predictive entropy.

### Strengths
- **Important problem**: The paper tackles uncertainty quantification for LLMs, a crucial issue for reliable deployment. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3)
- **Efficient single-pass variant**: ASEU estimates uncertainty in a single forward pass, significantly reducing computational cost while achieving strong results. (Reviewers 1, 3) Reviewer 2 also notes this as a novel contribution.
- **Novel approach**: Introducing semantic embedding–based uncertainty (SEU) is a clear and interesting innovation that moves beyond reliance on strict bidirectional entailment. (Reviewer 2, 3)
- **Clear empirical validation**: The method is evaluated across multiple QA datasets and frontier LLMs with good comparisons to existing baselines (e.g., SE, length-normalized entropy). (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Well-written and structured**: The paper presents a clear storyline, making the contributions easy to follow. (Reviewers 2, 3)
- **A significant step forward**: The amortised version is particularly interesting and efficient. (Reviewer 3)

### Weaknesses
1. **Reliability of cosine similarity as an absolute semantic measure** (Reviewers 1, 3): Cosine similarity in embedding spaces is not well-calibrated for absolute semantic equivalence. The embedding space is isotropic ([1]), and Sentence-BERT is trained for relative similarity, not absolute thresholds. High cosine similarity (e.g., >0.9) does not guarantee high semantic similarity. Moreover, Reviewer 3 notes that the claim that “cosine similarity provides a continuous metric” is not a unique advantage: bidirectional entailment can also output continuous scores using NLI probabilities/logits. Adding a baseline that replaces cosine similarity with NLI scores would clarify the benefit.

2. **Weak comparison with Semantic Entropy (SE)**: SEU encodes responses independently, missing the fine-grained interaction that NLI-based SE provides (Reviewers 1, 3). The claim that SEU is more robust is not convincingly supported; experiments only use one NLI model (Reviewer 1) – comparisons across multiple NLI classifiers are needed. Reviewer 3 considers SEU a “simple adapted version” of SE, lacking sufficient scientific contribution. Additionally, SEU yields worse FPR on some metrics (Table 2, Reviewer 3).

3. **Generalization beyond QA**: The method is validated only on short-answer QA datasets. Its applicability to other NLP tasks (text summarization, machine translation) where criteria like coherence/fluency matter more than semantic similarity is untested (Reviewer 2). Even within QA, evaluation on long-form answers (e.g., TruthfulQA) is missing (Reviewer 3).

4. **Missing embedding-based baseline and metrics**: A key baseline is missing: INSIDE (ICLR 2024) which uses EigenScore for semantic consistency in embedding space (Reviewer 3). Also, the paper only reports AUROC; additional uncertainty metrics (Brier score, ECE) are necessary (Reviewer 3). The evaluation metric ROUGE-L is insensitive to semantics; BERTScore or LLM-as-Judge should be used (Reviewer 3).

5. **Computational and training costs for ASEU** (Reviewer 2): While ASEU reduces inference cost, the training phase involves inference and optimization of latent variables, which may require high computational resources. The paper lacks a detailed cost analysis, making practical feasibility in resource-constrained settings unclear.

6. **Dependence on pre-trained embedding models** (Reviewer 2): SEU/ASEU rely on high-quality pre-trained language models for embeddings. Biases or inaccuracies in those models could propagate; the paper does not discuss sensitivity to the choice of embedding model or mitigation strategies.

7. **Limited model size diversity in experiments** (Reviewer 2): Figures 1-3 compare only a narrow set of model sizes. A broader range (e.g., 3B to 70B parameters) and within-architecture size variations (same architecture, different sizes) are needed to understand scalability and whether performance depends on size or architecture.

8. **ASEU requires fine-tuning while baselines are unsupervised** (Reviewer 3): ASEU requires additional fine-tuning, which limits its generality compared to zero-shot baselines. The paper does not discuss how this affects fair comparison or applicability.

9. **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (Rating 5) found the paper less novel and more limited in evaluation than the other two reviewers (both Rating 6). This reviewer particularly emphasized weaknesses in generalization, computational costs, and model size analysis.
