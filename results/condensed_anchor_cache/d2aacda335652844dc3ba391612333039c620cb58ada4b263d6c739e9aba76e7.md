## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CLAT (Criticality-Leveraged Adversarial Training), which identifies layers that are most vulnerable to adversarial perturbations (based on an approximate Lipschitz-criticality metric) and only fine-tunes those layers during adversarial training, keeping the rest fixed. It claims to mitigate adversarial overfitting and improve both clean and robust accuracy by over 2% compared to baselines. The method is tested on CIFAR-10/100 with various CNN architectures and combined with several AT variants.

### Strengths
1. The core idea—selectively fine-tuning only robustness-critical layers rather than the entire network—is interesting and less studied. It has the potential to improve the accuracy-robustness trade-off by reducing over-regularization. (Reviewers 1,5)
2. CLAT can be applied on top of any adversarial training method (e.g., PGD-AT, TRADES) and is validated across multiple architectures. (Reviewer 1)
3. The evaluation is thorough: it includes white-box and black-box attacks, strong attacks like AutoAttack, ablation studies (Fig 2, overhead analysis), and comparisons with several baselines. The results and ablations clearly show advantages and limitations of CLAT over pure adversarial training. (Reviewers 1,3)
4. The presentation is clear, the experimental section is well-organized, and the method is intuitive. (Reviewers 2,4)
5. The fraction of parameters actually updated is often less than 4% of the total network, suggesting computational efficiency during fine-tuning. (Reviewers 3,5)

### Weaknesses
1. **Problem statement and evaluation scope** – The paper claims to address “adversarial overfitting” but does not define it clearly. This is confusing because there already exist defined phenomena such as “robust overfitting” and “catastrophic overfitting.” The evaluation criteria for overfitting (e.g., reporting both peak and final accuracy) are missing, making it unclear whether the method improves overfitting or the clean-robust trade-off. (Reviewers 1,5)
2. **Weak theoretical justification** – The rationale for why updating only critical layers inherently leads to better generalization is not adequately explained. The approach appears similar to simply reducing the learning rate, and the paper does not rigorously justify why it should outperform standard regularization. (Reviewer 4)  
   - One reviewer notes that the criticality metric is essentially an estimate of the ratio of local Lipschitz constants across layers; they suggest using standard terminology and adding a discussion comparing other Lipschitz regularization defenses. (Reviewer 3)
   - Another reviewer questions whether the method is fundamentally different from reducing the learning rate or fixing the max number of epochs; they argue that without training to convergence, the method may just prevent the model from fitting further. (Reviewer 4)
3. **Limited experimental scope** – Experiments are only on CIFAR-10/100 with CNN architectures (e.g., ResNet, WideResNet). Missing larger-scale datasets (e.g., ImageNet) and evaluations on vision transformers (ViTs). (Reviewers 2,3)  
   - A hypothesis to test is whether a more complex dataset also requires a similar fraction of CLAT parameters. (Reviewer 3)
4. **Weak adversarial evaluation** – The paper uses 10-step PGD without restart, which is considered too weak for reliable robustness evaluation. This undermines the soundness of the results, especially in Table 1. (Reviewer 5)
5. **Missing comparisons and ablations**  
   - Comparison with explicit overfitting reduction techniques like AWP (Adversarial Weight Perturbation) and SWA (Stochastic Weight Averaging), including best vs. final accuracy metrics. (Reviewer 1)  
   - Comparison with simply reducing the learning rate over the same number of epochs. (Reviewer 4)  
   - Ablation on fine-tuning layers with different criticality values (small vs. large \(C_{f_i}\)) to demonstrate the effectiveness of the Layer Criticality Index. (Reviewer 2)  
   - Tracking how the distribution of critical layers changes across training (since it is re-identified every 10 epochs). Are critical layers more prominent in earlier or later layers as training progresses? (Reviewer 3)  
   - Comparison of epoch wall-time for PGD-AT vs. PGD-AT + CLAT. (Reviewer 3)  
   - Combination with data augmentation methods known to mitigate overfitting (e.g., IDBH, generative models). (Reviewer 5)  
6. **Terminology and clarity** – The paper uses multiple inconsistent terms such as “weakness,” “robust-critical layers,” “sensitive layers,” and “non-robust features” without clear definitions. It is also ambiguous whether the method targets non-robust features or non-robust layers (weights). (Reviewer 1)  
   - The fine-tuning loss in Section 3.2 is similar to TRADES, but this connection is not discussed. (Reviewer 5)
7. **Methodological concerns**  
   - The approach requires inputting both clean and adversarial images to compute the difference, making it computationally cumbersome. (Reviewer 2)  
   - Section 3.1 introduces many mathematical tools that ultimately seem unnecessary, since the final objective minimizes from the initial starting point. (Reviewer 2)  
   - The approximation \(\frac{\partial F(x')}{\partial x'} \approx\) uniform vector in Eq.5 lacks justification. (Reviewer 3)  
8. **Lack of convergence analysis** – The paper fixes the maximum number of epochs rather than training to convergence, deviating from common practice. It is unclear how the method performs with more epochs. (Reviewer 4)  
9. **Detailed follow-up requests**  
   - Hyperparameter choice (e.g., the 5% threshold for selecting critical layers) is not justified; is it architecture-dependent? (Reviewer 1)  
   - Can the criticality be considered at sub-network or neuron level? (Reviewer 1)  
   - Figure 3’s behavior for clean accuracy is not explained; the hypothesis lacks supporting evidence. (Reviewer 1)  
   - Some tables need to bold best results for readability. (Reviewer 2)  
   - The plots (e.g., Fig. 2) have very thin lines; increase line thickness and font size. (Reviewer 3)

**Minority contrast**: While most reviewers find the idea interesting and the results promising, one reviewer (score 3) finds the method cumbersome, the math redundant, and the experiments too limited; another reviewer (score 5) questions the theoretical significance and whether it is simply slowing training. Conversely, several reviewers (scores 6) praise the novelty and thoroughness of the ablations.
