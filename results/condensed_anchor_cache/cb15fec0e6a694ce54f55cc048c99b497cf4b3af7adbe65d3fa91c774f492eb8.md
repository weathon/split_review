## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an end-to-end, differentiable system for agile quadrotor collision avoidance from depth images. It combines a neural network that predicts a safe corridor (sequence of spheres) with an embedded differentiable trajectory optimizer. A regularized library of motion primitives is used to capture multimodal trajectory distributions. The system is evaluated in simulation and real-world forest flights on an Orin NX computer, showing improved success rate and jerk over baselines.

### Strengths

- **Integration of optimization into the neural network**: The differentiable optimizer is jointly trained with the perception network, allowing the cost function of trajectory optimization to be part of the loss (Reviewers 2, 4). This bridges traditional planning and learning.
- **Lightweight and efficient design**: Real-time inference on Orin NX with few motion primitives, simple safe corridor geometry (spheres), and an energy optimization loop embedded inside the neural policy (Reviewer 1).
- **Strong empirical evaluation**: Ample benchmarking against state-of-the-art methods with solid success rate, jerk improvements, and competitive maximum velocities; real-world flight experiments in dense forests demonstrate practical deployment (Reviewers 1, 4).
- **Good visualization and figures** that clearly illustrate the approach (Reviewer 1).

### Weaknesses

- **Limited novelty**: Implicit differentiation to embed trajectory optimization is not new; similar ideas exist in differentiable planning works such as BarrierNet (Xiao et al., 2023) that solve CBF QPs during inference for visual end-to-end navigation (Reviewer 1). The technical contribution is more in systems engineering than ML/AI (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Dependency on precomputed motion primitives**: Unlike iPlanner (which eliminates expert training data), this method relies on a library of trajectories as training data, potentially limiting adaptability in environments not represented in training (Reviewer 4). Could the generated trajectories be used as adaptively updated primitives? (Reviewer 4)
- **Safety guarantees reduced to soft penalties**: Traditional corridor-based methods enforce hard constraints; here all constraints are soft penalties, risking violation under certain conditions (Reviewers 1, 4). Is there a feasible way to enforce \(L_{safe}\) as a hard constraint? (Reviewer 4)
- **Real-world environment not sufficiently cluttered** to impress given the central claim of agility (Reviewer 1). The paper would benefit from a video of real-robot tests (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing details and insufficient exposition**:
  - Explanation of the probability distribution over the primitive library (e.g., how deterministic trajectories from map-based planners are converted to a multimodal probability distribution for training) (Reviewer 2).
  - Justification for why training directly on the original task leads to undesirable local minima/saddle points (Reviewer 2).
  - Study of the effect of trajectory discretization \(N\) (number of pieces) on collision avoidance guarantees (e.g., sphere intersection, whether equation (3) guarantees safety over the entire trajectory) and on model complexity/optimization time (Reviewer 1).
  - Tuning process for loss weights (Appendix B2) is not described (Reviewer 1).
  - Channel bias in the primitive library: is it balanced, does it average to a straight line? (Reviewer 1).
  - Comparison of corridor quality (spatial coverage, tightness, suitability for high-speed navigation) with search-based methods, with quantitative results (Reviewer 4).
- **Exposition issues**: Contribution not clearly stated in the introduction; related work does not discuss similar differentiable combination methods; Figure 2 is difficult to parse (Reviewer 3). Minor: missing spaces and unclear wording in lines 019 and 037 (Reviewer 2).
- **Venue fit**: The paper is better suited for robotics venues (ICRA, IROS) than ICLR (Reviewers 1, 3).
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Two reviewers (scores 6) view the work as a strong systems contribution with effective integration, while two (scores 3) consider the technical novelty insufficient and the real-world demonstration unconvincing.
