## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces SCREENER, a fully self-supervised framework for anomaly segmentation in 3D CT images. It uses descriptor, condition, and density models to learn conditional distributions of local patterns given global context. The method is trained on over 30,000 CT scans and evaluated on four test datasets (1,820 scans) covering chest and abdominal pathologies. Reviewers find the problem meaningful and the use of a large dataset for self-supervised pretraining commendable. However, substantial concerns about clarity, missing details, inappropriate metric selection, limited comparative evaluation, and potential test set contamination are raised across all reviewers. Reviewer 3 is more positive (score 6, confidence 5) and highlights the innovation of conditional density modeling, while Reviewers 1 and 2 are more critical (scores 5, confidence 3) about presentation and evaluation.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses a meaningful and challenging problem: unsupervised pathology segmentation in 3D medical images.
- The training dataset is large (over 30,000 3D CT scans from NLST, AMOS, AbdomenAtlas), which is advantageous for self-supervised learning.
- The introduction and related work sections are thorough and well-written, providing a solid overview of existing methods and their limitations.
- The self-supervised learning approach (using a large CT dataset for pretraining) offers a valuable alternative to supervised feature extractors in label-scarce settings.
- The use of conditional density modeling to simplify anomaly scoring is considered innovative (noted by Reviewer 3). The model achieves promising results across multiple datasets given its unsupervised nature.
- The aim to make code and pretrained models publicly available is positive.

### Weaknesses
- **Unclear method description and missing pipeline figure:** The paper lacks a pipeline illustration, making it difficult to grasp the workflow. The distinction between novel contributions and prior work (e.g., modifications to UVAS, use of SimCLR/VICReg) is not clearly stated. Key terms (“conditional distribution,” “local image patterns,” “global image content”) are insufficiently defined. The adaptation of SimCLR to produce dense feature maps for pixel-level anomaly detection is vague.
- **Missing dataset statistics:** Statistics (size, density, pathology distribution) of the claimed large dataset are not provided. A summary and detailed description are needed.
- **Inappropriate metric selection:** The evaluation uses pixel-level AUROC for segmentation, which does not account for spatial contiguity. Segmentation-specific metrics such as Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Intersection over Union (IoU), Hausdorff Distance (HD), or Normalized Surface Distance (NSD) are missing. A maximum Dice comparison with supervised SOTA is also absent, making it hard to assess practical segmentation utility.
- **Limited comparative evaluation:** No results on standard anomaly detection benchmarks (e.g., brain datasets like MOOD) are provided, hindering positioning relative to established methods. The paper only compares against a limited set of unsupervised baselines. Additional metrics from benchmark challenges would strengthen the evaluation.
- **Missing architecture and sampling details:** The choices of downsampling size (h, w, s), upsampling strategy, number of layers, filter sizes, and activation functions are not specified, impacting reproducibility. Design decisions need justification.
- **Potential test set contamination:** Parts of the test datasets LiTS and KiTS are included in the training dataset AbdomenAtlas. The paper does not discuss this overlap or its potential impact on results.
- **Domain gap influence not addressed:** The effect of domain gaps between different CT datasets is not analyzed. No variance (standard deviations, confidence intervals) across domains is reported, and generalizability to different modalities or anomaly types remains unclear.
- **Insufficient ablation and component analysis:** No experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of individual components (descriptor, condition, density models). Qualitative comparisons showing component contributions are missing.
- **Related work gaps:** The paper misses citations of early synthetic anomaly detection methods (e.g., FPI) and CRADL, which shares a similar structure (SimCLR pretrained encoder with Gaussian/flow-based density model). The limitations of existing methods and how SCREENER addresses them are not clearly discussed.
- **Presentation issues:** Figure 1 (anomaly map) is unclear – the second column shows an image flagged as diseased but lacks a mask or clearer labeling. Some claims (e.g., about density models or scale of CT data) are not fully supported; additional examples and visualizations would improve clarity.
