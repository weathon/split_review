## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper explores the alignment of small language models (SLMs) by combining reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) with knowledge distillation (KD). It proposes two methods: Dual-Constrained Knowledge Distillation (DCKD), which applies KL-divergence constraints from an aligned teacher, and Advantage-Guided Distillation for Preference Alignment (ADPA), which leverages an advantage function from the teacher to deliver distribution-level reward signals. Experiments with three teacher-student model settings demonstrate improvements in alignment for SLMs.

### Strengths
- The problem of aligning small language models is important and under-explored; the proposal to combine RLHF with KD is well-motivated and principled.
- ADPA’s use of a distribution-level reward signal via an advantage function is a novel advancement over prior KD methods for preference alignment.
- The methods (DCKD and ADPA) are concise, easy to implement, and the paper provides detailed algorithm steps and formulas that are easy to follow.
- The experimental design is thorough, with numerous comparative and ablation experiments; KD baselines are comprehensively compared and teacher-student settings are representative.
- The paper is well-organized and the presentation is clear.
- Empirical results of ADPA are strong, showing appreciable improvement in small model alignment and narrowing the gap with larger models.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited alignment baselines.** Only DPO is included as an alignment baseline (Table 1). It is unclear whether directly applying more advanced alignment methods (e.g., other RLHF variants or iterative DPO) to small models could yield competitive performance. A broader set of alignment-only baselines is needed.
- **Computational overhead not quantified.** The overhead introduced by ADPA’s fine-grained signals (advantage computation) and by KD methods in general is not measured. The paper lacks quantitative metrics comparing training/inference costs against directly applying alignment to the student. A request to elaborate on the computational cost of the proposed methods versus direct alignment.
- **Evaluation scope is limited.** Experiments are run on a single evaluation dataset (exact name not given) and only on H2O-Danube3-500M and H2O-Danube2-1.8B-Base. Results on more popular or recent models (e.g., LLaMA-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-0.5B) and across broader domains/data are missing, which would make the findings more solid.
- **No study of teacher proficiency.** There are no experiments assessing the impact of using teachers with varying levels of alignment or quality on the student’s final performance.
- **Clarity on ADPA’s relation to preference alignment and motivation.** One reviewer noted that from Section 3.3 and Algorithm 1, ADPA appears not to require preference labels—only prompts and ground truth responses—raising the question of how the improvement is actually connected to preference alignment. This same reviewer (the most critical) contrasted with other reviewers who found the motivation clear. Further explanation is needed:
  - Why the difference between π_dpo and π_ref is used rather than just π_dpo (Table 2).
  - What the effect would be if π_dpo were removed from Equation (11) (i.e., using only -log π_sft).
  - Equation (11) also has an undefined term L_s (likely L_SFT, but it should be clarified).
- The paper would benefit from more ablation on ADPA’s components to isolate the source of improvement and its relationship to preference signals.
