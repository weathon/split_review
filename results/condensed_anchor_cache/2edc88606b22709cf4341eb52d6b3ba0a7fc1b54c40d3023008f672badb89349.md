## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies forgetting in continual learning of LLMs, proposing a distinction between true knowledge loss and “spurious forgetting” (loss of task alignment). Using a synthetic biography dataset, the authors analyze performance degradation from multiple perspectives (loss landscape, model weights, features) and connect it to orthogonal updates in model weights. They propose a simple freezing strategy (freezing lower layers) that improves performance across four scenarios. Reviewers agree the perspective is novel and the analysis is generally thorough, but raise concerns about novelty of the method, empirical support for the orthogonal updates theory, generalizability beyond the synthetic dataset, weak baselines, and figure clarity.

### Strengths
- Novel and compelling claim distinguishing knowledge forgetting from spurious/task-alignment forgetting, challenging common assumptions in continual learning. (R1, R2, R3)
- Well-articulated and well-structured arguments with detailed explanations. (R1)
- Multiple perspectives (performance, loss landscape, model weights, features) effectively substantiate the central claim. (R2)
- Clear theoretical analysis based on orthogonal updates in model weights, with formal definition of “pseudo-forgetting”. (R2, R3)
- The proposed Freeze method (freezing bottom layers) is simple, easy to implement, and yields good performance improvements, outperforming baselines in four continual learning scenarios. (R1, R2, R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty of freezing approach and insufficient literature review**: Freezing lower layers for fine-tuning is a long-established technique (e.g., VGG paper). The paper does not adequately compare or differentiate from prior parameter-freezing work, limiting originality. (R1, R3) [R1 additionally notes lack of comparisons to established continual learning methods, not just SEQ.]
- **Weak baseline comparisons**: In real-world scenarios, the proposed method is only compared to the SEQ method, which is a weak baseline. Stronger continual learning baselines should be included. (R1)
- **Limited evidence for theoretical claims**:
  - The empirical support for the orthogonality assumption (Assumption 4.4) is insufficient. Figure 4(b) shows angles between weight updates mostly below 45°, which does not strongly support near-orthogonality. (R1)
  - Orthogonal updates occur in bottom layers only after the first 150 steps, but accuracy of Task 0 already declines significantly during those initial 150 steps. This contradicts the claim that performance declines are “largely a result of orthogonal updates in model weights.” (R2, who scored the paper higher at 8) – even the more positive reviewer finds the causal link unsupported.
  - The figures, especially Figure 3, are difficult to interpret. For instance, the gradient descent path appears to diverge from task 0, contradicting the paper’s claim about realignment. (R3, confidence 4)
- **Generalizability concerns**: Experiments focus primarily on a synthetic biography dataset. It is unclear whether the method and findings transfer to real-world datasets, other continual learning scenarios, or broader model architectures. (R3)
- **Methodological concerns and missing analyses**:
  - The theoretical framework relies on a linear model; it is unclear how well this assumption captures the complexities of large language models. (R1)
  - The choice of bases for the loss landscapes (Task 1 knowledge, Task 0 alignment) is not well justified. (R1)
  - Exact match accuracy is not used for all tasks; why not? (R1)
  - The angle computation in Figure 4 is based on column spaces rather than directly on weight updates – this needs justification. (R1)
  - The distinction between knowledge and alignment is not empirically supported; no ablation study is provided to isolate these effects. (R3)
  - The practical utility of the “pseudo-forgetting” concept is questionable: since the model must maintain alignment to be effective, what actionable insights does this distinction provide? (R3)
  - The initial training phase (first 150 steps) of new tasks: was a warm-up phase applied? Could the observed alignment shift be due to optimizer state instability rather than true alignment loss? Would a longer initial phase change the conclusions? (R3)
