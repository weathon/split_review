## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces and documents *generative monoculture*—a narrowing of LLM output diversity relative to the training data distribution. The phenomenon is studied on two tasks: book reviews (sentiment attribute) and code generation (algorithm efficiency attribute). Across multiple LLMs (including GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-3), the authors find consistent monoculture that is not meaningfully mitigated by adjusting sampling temperature, prompt diversity, or other simple countermeasures. They argue that the root cause likely lies in alignment procedures (RLHF), and that this behavior has important societal implications (e.g., loss of diverse opinions in education and search). Reviewers broadly agree the topic is important and timely, but raise substantial concerns about methodology, evaluation reliability, and the limited scope of experiments. One reviewer (R3) was notably more positive, stating the paper is “a nice, intuitive idea that deserves to be highlighted” and “warrants publication in the current form” despite weaknesses; others rated the paper lower (5s and 6s, with R1 and R5 scoring 5) and highlighted risks to validity.

### Strengths
- The paper formalizes a subtle but important phenomenon—generative monoculture—that is intuitive yet previously undocumented as a distinct concept. Reviewers (especially R3) note this gives the paper value as an “official cite” for the problem.
- The study focuses on task-specific, salient attributes (sentiment of book reviews, algorithms in code) rather than solely lexical measures. Reviewers consider this a meaningful advance over prior diversity metrics (R3, R1).
- The paper provides a well-structured methodology with multiple diversity metrics: entropy, mean pairwise similarity, standard deviation, and distributional comparisons. This is praised by R1.
- The authors clearly discuss nuances around when diversity is desirable vs. less important (e.g., correct code may not need diversity). R2 and R1 highlight this as a strength.
- The paper tests several mitigation strategies (temperature adjustment, prompt diversification) and reports negative results, which reviewers find insightful (R4).
- The paper includes discussion of societal and code security impacts, which strengthens its importance (R1).
- R3 appreciates that the paper uses task-specific attributes rather than e.g., vocabulary distributions, making findings more relevant to real-world deployment.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing appendix / incomplete methodology**: The paper claims crucial methodological details (e.g., dataset construction, evaluation prompts) are in the appendix, but no appendix was included in the submission. R2 states this makes it impossible to fully assess and reproduce the work, and gave a score of 1 (strong reject) despite otherwise positive feedback. This is a major procedural gap.
- **Reliance on automatic evaluation, especially GPT-3.5 as judge**: Multiple reviewers (R3, R4, R5, R2) note that many evaluations—sentiment classification, code correctness, big-O complexity, code summary tags—are performed by GPT-3.5. This is problematic because: (1) LLMs are known to be biased toward their own outputs, creating a potential circularity when evaluating other models (R3, R4); (2) GPT-3.5 is weaker than the models being evaluated (GPT-4, Claude-3) so accuracy is uncertain (R4); (3) no rigorous validation of GPT-3.5's judgments is provided for the efficiency or tag-assignment tasks (R2, R5). Reviewers strongly suggest human evaluation or at least comparison with ground-truth measurements (e.g., actual runtime for code, human-annotated sentiment) to validate findings.
- **Uncertainty about source dataset being in training data**: The study measures monoculture against a source distribution from GoodReads (book reviews) and a code dataset, but R5 raises serious concerns: (1) no evidence that these datasets are actually part of the LLMs’ training data; (2) even if included, the selected subset (742 books with 10 reviews each for books; 100 easy coding problems with 20 correct solutions each for code) may not represent the full training distribution; (3) filtering by length (300–700 words for book reviews) may skew sentiment. R5 recommends using models with fully known training data (e.g., OLMo) for a controlled measurement. Other reviewers also note the dataset sizes are small, limiting generalizability (R4).
- **Claim that RLHF hurts diversity is under-supported**: R2 notes that Takeaway #3 (RLHF reduces diversity more than other factors) is based on only one pair of models (GPT-4 vs. GPT-4 instruct). Additional experiments with other model pairs (e.g., instruction-tuned and RLHF’ed OLMo) are needed, or a caveat should be added. R1 also wanted to see alignment data compared directly to the training data of CPT.
- **Limited scope of tasks**: While book reviews and code are complementary, R1 suggests testing additional tasks such as dialogue/chatbots to assess generalizability. The code dataset is restricted to “easy” problems, raising questions about real-world coding complexity (R4).
- **Countermeasures insufficiently explored**: R1 notes that the tested mitigations showed limited efficacy, but more experimentation and ideation are needed (e.g., drawing from exploration/exploitation strategies in recommender systems). The paper does not redefine the alignment process itself.
- **Minor weaknesses**: (a) The claim that humans largely prefer positive reviews (Line 393) lacks citation; R2 and R4 both request evidence and provide counterexamples (Park & Nicolau; Willemsen et al.). (b) Figure 4 is too busy and confusing (R2: subfigure labeling overlaps). (c) Figure 4(d) shows that for “decay T p = 1.0” entropy exceeds the source distribution, which contradicts the paper’s statement (Line 379) that “there exists significant narrowing from the source to generation distribution in all attributes considered.” R5 flags this inconsistency. (d) The method for generating correct code solutions (Line 309: “generating k samples […] and verifying that at least 20 of them were correct”) is unclear; R2 and R4 ask for clarification on what happens if fewer than 20 are correct and how correctness is defined. (e) Plagiarism score in Figure 5(b) is not explained (R5).
- **Lack of human evaluation**: As mentioned above, R3 and others strongly recommend including human judgments to validate the automatic evaluation, even though they expect results would hold. R3 says this is a primary concern.
- **Connection to related literature**: R1 notes that the echo chamber / feedback loop problem is well studied in recommender systems (e.g., bandits, exploration/exploitation), and the paper could benefit from drawing on that literature. R2 also suggests a citation (Forcing Diffuse Distributions out of Language Models, arXiv 2404.10859) for the related work.
- **Role of model size not explored**: R1 asks whether model size/complexity was ablated; this is not investigated.
