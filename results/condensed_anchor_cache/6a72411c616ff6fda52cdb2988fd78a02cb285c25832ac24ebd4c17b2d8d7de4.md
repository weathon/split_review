## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes GARLIC, a retrieval method for long-document QA that constructs a Hierarchical Weighted Directed Acyclic Graph (HWDAG) from LLM-generated summary nodes (Information Points) and uses attention-based edge weights and a dynamic, LLM-guided search to retrieve relevant information. The method is evaluated on NarrativeQA, Qasper, HotpotQA, and MuSiQue, outperforming baselines including Llama 3.1 while maintaining RAG-like computational cost. The four reviewers gave scores 3, 5, 6, 6, indicating a range of opinions. One reviewer (score 3, confidence 3) found the paper particularly lacking in clarity and experimental analysis, while the other three scored 5 to 6 and were more positive overall, though all identified missing details and incomplete comparisons.

### Strengths
- **Novel graph structure and search.** The Hierarchical Weighted Directed Acyclic Graph replaces traditional tree structures, enabling multi-path dynamic retrieval and hierarchical summary nodes that flexibly adjust retrieval depth and information volume. (R1 S1, S2; R4 S1)
- **Dynamic stopping and attention-weighted paths** allow the method to avoid unnecessary computation common in exhaustive retrieval, while the priority-based BFS (rather than depth-first) is more flexible and still efficient. (R2 S1; R4 S2)
- **Strong empirical results.** GARLIC outperforms previous state-of-the-art baselines, including Llama 3.1 and recent methods, on NarrativeQA, Qasper, HotpotQA, and MuSiQue. (R2 S2; R4 S2) One reviewer noted “decent performance compared to other existing approaches for retrieval.” (R1 S3)
- **Practical compatibility.** The method works with long-context LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.1) and is feasible for single-GPU execution. (R2 S3)
- **Well-written and easy to read.** (R3 S1)
- **Interesting idea** of using LLM attention mechanisms for edge weights and dynamic progress control; can serve as a reference for other researchers. (R1 S2; R3 S2)

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clarity and missing details on edge weight calculation and attention-based relevance scoring.** Multiple reviewers noted the paper does not adequately explain how edge weights in the HWDAG are derived from attention mechanisms. (R1 W1; R4 W1) Specifically, it is unclear how token-level attentions are averaged to establish relationships between higher-level Information Points (IPs) and whether this averaging is appropriate. (R3 W5) The method for calculating query-IP relevance via attention (Section 3.2.2) is not clearly described, raising scalability concerns for new queries and documents. (R4 W2) The justification for multiplying attention weights by node position (line 292) is insufficient; the authors should compare with alternative scaling functions (exponential, logarithmic, learned) and analyze attention distributions. (R3 W1)
- **Insufficient comparative analysis and motivation.** The paper does not clearly explain why HWDAG is superior to tree-based RAG methods (e.g., RAPTOR, which also selects important nodes top-down via graph search). (R3 W2) The specific limitations of tree structures and examples where introducing refined information hurts performance are not elaborated. (R3 W2) Design distinctions and advantages over RAPTOR and MemWalker (beyond using greedy search) are not clarified. (R2) Exclusion of baselines like LongT5 XL and CoLT5 XL is not justified; GARLIC’s performance should also be discussed alongside RAPTOR’s Table 5. (R2) Further analysis is needed on why methods that search down to leaf nodes (gathering more information) perform worse than GARLIC, which may terminate early. (R1 W4)
- **Resource-intensive graph construction.** While retrieval is efficient, graph construction is computationally expensive, especially for single-query documents, potentially outweighing benefits. (R2 W2) Details of the iterative batching mechanism for generating hierarchical summaries need elaboration: batch size criteria, token counting (whether prompt tokens are included), adaptation to document length, and impact on summary quality. (R3 W3) The application of KV caching in dynamic progress control requires explanation. (R1 W2)
- **Attention normalization and scalability concerns.** Normalizing attention weights across all layers may lose fine-grained information and limit scalability to larger graphs or complex attention patterns, especially in deeper networks or with very long documents. (R2 W1) The ability to handle queries spanning multiple disparate sections of a document may be limited; scaling behavior and potential thresholds need investigation. (R2)
- **Redundancy of embedding similarity.** Including embedding similarity alongside attention-based retrieval suggests the latter is not fully sufficient, yet the benefit is minor; this raises questions about its necessity and whether it adds unnecessary complexity. (R2 W3)
- **Granularity of Information Points (IPs).** The claim that IPs focus on “a single event or very few events” relies only on prompt instructions; no rigorous justification or analysis of generated IPs is provided, and potential limitations are not discussed. (R3 W4) One reviewer asked how the system handles differences in event granularity and whether shorter events lead to inconsistencies in information distribution across the graph. (R2)
- **No code released**, making it difficult to use or reproduce results. (R1 W3; R4 W3)
- **Additional questions that overlap with weaknesses** (also included as criticisms): (i) Has dynamically adjusting stop patience based on query complexity or node connectivity been considered? (R2) (ii) How does graph search efficiency vary in contexts with mixed single- and multi-query documents, especially in fragmented narratives like NarrativeQA? (R2) (iii) Given that Llama 3.1 alone already outperforms other baselines (Table 1), how would results compare if Llama 3.1 were used with RAPTOR instead, considering RAPTOR's higher efficiency (0.5:1 on 1 dataset, always below 1 on remaining)? (R2)
