## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper empirically studies experimental design in nonstationary optimization (continual learning/plasticity loss). Over 27,000 trials compare existing plasticity-preserving methods under a unified setting and examine hyperparameter selection protocols, number of seeds, train vs. test accuracy, and task sequence length. It provides recommendations for future experimental practice.

### Strengths
- Novelty: First explicit focus on experimental design in plasticity research; could speed up progress and unify the field.
- Timely, understudied, and important problem; empirically poses and answers several best-practice questions.
- Independent comparison of existing methods under unified setting aids identifying effective techniques and guiding future research.
- Hyperparameter selection protocol studies (protocol 2 vs. protocol 1, multiple task streams) are useful and could help reduce overfitting.
- Evidence that the community should focus on test plasticity rather than trainability alone.
- Study on the number of seeds needed for hyperparameter selection and method evaluation is good; could save computation in future experiments.
- Provides evidence that maintaining trainability does not correlate with generalizability in nonstationary settings.
- Shows large number of seeds are not necessary for finding good hyperparameters.
- Provides direction on resource-constrained experiments.

### Weaknesses
- **Writing and presentation quality**: Numerous typos, missing labels, and unclear wording lower confidence (Reviewers 1 and 4). Specific errors: duplicate words (“the added”, “be” in line 142); “boostrapping” throughout; line 283 “used for selection” likely meant “used for evaluation”; line 375 missing “need”; line 722 missing batch size for ResNet-18; typo in section 4.4 title; Figure 3 and Figure 2 lack labels/legends; Figure 4 lines are hard to differentiate. Reviewer 1 suggests using a grammar tool.
- **ResNet + permuted CIFAR experiments are not useful**: Permuting inputs destroys spatial information, making convolutional networks inappropriate; the community typically uses fully-connected networks on permuted tasks. Reviewer 1 recommends removing these experiments.
- **Figure 2 is unclear and hard to interpret**: It is not specified whether the plot shows the best hyperparameter configuration or the average across configurations, nor whether “best” refers to training or test accuracy. The many baselines and settings in a single graph make it difficult to infer conclusions; a table might be better (Reviewers 1, 2). The ranking may be sensitive to statistically insignificant performance differences (Reviewer 2 asks if reporting average test accuracy would change results).
- **Claim on trainability–generalizability correlation (Section 4.3, lines 370–371) is overstated**: Figure 4a shows a clear positive correlation between training and test accuracy; the weak correlation in Figure 4b is only for the best hyperparameters, indicating that after a point trainability does not improve generalizability—not that there is no correlation. Reviewer 1 requests rewording. Reviewer 2 adds that this conclusion may depend on the plasticity method, as the aggregated analysis hides method-specific correlations. Reviewer 3 asks to quantify the gap between train and test accuracy rankings, as the performance difference might be small.
- **Protocol comparison (Section 4.1) is inconclusive**: Figure 3 lines are unlabeled; the difference between protocol 1 and protocol 2 appears small except on shuffled CIFAR-10. The conclusion that “protocol 2 transfers better to unseen data” is not well-supported (Reviewer 2). The seed counts between protocols are unequal—protocol 1 uses 10 seeds for selection and 10 for evaluation, but protocol 2 uses only 5 seeds in total—needs clarification (Reviewer 2).
- **Bootstrap explanation unclear (Reviewer 2)**: What exactly is being resampled? “Resampling the seeds” is not standard bootstrapping.
- **Section 4.2 lacks a clear takeaway (Reviewer 2)**: It would be helpful to investigate why some methods perform well—e.g., are they more robust to hyperparameters? Appendix C does not answer this.
- **Number of seeds needed (Section 4.4)**: The required number of seeds likely depends more strongly on the training method than on aggregate analysis (Reviewer 2).
- **Protocol 3 is considered critical for lifelong learning, but no comparison to other protocols is provided to prove that claim (Reviewer 4)**.
- **Study setting is unrealistic for continual learning (especially Reviewer 3)**: Distribution shifts used (pixel permutation, label permutation, noisy labels) are simple and unrealistic; pixel permutation never occurs in practice, and using CNNs on permuted images is questionable (also Reviewer 1). More realistic shifts exist (e.g., Dohre et al., 2024). In a true continual learning setting there is no separate train/test set (continuous stream; cumulative online loss is the measure), random seeds are not used (especially in long streams), and hyperparameter optimization should be continual (e.g., IDBD, Hypergradient Descent). Reviewer 3 argues that such limitations are acceptable for algorithm introductions but insufficient for an empirical study aiming to provide guidelines. The scale of experiments and models is relatively small for a full empirical study.
- **Online baseline outperforms some LoP algorithms in Fig. 9 (Reviewer 3)**: Could this be due to insufficient hyperparameter tuning?
- **How would CReLU perform if it used the same number of weights? (Reviewer 3)**
- **Ambiguity in “How many tasks do you need to include in your training sequences?” (Section 4.5)**: In lifelong learning the answer is infinity; the authors likely mean for hyperparameter tuning (Reviewer 1).
- **Plot training loss vs. test loss in Figure 4 (Reviewer 1)**: Could reveal more correlation, especially for ResNet which reaches near 100% train accuracy.
- **Figure 5d legend (Reviewer 1)**: Does it mean p(Method ranking does not change)?
