## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers are mixed: one reviewer (Rating 8) strongly endorses the work as simple, effective, and well-structured; three reviewers (Ratings 5) find the contribution overstated, the lifelong learning framing unconvincing, and the experimental evidence insufficient. All agree the topic (task-unaware lifelong robot learning) is important, but raise concerns about concept–evaluation mismatch, statistical reliability, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- The key idea—retrieval-based adaptation with selective weighting to avoid explicit task IDs—has merit and shows potential (Reviewers 1, 2, 4). One reviewer (R4) highlights its simplicity and effectiveness.
- Related work compilation is interesting (R1). The discussion covers relevant memory-based and lifelong learning literature (R2, R4).
- Figures are well-illustrated: Figure 3 aids exposition (R1, R2, R4); PCA visualization based on SS Model (Figure 4b) is interesting (R1).
- Comprehensive experimental evaluation across LIBERO benchmark variants with detailed ablation studies (R2, R4). Results and benchmark comparisons are well-structured, clearly supporting the questions in Section 5 (R4).
- Good documentation of implementation details and appendices (R2).
- Dual use of episodic memory for training (experience replay) and testing (local adaptation) is a novel combination (R2).
- The problem is well-motivated and addresses a relevant challenge in robotics (R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Overstated contributions / conceptual mismatch**: The paper claims “task-unaware” lifelong learning, but the approach still relies on task-based retrieval (matching current scenarios to stored demonstrations) and evaluation is conducted on clearly separated benchmark tasks with language descriptions, which effectively provide task specification (R1, R2, R4). “Blurred task boundaries” is misleading: the paper uses language-described tasks rather than temporal boundaries between task executions (R4). The lifelong learning setting is not fully addressed: evaluating over only 20 episodes is insufficient (R1); the setup does not match typical lifelong learning definitions (R1, R2).
- **Statistical concerns**: Table 1 results show large uncertainties (e.g., 45.17 ± 31.86 vs 34.08 ± 28.55) that do not appear statistically significant (R3). Seeds are 1, 21, 42—a suspiciously arbitrary selection, raising concerns about cherry-picking (R3). Reviewer 3 requests additional experiments to tighten standard deviations and proper statistical analysis.
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**: No comparison to meta-learning approaches (e.g., [2] from R1’s list) despite conceptual similarity (R1). Related work omits older key references (R1 lists [1-6]). Missing comparison with a prior lifelong robot learning from demonstration work [1]—either conceptually or empirically (R4).
- **Experimental clarity**: Description of experiments and results is vague and hard to follow; e.g., what specific tasks, how demonstrations are generated, whether each test task has a corresponding relevant demonstration (R1). It is unclear from Section 4.1 whether retrieval is at timestep- or demonstration-level; a clearer pseudocode of the entire pipeline would help (R4). No justification for why the evaluation setup suits lifelong learning (R1).
- **No real-world validation**: All results are from simulation; no real-world experiments or failure mode analysis (R2).
- **Technical gaps**: Limited discussion of hyperparameter impact (R2) and how hyperparameters (especially language/image distance weights α_v and α_l) are tuned (R4). Memory requirements could scale poorly with task number (R2). No discussion of potential catastrophic forgetting during local adaptation (R2). Manual thresholds in selective weighting are not justified; sensitivity analysis not performed (R2).
- **Privacy contradiction**: The abstract claims storing past data raises privacy concerns, yet the proposed method also stores demonstrations in memory M, facing the same issue (R4).
- **Overstated contributions**: The paper’s claims are significantly overstated relative to the actual scope (R1).
