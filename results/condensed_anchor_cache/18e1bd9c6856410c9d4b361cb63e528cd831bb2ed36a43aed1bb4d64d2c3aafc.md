## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper asks whether a single-modality model can synergize training of another modality model without paired multimodal supervision. The authors propose an additional latent loss to align representations of the target modality with outputs from a pre-trained encoder of a supportive modality, provide a theoretical framework showing that even imperfectly paired data can help approximate a better representation, and empirically demonstrate improvements across vision, language, and audio modalities (e.g., 1.5–2.5% on ImageNet classification). Reviewer scores were 6, 8, and 5. One reviewer praised the novel insights and rigorous theory (score 8, confidence 4), while another found the contribution less novel and the noisy‑label construction unrealistic (score 5, confidence 4); the third was moderately positive but raised concerns about overstating the field’s paired‑data requirement and about lacking discussion of related work (score 6, confidence 3).

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: obtaining paired multimodal data is often challenging.
- Provides a theoretical foundation, including bounds on the interpolation coefficient \(\alpha\) and a proof that an interpolated representation can outperform single‑modality representations even with imperfect cross‑modal supervision.
- Empirically validated across multiple modality pairs (Vision–Language, Vision–Audio, Language–Audio) and various architectures, with consistent improvements not only on classification but also on out‑of‑distribution generalization and robustness benchmarks.
- The mathematical framework is intuitive and easy to follow.
- The paper is well‑structured, with illustrations that clarify key messages.

### Weaknesses
- Overstates the requirement of “perfectly paired datasets” in prior multimodal learning. CLIP and similar methods are trained on noisy web‑scale paired data (e.g., image alt‑text), and training with noisy labels is widely established. The paper does not accurately represent the field’s capabilities.
- Does not cite or discuss related works that align multiple modalities without direct paired supervision, such as ImageBind (Girdhar et al., 2023) or 4M (Mizrahi et al., 2024). Those works directly address the limitations claimed in Section 2.3, which limits the novelty of this paper. A reviewer further noted that using an unpaired modality to improve performance has been explored in prior work (e.g., image→video→audio, text→image‑text, and missing‑modality handling [references 1–5 in the review]) and that this discussion is missing.
- The construction of noisy labels for the Language→Vision case (Table 5: “This is about (Class|Emotion) #.”) is arguably not noisy for the downstream classification task—it perfectly represents the target class. The small improvement seen when replacing this with a LLaVA caption (Table 4) may therefore be unsurprising. More realistic or varied noise functions (e.g., caption shuffling, different levels of misalignment) should be explored.
- For the Vision→Audio case in AVMNIST, the paper uses randomly shuffled images as \(\hat{z}_j\). The description does not clarify whether the random image is drawn from the entire dataset or from the same target class; this choice introduces a very different noise level than other settings. Similar exploration of noise levels across modalities is missing.
- Remark 2.1 (“\(\delta\) does not hinder the synergy”) may be too strong: the statement depends on how far \(\hat{P}_j\) is from \(P_j\) and how aligned \(P_i\) and \(P_j\) are. Counterexamples could be constructed with adversarial \(\delta\). The claim would benefit from empirical verification with varying degrees of noise.
- The choice of an L2 loss between feature spaces is not convincingly justified. Other fusion techniques (e.g., concatenation, addition) or auxiliary losses could be used, and the paper lacks theoretical or empirical justification for this specific design.
- The language prompts used (e.g., “This is about Class #”) are very basic; performance with more complex or varied language supervision should be investigated.
- Theoretical assumptions (e.g., Assumption 1: \(\Delta_{ij} \geq 0\)) could use more empirical validation or discussion of when they might break.
- All experiments use relatively small models and classification tasks. Scalability to larger, more complex, or generative models (e.g., multimodal LLMs, CLIP) is not demonstrated, leaving the generality of the approach unclear.
- The proposed latent alignment loss is similar to knowledge distillation. Prior work (e.g., Born Again Networks, Furlanello et al., 2018; Revisiting Knowledge Distillation via Label Smoothing Regularization, Yuan et al., 2020) suggests that such auxiliary losses can improve performance simply through regularization or label smoothing, independent of cross‑modal transfer. The paper does not discuss this alternative explanation or control for it.
