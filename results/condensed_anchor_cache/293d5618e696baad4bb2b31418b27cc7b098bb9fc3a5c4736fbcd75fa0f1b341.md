## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes PreSa (also called POHF), an offline safe preference-based RL framework that learns from pairwise reward preferences and binary safety labels without explicit reward or cost models. It combines contrastive preference learning (CPL) with human-aware loss (HALO) safety alignment via a Lagrangian objective, tested on 29 continuous control tasks from SafetyGym and BulletGym (DSRL dataset). All reviewers acknowledge the motivation and extensive experiments, but concerns dominate: perfect safety labels are unrealistic, the approach is largely an incremental combination of existing methods (CPL, HALOs, Lagrange), and the evaluation lacks comparisons with other offline RLHF baselines and more complex environments. One reviewer (R2, score 5) found the method well-motivated and the safety alignment intuitive, while the other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) rated it lower, citing limited novelty and strong assumptions.

### Strengths
- Strong motivation for avoiding explicit reward and cost models, reducing potential inaccuracies from model learning. (R2, R3, R4)
- Comprehensive empirical evaluation across 29 tasks in two environments (SafetyGym, BulletGym) with diverse baselines including both conventional offline RL and PbRL methods. (R1, R2, R3)
- Well-structured and clear presentation overall, with easy-to-follow implementation details. (R1, R2; note R3 found writing disjointed – see Weaknesses)
- The use of HALO-based safety alignment is intuitive, and constraining the feasible policy set is a principled way to integrate safety. (R2)
- The method’s ability to learn directly from human feedback without reward/cost engineering is a practical contribution. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Strong assumption of perfect binary safety labels:** The paper assumes perfectly accurate safety labels per trajectory segment, which is unrealistic in practice. No discussion or experiments address noisy, subjective, or context-dependent safety labels. (R1, R3, R4)  
  - Related: only hard constraints are considered; soft or discounted cumulative constraints (e.g., Eq.1) are not handled, and adapting PreSa to such settings is unexplored. (R4)
- **Limited novelty – incremental combination of existing methods:** The framework is a direct combination of contrastive preference learning (CPL), human-aware losses (HALOs), and a Lagrangian safe RL objective. The setting is a transfer of Safe RLHF from LLMs to continuous control. (R1, R3)  
  - Treating reward and safety separately via Lagrange adds complexity; a more unified preference (e.g., prioritizing safe high-reward trajectories) could simplify optimization. (R1)
- **Unfair comparison and missing baselines:**  
  - Comparing against offline RL methods with ground-truth rewards/costs is less relevant; the paper should compare to other offline RLHF baselines (e.g., training a cost model from binary safety labels, or using a unified safe-reward preference for CPL). (R1)  
  - The generation of cost preferences for the Safe-RLHF baseline is unclear, making the comparison unfair. (R1)  
  - Experiments are limited to SafetyGym and BulletGym; more complex environments (e.g., CommonRoad) are needed to demonstrate robustness. (R4)
- **Issues with labeling and threshold choices:**  
  - CPL in the original paper uses advantage (reward + policy entropy) for labeling, but PreSa uses cumulative rewards. Justification for this change is missing. (R2)  
  - Binary safety labels are generated using reshaped thresholds based on segment length; the influence of this approximation on safety performance is not discussed. (R2)  
  - The hyperparameter δ in Eq. (11) is set to 0.9 or 0.95; the criteria for this setting and its impact on performance are not explained. (R2)
- **Uncertainty and misspecifications in offline datasets:** The paper argues that explicit reward/cost learning suffers from inaccuracies, but the proposed method also faces uncertainty (distributional shift, biased preferences, misspecification). No evidence is provided that the claimed inaccuracies are significant, nor is the robustness of PreSa to such issues analyzed. (R2, R4)  
  - Also, the decision to consider reward preferences even in the unsafe subset may lead to unsafe yet reward-maximizing behaviors; rationale is needed. (R4)
- **Tuning and theoretical guarantees of Lagrangian optimization:**  
  - Tuning the Lagrange multipliers (learning rate, initial values) is challenging; guidelines are missing. (R4)  
  - Lagrangian methods do not guarantee safety at convergence, and strong duality (e.g., Slater’s condition) is not discussed. (R4)  
  - No theoretical analysis or safety guarantees are provided. (R3, R4)
- **Writing and presentation issues:**  
  - The paper’s logical flow is disjointed; the background section (5.1.1) should be integrated with the preliminaries (Section 4). (R3)  
  - Notation confusion: in Eq. (4), does \(P_{\pi_\theta}\) substitute \(\pi^*\) from Eq. (3)? This should be clarified. (R2)  
  - Citation and formatting errors (e.g., CPL citation, DSRL citation, Equation numbers, inconsistent spelling of “Lagrange”). (R3)
- **Limited scope discussion:** Applicability to discrete environments (e.g., discrete action spaces) is not addressed. (R3)  
  - Potential algorithmic improvements and further robustness studies are only weakly suggested. (R3)
