## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper uses sparse autoencoders (L1 penalty, ReLU encoder) to decompose language model internal activations into sparse, putatively monosemantic features, aiming to resolve superposition. The learned features are evaluated via automated interpretability scores, activation patching on the indirect object identification task (Wang et al., 2022), and case studies. Four reviewers rated the paper positively (6,6,6,5) and one reviewer rated it very negatively (1, with confidence 5). The majority find the approach promising and the results useful, with several sharing specific concerns about novelty, reconstruction loss, baselines, and missing details. The minority reviewer (reviewer 3) considers the approach overly complex, poorly evaluated, and lacking real baselines.

### Strengths

- Tackles an important problem–polysemanticity and superposition–with an intuitive and theoretically motivated approach. *R1,R2,R3,R4,R5*
- The method is scalable, unsupervised, and requires only unlabeled activations. *R2*
- The learned features are shown to be more interpretable than baselines (PCA, ICA, random projections) according to automated interpretability scores. *R1,R2,R5*
- Activation patching experiments demonstrate that the features localize counterfactual behaviour more precisely than prior decompositions, validating practical value beyond interpretability scores. *R1,R2*
- Case studies (e.g., Figure 4, Figure 5) show highly monosemantic individual features that influence model outputs in predictable ways; clear visual comparisons to standard residual stream features. *R2,R4*
- The paper is well written and easy to follow. *R1*
- The improvement in interpretability is noticeable and evaluation is diverse (automated scores, patching, case studies). *R4*

### Weaknesses

- **Novelty and baselines:** Using dictionary learning/sparse autoencoders for superposition is not particularly novel. *R1* Many components (sparse autoencoder, interpretability score) are pre-existing; the demonstration that sparse features yield greater interpretability has been shown before for neural networks. *R5* Limited comparison to other disentanglement techniques (e.g., beta-VAE, infoGAN, FactorVAE, Michaud 2023 spectral clustering). *R2* No real baseline comparison reported (reviewer 3). *R3* Claims of greater monosemanticity vs. other methods (abstract/conclusion) are not supported in the paper (e.g., Section 5.1). *R5* Previous work on sparse autoencoding for word embeddings (Subramanian et al., Zhang et al.) is not discussed. *R2*
- **Reconstruction loss and faithfulness:** Reconstruction loss is not fully minimized, indicating the dictionaries do not capture all information. *R2* High reconstruction loss and large increase in perplexity (Section 6.2) are problematic for faithfulness. *R4* The autoencoder will likely add noise to the results. *R3* Possible reasons for poor reconstruction: insufficient encoder expressivity, lack of a sparse linear basis, or no compact natural language description for features. *R5*
- **Missing experimental details:** Important hyperparameters (R, α) and model identity are not stated in main text for Table 1, Figures 2, 4, 5; R is only explained in Appendix C. *R4* Results lack discussion of how reconstruction loss varies with R, α, and layer. *R4* Most figures do not mention which R and α were used. *R4*
- **Limited evaluation scope:** Activation patching conducted on only one relatively synthetic dataset (indirect object identification). *R1* Method works less well for later layers and MLPs compared to early residual layers. *R2* Only the top hidden layer (token embedding space) is evaluated; lower layers should also be assessed. *R5* Study only looks at the most interpretable features, but safety guarantees would require making sense of all features. *R5*
- **Generalizability and scaling:** Need more analysis on generalizability across tasks and sparsity of dependencies between features. *R2* Scalability not demonstrated on very large models (billions of parameters). *R2* How well do features transfer across different models/architectures? *R2* How does approach scale with task complexity / number of possible features? *R1*
- **Encoder choice:** Linear encoder (ReLU) is suboptimal; should compare deeper encoders or optimization-based sparse coding mechanisms. *R5* Why is ReLU applied to default/random basis to make activations non-negative? The original model uses negative activations; justification needed. *R4* Is identity ReLU the default basis in Figure 2? *R4*
- **Auto-interpretation score limitations:** The score has not been shown to correlate with actual interpretability use cases. *R1* The method is bottlenecked by the auto-interpretation method? *R1*
- **Intended use unclear:** Are learned features meant to be used as an expanded network for explainable decisions, or as an approximation to explain the unmodified network? *R4*
- **Overall presentation and interpretation:** Experimentation is weak and results difficult to interpret (reviewer 3). *R3* The phrase "real-world features" (Sec 5.1) is unclear; for non-single-token concepts, representing them as sequences of words may not be feasible. *R5*
- **Missing discussion:** Need more main-text discussion of basic methods (reconstruction loss, content from Appendices B and C). *R4* Could incorporating model weights or adjacent layer features improve dictionary learning? *R2* What changes allow learning overcomplete MLP bases? *R2* How do features generalize to unseen tasks? *R2*
