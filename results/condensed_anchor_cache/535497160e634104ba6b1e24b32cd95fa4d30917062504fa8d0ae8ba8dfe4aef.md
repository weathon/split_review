## Merged Review

### Summary  
The paper proves that training a sum of 1‑neuron‑per‑layer (narrow) deep networks with the absolute‑value activation and \(\ell_1\) regularization can be reformulated as a convex Lasso problem. Features are expressed using geometric algebra and encode reflections across hyperplanes spanned by training data. The analysis reveals multilevel symmetries in the fitted functions that become more complex with depth. Numerical experiments on synthetic data and on embeddings from a pre‑trained LLM illustrate the claimed symmetries. The work is positioned as shedding light on how deep networks learn features, but it relies on several strong simplifications: a narrow architecture (effectively a sum of chains), the absolute‑value activation, and non‑standard \(\ell_1\) regularization. Two reviewers (rating 5) find the contributions novel and a nice addition to the literature; three reviewers (rating 3) raise major concerns about generalizability, clarity, and experimental evidence.

### Strengths  
- The reduction of a specific neural‑network training problem to a convex Lasso problem is clearly stated and could inspire alternative training approaches (R1, R3, R5).  
- The paper reveals geometric structures (reflection hyperplanes, multilevel symmetries) that are explicitly derived from the network’s form and activation, giving some interpretability to learned features (R1, R2, R4, R5).  
- Explicit construction of the Lasso dictionary matrices is provided, enabling direct application of convex‑optimization solvers (R5).  
- The results cover multi‑variate inputs, not just scalar ones (R2).  
- The search for symmetry in neural‑network learning is a natural and potentially fruitful direction; the approach is novel even if limited (R3).  
- Ideas such as the “sparsity factor” (R4) and the notion of “concept” as a reflection hyperplane (R4) add conceptual value.  
- The minority positive reviewers (R3, R5, both rating 5) consider the work a nice addition to the existing literature and highlight the interesting nature of the reflection discovery.

### Weaknesses  
- **Architecture too simplified to support general claims.** The “narrow” network is a sum of 1‑neuron‑per‑layer chains; most results are for 3‑layer narrow networks. This is far from standard deep networks, and the paper does not adequately justify why insights from this chain structure should carry over (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5). R4 says the claim of an “equivalence between neural networks and Lasso” is misleading given the specialization. R3 calls the network a “chain”.  
- **Activation‑function dependence.** The absolute‑value activation is symmetric, so the observed multilevel symmetries may be an artifact of composing \(|x|\) rather than a general property of depth (R1, R3, R5). R1 recommends showing similar behaviour for ReLU networks. R5 asks what the counterpart for ReLU (half of absolute) would be.  
- **Experiments are weak and poorly connected to theory.** Synthetic experiments use only hidden dimension 1, making symmetry a trivial consequence of composition; a quantitative metric for higher dimensions (e.g., 128) is missing (R1). The LLM‑embedding experiment bi‑classifies pre‑trained embeddings that are likely linearly separable, so even linear regression would suffice, providing no convincing evidence (R4). The paper does not clarify how the numerical results support the theoretical model beyond vague symmetry (R3). Axes in Figure 3 are unspecified (R4).  
- **Notation, clarity, and presentation are serious barriers.** Multiple equations are undefined or erroneous:  
  * Equation 11 appears not to multiply weights beyond the first layer (R1).  
  * Equation 12 uses \(n^{(l)}\) in an inconsistent way (“how can these be consistent?” – R4).  
  * Equation 13 uses \(\sigma\) without definition (R1).  
  * Theorem 4.1’s indexing: \(A_{i,j}=f_j(x_i)\) but the role of \(i\) is not specified in \(f_j(x)\) (R5).  
  * \(x'\) in Theorem 3.2 is never defined (R5).  
  * The abundance of indices in geometric‑algebra formulas makes them hard to digest (R1).  
  * Typos: “Interpretibility” (line 311), “orignal” (line 499), “github” uncapitalized (line 531) (R1).  
  * Presentation scores are low: 2, 1, 2, 2, 2.  
- **Abstract and framing overclaim.** The abstract does not mention that the network is narrow, that \(\ell_1\) regularization is used, or that most results hold only for 3‑layer networks (R2).  
- **Key question about deep vs shallow remains unanswered.** The paper asks whether deep and shallow networks learn fundamentally different functions, but the answer (deep favors symmetric structures) seems to follow from composing \(|x|\), not from depth per se (R1). Extension to ReLU is only sketched for 2‑layer networks with skip connections (R1).  
- **Claimed interpretability is not substantiated.** The explicit features in Theorem 4.1 require knowing which subset of \(2d-1\) points is involved; the paper does not explain how to identify that subset before training (R2).  
- **Unclear improvement over prior work.** The relationship to (Pilanci 2023b) is not discussed; it is not clear what additional capacity or insight the new formulation provides (R4). Prior literature on feature extraction (Large et al. 2024) and universal approximation (Hornik 1989) is overlooked in the motivation (R1).  
- **Questions from reviewers that overlap with missing experiments or comparisons:**  
  * “Do results extend to ReLU/GeLU, transformers, or standard architectures?” – No evidence or discussion is provided (R1, R3, R5).  
  * “Does Theorem 3.1 apply only to narrow abs‑value networks?” – Scope is not clarified (R2).  
  * “What is the benefit of the symmetry results for the ML field?” – Not convincingly argued (R1).  
  * “Why is geometric algebra necessary?” – Not justified (R1).  
  * “Is there any additional capacity in a 3‑layer narrow network over a 2‑layer standard one?” – Not addressed (R4).  
- **Techniques appear highly specialized and unlikely to generalize** to architectures closer to practice (R5).
