## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a joint training framework for privileged teacher-student imitation learning to address observation-space asymmetry. The teacher is trained with a reward penalty that approximates the action difference between teacher and student (via a proxy student network that shares the teacher’s privileged observations), and a supervised alignment step is added. The method is evaluated on maze navigation, vision-based quadrotor obstacle avoidance, and vision-based drawer opening with a robotic arm.

### Strengths

- Simple and sound approach: penalizing the teacher for behaviors that cannot be reproduced from the student’s observation space is a straightforward idea that directly addresses the teacher-student asymmetry problem (R1, R4). The method is well-motivated and grounded in the theoretical performance bound of imitation learning (R2).
- The emergence of perception-aware behaviors is impressive and clearly demonstrated across multiple environments, including quadrotor flight and manipulation (R4).
- The method is evaluated on diverse simulated tasks, covering both discrete and continuous control, and shows improvements over baseline imitation learning approaches (BC, DAgger) (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer noted that teacher policies trained with the constraint obtain higher returns than unconstrained teachers, possibly because learning to act with less input leads to more robust and generalizable behavior (R1).
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, with a clear problem statement and detailed method description (R2). The motivation of the problem is presented intuitively (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Missing comparisons to prior methods that handle asymmetry:** The paper incorrectly positions itself as the only work addressing information asymmetry in teacher-student frameworks. Several prior works (e.g., ADVISOR, COSIL, [1-8]) have tackled this problem under similar assumptions, and many train the student with RL to achieve behaviors distinct from the teacher. The paper only compares to BC and DAgger, which are not designed for asymmetry and are bound to fail. The authors must include a thorough discussion of this class of methods and compare to at least some of them (R1, R3, R4). R3 explicitly states that the paper "does not establish novelty" without these comparisons.
- **Insufficient experimental evaluation:** The experimental section is considered sparse and lacking in rigor. Specific concerns include:
    - Too few standardized benchmarks; more environments from prior work (e.g., [1,2]) are needed (R1).
    - Baseline training details are missing, even for well-known methods like BC and DAgger (R1).
    - Real-world robot results would round out the evaluation, though not a strict requirement for ICLR (R1).
    - The toy maze navigation experiment is given too much space and could be moved to the appendix (R1).
- **Lack of ablation studies:** It is unclear how each component contributes. Ablations are needed to isolate the effect of:
    - The reward penalty vs. the KL-divergence supervision term (R2, R4).
    - The shared action decoder (R4).
    - The weight of the KL-divergence penalty term (R2, R4 question this sensitivity; R3 notes multiple hyperparameters with no implications described). R2 specifically asks for experiments with only the reward penalty, only the KL supervision, and neither.
- **Clarity and missing methodological details:**
    - The alignment phase (Sec 4.2) needs a clearer description, including what subset of experiences is sufficient for training the proxy student and how divergence between student and proxy is tolerated. A pseudocode would help (R3).
    - The proxy student network definition and its update rule are unclear (R2).
    - The justification for equal covariance matrices in equations (9) and (10) needs more thorough explanation (R2).
    - The paper does not specify the ratio of privileged to high-dimensional observation samples, nor the number of updates per phase for each component (R4).
    - The baseline “w/o align (ours)” is not clearly explained, and it is puzzling why its behavior differs from BC and DAgger, especially in the quadrotor environment (R3, R4).
- **Need to train teacher from scratch:** The method requires training a teacher policy from scratch using RL with many samples (e.g., 10^8 for maze, 100M for manipulation). In contrast, other methods (e.g., ADVISOR, COSIL) assume a pre-existing teacher, which is often easier to obtain in robotics (e.g., via scripting or pre-existing controllers). This practicality concern is not discussed (R1).
- **Limited theoretical analysis:** The paper provides no analysis of convergence rates or sample complexity, nor a theoretical comparison with methods using adversarial training or domain adaptation (R2).
- **Rushed presentation of results:** One reviewer (R1) noted that the submission feels rushed, with important results (comparisons and ablations) only appearing during the rebuttal period, which limits the confidence in those results given the short time frame.
