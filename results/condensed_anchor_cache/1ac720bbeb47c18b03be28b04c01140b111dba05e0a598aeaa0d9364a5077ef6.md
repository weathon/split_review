## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Boltzmann Alignment, a novel thermodynamics-inspired approach that connects pre-trained inverse folding models (e.g., ProteinMPNN) to ΔΔG prediction by introducing a physical inductive bias via the Boltzmann distribution and a thermodynamic cycle that explicitly accounts for unbound states. It achieves state-of-the-art performance on SKEMPI v2 (Spearman 0.3201 unsupervised, 0.5134 supervised, significantly surpassing previous bests of 0.2632 and 0.4324) and demonstrates applicability to binding energy prediction, protein–protein docking, and antibody optimization. While reviewers praise the creativity and results, several note strong assumptions (e.g., treating unbound states as independent chain probabilities, coarse backbone-only representation, no dynamics), limited validation on a single dataset, lack of ablation studies, and missing discussions on uncertainty, force-field methods, and mutation-type complexity. One reviewer (R3) identified no significant weaknesses but raised a question about mutation-type performance; the other three reviewers provided multiple concrete concerns.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow for both ML and biology audiences; limitations are appropriately discussed.
- Creative integration of the Boltzmann distribution and thermodynamic cycle to link binding energy (ΔΔG) with the log-likelihood from inverse folding models, introducing a physically motivated alignment.
- Achieves state-of-the-art results on SKEMPI v2—Spearman 0.3201 (unsupervised) and 0.5134 (supervised)—significantly outperforming previous methods (0.2632 and 0.4324) and also surpassing SFT and DPO baselines.
- Demonstrates broad applicability: binding energy prediction, protein–protein docking, and antibody optimization.
- Ablations and controls (e.g., using predicted rather than crystal structures) strengthen confidence in the method’s robustness.

### Weaknesses
- Code not released; open-sourcing upon publication is expected.
- Data splitting (3-fold by structure) may not fully prevent leakage because different complexes can share similar binding interfaces; the partition process needs clearer explanation.
- Method is structure-based: requires either an experimentally resolved or a confidently predicted complex structure.
- Coarse representation: only backbone atoms are used; side chains and solvent are omitted, though they are important for protein–protein interactions.
- No explicit modeling of dynamics; static structures may fail for mutations that induce large conformational changes.
- Performance is notably better on point mutations and declines for multi-point mutations. Reviewers request: (i) separate models for single vs. multiple mutations should be considered; (ii) insights on which mutation types or interaction classes the method handles well or poorly are missing.
- Strong assumptions in the unbound state approximation: treating each chain independently (Eq. 9) ignores weak, transient inter-chain interactions that can contribute to free energy. Moreover, the probabilities come from an inverse folding model (not ground truth), introducing biases.
- Validation is limited to the SKEMPI v2 dataset; generalizability to diverse protein complexes and binding scenarios remains unclear.
- No detailed ablation study to quantify the contribution of each component (Boltzmann alignment, thermodynamic cycle integration). It is unclear which module drives the improvement and how improvements in the inverse‑folding model would translate to gains. One reviewer questions whether the sophisticated design provides additional information gain beyond the correlation observed.
- Antibody optimization evaluation uses only point mutations from the Shen et al. (2022) dataset, ignoring double/triple mutants and multiple optimization rounds; a more extensive evaluation is needed to boost trust in a typical design scenario.
- The preference probability metric used in antibody optimization is abstract; a ranking metric would be more interpretable and directly related to how many designs need screening.
- Uncertainty estimation is not discussed; it is important for practical design scenarios (e.g., prioritizing mutations).
- Terminology in Appendix B (“multi-point” vs. “all-point” mutations) is unclear; “all-point” appears to be the union of single- and multi-point mutations but this needs clarification.
- Missing comparison or discussion with non‑ML force-field methods (MM‑GBSA/PBSA, FEP, TI) that also use thermodynamic cycles.
- Low MAE values in Table 2 require explanation (noted by one reviewer).
- Not all reviewers saw major weaknesses (R3 rated weakness as minimal), but the majority of reviewers raised the above concerns.
