## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper tackles two barriers to practical linear attention: the O(ND²) memory footprint (reduced to O(ND) by reordering matrix multiplications in forward and backward passes) and the inapplicability of standard dropout (addressed with an alternative regularization mechanism). Experiments confirm linear scaling in wall-clock time and memory, and the dropout alternative is validated on LRA and Tiny Stories. However, all four reviewers agree that the experimental evidence is far too limited, the technical novelty is modest, and many practical concerns remain unanswered.

### Strengths
- Well-written and easy to follow (Reviewer 1).
- Topic is important and timely; making linear attention efficient is of high interest to the community (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- Reducing memory cost is crucial for scaling attention-based models (Reviewer 2).
- Theoretical derivations for both forward and backward passes are detailed and clean (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Motivation is clear (Reviewer 3).
- The proposed dropout alternative is a meaningful contribution, and its effectiveness is verified on LRA and Tiny Stories datasets (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty:** The matrix multiplication factorization is a standard operation in existing linear attention methods (Reviewers 3, 4). The dropout alternative is novel but validated only at small scale (Reviewer 3, 4).
- **Insufficient benchmarks and evaluations:**
  - Only one small model (Pythia‑14M) is used for LLM experiments, and only a training loss curve is shown—no perplexity, accuracy, or standard LLM benchmarks (MMLU, GLUE) (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4).
  - Long-context LLM benchmarks are missing (PG‑19, LongBench, Booksum) (Reviewer 2).
  - No vision model experiments, despite the method’s potential for image/video (Reviewer 3).
  - No comparison to RNN‑like efficient alternatives (Mamba, RetNet) (Reviewer 3).
  - LRA results are far lower than those reported in the S4 paper, and the hyperparameters are not described (Reviewer 4).
  - The LLM experiment lacks justification for the model/dataset choice; the “linear (ours)” curve was not trained for as many steps as the baseline (Reviewer 4).
- **Scaling experiments are too narrow:** Only one head configuration (H=16, D=32) is tested for varying token length; this setting is not common in popular models (e.g., ViT‑Base uses H=12, D=64). Additional head/dimension combinations should be shown (Reviewer 4).
- **Ablation study placement:** The dropout ablation results (LRA accuracy) are relegated to a separate section and not included in the main results table; it is unclear why they are not part of the primary evaluation (Reviewer 4).
- **Performance equivalence not guaranteed:** While the computation is algebraically equivalent, numerical stability or gradient flow may differ (citing RepVGG); the paper should verify that the modified forward/backward yields the same performance as original linear attention (Reviewer 4).
- **Practical usage unclear:** Can the method start from a pre‑trained LLM and directly convert it to efficient linear attention? Or does it require training/fine‑tuning from scratch? (Reviewer 1). Hardware efficiency (e.g., throughput, utilisation) is not addressed (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation issues:**
  - No references are cited when categorising prior linear attention work (Sparsified/Localised vs. Kernel Separation) in the introduction (Reviewer 3).
  - Index subscripts (i, n, j) in equations (e.g., eq.1) are incorrectly bolded (Reviewer 3).
  - The FLOPs example in Sec. 3.1 should use general matrix notation instead of a 3×3 toy example (Reviewer 3).
  - Secs. 3 & 4 contain excessively detailed derivations that could be moved to an appendix (Reviewer 3).
  - Formula error in line 90: \(f(x) = \exp(q \cdot k / \sqrt(D))\) has no \(x\) on the right‑hand side (Reviewer 4).
  - Unfixed LaTeX bug in Appendix D.1 (line 860) (Reviewer 4).
  - The complexity and inference latency of the new dropout mechanism are not discussed (Reviewer 4, question).
  - Reviewer 4 questioned the FLOPs saving claimed in Sec. 3.1, noting that the benefit may depend on the dimension ordering (question).

**Reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (score 5, confidence 3) was more positive about the topic, derivations, and dropout alternative, but still raised substantial concerns about experiments and novelty. The other three reviewers (each score 3, confidence 4) found the contributions insufficient and the experimental support lacking. All reviewers converged on the need for stronger empirical validation and broader evaluation.
