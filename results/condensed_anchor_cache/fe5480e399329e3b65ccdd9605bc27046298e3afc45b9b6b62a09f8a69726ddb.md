## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Preference Proxy Evaluations (PPE), a benchmark for reward models that directly links to post-RLHF human preference performance. It evaluates reward models on large-scale human preference (16,038 labeled pairs) and verifiable correctness datasets, then compares 12 metrics against gold-standard RLHF outcomes from a crowdsourced human preference platform. Reviewers generally find the problem important and the empirical effort substantial, but raise significant concerns about experimental design (offline DPO vs online PPO, single base model choice), lack of explanation for counterintuitive results (low RewardBench correlation), computational cost under-discussion, and clarity issues. One reviewer (score 8) was very positive with no weaknesses; the other three (scores 5,6,6) identified multiple shortcomings.

### Strengths
- Addresses a highly relevant problem: reward model effectiveness should be measured by post-RLHF LLM performance, not just static preference prediction.
- Achieves 77% Pearson correlation with human preference ELO, demonstrating the utility of the proposed metrics.
- Collects and releases a large-scale human preference dataset (16,038 labeled pairwise comparisons, multilingual queries), providing a valuable community resource.
- Extensive experiments use real human preferences, grounding the benchmark in actual downstream outcomes.
- Well motivates why best-of-K scores, row-wise Pearson correlation, and accuracy are relevant for downstream RLHF success.
- Selection of models, prompts, and correctness metrics is well motivated.
- Provides more diverse and larger-scale datasets than prior benchmarks like RewardBench.
- Paper is detailed and easy to follow; explanations for each metric and evaluation method are convincing.
- Section 7 offers useful predictive guidance for researchers who lack resources to run full post-RLHF evaluations.

### Weaknesses
- Uses DPO (offline) as the RLHF algorithm instead of online PPO, which may not fully capture the dynamic interaction between reward model and policy during RLHF. This undermines the claim of being the first benchmark explicitly linked to post-RLHF performance.
- Only uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as the base model. Should explore a broader range of model scales (both smaller and larger) to assess whether correlations hold across capacities. Moreover, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct itself already underwent DPO RLHF training, which may confound results; using a purely SFT-trained model is recommended to isolate the effect of the new reward models.
- Numerous surprising results lack sufficient discussion: low correlation with RewardBench (Figure 4), low Pearson correlation for “chat hard,” and inverse correlation between RewardBench and human Elo. Intuitive explanations and hypotheses are requested.
- Findings are not explored in sufficient depth—e.g., why low quantile aggregation correlation correlates more strongly with downstream performance, and what implications this has for reward model design. Plead for more detailed analysis and variance/std information across subdatasets and best-of-K curves.
- Computational expense of RM evaluations in PPE (Best-of-K curves, pairwise accuracy) is underdiscussed. Request for runtime estimates for different K values and strategies to improve efficiency. Also ask about inference cost scaling with K, what feasible K is for current models, and whether predictive accuracy plateaus after a certain K.
- Potential overfitting of pairwise accuracy-like metrics; request for discussion and empirical evidence.
- Clarity and presentation issues: Section 4 feels out of place; confusion over multiple datasets (16,038 preferences in Sec4, 12,190 votes in Sec6, 50,000 votes in Sec6.1) and how they are used. Figure 2 caption is unclear (difference between left and right plots not explained). More figures/plots (e.g., converting Table 3 to a chart) would improve readability. The paper needs another copy-editing pass (e.g., “We use the reward models to RLHF Llama-1.1-8B”, “Mean across all metrics is well correlated across all metrics”). 
- Using already RLHF-tuned models as data source may introduce biases and implicitly reward similarity to existing RLHF strategies; authors should address this.
- The finding that “Reward Model Accuracy” is the best metric is not novel, although its validation against human preferences is useful. Overall, clear lessons for reward model design (e.g., larger vs smaller models) are still hard to draw.
- One reviewer (R4) found no obvious weaknesses, asserting the work fills a significant gap and the effort is thorough. However, the majority of reviewers identified the above important shortcomings.
