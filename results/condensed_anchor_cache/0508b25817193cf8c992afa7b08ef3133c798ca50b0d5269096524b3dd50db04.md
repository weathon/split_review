## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies adversarially robust learning for two-layer neural networks on multi-index models under $\ell_2$ perturbations and squared loss. It proves that the Bayes-optimal adversarial predictor depends only on the low-dimensional target subspace, and with a two-stage procedure (standard feature learning for the first layer, adversarial training for the second layer), the sample complexity for robustness is independent of ambient dimension. The results rely on assumptions like independence between relevant and irrelevant coordinates, subGaussian inputs, and ReLU/polynomial activations. The paper is well-motivated and combines prior feature-learning results with adversarial training analysis, but has limitations in scope (e.g., $\ell_2$ only, constant perturbation budget, specific activations) and some presentation issues.

### Strengths
1. **Novel problem and important insight.** The paper is the first to theoretically study adversarially robust feature learning for multi-index models, showing that robust learning does not require additional samples beyond standard learning in high dimensions (R1-R7). The optimal adversarial features are the ground-truth latent features, which is a valuable finding (R2).

2. **Comprehensive theoretical analysis.** The paper presents rigorous bounds for sample complexity and model complexity, combining tools from statistical learning (Rademacher complexity, covering numbers), geometric analysis of low-dimensional subspaces, and approximation theory (infinite-width neural networks) (R3). The proofs are generally careful and complete (R6).

3. **Well-structured and mostly accessible.** The paper clearly states the setup, assumptions, and results (R1, R6). Many reviewers found the exposition clear and easy to follow (R1, R3, R6).

4. **Numerical validation.** The experiments, though limited, provide support for the theoretical claims (R3, R4, R6). The figures show that standard training followed by adversarial last-layer tuning (SD+AD) can outperform full adversarial training (R2, R7).

5. **Connection to existing literature.** The paper builds on prior feature-learning results (e.g., Damian et al. 2022) and shows that oracles for the target subspace can be realized via gradient-based algorithms (R1, R5, R7).

### Weaknesses
1. **Clarity and completeness of definitions and statements.** Several reviewers found key definitions lacking precision:
   - DFL (Definition 2) does not explicitly state that weight vectors $w_i$ are constrained to lie on the unit sphere; without this, the definition is trivially satisfied by scaling. The connection to optimization algorithms is not explained (R1, R3).
   - Definition 3 (SFL) is not fully understandable, especially its last sentence (R3).
   - Theorem 1 is confusing: unclear whether the multi-index model is still assumed, the phrase “with equality when $f^* \in \mathcal{F}$” is misleading, and the representation of $h$ is ambiguous (R2). The expectation in the towering property should specify the random variables (R5).
   - The Landau notations ($\tilde{\mathcal{O}}, \tilde{\Omega}, \tilde{\Theta}$) are used without definition; it is unclear whether bounds are asymptotic or non-asymptotic, and whether constants depend on dimension (R3).
   - The range of parameters $\alpha, \beta$ in Definitions 2 and 3 is missing (R7).

2. **Limited scope of theoretical results and assumptions.** 
   - The paper considers only $\ell_2$-bounded perturbations; the proof techniques rely on spectral norm and do not extend to $\ell_\infty$ (common in practice). No discussion on challenges for $\ell_\infty$ is provided (R4, R7).
   - Results assume squared loss; the analysis does not cover classification losses like logistic loss (standard for adversarial robustness) (R4).
   - The adversarial perturbation budget $\epsilon$ is taken as $\mathcal{O}(1)$, whereas in practice $\epsilon$ scales with $\sqrt{d}$ (R7). The justification in lines 218–219 could be clearer (R3).
   - Only ReLU and polynomial activations are analyzed; extension to common activations like sigmoid or tanh is not discussed, and proofs heavily exploit properties like homogeneity or algebraic structure (R3, R7).
   - For pseudo-Lipschitz functions, the sample complexity has an exponential dependence on $k$ (the intrinsic dimension), which may be high even though it is dimension-independent (R5).
   - No characterization of robust test risk beyond sample complexity; prior works (e.g., Hassani & Javanmard 2024) provide asymptotic risk formulas that allow understanding the effect of $\epsilon$ and dimensions (R7).

3. **Assumption 1 (independence between $x_\parallel$ and $x_\perp$).** 
   - This is a strong and unrealistic assumption for natural data. It does not hold for low-rank data where $\tilde U x$ may not vary independently of $U x$ (R5). The paper claims results extend to non-isotropic Gaussian and symmetric distributions without rigorous demonstration (R6, R7). The connection between Assumption 1 and the lower bound on $d\mu/d\tau_k$ is unclear (R5).

4. **Theoretical novelty and reliance on prior work.** 
   - The core analysis for the second layer uses Rademacher complexity arguments that are similar to generalization bounds in existing feature learning works (e.g., Damian et al. 2022). The specific difficulties in combining finite-dimensional guarantees with feature learning are not emphasized, making the incremental technical contribution hard to assess (R1).
   - The proof of Lemma 16 contains typos (line 894: should be $A_i(a_i - a'_i)$) and undefined quantities ($k_i, k'_i$), making it hard to verify (R4).
   - No high-level proof ideas for Theorems 4–7 are provided, leaving the reader to decipher the appendix (R3).

5. **Presentation and organization.** 
   - The structure is confusing: Theorems 4 and 5 (SFL) and Theorems 6 and 7 (DFL) essentially repeat each other; they could be combined or presented more concisely (R4).
   - The full proof of Theorem 1 is placed in the main text, interrupting the flow; an outline would suffice (R3). The discussion in lines 405–423 could be shortened (R3).
   - The complexity results are cluttered with many parameters, reducing readability (R7).
   - Some minor typos: equation (4.1) uses $y$ instead of $y^{(i)}$ (R6); non-standard asymptotic notations are used inside other asymptotic notations (e.g., $\tilde{\Omega}(\cdot^{\mathcal{O}(k)})$ when $k$ is assumed $\mathcal{O}(1)$) (R7).

6. **Numerical experiments are insufficient.** 
   - The first row of Figure 1 uses ground-truth representations, which is not a realistic comparison; the second row is more meaningful but lacks discussion (R2).
   - Missing experimental details: step size, whether shaded bands denote variance (and how much), selection of the direction $u$ (R2, R7).
   - Weird behavior in Figure 1: the robust test risk oscillates for SD+AD and AD+AD while full AD is smooth and converges faster; yet the paper claims full AD is slower or worse. This requires explanation (R7).
   - No experiments compare polynomial vs ReLU activation (R7).
   - No ablation on $\epsilon$, dimensions, or number of samples (R7).
   - The experiments do not use the actual algorithms defined (Algorithms 2, 3) (R4).
   - The observation in the second row that continuing to train $W$ (full AD) improves performance despite the theoretical recommendation to fix $W$ seems to contradict the theory (R4).

7. **Overall gap to practice.** 
   - The assumptions (subGaussian inputs, independence, specific activations, $\ell_2$ perturbation, constant $\epsilon$) deviate significantly from practical adversarial robustness settings. The paper acknowledges some limitations but does not discuss how to bridge the gap (R7).
   - The paper does not provide actionable insights for practitioners beyond the high-level suggestion that only the last layer needs adversarial training (R2, R7).
   - Adversarial training of the first layer is not allowed in the analysis, yet in practice it is commonly used and the provided experiments suggest it can hurt performance; this is left as future work without explanation (R7).

*Note: There was disagreement among reviewers about the clarity and novelty of the paper: some found it very well-structured and a clear contribution (R6, R7), while others found it hard to follow and the novelty unclear (R1, R4).*
