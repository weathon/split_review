## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviewers describe QAvatar as a cross-domain RL framework that combines source and target Q functions via a weight decay function to handle distinct state/action spaces and avoid negative transfer. Reviewers generally find the paper well-written, the motivation clear, and the experiments extensive across locomotion, robot arm manipulation, and autonomous driving tasks. Most reviewers (three out of four) assign a score of 6, while one reviewer (score 5) is more critical of theoretical novelty and baseline comparisons. Across reviews, there is agreement that QAvatar shows improvement in data efficiency and robustness, but several weaknesses are noted regarding practical training cost, reliance on strong assumptions, and limited performance gains over learning from scratch.

### Strengths
- The inter-task mapping learned via a Bellman-like loss function is considered novel (R1).
- Both theoretical analysis and empirical experiments are provided, and the experimental conclusions align well with the theory (R1, R3).
- The cross-domain Bellman loss function provides performance guarantees (R2).
- The paper is well-written, with clear notation, helpful visualizations, and easy-to-follow exposition (R3, R4).
- The main idea and motivation are clear and practical (R3).
- QAvatar improves sample efficiency and avoids negative transfer; it is robust to low-quality source-domain policies and negative transfer scenarios (R3).
- The authors provide reproduction and sanity checks for baseline methods (R3).
- Extensive experiments are conducted on diverse tasks (locomotion, robotic manipulation, autonomous driving) with various state/action dimensions (R2, R3, R4).
- The framework shows potential for improving data efficiency in RL applications (R2).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical novelty limited**: The theoretical analysis heavily depends on previous work, and the key implication 2 of Theorem 1 (using a decay function to prevent negative transfer with large t) is not original—most transfer methods include a decay function (R1).
- **Missing related work and baseline comparisons**: Some related work (R1’s [1-3]) should be discussed. At least one method with a theoretical guarantee (e.g., R1’s [3,4]) should be compared to support empirical claims (R1). Additionally, comparison with other RL algorithms such as TD3 or PPO is requested (R4, question).
- **Limited practical improvement over learning from scratch**: In Fig 1, SAC learning from scratch often matches or outperforms QAvatar, and QAvatar’s advantage is slight (some errorbars overlap, so not statistically significant). Since QAvatar requires training in both source and target domains (doubling training time compared to SAC), the claim of improved data efficiency is questionable (R1, R4). In low-quality source scenarios (Fig 4), QAvatar provides no benefit over training directly in the target domain; more experiments with baselines like FT and CAT under these settings are needed (R4).
- **Assumptions limit applicability**: The method assumes access to \(Q_{src}\) and \(V_{src}\) in addition to \(\pi_{src}\). In many real-world scenarios only a pre-trained policy is available, which may break the framework (R3). The framework currently handles only one source and one target domain, and the inter-domain mapping must be trained from scratch for each pair (R3).
- **Performance variance across environments**: The margin between QAvatar and baselines varies dramatically (e.g., CAT outperforms QAvatar in table wiping in Robosuite, while QAvatar does better in block lifting and door opening). More explanation is needed for these inconsistent gains (R3). Similarly, baselines like CAT, DCC, and CMD fail to transfer in some locomotion tasks even though CMD used the same tasks originally; the reasons are not explained (R4).
- **Brief analysis for low-quality source**: The experimental section on lower-quality source policies is too brief; why does QAvatar perform so well on lower-quality sources? (R2).
- **Adversarial source not tested**: What happens if the source domain policy is adversarial (i.e., \(Q_{src}\) is “opposite” to \(Q_{tar}\))? The weighted sum mechanism might harm training (R3, question).
- **Need for additional negative transfer experiments**: Experiments on negative transfer should include other locomotion tasks such as Hopper, HalfCheetah, and Ant, and identify scenarios where QAvatar might fail (R4).

*(Note: One reviewer (R1) was distinctly less positive, particularly regarding theoretical contribution and the significance of the experimental results relative to learning from scratch.)*
