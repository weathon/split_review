## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces FedWD, an algorithm to compute the Wasserstein distance between two distributions stored on separate clients in a federated setting. It leverages the triangle inequality and geodesic interpolating measures: the server iteratively sends a measure ξ to each client, which returns a barycenter between ξ and its private distribution (e.g., ξ_μ). The server then combines the two received barycenters to update ξ. Theoretical convergence is established, and experiments on toy Gaussian data, federated coresets, geometric dataset distances, and boosting FL methods show that FedWD approximates the true Wasserstein distance with small error.

### Strengths
- The problem of computing Wasserstein distance in a federated manner is novel and well-motivated, with clear practical relevance for privacy-sensitive applications.
- The writing is clear and the concepts (especially Figure 1 on the geodesic fixed-point structure) are easy to follow.
- Theoretical guarantees are provided: convergence of the iterative scheme (Theorem 2), and a special case (Theorem 3) where the distance is obtained in a single communication round.
- The algorithm’s computational complexity and the approximation using a fixed support size (S) are discussed.
- Empirical validation on multiple applications (coreset classification, geometric dataset distances, boosting FL) demonstrates that FedWD yields transportation costs close to the exact Wasserstein distance.
- The use of McCann’s interpolating measures as transferable objects is a novel and key contribution.

### Weaknesses
- **Privacy concerns are not adequately addressed.** Multiple reviewers show that the server can reconstruct raw client data from the communicated measures.  
  – Reviewer 1: For uniform discrete measures with optimal transport map T, the server knows ξ and the returned ξ_μ (where ξ_μ = (1−t)ξ + tμ). By computing the optimal transport between ξ and ξ_μ, the server infers T and thus the raw data points of μ.  
  – Reviewer 2: In a Gaussian setting, after two communication rounds the server knows the means (m_ξ, m_{ξ_μ}) and (m_{ξ'}, m_{ξ'_μ}); the lines through these pairs intersect at the true mean m_μ, revealing the first moment of μ.  
  – The paper’s suggestion of randomizing t to hide distances does not prevent these reconstructions.

- **Theoretical gaps exist between the practical algorithm and the claimed guarantees.**  
  – The algorithm uses a fixed small support size S for ξ (e.g., S=10), but the convergence theory (Theorems 1 and 2) assumes the full measure. The consistency of this approximation is not justified.  
  – The proof of Theorem 2 (convergence) appears flawed: it states “by choosing t=0 and t=1 in the definition of the interpolating measure,” but (i) such t values are not allowed in the algorithm and (ii) there is no mechanism to drive t to 0 or 1. The argument seems insufficient for the claimed convergence.

- **Motivation and presentation of Theorem 1 are unclear.** It is less general than Theorem 2 and its specific use case (exact recovery under which conditions?) is not explicitly stated, making it hard to see when Equation 10 gives the same result as Equation 5.

- **Missing experiments.**  
  – No experiment with unbalanced dataset sizes (e.g., one dataset a constant multiple of the other) to test robustness.  
  – No comparison of convergence speed under Gaussian vs. non-Gaussian data distributions.

- **The setting is restricted to discrete measures.** The paper does not discuss how FedWD would work for continuous measures (where closed-form interpolating measures are not available unless a transport map is known). This limits applicability.

- **Gradient approximation is not considered.** Many applications require gradients of the Wasserstein distance with respect to measure parameters; FedWD’s ability to provide such approximations is not analyzed.

- **Minor issues:**  
  – The symbol d_{μ,ξ^{(k)}} in Algorithm 1 is used before being defined.  
  – The application of FedWD to the Wasserstein Coreset problem is not explained clearly (which agents hold which distributions and where FedWD enters).  
  – For ground costs that are not metrics (e.g., in domain adaptation), the triangle inequality fails; whether FedWD can be adapted is not discussed.  
  – The effect of the parameter t on performance is not studied.

- **Contrast among reviewers:** One reviewer (Score 8) found the paper stronger overall but raised the same core privacy and theoretical concerns, and also noted the lack of continuous extension and gradient discussion as limitations.
