## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a fine-grained taxonomy of hardness types (mislabel, OOD, atypical) and introduces H-CAT, a toolkit to benchmark 13 HCMs across 8 hardness types and 14K+ setups on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and two tabular datasets. Evaluation reveals that many HCMs disagree, learning dynamics methods are more effective, and the best HCM depends on the hardness type. Reviewers generally praise the taxonomy and toolkit but raise significant concerns about limited dataset scope (only MNIST/CIFAR-10 plus two tabular), artificial hardness simulation, the assumption that the hardness type is known a priori, missing baselines, restriction to image classification, insufficient technical rigor, and questionable relevance to modern deep learning (LLMs, etc.). One reviewer (score 1) is highly critical, questioning the fundamental usefulness and categorization; the other reviewers (scores 8, 8, 6, 8) find value but note important limitations.

### Strengths
- **Taxonomy**: The organization of hardness types (mislabel, OOD, atypical) is sound, clearly explicated, and formalizes observations from prior literature, relating to previous methods and frameworks (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5).  
- **Comprehensive benchmarking**: Evaluation of 13 HCMs across 8 hardness types and over 14K setups provides robust validation (Reviewers 1, 3, 4, 5). Experiments using the H-CAT toolkit carry significance (Reviewer 5).  
- **Toolkit design**: H-CAT offers a unified interface for evaluation and is easily extensible to new HCMs, datasets, and hardness types (Reviewers 1, 3, 5). Detailed instructions and a voluminous appendix are provided (Reviewer 5).  
- **Valuable takeaways**: Practical tips on HCM selection, highlighting efficacy of learning dynamics–based HCMs, lack of efficacy of computational-efficiency–motivated methods, importance of significance testing, and variation in stability across seeds, backbones, and parameterizations (Reviewers 3, 4, 5).  
- **Motivation and presentation of need**: Graphical illustrations (Figure 1) and table analysis (Table 1) effectively underscore the need for a systematic evaluation framework (Reviewer 5). The paper diligently considered recently proposed HCM algorithms (Reviewer 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited dataset scope**: Experiments are only on MNIST and CIFAR-10 (plus two tabular datasets). These are small, low-resolution datasets with few classes, limiting generalizability to larger, more complex datasets or higher-resolution images. Artificial mislabeling could have been applied to larger datasets like ImageNet (Reviewers 2, 4).  
- **Oversimplified hardness simulation**: The perturbations for Near OOD (Gaussian noise, texture changes), Far OOD (exchanging MNIST/CIFAR), and atypical (shifts, zooms) are unrealistic. Real-world OOD and atypical examples involve more nuanced phenomena (e.g., subtle lighting changes, adversarial examples, rare feature combinations). The taxonomy is manually defined on clean datasets rather than derived data-driven from real-world data (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  
- **Taxonomy completeness questioned**: It is unclear why mislabel is a separate hardness type and whether the three categories (mislabel, OOD, atypical) cover all possible types of hardness (Reviewer 2).  
- **Assumption that hardness type is known**: The evaluation framework requires knowing the type of hardness present in data, which is unrealistic in practice; real-world data often contains multiple simultaneous or unknown hardness types (Reviewers 1, 3). This undermines the practical recommendations.  
- **Missing baselines and evaluation paradox**: The paper criticizes indirect evaluation via downstream performance but then uses downstream classification (CIFAR-10 and MNIST accuracy) as the evaluation metric, making the solution unclear (Reviewers 1, 2). Baseline experiments are missing: training models without HCMs under the same corruption levels, and training only on easy examples after removing corrupted samples, which would assess whether HCMs are needed at all (Reviewer 1).  
- **Evolving hardness**: The paper does not address that sample hardness can change during training (e.g., hard at epoch 1 may become easy at epoch 10) (Reviewer 3).  
- **Limited modality and task scope**: The framework handles only image classification, not object detection, segmentation, NLP, RL, or LLMs. The relevance to current large-scale models (LLMs, autoregressive, self-supervision) is unclear; OOD and hardness characterization for LLMs/LMMs are not discussed (Reviewers 2, 5).  
- **Presentation and rigor issues**: The writing style is “flashy” (bold text, colored boxes, etc.), distracting and giving the impression of selling the toolkit rather than presenting solid conclusions (Reviewer 1). The paper reads like an engineering document, lacking sufficient scientific insight. Technical details and unified notation for HCMs (e.g., AUM) are often in the appendix but more explanation and equations are needed in the main text (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The taxonomy and toolkit should be introduced earlier for clarity (Reviewer 3).  
- **Reviewer disagreement**: One reviewer (score 1) is highly critical, arguing the paper lacks novelty, does not address modern ML, and that the categorization is unsatisfying. Another reviewer (score 8, Reviewer 3) post-rebuttal explicitly disagrees, stating that the community’s focus on LLMs does not make this work worthless and that the paper has significant potential. (Minority contrast preserved.)  

**Reviewer questions overlapping with criticisms (included as weaknesses):**  
- Whether HCMs are needed at all vs. simply cleaning datasets (Reviewer 1; overlaps with missing baselines).  
- Why categorize into mislabel/OOD/atypical and whether these cover all hardness (Reviewer 2; overlaps with taxonomy completeness).  
- Whether the use of downstream performance contradicts the criticism of indirect evaluation (Reviewers 1, 2; overlaps with evaluation paradox).  
- How perturbation severity affects HCM scores and whether augmentations change hardness predictions (Reviewer 4; overlaps with oversimplified perturbations and missing ablations).  
- Whether continuous weighting could be preferable to binary exclusion of hard samples; how the framework performs “direct” evaluation; how Spearman correlation was computed from multiple runs (Reviewer 5; overlaps with missing baselines, evaluation criticism, and technical detail request).
