## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes DeComFL, a federated learning (FL) algorithm that uses zeroth-order (ZO) optimization to reduce per-round communication from O(d) to O(1) by transmitting only scalar magnitudes and random seeds (from which perturbation vectors are recovered). Convergence guarantees are provided for non-convex functions, showing linear speedup in number of clients and local steps, and dimension-independent convergence under a low effective rank assumption. Experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and fine-tuning OPT-1.3B demonstrate dramatic communication savings (e.g., ~1 MB total to fine-tune a billion-parameter model). Reviewers agree the problem is important and the communication savings are substantial, but raise concerns about novelty (similar ideas in prior ZO FL works such as Fang et al., 2022 and references [1], [2]), the necessity of downgrading to ZO when first-order gradients are available, experimental comparisons (limited baselines, learning rate tuning, missing FedAvg baseline for OPT), validation of the effective rank assumption, and technical details (seed generation, algorithm notation). One reviewer (R1, score 5) is more critical, while another (R4, score 8) is more positive but still questions novelty; R3 (score 6) considers the contribution valuable and novel.

### Strengths
1. The problem of communication overhead in FL is important and timely, especially for large models (R2, R4).
2. DeComFL achieves drastic communication reduction: per-round cost O(1) regardless of dimension; empirically, only ~1 MB total is transmitted to fine-tune OPT-1.3B, saving orders of magnitude (e.g., ~1000× in experiments) compared to standard methods (R1, R2, R3, R4).
3. The convergence analysis is provided and appears reasonable; the discussion of the low effective rank assumption to improve the pessimistic d-dependent bound is interesting (R1, R4). The theoretical results are clean (R4).
4. The algorithm design is sound, and the modification to prior ZO methods (e.g., Fang et al., 2022) is clever (R1, R3).
5. Experiments cover both classic deep learning (MNIST, CIFAR) and large language model fine-tuning (OPT-1.3B), demonstrating practical applicability (R2, R3).
6. The paper is generally well-written and has a good flow (R1).
7. [Minor positive] One reviewer (R3) finds the use of ZO optimization for FL novel and a valuable contribution. (This contrasts with R1 and R4 who question novelty.)

### Weaknesses
1. **Novelty and relation to prior work (disagreement among reviewers).**  
   - R1 and R4 argue the core idea is not entirely new: R1 asks for a clearer comparison with FedZO (Fang et al., 2022) and notes it is unclear if DeComFL is merely an extension that adds the effective rank assumption. The related works discussion is insufficient (R1).  
   - R4 states that the same idea of using Assumption 4 (low effective rank) in FL appeared in prior work [1] (unnamed in review), with a similar gradient estimator (if μ→0, the estimators are equivalent). Additionally, the theoretical framework heavily replicates [2] adapted to the multi-client setting. The authors should more clearly differentiate their approach and articulate novel theoretical contributions (R4).  
   - In contrast, R3 considers the use of ZO for FL novel and valuable.

2. **Necessity of zeroth-order optimization.**  
   - R1 is not convinced that ZO is essential: downgrading to ZO when first-order gradients are accessible introduces approximation errors. R1 questions the claim (Line 63) that the decomposition into gradient scalar and perturbation vector is unique to ZO gradients—projecting a first-order gradient onto a random direction could also transmit only a scalar and seed. Justification is needed (R1).

3. **Experimental setup and comparisons (general concerns).**  
   a. R1: Table 2’s last column is misleading because it shows a larger P than FedZO (column 4); suggest removal.  
   b. R2: In MNIST experiments, the learning rate is set constant across algorithms; for fair comparison, it should be tuned separately for each method.  
   c. R2: Experiments on OPT-1.3B do not compare to federated averaging (FedAvg) fine-tuning; it is unclear how close DeComFL’s performance is to FedAvg.  
   d. R2: In Fig 3, FedAvg+Topk converges much faster per round; if k is reduced so that FedAvg+Topk’s per-round communication matches DeComFL’s, would ZO still yield savings? This comparison is missing.  
   e. R4: Using only FedAvg as a baseline is limiting; more modern methods (e.g., with variance reduction or adaptive learning rates, e.g., [3]) should be compared for a robust evaluation.

4. **Validation of the low effective rank assumption.**  
   - R1: No experimental validation of the effective rank assumption. Suggests comparing convergence rates for differently-sized LLMs; if rates are similar (in terms of communication cost), then d-dependence is pessimistic.  
   - R3: The assumption in Theorem 2 (κ-effective rank) is not standard. It is unclear whether κ can be considered O(1) and independent of d. What if κ scales with d (even if not Θ(d))? The authors should provide evidence for this assumption (R3).  
   - R1 additionally asks for theoretical clarification: what happens if κ scales with d but not as Θ(d)?

5. **Technical details and presentation.**  
   a. R2: The paper does not specify how random seeds are chosen; this affects the distribution of generated perturbation vectors. A formal statement about the generated sequence and seed generation process (deterministic vs. uniform) is needed.  
   b. R2: The variable m (number of clients) is not formally introduced; it is understood as |C_r|. Also, the role of constant P in the algorithm is unclear—on pages 4–5 P appears to be set to 1, causing confusion (R3).  
   c. R3: In Assumption 4, the second maximum may be over ξ_{i,r}? Possible typo.  
   d. R2 raised several technical questions that require clarification:  
      - What is the difference in analysis from prior ZO FL work (Fang et al., 2022)? Algorithmically similar; does the new communication scheme pose extra challenges?  
      - Why are all z_r^k equal on different nodes? Could allowing different perturbations per client (requiring more server memory) provide faster convergence?  
      - The claim on lines 52-53 that “communication becomes a bottleneck” may need rephrasing because computation can scale quadratically with dimension (see Ryabinin et al., 2021).  
      - The communication cost analysis might not be accurate?  
      - Provide intuition on server memory size vs. saving the full model.  
      - In Corollary 1, setting μ as described may not give the desired result because the term 2 μ^2 L^2 (d+3)^3 would still have d in the numerator (instead of √d)?  
      - In Assumption 4, which matrix norm is used?  
      - In Theorem 2, d_κ is not defined (presumably effective rank).  
      - Could local steps be analyzed in Theorem 2? What is the difficulty?  
      - The condition κ ≫ P could be replaced with κ > P.  
      - In Theorem 2, why does sufficiently large R allow removal of σ_G^2 + σ^2 term?
