## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Quantum Parameter Adaptation (QPA), a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm that uses quantum neural networks (QNNs) and a classical MLP to generate parameters for parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) of LLMs. QPA decouples quantum hardware from inference by using quantum circuits only during training. Experiments on GPT-2 and Gemma-2 with PEFT methods like LoRA show parameter reductions (e.g., 52.06% of original LoRA parameters for GPT-2, 16.84% for Gemma-2) while maintaining or slightly improving text generation performance.

### Strengths
- **Novel hybrid paradigm.** QPA innovatively applies quantum circuits to generate classical model parameters, shifting the QML focus from using quantum devices as learning models to parameter generation for classical models. This smartly sidesteps known QML issues like expensive quantum inference.
- **Effective parameter reduction on real LLMs.** QPA demonstrates significant parameter reduction on modern LLMs (GPT-2, Gemma-2) while maintaining or slightly improving performance (e.g., 0.75% gain for GPT-2, 0.07% for Gemma-2) compared to classical PEFT baselines like LoRA, DoRA, and Prefix Tuning. This brings quantum parameter generation closer to practical classical ML.
- **Practical deployment via classical inference.** Because quantum hardware is used only during training, inference can be performed entirely on classical systems, greatly reducing deployment complexity and enhancing real-world applicability.
- **Flexible and extensible framework.** QPA allows exploration of different configurations (block size, QNN repetitions, PEFT methods) and is shown to work with multiple PEFT approaches beyond LoRA, expanding the scope of prior quantum parameter generation work.

### Weaknesses
- **Computational overhead and noise ignored.** QPA assumes exact measurement probabilities and ignores the cost of full quantum state tomography, which scales exponentially with qubits and likely exceeds classical simulation. The paper does not present experiments with finite measurement shots or realistic noise models. Optimization of QNN parameters is prone to barren plateaus, and the gradient extraction from quantum measurements introduces additional complexity. All reviewers consider these issues critical for assessing real-world feasibility.
- **Parameter reduction claim is theoretically questionable.** To represent an arbitrary state of dimension \(r(d+k)\) (low-rank matrix size), the quantum circuit itself may need \(\mathcal{O}(r(d+k))\) parameters—potentially negating the parameter advantage claimed over classical methods. This scaling argument has not been rigorously addressed.
- **Ansatz choice (RY+CNOT) lacks justification.** No experimental or theoretical basis is given for preferring RY and CNOT gates over alternatives (RX, RZ, more expressive circuits). The chosen ansatz restricts exploration to the real-valued subset of Hilbert space; more expressive circuits could improve performance but are untested.
- **No theoretical performance guarantees.** The paper defers analysis of convergence, trainability, and learnability to future work. Without such guarantees or strong experimental results (all results are simulations), the method’s potential remains unclear. Using tensor network theory to explain why QPA outperforms classical PEFT (e.g., LoRA, DoRA) is suggested as a way to strengthen theoretical foundations.
- **Misleading claims about fault-tolerant quantum computing.** The statement that IBM has demonstrated 12 logical qubits with 288 physical qubits is incorrect; they have only discovered a code. Moreover, implementing the proposed PQC in a fault-tolerant manner would be extremely expensive due to required small-angle Y rotations—a challenge not addressed.
- **Synthetic data to avoid finite shots is not well justified.** The proposal to use generative models to produce measurement outcomes is itself costly; alternative approaches (e.g., quantum shadow tomography) are not explored.
- **Time cost and fair resource comparison missing.** There is no discussion of wall-clock time, quantum circuit simulation overhead, or measurement delays relative to classical PEFT methods. A fair comparison would require keeping computational complexity constant and quantifying the overall time/resource trade-off.
- **Scalability to larger models and deeper circuits is unclear.** The number of qubits required for billion-parameter models (e.g., GPT-3) is not discussed, nor is the effect of deeper QNN circuits on trainability and measurement overhead.
- **Hyperparameter choice is ad hoc.** No general method is provided for selecting the optimal block size \(n_{\text{mlp}}\) or number of QNN repetitions \(L\) for different PEFT methods or model sizes.
- **Quantum component may be replaceable.** Several reviewers question whether the QNN is necessary—a classical generative model or tensor networks might achieve similar results without QML difficulties (barren plateaus, exponential tomography). The paper itself (Appendix B) acknowledges a classical alternative.
- **Overstated introductory claims.** Statements implying that quantum computing excels in optimization or that QML can enhance training of large models are not supported and should be tempered.
