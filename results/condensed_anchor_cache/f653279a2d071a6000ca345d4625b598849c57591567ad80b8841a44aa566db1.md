## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proves a lower bound on the memorization capacity of a single multi-head attention (MHA) layer, using milder input-data assumptions (linear independence of context vectors and a Kruskal-rank condition on merged representations) that relax the General Position assumption. The main result states that an MHA layer with \(H\) heads, dimension \(d\), and context size \(n<d\) (parametrized by \(\Theta(H d^2)\)) can memorize \(\Omega(H n)\) examples. The analysis shows that memorization increases linearly with the number of heads \(H\), monotonically with context size \(n\), and monotonically with head dimension \(d_h\) up to \(d_h < n\) (no gain beyond). The authors validate the data assumptions empirically (linear independence and approximate Kruskal Rank) on real data and confirm the theoretical findings with synthetic experiments.

### Strengths
- **Important theoretical result**: The paper provides a lower bound on the memorization capacity of MHA layers, addressing a gap in theoretical understanding of transformer architectures.
- **Milder and practical assumptions**: The two input-data assumptions are more relaxed than the General Position assumption used in prior work. The authors demonstrate their reasonableness through sampling tests on real data (e.g., image patches and text), which increases confidence in the assumptions’ practical validity.
- **Enlightening conclusion on head dimension**: The result that increasing \(d_h\) only helps up to \(d_h < n\) and provides no memorization gain beyond that is insightful and can guide hyperparameter choices (e.g., number of heads, dimensions) in real-world models.
- **Well-organized and clearly written**: The paper is logically structured, the argument is easy to follow, and figures are of high quality. The proof steps appear sound (though not checked in full detail by all reviewers).
- **Empirical validation**: Both the data assumptions and the theoretical lower bound are experimentally verified with synthetic data, and the assumption rationality is tested on real data.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited empirical testing**: The experiments use only synthetic data; real-world benchmark evaluations (e.g., vision or NLP tasks) are missing. While synthetic tests isolate effects, the practical implications of the theoretical memorization capacity remain unclear on complex datasets. A minority reviewer (R1, score 6) emphasizes that benchmark experiments would strengthen practical relevance.
- **Lack of clarity on “memorization” definition**: It is not clearly defined what the attention mechanism is memorizing—whether it is perfect reconstruction of training outputs, learning a generalizable mapping, or something else. The paper would benefit from a more precise definition or even a visualization of what is being memorized (e.g., attention weights or output vectors). This overlaps with a question about the relation between memorization and generalization/overfitting.
- **Insufficient comparison with existing work**: The paper lacks detailed analysis and comparison with related works, such as “Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Are Key-Value Memories” and connections to Hopfield networks. How does attention-layer memorization differ from feed-forward-layer memorization? Do observations from interpretability or model editing support the findings? A deeper discussion of these connections would significantly strengthen the contribution.
- **Single-layer focus without multi-layer insights**: The analysis is restricted to a single MHA layer. The paper does not address how memorization capacity scales with depth or what obstacles prevent extending the proof technique to multi-layer networks. Although the authors mention potential extension, they provide no concrete results or insights.
- **Uncertainty about assumption generality across modalities**: One reviewer (R2) questions whether the assumptions, verified on image patch tokens (ViT), hold equally for NLP tasks (language tokens). A related question asks whether Assumption 2 (involving positional encoding) still holds when positional encodings are learnable.
- **Kruskal Rank approximation quality**: The paper notes that computing the Kruskal Rank is NP-hard and uses an approximation, but provides no quantitative analysis of the approximation error. This makes it hard to assess the practical reliability of the theoretical results.
- **Rationale for data assumptions**: It is unclear whether the specific assumptions were derived independently from empirical observations or tailored to fit the proof technique. More detail on the thought process would be beneficial.
- **Lack of reproducibility details**: Code for the synthetic experiments and assumption-validation tests is not provided; making it available would increase reproducibility.
- **Practical impact discussion**: The paper could include a broader discussion on how the findings guide practical model design (e.g., choosing the number of heads or dimensions in real-world applications).
