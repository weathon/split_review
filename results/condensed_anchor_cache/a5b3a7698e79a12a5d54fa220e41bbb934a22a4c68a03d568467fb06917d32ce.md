## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates spurious correlations between logos in web-scale training data and Vision-Language Model (VLM) predictions. It constructs a new dataset CC12M-LogoBank, proposes a semi-automatic algorithm to uncover spurious logos, and evaluates on three tasks: person attribute classification, object classification, and harmful content detection. Key finding: logos like Adidas cause a model to classify a person as “greedy”; the NASA logo increases the prediction score for “harmless” content. The paper frames this as an attack (logo as an adversarial patch) and proposes two mitigation strategies: cropping and logo masking. Reviewers are split: two give score 5 (positive overall), two give score 3 (critical). Positive reviewers praise the novelty of studying logos as shortcuts, the dataset, and practical mitigations. Critical reviewers find limited evaluation (only CLIP models, single metric, no exploration of origins), weaknesses in the attack/defense framing (cropping assumptions, attacker evasions), and lack of ablations and realistic scenario validation.

### Strengths
- **Novel problem focus**: The study of how logos can act as spurious shortcuts in VLMs due to web-scale training data is original and previously underexplored. The paper brings attention to an attack surface that could bypass automated content moderators.
- **New dataset**: CC12M-LogoBank is a curated collection of logos (87k images) that enables systematic evaluation of logo-based spurious correlations; useful for future research on this specific instance of shortcuts.
- **Solid methodology for mining spurious logos**: The algorithm verifies logo shortcuts over multiple models and multiple source images (not single input), and the study in Section 3.1 is generally well executed.
- **Broad scope**: Analysis covers three tasks (content moderation, object classification, negative adjective association) highlighting the pervasive issue.
- **Threat model is reasonable**: Using logos as an attack is easy to execute by attackers, and framing it adversarially (backdoor-like, adversarial patch) is appealing.
- **Practical mitigation strategies**: Cropping and logo masking integrate with zero-shot inference; the paper demonstrates some reduction in spurious effects.
- **Clear presentation**: Overall writing is easy to follow and related works on spurious behavior of VLMs are well discussed.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited VLM evaluation**: All experiments use only CLIP (ViT-L, ViT-B). No evaluation on other VLMs (e.g., LLaVA, BLIP-2, Llama 3) to support the claim that large-scale VLMs in general contain such spurious correlations. Other training methods (not contrastive) might behave differently.
- **Single evaluation metric**: Only “success rate of label swap” (hard label). No relative metrics (e.g., confidence increase, label flip rate, Relative Bias [Struppek et al., JAIR 2023]) that would provide finer insight into biasing effects even when label doesn’t flip.
- **Mitigation strategies are not robust**:
    - Cropping assumes logo is placed only once in a corner with small size; attacker can place logos at many locations, sizes, and angles, easily evading cropping. Cropping-based defenses have been proven ineffective in adversarial ML literature.
    - No discussion of prior inference-time shortcut mitigation techniques applicable to logo setting.
- **Attack study lacks depth**: Logo placement is limited to a few positions and fixed sizes. Attacker can vary location, size, rotation, etc. The “spuriousity metric” does not characterize robustness to such transformations.
- **False positives in dataset**: CC12M-LogoBank is built with a noise level (false positive rate) of 2%. Examples of false positives are not shown, and these 2% of non-spurious logos remain in the dataset, potentially affecting evaluations. How would this impact future use?
- **No exploration of origins**: The paper does not investigate why certain logos become spurious (e.g., how logos like sports clubs are represented in training captions, whether they co-occur with positive/negative words).
- **Limited novelty vs prior shortcuts** (e.g., Chinese watermark): The main justification (requiring prior knowledge of possible correlations) is weak – knowing logos can be shortcuts is itself prior knowledge. The semi-automatic process may not be sufficiently differentiated.
- **Unrealistic logo applications**: Many examples (e.g., handicap symbol on a smiling portrait) are not believable in practical scenarios; the paper fails to motivate the problem for VLMs in realistic settings or provide numbers gauging real-world impact.
- **Design choices lack comprehensive ablations**:
    - Logo placement only on borders.
    - Cropping/masking introduces empty patches.
    - Choosing top patches, using CLIP for evaluation – all these choices may paint an incomplete picture.
- **No limitations section** and limited future directions: The paper does not suggest how to detect/prevent spurious logo correlations when logos are naturally integrated (e.g., milk cartons with Chinese characters) or how to train more robust VLMs (instead of evading logos).
- **Dynamic nature of logos**: Associations change with marketing campaigns/public perception; no method proposed to address this.
- **Potential dataset overlap**: The evaluation datasets may overlap with CLIP training data; the effect of such overlap is not explored.
- **Spuriousity metric unclear**: Definition of P(M(·)) is missing; output space of M is not defined. (Eq2: what exactly is measured – hard label prediction or average softmax probability?)
- **Figure 4(b) presents only n=2 samples per condition**, results are inconclusive (minimal decrease/increase).
- **Figure 7 last row**: All logos have same effect as no-logo baseline, suggesting failure of some spurious logos.
- **Use of OWLv2 for object detection**: OWLv2 itself is a VLM that may also be affected by logo spurious correlations; this potential circularity is not addressed.
- **No evaluation of splice detection** (e.g., pixel discontinuities, lighting/shadow inconsistencies, JPEG artifacts) as a defense against non-expert attackers who simply paste logos.
- **Missing references to backdoor attacks (BadNets) and adversarial patches [Brown et al.]** to properly place the work in adversarial ML literature.
- **Why three scenarios (content moderation, general classification, negative adjective association)?** Rationale not given; other scenarios may also be relevant.

**Minor / Presentation Issues**:
- Abbreviation VLM not formally introduced; used inconsistently (e.g., L116 “vision-language models” again).
- “Noise level” is better termed “false positive rate” if it refers to FPR.
- Line plots with only two x-axis points would be better as bar charts.
- How model size vs patch size affects results (larger patch may cut logos in pieces) not disentangled.
- Evaluation of logo visibility (size, alpha) and impact on success missing.
- Lack of exploration using synthetic logos (e.g., generated by diffusion models) to isolate features like color, shape.
- Majority voting augmentation as mitigation already exists in literature [Sarkar et al., IEEE D&T].
