## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an insert-and-detection method for membership inference in LLMs to protect copyrighted content. Users embed unique identifiers—"ghost sentences" composed of random word sequences—into their text. Detection uses a perplexity test (comparing ghost sentence perplexity against random sentences) and a user-friendly last‑k words test (prompting the model to complete the sentence). Both tests yield interpretable p‑values. Experiments use LLaMA models (13B, OpenLLaMA-3B) and show, e.g., 30 ghost sentences repeated ~7 times in 148K examples produce 0.891 ROC AUC (perplexity test), and 11/16 users with ~24 examples each detected their data from 1.8M examples (last‑k test). The authors also examine model size, data scale, repetition, insertion position, and alignment training.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important, well-motivated problem of copyright protection in LLM training (reviewers 1,4,5).
- Proposes accessible detection techniques for non-technical users, including a code-free last‑k words test (reviewers 1,2,5). Not relying on output probabilities is an advantage for commercial models (reviewer 5).
- Includes detailed ablations on repetition rates, model sizes, ghost sentence length, insertion position, and training data scale (reviewers 1,2,3).
- Provides interpretable p‑values for both tests (reviewer 1).
- Experiments cover multiple settings (different model sizes, data scales, alignment training), and the paper gives a good overview in the introduction (reviewer 5).
- One reviewer found the approach innovative and the experimental validation extensive (reviewer 3), and another praised the experiments as solid and effective (reviewer 4—note this reviewer gave the highest score, 6). Reviewer 5 appreciated the coverage of alignment training.

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of novelty and insufficient comparison with prior work.** Multiple reviewers (1,5) note significant overlap with Wei et al. [1] (random character sequences) and Meeus et al. [2] (loss‑based detection). The three claimed disadvantages of [1] (filtering risk, false positives, user‑friendliness) are only partially justified: random sequences can also be filtered, collisions are unlikely with sufficient length, and the last‑k prompt could likely be adapted to character sequences (reviewer 5). A head‑to‑head comparison in detection success and experiments confirming/rebutting the third point are missing (reviewers 1,5).
- **Perplexity test is not new** (Carlini et al. [3]) and is similar to the loss‑based metric used in [1] (reviewer 1). The last‑k test resembles prior prompting‑based membership inference (Lukas et al. [4], Chang et al. [5]) (reviewer 5).
- **Low recall and high repetition requirement for the perplexity test.** It requires at least 5 repetitions and yields a recall of only ~0.393 (reviewer 1). For pretraining-scale data, an unrealistically large percentage of the training set would need to consist of ghost sentences (cf. Table 3b) (reviewer 5). A test on a model with publicly available training data (e.g., BLOOM) could show whether this percentage decreases for large models (reviewer 5).
- **Experiments limited to the LLaMA family.** Only LLaMA and OpenLLaMA are tested; alternative open‑source models like Mistral and proprietary models (e.g., GPT‑4o) are missing (reviewer 3). This raises concerns about generalizability (reviewers 3,4).
- **Lack of baseline comparisons.** No comparison with established membership inference methods (e.g., min‑k test, loss‑based attacks) or with the prior watermarks [1,2] in terms of detection effectiveness (reviewers 2,5). The min‑k test baseline with best k is specifically requested (reviewer 1).
- **Robustness to data filtering methods not addressed.** Perplexity-based filtering is common in pretraining data cleaning; how the ghost sentences would survive such preprocessing is unclear (reviewers 2,4). Adaptation strategies are needed (reviewer 2). Effect of LLM‑based rewriting/rephrasing is not studied (reviewer 2).
- **Role of wordlist choice.** The paper does not analyze how different wordlists affect detection effectiveness, nor the trade‑offs between using the full LLM vocabulary and a smaller curated set (reviewer 2).
- **Impact on data quality and readability.** Multiple reviewers ask whether embedding ghost sentences harms the naturalness or fluency of the original text, and whether it lowers the model’s generative fluency (reviewers 3,4).
- **Finetuning and pretraining disconnect.** The main experiments inject ghost sentences via finetuning, but the problem is detecting pretraining data. Findings from finetuning may not transfer to pretraining (reviewer 4). The instruction tuning task “continue writing the given content” is too similar to the pretraining objective (reviewer 5). The perplexity test’s applicability to closed‑source models without output probabilities is questionable (reviewer 4).
- **Methodological and presentation concerns.** Writing needs improvement: Figure 1 is not self‑explanatory, notations are confusing (Section 3.1 uses both $x_i$ and $x$ ambiguously), there are typos (e.g., “Figure Figure 1”, line 185, ll.324–329), and the term “word length” should be “sentence length” (reviewers 3,5). The use of beam search instead of sampling‑based decoding is not justified; results may differ with sampling (reviewer 5). The requested detail on token counts (reviewer 1) and an upper‑bound clarification for Equation 3 (reviewer 5) are missing.
- **Minor reviewer disagreement:** While most reviewers point out the lack of novelty, one reviewer (4) scores 6 and finds the method effective and the experiments solid, and another (3) scores 5 but gives low soundness/presentation/contribution. The two lowest scores (3) from reviewers 2 and 5 emphasize missing baselines and novelty concerns.
