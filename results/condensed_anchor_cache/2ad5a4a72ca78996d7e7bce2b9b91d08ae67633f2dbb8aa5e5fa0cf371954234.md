## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Dynamics Diffusion (DyDiff), a method that combines a single-step dynamics model with a diffusion model (DM) for long-horizon trajectory generation in offline reinforcement learning. DyDiff addresses the mismatch between the behavior policy implied by the DM and the learning policy by iteratively refining trajectories: the learning policy and single-step model produce initial rollouts, then the DM generates state sequences conditioned on those actions, and the process repeats. Theoretical analysis is claimed to show DMs have less compounding error than single-step models. Experiments on D4RL tasks (MuJoCo locomotion, Maze2d) and ablations (Fig. 3a, Sec. 5.4) are provided. Reviews are mixed: three accept (6) and one reject (3). The minority reviewer (R2) considers the theoretical justification insufficient and the contribution limited without it; the others find the idea novel but raise concerns about result significance, variance, and missing analyses.

### Strengths
- The core idea of iteratively combining a learning policy with a diffusion-based dynamics model for policy-consistent rollouts is novel and interesting (R1, R4, R2).
- The motivational example (Figure 1) and the overview in Figure 2 are well done (R3).
- The related work section gives a good overview of diffusion in RL and model-based offline RL (R3).
- The writing is generally clear and easy to follow (R1, R3, R4).
- Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) is provided to support the method, though its validity is debated (R1, R2, R4).
- Experiments on D4RL benchmarks (MuJoCo, Maze2d) show DyDiff improves performance over base algorithms in some settings (R1, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Inconsistent improvement and lack of statistical significance**: In most datasets, improvement is minor and within standard deviation (R4). Results in Sec. 5.2 show improvement in some settings, decrease in others, and near-identical performance in the rest. The claim that DyDiff “significantly improves … without any additional hyperparameter tuning” is overblown without significance tests or confidence intervals (R3).
- **High variance**: Main results (Sec. 5.2) and ablation studies (Sec. 5.4) suffer from high variance, making it hard to draw reliable conclusions about component contributions (R3, R4).
- **Theoretical claims lack support**: 
  - Theorem 1’s conclusion that the single-step error bound εₘ is less than the accumulative multi-step error ε_d (due to DM’s superior modeling) is not theoretically justified (R2). Only a simple MSE experiment is provided, which is insufficient (R2).
  - The claim that DyDiff tightens the return gap holds only if εₘ ≥ ε_d, a condition not generally satisfied; the theorem is therefore considered meaningless by R2 (minority view).
  - No theoretical guarantees are given for why simply replacing generated actions with learning policy actions resolves the mismatch issue (R2).
- **Ablation study (Fig. 3a) counterintuitive**: More iteration steps M should improve performance, but it degrades. The authors’ explanation (higher probability of falling out of distribution) is unconvincing; further analysis of state distributions and dynamics consistency across iterations is needed (R1). Also, sensitivity to M lacks a clear strategy for choosing M based on dataset/environment (R4).
- **Missing comparisons**:
  - With other data augmentation methods (e.g., MTDiff) (R1).
  - With diffusion-based planning algorithms using the learning policy as guidance (R1).
  - With Decision Transformer (R1).
  - With DecisionDiffuser (advantages of generating both state and action vs. only state) (R2).
  - With concurrent work (Jackson et al.) (R3).
- **Limited applicability**: The method trains a single-step dynamics model to seed the diffusion model; its effectiveness is limited when the dataset does not provide sufficient transition coverage (R4). The impact of sub‑optimal initial policies/dynamics models is not analyzed (R4).
- **No computational cost analysis**: Training time (only DyDiff’s reported) and model sizes are not compared with baselines (R2, R4).
- **Questions about policy and iteration**:
  - How is the learning policy π obtained in the generation process? Does it update iteratively with trajectory generation, and at what frequency? (R1)
  - Why does DyDiff improve much more on Maze2d than on MuJoCo? Could be due to sparse rewards or easier dynamics? (R1, R4)
  - Why does CQL appear relatively incompatible with DyDiff yet show similar benefit as other bases in Sec. 5.3? (R3)
  - What are the conditions for Assumptions 1 and 2? (R2)
- **Notation confusion**: Superscripts for diffusion step (i) and trajectory improvement iteration (k) are inconsistent, e.g., in Sec. 4.2 (R3). Clarification of Fig. 2 and lines 182–186 is needed (R3).
- **Minority reviewer (R2) negative view**: Without Theorem 1 and its conclusion, the paper’s contribution is quite limited. The main idea—using learning policy actions to enforce trajectory distribution—lacks theoretical support. Simple MSE experiment is not enough to establish ε_d > εₘ.
