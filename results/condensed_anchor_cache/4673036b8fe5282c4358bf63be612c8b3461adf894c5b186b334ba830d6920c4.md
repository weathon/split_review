## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Shifted Feature Reweighting (SFR), a method to improve group robustness of vision-language models (e.g., CLIP) against spurious correlations. SFR assigns instance-level importance weights based on disagreement with an ERM baseline and introduces feature dropout to prevent representation collapse. Empirical evaluations on Waterbirds, CelebA, and other benchmarks show worst-group accuracy improvements. Reviewers are deeply divided: one (score 1) suspects substantial overlap with the CFR paper (CVPR 2024) and finds the literature review deficient, while others (scores 3, 5, 6, 5) acknowledge the problem’s importance but raise concerns about novelty, baselines, and scope.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important and under‑explored problem: spurious correlations in pre‑trained vision‑language models. (R2, R4)
- The disagreement‑based instance‑level reweighting strategy is reasonable, practical, and appears correctly implemented; focusing on individual sample difficulty rather than fixed group weights is a refreshing perspective. (R3, R4, R5)
- Empirical results show improvements in worst‑group accuracy over several baselines on multiple benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, etc.) using both ResNet‑50 and ViT architectures. (R2)
- Theoretical analysis in the appendix provides grounding for the method and adds credibility. (R4, R5)
- The paper is easy to read and visualizations (figures) are clear and informative. (R1, R5; note that R5 rated presentation low due to other issues, see Weaknesses)
- One reviewer (R4, score 6) considered the approach novel and effective, and described the reweighting strategy as both practical and effective.

### Weaknesses
- **Potential plagiarism / insufficient attribution:** The structure, figures (Fig. 1, 4, 5), table layout (Table 1), and equations (Eq. 3, 4) are nearly identical to those in the CFR paper (You et al., CVPR 2024) without citation. This strong similarity raises ethical concerns and suggests inadequate acknowledgment of prior work. (R1)
- **Limited literature review:**  
  - Lines 23–24 claim existing strategies “uniformly weigh all instances within a group,” but instance‑specific weighting methods already exist (e.g., LfF, DisEnt, LWBC). Line 90‑91 states this remains an open question, contradicting prior work. (R1)  
  - Lines 284–285 incorrectly label Nam et al. (LfF) as “pseudo‑labeling” – LfF uses instance‑wise weights, not pseudo‑labels. (R1)  
- **Unclear technical contribution:** The method largely builds on DFR (last‑layer fine‑tuning) and SaC (spurious‑aware contrastive loss); line 366 further confuses by saying L_spurious can be chosen as L_SCL. The primary improvement over SaC appears to be computational efficiency, which may not meet the novelty bar. (R2)  
- **Missing baselines / comparisons:**  
  - No comparison with zero‑shot CLIP, prompt‑tuning methods, or inference‑time robustness techniques (e.g., Zero‑Shot Robustification, Amend to Alignment). (R2, R3)  
  - No comparison with existing instance‑weighting methods (e.g., LfF, DisEnt, LWBC) despite claiming instance‑specific weights as a novelty. (R1)  
  - No comparison with Zhang & Ré 2022 (Contrastive Adapters), which is cited in the paper. (R1)  
  - Missing comparison with previous distribution shift methods applied to CLIP. (R3)  
- **Insufficient ablation studies:** The effect of individual components (disagreement‑based reweighting, feature dropout) is not isolated. Deeper validation of why instance‑level reweighting outperforms group‑level reweighting is lacking. (R4)  
- **Dependence on group labels:** The method requires group labels, whereas several recent approaches learn without them. (R1)  
- **Outdated evaluation settings:** The CLIP variant is not clearly specified. Current OpenCLIP models achieve near‑perfect zero‑shot accuracy on simple datasets like Waterbirds, making the current benchmarks insufficient to test the method’s robustness. (R3)  
- **Scope limited to CLIP:** Only CLIP (visual/text branch) is tested; applicability to other architectures (e.g., ALIGN, BLIP) or unimodal pre‑trained models is not explored. (R5)  
- **Presentation and clarity issues:**  
  - No comprehensive pipeline illustration of the overall method, making implementation hard to follow. (R5)  
  - Reference inaccuracy: Figure 3 incorrectly references Eq. 2. (R5)  
  - Figure 4 uses an uncoordinated color scheme that hinders clarity. (R5)  
  - The limitations section reads more like a summary rather than discussing actual constraints. (R4)  
- **Missing reporting of variance:** No means and standard deviations are reported; some improvements are within 1‑2%. (R2)  
- **Question on Eq. 2 motivation:** Using the inverse of the disagreement score as the weight may push the target model toward the ERM distribution, contradicting the goal of mitigating spurious correlations. (R2)  
- **No efficiency quantification:** Despite claiming the method is lightweight, no concrete comparison of training time or FLOPs is provided. (R5)  
- **Empty GitHub repository** does not provide any supplementary code. (R5)
