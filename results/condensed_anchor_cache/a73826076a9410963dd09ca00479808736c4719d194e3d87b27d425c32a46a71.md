## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces ThinShellLab, a fully differentiable simulation platform for robotic interactions with diverse thin-shell materials (e.g., paper, cloth) featuring varying bending-stiffness, bending plasticity, and frictional contact. It defines a benchmark of ten tasks (lifting, separating, following, folding, pick-folding, forming, plus three inverse design tasks) to evaluate RL, sampling-based (CMA-ES), gradient-based (GD), and hybrid trajectory optimization methods. A hybrid scheme combining sampling and gradient optimization is proposed to overcome challenges such as sensitivity to action variation and frequent contact changes. Real-robot deployment via system identification (sim-to-real) is demonstrated on a subset of tasks. All five reviewers rated the paper 8/8.

### Strengths

- ThinShellLab is the first simulator to support a diverse range of thin-shell materials while remaining differentiable, enabling gradient-based optimization (R1, R2, R4, R5).
- Unlike existing simulators, it models bending-stiffness, bending plasticity, and frictional contact between thin-shell objects and end-effectors (R2, R4).
- The manipulation tasks are well-designed to reflect properties of thin-shell materials, and the benchmarked methods (RL, CMA-ES, GD, hybrid) have good coverage (R5). The accompanying website clearly conveys the core contributions (R3).
- The hybrid optimization method is shown to improve learning efficiency and converged performance across tasks, with analysis explaining why it helps (R3).
- The paper overall is easy to follow (R1, R5), though this view is not universal (see weaknesses).

### Weaknesses

- **Simulator description is insufficiently detailed.**  
  The core simulation is described in barely one page, covering only high-level concepts and methods (R1). There is no discussion of the pros and cons of the chosen modeling approach versus alternatives (e.g., barrier vs. quadratic contact energy) (R1, R5). Specific missing details include: how contact penetration is correctly identified when an element’s normal flips (R5), how the rigid-body action space is achieved for the robot (R5), and how Newton’s method solving the large linear system is implemented on GPU (e.g., sparse matrix, conjugate gradient) (R5). The system’s support for rigid-body simulation coupling is not fully shown (R5), nor are tasks involving constraint simulation (e.g., cloth with one end fixed) (R5).

- **Task definitions and success metrics are unclear.**  
  The exact objectives (reward functions, success criteria) for each task are omitted, making it impossible to interpret what scores correspond to (R1, R2). Table 2 reports mean ± std values without explaining that this is the format, and one row lacks ± values without justification (R1). Cells missing values are not explained (R1). The maximum scores reported in experiments reflect “optimization” performance rather than “generalization,” and it is unclear how timesteps are counted for CMA-ES and GD methods, and what “episode” on the x-axis means for GD and CMA-ES in Figure 3 (R2). Adding explicit success indicators (e.g., success rate) would help readers understand performance differences (R2).

- **Real-world experiments are not convincing.**  
  Only a single case is shown; it is not convincing that the described system identification method will generally improve sim-to-real policy deployment (R5). The section conveys no experimental data and merely refers to an appendix with a handful of plots; it should include actual results and discussion (R1). Details on how sim-to-real system identification is performed and how disparity between simulation and reality is computed are missing from the main text (R3).

- **Hybrid method and its tuning are under-described.**  
  Details of the hybrid approach are extremely limited (R1, R3). While the idea of combining global search with local refinement is sensible, it is unclear why it was added given that RL already explores by default (R1). The amount of effort required to tune/balance gradient-free and gradient-based steps is not mentioned (R3). The large variance in some learning curves (Figure 3) is not discussed (R1).

- **Presentation and figures need improvement.**  
  Figure 2 has poorly aligned image bounding boxes, no clear boundary between “left” and “right” tasks as stated in the caption, and within the Sliding subfigure image edges do not match (R2, R3). The three inverse design tasks (Sliding, Bouncing, Card) are not clearly labeled in Table 2 or Figure 3, causing confusion (R4). Figure 3 contains a typo: “Separatering” (R3). Overall figures feel less refined than the website (R3).

- **Writing clarity is inconsistent.**  
  While R1 and R5 found the paper easy to follow, R2 noted the writing is not clear enough (see questions in the review). Important implementation details (e.g., how observations with unordered point sets are handled by PPO/SAC, the meaning of 6DoF vs. 7DoF actions) are ambiguous (R2).

- **Missing conclusions and guidance.**  
  The paper does not draw clear conclusions or provide suggestions to guide readers in selecting methods for different types of tasks (R4).

- **Simulation speed and parallelization.**  
  Questions from multiple reviewers (R1, R3, R4, R5) about simulation speed (real-time? faster? slower?) and GPU parallelization (does it benefit CMA-ES/RL only or also GD?) remain unanswered in the main text.
