## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper identifies three key limitations of the Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) – non-Gaussianity of I3D features, insensitivity to temporal distortions, and impractically large sample sizes – and proposes a new metric called JEDi (JEPA Embedding Distance). JEDi uses features from a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (V-JEPA) and computes distribution distance via Maximum Mean Discrepancy with a polynomial kernel. Experiments on open-source datasets show that JEDi requires only 16% of the samples to reach a steady value compared to FVD and improves alignment with human evaluation by 34% on average. Reviewers agree that the systematic analysis of FVD’s flaws is valuable and that JEDi shows clear empirical improvements, but they raise concerns about the limited scope of experimental validation (few models, few distortion types, small human evaluation), insufficient comparison with related metrics (single-video quality, no-reference metrics like NIQE, large-scale AIGC databases), and the paper’s presentation and clarity. One reviewer (score 3) found the explanation of JEDi limited and the sample‑size argument less critical.

### Strengths

- The paper systematically identifies and analyzes three major limitations of FVD: over‑reliance on Gaussianity assumptions (non-Gaussian I3D features), insensitivity to temporal distortions, and the need for impractically large sample sizes for reliable estimates. These are important challenges in video generation evaluation.
- JEDi eliminates the parametric Gaussian assumption by using Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a polynomial kernel, which does not assume a specific distribution.
- JEDi shows a significant improvement in sample efficiency: it requires only 16% of the samples needed by FVD to reach a steady value, making it more practical for real-world use.
- Alignment with human evaluation is increased by 34% on average (cosine similarity with human ratings), which represents a substantial advance over FVD.
- The authors conduct comprehensive comparison experiments that yield valuable insights, such as the relative importance of feature space vs. distance metric choice and the selection of distortion‑aware feature spaces.
- The proposed metric is well‑justified and clearly explained, and its effectiveness is verified on multiple open‑source datasets with both image‑to‑video models and noise distortions.
- The paper documents the correlation between the metric and distortion level as well as training duration, providing useful observations for improving FVD.

### Weaknesses

- **Small‑scale human evaluation with insufficient detail.** The subjective study is very small and lacks crucial information: the number of subjects, their qualifications/background, the sampling strategy for video selection, and whether overall quality ratings (single stimulus) were collected in addition to pairwise comparisons. The appendix section on human evaluation is empty. Reviewers strongly suggest evaluating JEDi on existing large‑scale AIGC video quality databases (e.g., T2VQA‑DB, LGVQ, GAIA) to obtain more reliable and generalizable human alignment results.
- **Limited scope of experiments.** Only two image‑to‑video models and three noise distortion types (blur, noise, artifacts) are tested. There is no evaluation with high‑level distortions (semantic, composition) or with a wider variety of models (both proprietary and open‑source). The paper also lacks analysis of long‑term temporal distortions (e.g., subtle motion drift over several seconds, long‑range dependencies). One reviewer notes that I3D and VideoMAEPT are not ideal for capturing blur; the paper should examine how JEDi handles other distortions.
- **Missing comparisons with related metrics and literature.** The paper does not compare JEDi with state‑of‑the‑art single‑video quality assessment metrics for AIGC content, nor does it review and discuss similar distribution‑based metrics such as NIQE (which uses Mahalanobis distance in a multivariate Gaussian model). Including NIQE, Q‑Align, and other no‑reference quality metrics in the experiments (e.g., to quantify blur) would provide a more holistic evaluation. The authors should also discuss the similarities and differences between distribution metrics and single‑video quality metrics.
- **Content organization and clarity issues.** Several reviewers note that Sections 2.1 and 2.2 contain too much background information (I3D, Video Masked Autoencoder, Fréchet distance) that should be moved to the appendix, while more related work on evaluation metrics (including no‑reference VQA) is missing. Figure 3 is difficult to interpret without zooming; Table 1 lacks explanation of red‑font items and the meaning of “blur (low, mid, high)”. The equations in Appendix B need better formatting. The paper could reduce repetition of existing metric critiques and increase logical exposition of JEDi itself.
- **One reviewer (score 3) questions the emphasis on sample size convergence**, arguing that many datasets do have sufficient samples and that this is not the primary concern for an evaluation strategy. While others view sample efficiency as a strength, this reviewer believes further validation on other aspects is needed.
- **JEDi is a distribution‑based metric and cannot evaluate single generated videos.** The paper does not discuss how it could be applied for single‑video quality assessment, nor does it compare with single‑video AIGC quality models. This is seen as a limitation given the practical need to assess individual outputs.
- **Title is too broad.** The paper focuses on only one aspect of video generation quality (distribution matching), while the title suggests a comprehensive evaluation.
- **Resolution handling concern.** The method relies on StreamDiffusion based on SD‑Turbo/LCM for feature extraction, and one reviewer questions whether the metric can reliably handle high‑resolution videos not seen during SD training (specifically: “Can Promptus effectively transmit videos with various resolutions and high resolutions that it has not seen during SD training?”). (Note: “Promptus” appears in the review; may refer to a specific component of the pipeline.)
- **Small‑scale subjective evaluation lacks overall quality ratings** (only pairwise comparison ratings are obtained). The monotonicity with training iterations shown in Fig. 5 is informative but does not directly measure quality evaluation accuracy; standard indices like SROCC, PLCC, RMSE should be reported.
- **Experimental details missing.** The degree of blur applied in Section 5.1 (kernel size, standard deviation) is not specified, and no objective no‑reference metric is used to quantify the distortion level.
