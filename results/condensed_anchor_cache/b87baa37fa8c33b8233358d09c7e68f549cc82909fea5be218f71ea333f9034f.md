## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes MoE-Pruner, a one-shot pruning method for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLMs that weights are pruned based on magnitudes multiplied by input activations and router gate values per output neuron. An expert-wise knowledge distillation step is used to recover performance. Evaluations on Mixtral-8x7B and Mixtral-8x22B show that the method outperforms baseline pruning techniques designed for dense LLMs.

### Strengths
- The method is conceptually simple and can be applied to various existing MoE models without retraining. (Reviewer 2, 3, 4)
- The paper provides insights into how expert initialization methods influence load balance and expert similarity. (Reviewer 1)
- Perplexity improvements are achieved by leveraging gate values, outperforming previous pruning methods for general transformers. (Reviewer 1)
- A detailed ablation study of each component and hyperparameter is given, clarifying the role of each part and sensitivity to hyperparameters. (Reviewer 2)
- Pruning MoE is an important and impactful research problem, and the proposed method is efficient and practical for real-world deployment. (Reviewer 3, 4 – Reviewer 4 minority positive)
- The method is clearly explained, with a focus on practical implementation. (Reviewer 4 – minority positive)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited technical novelty and justification.** The method is a straightforward extension of Wanda’s formulation to MoE without providing theoretical justification for how router scores are incorporated, an analysis of the impact on pruning behavior, or why expert-wise knowledge distillation is optimal for MoE. (Reviewer 1, 3, 4)
- **Evaluation is restricted to Mixtral models only.** The method’s scalability to other MoE architectures (e.g., DeepSeek-MoE, MiniCPM-MoE, Qwen) is not tested, raising questions about generalizability. (Reviewer 2, 3, 4)
- **No efficiency evaluation.** The paper lacks wall‑clock speedup, memory reduction, and inference speedup measurements. Also missing is an analysis of the memory overhead of storing sparse masks. (Reviewer 1, 3)
- **Incomplete experimental comparisons.** No comparison with existing expert pruning or merging methods (e.g., Liu et al., Lu et al., Muzio et al., Zhang et al., Lee et al.) is provided, making it unclear whether the method is truly state‑of‑the‑art. (Reviewer 2, 4)
- **Equations and Algorithm 1 lack clarity.** Equations 7–9 do not define indices i and j; in eq. 9 “S” is used instead of S_{i,j}. Equation 10 does not specify whether cross‑entropy is the global next‑token loss or local MoE gate loss. Algorithm 1 does not explain how “unstructured” sparsity is reflected, which weight tensor W corresponds to (which linear layer in SwiGLU, all experts or one expert). (Reviewer 2)
- **Claims are unsupported.** The statement “MoE mitigates catastrophic forgetting in continual learning” (Line 44) lacks references. The claim that “expert activation frequency is task‑agnostic” (Line 161) is given without experimental or logical evidence. (Reviewer 1)
- **Minor improvement from distillation.** The distilled step shows only minor gains (Table 4), and the underlying reason for this limited improvement is not explained. (Reviewer 4)
- **Poor presentation.** Section 2 is too short; Sections 3.1 and 3.2 are essentially a literature review. Figure 1 does not clearly show how the score S interacts with other components. (Reviewer 3)
- **Potential task‑dependent limitations.** Pruning the least‑activated experts may harm performance on rare but crucial tasks (e.g., math, coding); the paper does not validate whether the pruned model preserves capabilities on specific tasks. (Reviewer 1, 2)
- **Missing ablation on pruning ratios and distillation parameters.** A comprehensive study of the sensitivity to different sparsity levels and distillation hyperparameters is lacking. (Reviewer 3)
