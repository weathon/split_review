## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces VideoPhy, a benchmark of 688 human-verified prompts for evaluating physical commonsense in text-to-video generation. It evaluates both open (e.g., CogVideoX-5B) and closed models (e.g., Lumiere, Dream Machine) with binary (0/1) human ratings on Semantic Adherence (SA) and Physical Commonsense (PC), finding even the best model (CogVideoX-5B) achieves only 39.6% adherence. An automatic evaluator (VideoCon-Physics) is fine-tuned from VideoCon. Reviewer opinions diverge: Reviewer 4 is notably more positive (score 8, high confidence), praising the dataset and automatic evaluation, while Reviewer 3 is more critical (score 5, low confidence), questioning category definitions, annotation trustworthiness, and the validity of the auto-evaluator.

### Strengths
- Evaluates physical commonsense, a vital aspect of realism in video generation that has been largely overlooked. (R1, R2)
- Comprehensive model selection covering open- and closed-source models (CogVideoX, Lumiere, Dream Machine, Pika, etc.). (R1, R3)
- Well-organized, clear presentation with key evaluation numbers. (R1, R4)
- Provides detailed failure modes and insights to guide future research. (R2)
- Introduces an automatic evaluation pipeline (VideoCon-Physics) enabling scalable assessment. (R2)
- High-quality, human-verified captions with balanced categories (solid-solid, solid-fluid, fluid-fluid). (R3, R4)
- Dataset, generated videos, and human annotations will be released, boosting future work. (R4)
- Shifts community attention from general visual quality to physical fidelity. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Binary feedback (0/1) is too coarse**: it cannot distinguish between models that partially satisfy a prompt (e.g., 8/10 vs 6/10 physical movements). Relative metrics like ELO scores are suggested as more discriminative. (R1)
- **Physical commonsense assessment is confounded with semantic adherence**: if a model fails to generate the described scenario (e.g., never shows milk being poured), the PC score reflects general generation ability rather than physics understanding. The authors should evaluate PC only on videos that already satisfy SA. (R2)
- **Assumption underlying VideoCon-Physics is unverified**: the V2T model must have better physics understanding than the T2V models it evaluates, but this is not justified. A controlled experiment—fine-tuning a T2V model on the VideoPhy training split and comparing its generations to the V2T evaluator’s scores—would test this assumption. (R2)
- **Category definitions and sample ratios are questionable**: classification by broad material type (solid vs fluid) misses important distinctions in graphics (rigid bodies, soft bodies, fabrics, characters, etc.) and does not align with interaction types or physical properties. Despite solids requiring more constitutive models, solid-solid and solid-fluid counts are nearly equal (289 vs 291). (R3)
- **Training data for VideoCon-Physics is unreliable**: each training sample was labeled by only one annotator, yet human agreement on the benchmark is 75% (SA) and 70% (PC). This low per-sample reliability weakens the training set. (R3)
- **Fine-tuning VideoCon without injecting explicit physical knowledge or reasoning is insufficient**: reported improvements may simply reflect domain bias; no analysis of domain shift or automatic evaluation–human disagreement (e.g., Luma Dream Machine, Pika) is provided. (R3)
- **Potential annotator bias in VideoCon-Physics**: since training and benchmark annotations come from the same group of annotators, the auto-evaluator may inherit their biases. A separate human evaluation of the auto-evaluator’s outputs is needed to confirm correctness. (R4)
- **Missing fine-tuning details** for VideoCon-Physics, which would support reproduction and transferability. (R1)
