## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper develops Bisection Projection, an approach to ensure neural network (NN) solution feasibility for constrained optimization over general compact sets with non-empty interiors, extending beyond previous ball-homeomorphic assumptions. The method identifies multiple interior points (IPs) within the constraint set using an eccentricity measure, and trains an IPNN to map inputs to these IPs. For infeasible NN solutions, a bisection procedure adjusts them towards the IPs, guaranteeing feasibility with bounded optimality loss. Extensive simulations on non-convex optimal power flow problems demonstrate effectiveness.

### Strengths
- The method is original and innovative, applying bisection projection between infeasible solutions and interior points to ensure feasibility (Reviewer 1: originality; Reviewer 4: innovative).
- Broadens feasibility guarantees beyond ball-homeomorphic sets to general compact sets with non-empty interiors, making it more widely applicable than prior methods (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Provides solid theoretical analysis, including feasibility guarantees and bounds on optimality loss under mild conditions (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
- Introduces an eccentricity metric for evaluating interior points and the use of multiple interior points for feasibility restoration (Reviewer 2: acknowledges these as contributions, though questions whether both are needed). 
- Employes an IPNN to predict interior points, substantially reducing runtime in real-time operation (Reviewer 3).
- The paper is well-written, with a clear logical structure and good presentation (Reviewers 1, 4, 5).
- Demonstrated strong performance on non-convex optimal power flow problems, outperforming existing methods in feasibility and computational efficiency (Reviewer 1; Reviewer 4: significantly faster on evaluated datasets).

### Weaknesses
**Methodological assumptions and limitations**
- The method assumes the availability of an interior point for the constraint set. This is a very strong assumption, especially for general non-convex problems; obtaining interior points may be as hard as solving the original optimization problem (Reviewer 2, confidence 5). The paper’s claim of feasibility guarantees for “arbitrary sets” is conditional on this assumption and other implicit assumptions, reducing the scope (Reviewer 2). The theoretical results fail to state their intrinsic limitations (Reviewer 2).
- Handling of equality constraints is insufficiently justified: the re-parameterization via the implicit function theorem is only local and may not be globally valid for non-convex constraints; discontinuities in the mapping are not discussed (Reviewer 5). The equality completion technique lacks sufficient detail to evaluate practical applicability (Reviewer 4). The method primarily focuses on inequality constraints; the scope should be clarified earlier in the paper (Reviewer 4).
- The eccentricity metric in Definition 4.1 assumes a compact (bounded) feasible set, eliminating many problems with unbounded feasible regions (Reviewer 2).
- Proposition 4.1 assumes availability of a trained model with arbitrary accuracy, which is unrealistic in practice (Reviewer 2).

**Theoretical analysis issues**
- Theorem 1 lacks clear definitions of constants \(C_0\), \(C_1\), \(C_2\) (or \(C_3\)) in the main text; these are only defined in the appendix. This makes it difficult to assess the theorem’s practical implications (Reviewers 2, 4, 5). The constants depend on the constraint boundary variation, IPNN Lipschitz constant, and inner ball radius, indicating sensitivity to problem structure and IPNN quality (Reviewer 4). The definition of \(C_0\) (appendix) depends on the rate of variation of the constraint boundary and may be infinite for sets with boundary jumps (Reviewer 2).
- The sample-based condition in Theorem 1 requires an \(r_c\)-covering dataset, which generally requires an exponential number of points (exponential in input dimension) and may be impossible if the input support is unbounded (Reviewer 2).
- The verification-based condition re-states the definition of a feasible point and requires solving a computationally intractable NN verification problem; relaxing integrality may yield a weak bound (Reviewer 2).
- Proposition 5.1’s bound on the number of interior points is not asymptotically better than a simple hyperbox covering, and the required number may be unbounded with respect to \(\theta\) (Reviewer 2).

**Experimental limitations**
- The data generation procedure for the problem instances is not described; a complete description is needed (Reviewer 2).
- Convex QCQPs and SOCPs are equivalent problem classes, yet they are treated separately (Reviewer 2).
- Chance constraints used in experiments are convex and representable with second-order cone constraints, biasing results towards baseline methods (Reviewer 2).
- Table 5 (verification-based IPNN performance) does not include AC-OPF instances (Reviewer 2).
- Experiments are limited to power grids with up to 200 nodes, which are not representative of real-life systems; experiments on larger grids (e.g., 6000 nodes) are needed (Reviewer 2).
- The paper lacks a principled ablation study to demonstrate that both the eccentricity metric and the use of multiple interior points are necessary. Existing methods (RAYEN, Gauge mapping, Homeomorphic projection) already assume an interior point and could potentially benefit from multiple interior points; a comparison showing the advantage of the proposed components is missing (Reviewer 2).
- The speedup metric and abbreviations (WS, D-Proj, H-Proj, B-Proj) are not clearly explained (Reviewer 4).

**Novelty and scope concerns**
- Bisection (line search) has been used previously in the homeomorphic projection method (Ref. [LCL23]), reducing the novelty of the core technique (Reviewer 2). The paper should better distinguish its contribution (Reviewers 1, 2).
- The method’s relevance to the ICLR community is unclear; the applications focus on power systems, and more numerical studies on broader machine learning problems might be needed (Reviewer 1).
- The paper should cite “A New Computationally Simple Approach for Implementing Neural Networks with Output Hard Constraints” (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.10459) (Reviewer 2).
- The heading “Conclusions and Limitations” does not discuss limitations; the paper should either update the heading or include a limitations section (Reviewer 4).
