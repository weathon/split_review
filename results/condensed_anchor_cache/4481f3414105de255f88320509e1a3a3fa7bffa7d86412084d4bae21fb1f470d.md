## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper argues that RLHF misalignment (sycophancy, deception) arises from immediate feedback that ignores downstream consequences. It proposes Reinforcement Learning from Hindsight Simulation (RLHS), which simulates plausible outcomes of a response and then elicits feedback based on those simulated consequences. RLHS is applied to PPO and DPO. Experiments in a marketplace chatbot environment show RLHS reduces misalignment and improves user satisfaction/true utility, including a human study (Prolific). A theoretical analysis (Lemma 1, Theorem 1) claims that the hindsight horizon N exponentially improves utility estimation accuracy.

### Strengths
- **Theoretical insight:** Lemma 1 & Theorem 1 show that the difference in finite‑hindsight utility estimation approaches the true utility difference as N increases; this is described as a unique and strong result (R1) although one reviewer finds the theory trivial (R2).
- **Applicability:** RLHS works with both online (PPO) and offline (DPO) preference optimization (R1, R3).
- **Well‑defined metrics:** True utility and satisfaction rating are clearly defined (R1).
- **Clear presentation:** Figures 1–5 and Tables 1–2 are visually well‑presented; the paper is well‑written and understandable (R1, R3, R4).
- **Evidence of effectiveness:** Consistent improvements in true utility even under partial hindsight (Figure 3, Table 1); human study (R1, R3, R4).
- **Originality (minority reviewer):** The idea is original and addresses a real problem with RLHF (R3, score 8). The problem setting is clearly justified and the insight is important for the RLHF community (R4, though R4 also notes the problem is not new).

### Weaknesses
- **Problem novelty and contribution (majority view):** The problem setting and the central insight (utility depends on consequences) are not new; Lang et al. (cited) already explored this. The proposed solution (simulating outcomes and presenting them to labelers) is incremental—no new algorithmic or theoretical developments beyond basic prompting (R2, R4). One reviewer notes that the approach “feels incremental” combined with weak empirical evidence (R4).
- **Empirical scope is too narrow:** Evaluation only in a single marketplace/chatbot environment introduced by the authors. Generalizability to other domains is unproven; at least one additional environment is needed (R3, R4). One reviewer argues the claim that “immediate human feedback *frequently* misrepresents true utility” is unsupported—the paper only shows it in one designed protocol (R4).
- **Theory versus practice disconnect:** The theory (Lemma 1, Theorem 1) appears to be a trivial consequence of discounting—it says utility can be approximated in a short horizon, which contradicts the paper’s emphasis on end outcomes. What practical guidance does the theorem provide? (R2). Another reviewer asks for empirical analysis of sensitivity to N (optimal N, or effect of different N values) (R1).
- **Assumptions about human feedback:** The model assumes human evaluators follow \(P(s \succ s') = \sigma(\beta (R_T(s) - R_T(s')))\) without considering error correction, bounded rationality, or cognitive biases. The paper should at least discuss how hindsight simulations would handle systematic biases (R1).
- **Simplistic utility and evaluation design:** The evaluation utility (U = 0, −1, 1) is too simplistic; should consider risk aversion and bounded rationality concepts (R1). The normalization of satisfaction ratings to [−1, 1] (instead of a Likert scale) needs clearer justification; distributions of ratings across conditions should be analyzed (R1).
- **Data labeling vs. context issue:** The main failure case (deceptive responses that contradict given context) might be better addressed by simply checking context‑adherence (either by a human labeler or a strong LLM). Tracking end outcomes in a marketplace is uncertain (users may browse without purchase) (R2). Tuning on a good instruction‑following dataset (e.g., for Llama 3.1 70B, GPT‑4o, Claude 3.5) could resolve the problem more directly; it is unclear why only satisfaction‑rate data is used (R2).
- **Claim about online vs. offline misalignment:** The paper claims that “immediate feedback in online training introduces more misalignment gaps than offline training” based solely on a PPO vs. DPO observation in one domain, without hypothesis, justification, or further experiments/ablation to support generalization to all online/offline methods (R4).
- **Dependence on simulation quality:** Results heavily depend on the quality of the hindsight simulation; the paper should analyze the effect of using different‑sized models for the simulation (R3).
- **Human study details missing (R4):** No information on subject selection, population, assignment to conditions, compensation, data filtering, or IRB approval for the Prolific study. Also, “long‑term user satisfaction rate” and “regret rate” in Hypothesis 1 are introduced without definition.
