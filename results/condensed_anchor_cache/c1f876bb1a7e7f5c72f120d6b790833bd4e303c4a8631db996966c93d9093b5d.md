## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes the “rotation trick” for VQ-VAEs to replace the straight-through estimator (STE). Instead of setting ∂q/∂e = I, the trick uses a scaled rotation ∂q/∂e = (||q||/||e||)·R where R is an orthogonal matrix (implemented with two Householder reflectors) such that q = (∂q/∂e)·e. This preserves the angle between the encoder output e and its gradient during backpropagation, yielding a “push‑pull” effect that improves codebook utilization and reconstruction. Experiments on 11 VQ‑VAE/VQ‑GAN training paradigms show consistent gains in reconstruction metrics, codebook usage, and quantization error.

### Strengths
- The core idea is novel, intuitive, and mathematically principled, offering a clean improvement over STE.
- The method is simple and elegant: a natural “multiplicative counterpart” to STE that captures the relative position of e and q.
- The motivation is well‑presented, with a clear review of VQ‑VAE/STE limitations and helpful illustrative figures (e.g., depicting STE shortcomings and the push‑pull effect).
- The algorithm is clearly formulated and computationally efficient—two Householder reflectors incur no additional training cost.
- Empirical validation is extensive and rigorous across 11 diverse VQ‑VAE/VQ‑GAN training paradigms, showing superior reconstruction metrics, codebook utilization, and quantization error compared to STE.
- The paper is well‑written and well‑structured, making the ideas easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Missing baseline comparisons**: The experiments primarily compare the rotation trick to STE. Stronger validation would require comparison with other methods that improve upon STE, such as Huh et al. (2023), Gautam et al. (2023), and Takida et al. (2022). (Reviewer 1)
- **Hessian idea not adequately explained**: The paper argues that the “Hessian idea” (which adds a curvature term to the STE gradient) does not work, but the explanation is unclear. The analysis in Section 4.3 (push‑pull effect) seems to apply equally to the Hessian idea, yet experiments show it performs poorly. A stronger justification for why the rotation trick succeeds where the Hessian idea fails is needed. (Reviewer 1)
- **Derivation of the rotation matrix (Appendix A.4) appears incorrect**: Remark 3 states the final matrix is \((I-cc^T)(I-bb^T)\), which seems erroneous. The correct composition likely is \((I-2cc^T)(I-2aa^T)\) or \((I-2bb^T)(I-2cc^T)\). The paper should re‑use the \(e, q, r\) notation and include a detailed derivation showing \(R = (I-2cc^T)(I-2aa^T) = (I-2bb^T)(I-2cc^T) = I - 2rr^T + 2qe^T\). (Reviewer 2)
- **Notation and geometric interpretation are confusing**: Section 4.3 allows \(\tilde{\theta}\) to be negative or exceed \(\pi\), while the angle between vectors is normally in \([0,\pi]\). The top panel of Figure 5 defines \(\tilde{\theta}\) in a non‑standard way (e.g., opposite directions yield \(\tilde{\theta}=0\) instead of \(\pi\)). Renaming \(\tilde{\theta}\) to \(\phi\) would avoid misreading. (Reviewer 2)
- **Limited analysis of gradient dynamics**: The effect of the rotation trick is shown only at a high level (codebook usage, quantization error). Figure 4 is a conceptual diagram, not generated from an actual numerical simulation, and only illustrates one gradient update step. A comprehensive analysis over multiple steps showing how codebook vectors evolve with the rotation trick (vs. STE) is needed. (Reviewer 2)
- **Unclear justification and potential instability of the rescaling factor**: The algorithm rescales the gradient by \(||q||/||e||\), which is necessary to map \(e\) to \(q\) but may not be needed for gradient approximation (a pure rotation would already preserve the angle). Moreover, when \(||q||\) is close to zero, this rescaling could hinder gradient propagation. The paper should explain why this scaling is included and how it affects encoder and codebook training. (Reviewer 4)
- **Missing ablation experiments**:
    - Would a single Householder reflection (\(R = I - 2rr^T\)) work? It still preserves the angle (with \(\tilde{\theta} \in [0,\pi]\)) but flips push/pull; since both are beneficial, the result may be similar. An experimental test is requested. (Reviewer 2)
    - Does the rotation trick depend on the coordinate origin? If all \(q\) and \(e\) are shifted (e.g., \(x' = x+10\)), the trick may fail unless the center is subtracted first. This should be tested. (Reviewer 2)
    - Could the rotation trick be combined with STE? For instance, a two‑parameter family \(\tilde{q} = \lambda^\alpha R_{\alpha,\beta} e + \beta (q - R_{\alpha,\beta} e)\) might preserve both magnitude and angle. A small‑scale experiment would clarify the benefit of the pure rotation trick. (Reviewer 3)
- **Lack of discussion of limitations**: The paper does not discuss when the rotation trick might be disadvantageous. For example, what if the rotation matrix \(R\) becomes ill‑conditioned, or the scaling factor \(||q||/||e||\) becomes unstable? Under what conditions might the assumption that the gradient at \(q\) is a good proxy break down? (Reviewer 3)
- **Presentation issues**:
    - The method’s name (“Rotation Trick”) is misleading because the operation is actually two Householder reflections; “Reflection Trick” would be more accurate. The derivation should be moved from Appendix A.4 to the main text for clarity. (Reviewer 2)
    - Figure 2 uses tick and axis label fonts that are too small (illegible at 100% zoom). (Reviewer 3)
    - Typo: extra “a” on line 113. (Reviewer 2)
    - Typo: “a a convex combination” in main text. (Reviewer 4)
    - In Definition 1 (Householder Reflection Matrix), the vector \(a\) should have unit norm. (Reviewer 4)
