## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CBraMod, an EEG foundation model with a criss-cross transformer backbone that separately models spatial and temporal dependencies. It uses an asymmetric conditional positional encoding (ACPE) scheme and time-frequency augmentation. Pre-trained on the Temple University EEG (TUEG) corpus (27,000+ hours), it is evaluated on 10 downstream BCI tasks (12 datasets) and reports state-of-the-art or competitive results against several baselines. Reviewers acknowledge architectural novelty and extensive experiments but raise significant concerns about generalizability claims, data leakage, baseline adequacy, interpretability, and the true foundation-model nature of the approach.

### Strengths
- The criss-cross transformer is a novel architectural contribution that models spatial and temporal EEG dependencies separately via Spatial-Attention and Temporal-Attention, addressing the heterogeneous nature of EEG signals (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- The asymmetric conditional positional encoding (ACPE) dynamically encodes spatial and temporal positional information, potentially improving adaptability to diverse EEG formats (R1, R2, R4).
- Pre-training on the TUH corpus (over 27,000 hours of EEG) is comprehensive and aligns with other large-scale models (R1, R3).
- The explicit time-frequency encoding via STFT provides a useful augmentation beyond temporal-domain convolutions (R1, R4).
- Evaluation spans 10 tasks across 12 datasets, providing a broad assessment (R1, R2, R4).
- The model shows encouraging incremental improvements over baselines BioT and Labram (R2, R4), and the work is a refreshing bridge between LLM techniques and EEG (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Inadequate baselines and missing comparisons**: Several reviewers note that comparisons with recent or task-specific deep learning models are lacking. Missing baselines include BrainWave (R1), EEGNet, ShallowNet, EEGConformer, EEGSym, ATCNet, FBCNet, and TIDNet for motor imagery; USleep, DeepSleepNet, SleepTransformer, XSleepNet, SeqSleepNet, ChambonNet for sleep stage tasks (R4). Using only frequency-based encoders (e.g., BioT) as baselines inflates apparent gains, as they do not capture critical temporal and spatial information for tasks like motor imagery (R4). The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess true novelty and performance gains (R1, R4).
- **Results below state-of-the-art on key tasks**: On the Physionet-MI motor imagery dataset, CBraMod achieves 64.17% accuracy, whereas published methods (EEGSym: 88.6±9.0%, Zoumpourlis et al.: 86.36%, TIDNet: 73.60%) exceed this by over 20% (R4). Similarly, sleep stage classification benchmarks (e.g., SleepEDF+, MASS, SHHS) typically exceed 80% accuracy, and emotion classification on SEED has been outperformed by other methods (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024) under different train-test splits (R4). These gaps raise concerns about whether the model delivers practical BCI utility (R4, citing O. Alkob et al., 2018; Combrisson and Jerbi, 2015).
- **Data leakage from pretraining to evaluation datasets**: The TUEG corpus used for pretraining is a superset of the TUEV and TUAB datasets used for event-type classification and abnormal detection downstream tasks (as indicated on the Temple University corpus webpage). This overlap constitutes data leakage that compromises the validity of those evaluations (R4).
- **Limited adaptability to varying EEG formats despite design goals**: Several reviewers note that the model does not convincingly handle diverse channel configurations. Pretraining uses only 19 common channels, which is a simplistic solution that may over-specialize the model to that layout and limit generalization to datasets with different numbers or positions of channels (R1, R2, R3). The method for adapting to varying channels (including conceptual challenges like volume-conductor effects) is not adequately explained (R1 Q1, R2 Q6, R3 Q4). The claimed advantage of ACPE for diverse formats is asserted but not demonstrated through experiments showing improved performance on datasets with non-standard montages (R3).
- **Discrepancy between pretraining data and downstream tasks**: The TUEG dataset is heavily skewed toward medical/neurological conditions (e.g., epilepsy), while downstream tasks involve cognitive/motor/emotional EEG patterns (R3). This mismatch raises theoretical questions about the model's validity as a general-purpose foundation model (R3). The model may learn features specific to epileptiform activity that do not transfer well to other domains (R3).
- **Insufficient interpretability and feature analysis**: No visualizations or analyses of learned features (e.g., source topographies, frequency bands, channels) are provided. This is problematic because many downstream tasks have well-established neurophysiological hypotheses (e.g., motor imagery relies on mu/beta rhythms over sensorimotor cortex) that the model's embeddings should align with (R1). The contributions of the time-domain and frequency-domain branches are not separated or illustrated: it remains unclear what specific frequency components the frequency branch captures (R2 Q1). Without interpretability, it is difficult to assess whether the model learns generalizable EEG representations or task-specific artifacts (R1).
- **Embedding consistency analysis missing**: The paper does not examine whether learned representations cluster meaningfully by participant demographics (e.g., age, gender) or task type, which would strengthen the claim of learning generalizable features (R1). This analysis is absent.
- **Insufficient ablation studies**: Several architectural components lack rigorous ablation. Missing ablations include: (a) removing spatial or temporal attention separately to assess their individual contributions (R1); (b) replacing ACPE with standard positional encoding to justify the design (R1); (c) ablating the number of transformer layers to understand depth necessity (R2 Q5); (d) isolating the impact of time-frequency augmentation versus pure temporal encoding (R1 Q3); (e) pretraining ablation (e.g., with random weights) should be in the main text (R3). Without these, it is unclear which components drive performance and whether the architectural complexity is warranted (R1, R4).
- **Missing analyses of data efficiency and scaling**: The paper does not explore how performance changes with varying amounts of pretraining data or fine-tuning data, nor whether the foundation model enables learning with fewer labeled samples for specific tasks (R2 Q4). Questions about whether the model exhibits scaling laws with model size or data size remain unanswered (R3 Q1). Loss function curves during pretraining are not shown (R3 Q2).
- **Computational cost not evaluated**: There is no comparison of the computational cost (parameters, FLOPs, or runtime) of criss-cross attention against other attention mechanisms shown in Figure 5 (R2 Q3). This hinders assessment of efficiency versus alternatives.
- **Lack of clarity on fine-tuning and overfitting**: The paper does not specify whether the entire CBraMod or only specific layers are fine-tuned for downstream tasks (R1 Q4). Strategies to prevent overfitting on small downstream datasets (e.g., dropout rates, weight decay specifics beyond pretraining) are not described (R1 Q5).
- **Temporal window size effect unexplored**: Downstream tasks may require different temporal windows, but the paper uses a fixed 1-second window without ablating this choice (R1 Q2).
- **Positional encoding details unclear**: The CNN-based position encoder's ability to capture global versus local positional relationships is not explained, and it is not compared against traditional positional encoding methods (R2 Q2).
- **Motivation as a foundation model and application impact not clearly articulated**: The paper does not convincingly demonstrate how CBraMod facilitates EEG analysis or BCI application beyond reporting metric improvements, e.g., by showing faster fine-tuning or reduced data requirements (R2). The discussion lacks a clear perspective on the value and motivation of foundation models in EEG, and how CBraMod balances model size, computational cost, and performance (R3 Q3). (R3 is more critical on this point, while R2 and R4 view the contribution more positively; the weakness is that the foundation model claims are not fully substantiated.)
- **Dataset selection arbitrary**: The choices for motor imagery (Physionet-MI instead of BNCI 2014-004 or Stieger et al., 2021) and sleep stage (not the most standard benchmarks) are questioned, making it harder to contextualize results against the field (R4).
- **Clarity and scientific depth**: The writing could be improved to better guide readers through findings rather than merely describing tables, and there is insufficient discussion relating the work to other studies in the field (R4). This is a moderate weakness noted by one reviewer.
