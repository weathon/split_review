## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TimeMixer, an MLP-based architecture for time series forecasting that uses multiscale mixing via Past-Decomposable-Mixing (PDM) and Future-Multipredictor-Mixing (FMM). PDM decomposes multiscale series into seasonal and trend components and mixes them in fine-to-coarse (seasonal) and coarse-to-fine (trend) directions. FMM ensembles multiple predictors for future forecasting. Empirical results show state-of-the-art performance on long-term and short-term benchmarks with efficient runtime. Reviewers agree the paper is clearly written and the experiments are extensive, but they disagree on contribution significance and thoroughness. One reviewer (Score 8) finds the motivation solid and the framework coherent, while another (Score 3) criticizes missing baselines and insufficient ablation; the third (Score 6) finds limited innovation and marginal improvements.

### Strengths
- **Clarity and Presentation**: All three reviewers find the paper well-structured and easy to understand. The ideas are explained with intuitive textual descriptions and clear figures (R1, R2, R3).
- **Solid Motivation**: The multiscale analysis paradigm is a classic and well-justified approach to model complex temporal variations. The observation that fine-scale seasonality provides detailed information and coarse-scale trend provides denoised information is insightful (R2, R3).
- **Coherent Conceptual Framework**: The core idea of decomposing into scales and processing trend/seasonality asymmetrically (fine-to-coarse for seasonal, coarse-to-fine for trend) is intuitive, compelling, and empirically effective (R2, R3). The ablation study (Table 5) validates this design (R2, R3).
- **Rigorous Experimental Evaluation**: The paper includes extensive experiments on diverse benchmarks (long-term: traffic, weather, electricity, solar-energy; short-term: M4, etc.) with strong baselines. A new solar-energy dataset is introduced. Code and configurations are provided, enhancing credibility (R1, R2, R3). The ablation study convincingly shows the contribution of individual components (R2). The fully MLP architecture yields low computational and memory costs while achieving strong performance (R3).
- **Efficiency**: TimeMixer demonstrates favorable run-time efficiency due to its simple linear layers (R1, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing Important Baselines**: The paper does not compare against decomposition-based methods (e.g., [1]) or MLP-Mixer baselines (e.g., [2,3]), which are directly relevant given the decomposition and MLP architecture. This omission weakens the experimental validation (R3). Similarly, the paper does not discuss how PDM fundamentally differentiates from frequency-based methods like DFT, and whether moving average decomposition truly captures seasonal/trend components (R2).
- **Insufficient Ablation and Analysis**:
  - The key contribution of asymmetric processing (fine-to-coarse seasonal, coarse-to-fine trend) is only tested on a limited set of datasets in Table 5. More datasets are needed to rigorously prove its efficacy, especially since short-term forecasting (M4) uses $M=1$, which cannot adequately test the multi-scale design (R3).
  - There is no ablation setting where seasonal is coarse-to-fine and trend is fine-to-coarse, leaving the asymmetric design insufficiently validated (R3).
  - The paper lacks deeper analysis of *why* TimeMixer outperforms competitors. For example, spectral analysis or frequency-domain visualizations could show if TimeMixer captures a broader range of frequencies or is more sensitive to relevant bands (R2).
- **Marginal Performance Gains**: On large datasets (traffic, weather, electricity), TimeMixer’s improvement over baselines is slight, often only at the third decimal place (R1). Note that other reviewers acknowledge good performance overall, but this limitation is raised by one reviewer.
- **Limited Innovation**: One reviewer argues that the components (multiscale decomposition, seasonal-trend disentanglement) are already established, and FMM is essentially a linear layer that concatenates features. The main contribution appears to be integration of existing modules (R1). In contrast, other reviewers view the asymmetric mixing as a novel and effective design (R2, R3). Thus there is disagreement on novelty.
- **Naming and Figure Issues**: The labels "Up-mixing" and "Down-mixing" in Figure 2 may be reversed relative to the sampling direction (upsampling yields more points, downsampling fewer) (R1). Additionally, Equation (6) is not aligned with Figure 4: according to the code, the implementation sums (not averages) the predictors, but the equation implies averaging. The figure caption should clarify (R3).
- **Design Choices Need Justification**:
  - The use of average pooling for downsampling is not justified; alternatives (max pooling, learned pooling) could perform better and should be explored (R2).
  - For multivariate datasets, the model does not explicitly design MLPs to capture inter-feature dependencies, which may limit performance (R3).
- **Unclear Ablation Notation**: In Table 4, the meaning of "x" marks in the pasting mixing and future mixing columns is ambiguous; detailed implementation for each marked variant is not explained (R3).
