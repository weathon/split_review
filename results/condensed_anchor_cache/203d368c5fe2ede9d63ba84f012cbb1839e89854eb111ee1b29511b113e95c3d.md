## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes GAGA, a lightweight framework for representation learning on Text-attributed Graphs (TAGs). GAGA reduces annotation cost by selecting only 1% of representative nodes and edges via information density and k-means, then constructs an annotation graph and uses a two-level alignment module to integrate it with the original TAG. Experiments on node classification and link prediction across six datasets claim comparable or superior performance to state-of-the-art methods with minimal annotation.

### Strengths
- The combination of LLMs and TAGs is an important topic for graph research, establishing new benchmarks and challenges.
- The idea of prompting LLMs for broader knowledge and rationale behind edge formation (e.g., explanations for observed graph structure) is interesting.
- Experiments are comprehensive, covering node classification and link prediction, and the ablation study on the amount of labeled data vs. performance is particularly informative.
- GAGA achieves strong efficiency and scalability by annotating only a small subset of data (1%), reducing both time and cost compared to methods that enhance all nodes.
- The two-level alignment module is an innovative design that allows strong generalization with limited annotations.
- The paper explores a new aspect by examining LLM usage from both time expenditure and cost perspectives.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty is incremental**: The approach of annotating only representative nodes via LLMs has been applied in prior work ([1], [2]); the low-cost annotation idea is not new. The use of LLM explanations (line 169) is similarly well-explored.
- **Motivation is not strong**: The claim that existing methods *require* enhancing all nodes is overstated—enhancing a subset is a viable option, and the limitation is not inherent to those methods.
- **Writing and presentation need significant refinement**: The Introduction is overly lengthy. Figure 2 is difficult to interpret. The usage and description of mathematical symbols are chaotic (e.g., line 340). Figure 3 does not convincingly demonstrate the effect of annotation prototype projection.
- **Code is not publicly available**, hindering reproducibility.
- **Experimental improvement is limited**: From Table 2, the performance gains over baselines are small and not significant.
- **Core contribution of representative node selection is unconvincing**:
  - The assumption that information density equals node density is questionable; nodes in low-density areas can also be critical, and defining density thresholds is highly parameter-dependent.
  - Using k-means to select central nodes has high algorithmic complexity, undermining the claimed efficiency for large datasets.
- **Lack of motivation and clarity for key design choices**:
  - The use of vector quantization (VQ) and the prototype matrix \( Z_a \) is insufficiently motivated; it is unclear how \( Z_a \) is learned during training.
  - The rationale behind generating final node representations as a weighted sum of the prototype matrix (from the alignment stage) is not explained.
  - It is unclear whether the alignment stage must be performed per graph or can be pretrained and applied to unseen graphs.
- **Missing experiments and comparisons**:
  - Hyperparameter sensitivity for \(\alpha\) in the loss function is not examined; no guidelines for tuning are provided.
  - The method for selecting which nodes/edges to annotate is not sufficiently described or justified.
  - Experiments use only GPT-3.5; compatibility with open-source LLMs (e.g., Llama 3.2, Qwen 2.5) is not evaluated, and it is unclear which LLM characteristics are most impactful for GAGA.
  - It is unclear whether the two-level contrastive learning introduces additional training of the language models; no clarification is given.
- **Reviewer disagreement**: Reviewer 2 (score 6) is more positive about efficiency and the alignment module, while Reviewers 3 and 4 (scores 3) find the novelty incremental and the implementation unconvincing. Reviewer 1 (score 5) acknowledges the topic’s importance but criticizes the presentation and missing justification for core designs.
