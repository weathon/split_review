## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Lingual-SMoE, a sparse mixture-of-experts (SMoE) model for multilingual machine translation that incorporates language-specific representations into hierarchical routing and automatically adjusts the number of activated experts per language. Experiments on OPUS-100 with 16, 50, and 100 languages show improvements over dense and other SMoE baselines.

### Strengths
- **Innovative motivation and design:** The approach of using linguistic hierarchy (language families) and language complexity (data availability or difficulty) to guide expert allocation is convincing, well-motivated, and original (R1, R2, R3, R4). The hierarchical routing strategy and the adaptive expert allocation (Dynamic Expert Allocation) are intuitive and technically robust (R1, R3, R4).
- **Rich empirical evaluation:** The paper provides extensive experimental comparisons against multiple SMoE baselines (e.g., Switch, GShard, ST-SMoE, Residual-SMoE, Hybrid TaskMoE) and thorough ablations on design choices (linguistically guided routing, language router designs, expert allocation strategies) (R1, R2, R3, R4). The analysis of results is detailed and answers most expected questions (R1).
- **Consistent performance gains:** The proposed method shows consistent improvements across different language counts (16, 50, 100) and data resource levels, e.g., over 5% BLEU gain on OPUS-100 (R3). Results validate the core hypotheses (R2, R3, R4).
- **Well-written and organized:** The paper is easy to follow, with clear explanations of the method and experimental setup (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Concerns about baseline tuning and reproducibility:**
  * Reviewer 2 notes that the dense baseline BLEU scores on OPUS-100 (e.g., M2O avg 25.39 vs. 29.27, O2M avg 19.03 vs. 20.93) are much lower than those reported in Zhang et al. 2021, suggesting the dense model may not have been sufficiently tuned, which casts doubt on all sparse baseline comparisons.
  * Reviewer 4 requests the `sacrebleu` hash for reproducibility and recommends including more modern metrics like chrF and COMET (which correlate better with human judgment).
  * Reviewer 3 asks for model parameter counts and training/decoding time comparisons to verify that gains are not solely due to increased expert capacity.
  * Reviewer 1 asks about inference implications (theoretical trade-offs).
- **Benchmark and domain interference:**
  * Reviewer 2 points out that OPUS-100 has strong domain interference: low-resource languages often come from simpler, repetitive domains (e.g., religious or tech texts), making BLEU scores inflated for low-resource languages and blurring linguistic vs. domain-based routing patterns. The amount of data per language is therefore not a sufficient metric for linguistic complexity/difficulty. Reviewer 2 strongly recommends redoing experiments on a domain-controlled benchmark like a WMT combination.
  * Reviewer 1 notes that in Figure 5 medium- and low-resource languages may be mis-labeled or the trends are counter-intuitive, suggesting deeper analysis is needed.
- **Missing comparisons and baselines:**
  * Reviewer 2 highlights that the two most competitive baselines (ST-SMoE, Residual-SMoE) are omitted from the full 100-language evaluation (Table 2), leaving the competitiveness for realistically large setups unclear.
  * Reviewer 4 notes the paper did not compare with language-specific target routing in the encoder as in Pires et al. 2023, which could improve the baseline (1) and possibly LGR-SMoE. Dense pre-training (Dua et al., 2022; Pires et al., 2023) is also suggested as a beneficial addition.
  * Reviewer 4 asks for per-language-pair scores (not just aggregated by resource group) for all evaluation directions, and suggests a baseline with only target-language-specific decoder routing to match the English-centric corpus.
- **Unsustained claims about language difficulty:**
  * Reviewers 1 and 3 argue that the claim of modeling “language complexity” or “linguistic difficulty” is inappropriate; the method actually models data availability (resourcedness). Reviewer 3 notes lack of experiments or metrics to show the model captures intrinsic grammatical complexity, and no definition of how difficulty is quantified (cf. Figure 2).
- **Missing experimental details:**
  * Reviewer 4: No details on training epochs, checkpoint selection, or overfitting for low-resource languages.
  * Reviewer 4: Unclear how the approach affects zero-shot translation or code-switched data.
  * Reviewer 3: Equation (2) uses α,β=0.05 but implementation details say 0.98; clarification needed.
  * Reviewer 1: Inference overhead (parameter count, memory, speed) from the language router is not benchmarked.
- **Minor writing and formatting issues:**
  * Reviewer 1: Baseline notation is confusing (e.g., LS-SMoE, Hybrid-SMoE); suggest using longer names and organizing in a table.
  * Reviewer 3: Typos in citation formats and grammatical agreement in Related Work; Section 4.2 incorrectly cites “Table 4.2” instead of Table 1.
  * Reviewer 4: The Adam optimizer description uses “α” for learning rate but should refer to β1, β2, or ε, and cite the original paper.
- **Questions overlapping with weaknesses (included as requests for analysis/ablation):**
  * Reviewer 1: Why does adding DEA hurt low-resource languages on OPUS-16 and OPUS-100 but help on OPUS-50? Analysis of per-language expert counts needed.
  * Reviewer 1: Is the expert load imbalance in Figure 4 also a problem for dense models?
  * Reviewer 3: Why is xx-en translation less improved than en-xx, especially for medium/high resources? Analysis required.
  * Reviewer 4: Is the code open-sourced?
  * Reviewer 2: Are baselines reimplemented in the same codebase? (Answered in author response, but not clearly stated in paper.)

**Reviewer disagreement:** Reviewer 2 is notably more critical than the others (score 6 vs. 8, confidence 4 vs. 5/4), particularly regarding baseline tuning and the suitability of OPUS-100 as a benchmark. The other reviewers (1, 3, 4) generally view the empirical validation as strong and the contributions as solid, though they also raise important concerns. Reviewer 4 has a balanced view, noting both strengths and several missing details. Reviewer 3 suggests that the claimed handling of linguistic difficulty is not fully supported by evidence.
