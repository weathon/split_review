## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a lightweight joint guidance module for aligning frozen pre-trained single-modal diffusion models (audio and video) toward joint audio-video generation with minimal computational cost. The guidance is derived from the gradient of a discriminator that distinguishes real audio-video pairs from independently generated fake pairs, and a custom denoising loss stabilizes training. Evaluations on AIST++ and Landscape datasets show improved single-modal fidelity and multi-modal alignment over the MM-Diffusion baseline. However, concerns remain about dataset size, baseline diversity, limited OOD performance, missing computational cost reporting, and confounding from text conditioning.

### Strengths
- Tackles an important and practical problem: achieving multi-modal alignment without training large joint models, using cost-effective adaptation of pre-trained models.
- The joint guidance module is lightweight and model-agnostic (adaptable to different single-modal generators), enhancing broad applicability.
- Sufficient mathematical derivations and proofs are provided to show that discriminator-based gradient adjusts estimated scores toward the joint audio-video distribution.
- The custom loss function (denoising regularization) stabilizes the discriminator gradient during training.
- Extensive experiments are conducted on multiple datasets (AIST++, Landscape) covering both in-domain and out-of-domain settings, with toy data for controlled analysis.
- Results demonstrate improved alignment score (ImageBind) and better generation quality (FVD, FAD) compared to MM-Diffusion, while using only a small number of additional trainable parameters.
- The paper is generally well-written and clearly motivated.

### Weaknesses
- The experiments rely on relatively small datasets (AIST++, Landscape); generalizability to larger, more diverse datasets is not discussed.
- Only one baseline (MM-Diffusion) is tested, making it difficult to confirm the claimed model-agnosticism and general applicability across different pre-trained models.
- No subjective evaluation (e.g., human perceptual study) is provided; objective metrics alone are insufficient to assess perceived quality.
- The computational cost of training the discriminator (including repeated forward passes through frozen audio/video models) is not reported — GPU hours or similar metrics are needed. This cost should be compared to both the pre-training cost and a reasonable jointly trained baseline to substantiate the claim of low cost.
- Text captions provided to both diffusion models act as a weak alignment signal, confounding the evaluation. Experiments with minimal or no shared conditioning are desired to isolate the discriminator’s contribution.
- The description of how the discriminator achieves both semantic and temporal alignment is unclear; more intuitive explanation is needed.
- Improvements in OOD settings are limited. In Table 4, FVD and IB-TV scores for AudioLDM+AnimateDiff with the proposed guidance are even worse than the base models. The provided video files also show poor video quality with audio in OOD settings.
- Missing values in FVD columns of Tables 3 and 4 need explanation or correction.
- The paper mentions Xing et al. (2024) as related work but provides neither quantitative nor qualitative comparison with that method.
- Generalizability to other modality pairs beyond audio and video is not explored; the method’s value if jointly trained diffusion models become widely available is unclear.
- The impact of different base model strengths on guidance performance is not studied; it is unknown whether more powerful pre-trained generators would further improve results.
- The text in Sec 4.2 and 4.3 duplicates descriptions of datasets and evaluation metrics.
- Training stability of the discriminator (common in GANs) is not discussed; potential optimization difficulties should be addressed.
- Inference time overhead introduced by the discriminator is not quantified.
