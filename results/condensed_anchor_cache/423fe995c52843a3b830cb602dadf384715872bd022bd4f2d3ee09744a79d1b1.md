## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces OPTIN, a one-shot post-training pruning framework that removes weights with least impact on intermediate embeddings and final logits using intermediate feature distillation. It is evaluated on NLP, image classification, transfer learning, and semantic segmentation tasks, claiming state-of-the-art results without retraining.

### Strengths
- **Comprehensive experiments** across multiple modalities (vision, language, semantic segmentation) and architectures (transformers, CNN-style networks), ensuring a thorough evaluation. (R1, R3)
- **Strong overall performance** and convincing ablations (especially Table 1) that vet design choices; results are competitive or better than several baselines. (R1, R3)
- **Practical problem**: model compression without expensive retraining, saving computational cost. The paper is **well-written and easy to follow**, with a clear presentation of the method. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Vision model performance**: Without token reduction, OPTIN is inferior to VTP and PoWER for both models tested (Table 3). The strong results of `OPTIN_τ` appear mainly driven by token reduction, suggesting the core weight‑pruning mechanism is less effective on vision tasks than on language tasks. (R1)
- **PTF comparison incomplete**: The method is compared only to an “abridged” version of PTF (Section 4.1). It is unclear whether OPTIN would still outperform PTF if the same tricks PTF uses (e.g., mask rearrangement / mask tuning) were applied. The authors should add a direct comparison with the full PTF pipeline. (R3) 
- **Weak motivation**: The emphasis on “without re‑training” is already present in prior post‑training pruning work; the paper needs to more clearly articulate **what OPTIN adds over the most relevant baseline PTF** and why that difference is important. (R3)
- **Missing experimental details and ablations**:
  - No runtime/overhead is reported: how long does the search take (minutes/hours/days)? Is there significant latency from storing and fetching large activations, especially for vision models? (R3) R1 also asks for estimated pruning time across models/datasets. (R1)
  - The method for determining **layer‑wise sparsity** is not explained; is it global or layer‑wise ranking? (R1)
  - The meaning of “IM‑Dense (After dense embedding layer)” needs clarification. (R1)
  - In Table 1(d), why was λ_c=0.1 chosen when λ_c=0.01 gives better results? (R1)
- **Fairness of comparisons**: In Table 2, PTF results are cited from the original paper; were the pre‑trained checkpoints and fine‑tuning settings kept consistent across all experiments? (R1)
- **Need for more challenging settings**: The method should be evaluated on more complicated network architectures or datasets to demonstrate generality. (R2)
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Figure 1 is too complicated; it could be split or simplified (e.g., illustrate logit distributions as two histograms highlighting difference). (R3)
  - Figure 2 (right) uses a double y‑axis that is confusing and hard to read for color‑blind readers; consider a separate plot. (R3)
  - Minor typos: “(a)Explores” → “(a) explores” (Table 1); “Tab ??” (Section A.6); “Perforamnce” (Section 4.1); excessive italicizing of “without re‑training”. (R1, R3)
- **Minority reviewer viewpoint**: R3 is “very lackluster about the paper” – results are reasonable but motivation and presentation need improvement to be convincing. R1 is more positive but lists several concerns and is willing to adjust their rating if questions are addressed. (R1, R3)
