## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Light-MILPopt, a lightweight large-scale optimization framework for Mixed Integer Linear Programs (MILPs) that uses only a small-scale optimizer and a small training dataset. The framework has four stages: (1) Problem Formulation – MILPs are represented as bipartite graphs and partitioned using the FENNEL graph algorithm; (2) Model-based Initial Solution Prediction – Edge Aggregated Graph Attention Network (EGAT) and Multi-Layer Perceptron predict initial solutions for subproblems, which are concatenated; (3) Problem Reduction – variable dimension is reduced via a confidence threshold and active constraints are predicted using KNN; (4) Data-driven Optimization – neighborhood set updating, active constraint updating, and the REPAIR algorithm iteratively improve solutions. Experiments on four large-scale benchmark MILPs (Set Covering, Minimum Vertex Cover, Maximum Independent Set, Mixed Integer Knapsack) and a real-world case study show that Light-MILPopt outperforms state-of-the-art ML-based frameworks and advanced solvers (Gurobi, SCIP) in objective value within limited time, while requiring less computational resources.

### Strengths
- The paper is clearly structured and well-written (R1, R2).
- Impressive results on large-scale MILPs, which are significantly larger than those used in previous research (R1, R2, R4). The framework obtains better objectives in limited time than other benchmarks (R1, R2).
- Practical motivation: achieves improvement with only a small-scale optimizer and small training dataset, reducing required computational resources (R1, R2, R3, R4). One reviewer (R4) specifically highlights resource efficiency and being the first to tackle extensive MILPs with minimal resources.
- Integrates several advanced ML tools (graph partitioning, attention networks, KNN, REPAIR) to break the problem into subproblems and coordinate between them (R3).
- Effective problem reduction (variable and constraint) that boosts efficiency (R1, R4).
- Performance confirmed through rigorous testing on benchmarks and a real-world case study (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Missing comparisons and related work**: The paper lacks detailed comparison with previous ML-based optimization approaches and their empirical results (R2). For example, prior work using fixed thresholds for variable reduction is not discussed (R2).
- **Lack of ablation studies**: Recommended experiments are missing, e.g., directly feeding the initial solution from Model-based Initial Solution Prediction to Gurobi (R3); evaluating the effect of each module separately (R3, R4).
- **Feasibility guarantees**: The concatenation of predicted solutions from subproblems may yield an infeasible initial solution for the original problem; it is unclear which part of the framework handles this (R1). More generally, most tested problems admit trivial feasible solutions; the method’s performance on harder feasibility problems (e.g., set partitioning) is not demonstrated (R3).
- **Generalization and scalability**: The framework is trained on only 1% of large benchmark MILPs; evidence of generalization to unfamiliar or more complex MILPs is insufficient (R4). One reviewer (R4) raises concerns about scalability as problem size and complexity increase, and questions whether efficiency wanes.
- **Complexity and practical applicability**: The advanced problem formulation and division techniques may require specialized expertise, hindering practical application (R4). The simplification strategies in problem reduction might neglect essential problem aspects, compromising solution integrity (R4).
- **Need for deeper analysis and motivation of modules**: More insight is needed on design choices, e.g., why EGAT with half-convolutions is used and what alternatives were considered (R2). The motivation for the four-stage structure (stages overlap; contributions 1 and 2 could be combined) (R3).
- **Dual bound and optimality**: The framework focuses on the primal side and does not help improve the dual bound or certify optimality (R3). One reviewer (R4) questions whether solutions are global optimum or risk local optima.
- **Parameter sensitivity**: The choice of variable proportion parameter α is not analyzed; very small values (e.g., 0.01) may lead to subproblems of large scale – effect unknown (R3). Robustness to different sizes and complexities is unclear (R4).
- **Terminology and presentation issues**: The use of “large-scale/small-scale optimizer” is unconventional; more precise terms like “advanced MILP solver” and “lightweight solver” are suggested (R2). The paper has grammar and stylistic issues that reduce accessibility (R3). Minor typos: Section 2.4 punctuation, Figure 2 uses “MIP” inconsistently, Table 2 caption “Comparsion”, page 13 “feautres”, Table 3 “denots” (R3). Definition (1) on page 2 is actually ILP, not MILP (R3, R4).
- **Inflexibility**: The framework assumes the same number of variables/constraints as training data; handling slightly different problem sizes is not addressed (R1).
- **Benchmark fairness and dataset issues**: Comparisons with advanced solvers like Gurobi may be limited to certain metrics; Gurobi’s parameter settings could be tuned, leading to potential disputes (R4). The chosen benchmarks (Set Covering, etc.) may not represent the diverse spectrum of real-world MILPs; using MIPLIB is recommended (R4). Detailed comparative analysis of why baseline solvers underperform is missing (R4).
- **Training data and real-world analysis**: The effect of increasing training data on performance is not explored; is there a saturation point? (R4). The real-world case study may not be exhaustive; distinct constraints and challenges are not fully considered (R4).
- **Convergence**: Scenarios where the framework fails to converge efficiently are not identified (R4).
