## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies combining sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) with instruction tuning for LLMs. It shows that MoE models benefit more from instruction tuning than dense models: without instruction tuning, MoE models underperform compute-equivalent dense models on downstream tasks, but when instruction-tuned (using the FLAN dataset) they consistently outperform dense models. The most powerful model, Flan-MoE 32B, surpasses FlanPaLM 62B on four benchmarks while using only a third of the FLOPs. The paper provides extensive experiments and ablations across model scales, number of tasks, expert selection, number of experts, etc.

### Strengths
- **Thoroughness and comprehensiveness:** The paper presents an extensive collection of experiments and ablations across model scales, number of instruction-tuning tasks, expert selection methods, number of experts, and dataset mixtures. Reviewers found the scale and thoroughness impressive (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- **Timely and relevant:** Fine-tuning large-scale pre-trained MoE models for specific tasks is challenging; this study directly addresses that problem (Reviewer 3).
- **Plausible central claim:** The finding that instruction tuning is crucial for MoE models to outperform compute-equivalent dense models is well-supported by the data and provides high-quality empirical evidence (Reviewer 4). Most reviewers found the results convincing.
- **Acknowledges limitations and mitigations:** The paper acknowledges that MoE models can overfit and demonstrates the use of an auxiliary loss to mitigate this (Reviewer 1).
- **Well-documented findings:** Results cover a range of MoE sizes and include discussions of limitations and failure cases (Reviewer 3).
- **Strong empirical performance:** In most settings, the combination of instruction tuning and MoE shows strong performance over comparable dense models across various architectures (Reviewer 2).
- **Originality:** The significance of instruction tuning for MoE has not been extensively studied in this manner; the paper fills a gap (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of clear motivation and insight into mechanisms:** The motivation for combining MoE with instruction tuning is not clearly explained. There is little analysis into *why* instruction tuning helps MoE succeed (e.g., improvements in routing, expert specialization) or why direct task-specific finetuning of MoE fails (overfitting vs. other factors). The interaction with routing strategy is not explored (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- **Training/inference cost ambiguity and confound:** The claim that improvements do not come from increased computational resources or memory requirements is confusing and insufficiently supported. The instruction-tuning stage uses a large set of datasets and clearly adds training cost, but no direct training cost, inference time, or memory comparison is reported. Several reviewers note that it remains unclear whether the performance gains are due to the extra training budget rather than the combination itself. A detailed discussion of training/inference trade-offs is requested (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4). A minority reviewer (Reviewer 3) finds the conclusion about the necessity of instruction tuning unconvincing specifically because this confound is not addressed, while others find the claim plausible.
- **Contradiction with prior work:** The paper states that MoE models underperform dense models on downstream tasks without instruction tuning, but prior work (e.g., arxiv 2112.10684) has shown MoE outperforming compute-equivalent dense models on supervised finetuning. This discrepancy is not discussed, which weakens the paper’s narrative (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited generalizability:** Only one pre-trained MoE architecture (ST‑MoE‑32B) and one instruction-tuning dataset (FLAN) are used. It is unclear whether the findings generalize to other MoE backbones or other instruction-tuning datasets. The paper does not analyze the impact of different instruction-tuning datasets on final performance (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Presentation and clarity issues:** Several parts of the paper are confusing or contain errors:
  - The phrase “without increasing inference cost” is misleading because increased memory usage is also a cost (Reviewer 1).
  - The explanation of the discrepancy between pretraining and task-specific finetuning applies equally to dense and sparse models, creating confusion (Reviewer 3 question 1).
  - The difference between “FLAN‑MoE” and “MoE” in Section 4.1 is not defined (Reviewer 3 question 4).
  - Figure 1 (right) includes T5 and Flan‑T5 bars that are independent of the number of experts, which is confusing (Reviewer 4).
  - Table 1 omits Switch and GS performance at the 32G FLOP scale (Reviewer 4).
  - Figure 3 and Figure 6 lack adequate labeling (model sizes, expert utilization measure) (Reviewer 4).
  - Appendix Figure 7 has no line labels to distinguish dense from MoE (Reviewer 4).
  - Various typos: extra space before period (“specialized sub-networks .”), “Average zero performance” (missing “shot”), “issue may stes” → “issue may stem”, “future works” → “future work”, “yield” → “yields” (Reviewer 4).
  - Additional grammatical issues noted in explanation and discussion (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing experimental details:** No analysis of different instruction-tuning datasets is provided; it is unclear if benefits are specific to FLAN. The learning efficiency experiment (Figure 7) is not usable because lines are unlabeled. The model architecture is not fully described beyond ST‑MoE‑32B (Reviewer 3, 4).
