## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes IDGNN, the first implicit neural network for dynamic graphs, designed to overcome over-smoothing and capture long-range dependencies. The authors prove well-posedness (unique fixed-point solution) and introduce a bi-level optimization training algorithm that achieves up to 1600× speed-up. Experiments on real-world datasets show superiority over state-of-the-art baselines in classification and regression tasks.

### Strengths
- IDGNN is the first implicit model for dynamic graphs, filling a clear gap in the literature (all reviewers).
- The model outperforms state-of-the-art methods on several real-world datasets, with experimental validation (R1, R2, R4).
- The proposed bi-level optimization training algorithm significantly reduces training time (e.g., 1600× on Brain10, ~10× on other datasets) while maintaining competitive performance (R2, R4).
- The theoretical analysis includes proofs of well-posedness and unique fixed-point solution; synthetic experiments directly demonstrate the ability to avoid over-smoothing and capture long-range dependencies (R2, R3).
- The motivation to mitigate the dilemma between long-range dependencies and over-smoothing in dynamic graphs is sound and interesting (R3).

### Weaknesses
- The claim that implicit networks inherently avoid over-smoothing lacks rigorous theoretical justification; convergence alone does not guarantee distinct node representations – a key symptom of over-smoothing (R1).
- The assumptions in Lemma 2 are insufficiently explained: it is unclear under what conditions Equation 3 has a unique embedding \(z\) and whether uniqueness holds for any initialization. The notation “let \(W_{j+k}\) denote \(M_i\)” is confusing and needs a clear definition (R1, R3).
- The connection between Lemma 2 and the bi-level problem (8) is not clearly established. Equation 8 imposes a constraint only on the last timestamp, raising questions about the necessity of Lemma 2 and the role of \(M_i\). The approximations used in solving the bi-level problem are not justified, and their impact on convergence and optimality is not analyzed (R1, R3).
- The main theorem appears to be a direct consequence of prior work (Gu et al., 2020) (R4).
- The literature review is insufficient; it should discuss more recent implicit GNN works such as CGS, EIGNN, and USP (which also uses a bi-level optimization) (R3).
- The model is designed for discrete-time *cyclic* dynamic graphs, but the experimental datasets do not exhibit cyclic properties. The synthetic experiments may not correctly demonstrate long-range dependency capture because the architecture allows direct propagation from time step 1 to time step \(T\) through backpropagation, potentially bypassing meaningful temporal dynamics. A more thorough analysis varying temporal distance is needed (R4).
- The 1600× speed-up is only observed on the Brain10 dataset; improvement is much lower on other datasets. An analysis of why the algorithm performs well on Brain10 and a scalability study with respect to number of nodes and time steps are missing (R4).
- The experimental evaluation lacks results on common datasets like QM9 and TUdataset (R1).
- Experiment descriptions are unclear: Table 3 reports per-batch memory usage and runtime, but the batch formation method for a single graph (e.g., random node selection or neighbor sampling) is not specified (R3).
- The paper lacks a comprehensive discussion of the model’s limitations and practical implementation challenges (R2).
- Interpretability of the IDGNN model and how it captures temporal dynamics is not addressed (R2).
- The computational complexity and scalability of the single-loop training algorithm are not detailed (R2, R4).
- Several notation issues noted: (1) \(t\) is used for both layer depth and timestamp; (2) transpose superscript \(T\) conflicts with timestamp \(T\); (3) Omega (\(\Omega\)) is not defined (page 3, third line); (4) \(\ell\) in \(\nabla L(\omega)\) is undefined; (5) references for datasets are missing; (6) typos: \(l\) and \(d\) both used for node attribute dimension, extra column in matrix in Theorem 1; (7) Table 3 is missing a caption (R3, R4).
- Questions raised: Why is \(V\) shared across time while \(W\) is not? What makes the optimization “bi-level”? Additional background on bi-level optimization would be helpful (R4). Is the convergence guarantee necessary for implicit models, given that DEQ and MDEQ work well empirically without it? More explanation of the Hessian-vector product and whether modern autodiff can handle it is needed (R3).
