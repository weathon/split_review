## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an evaluation framework for assessing graphical perception of large multimodal models (LMMs) on charts, using the theory of graphical perception. The framework automatically generates tasks and evaluates responses at chart, visual element, and pixel levels. Experiments on several LMMs (GPT-4o, Gemini Pro, LLaVA, etc.) reveal limitations: poor generalization across chart types, difficulty understanding fundamental visual elements, and failure to cross-reference values within a chart. The framework and dataset will be released.

### Strengths
- The problem is well-known but lacks benchmarks that target specific perception failures vs. QA; the paper is a significant contribution to the field (Reviewer 2).
- The framework conducts highly detailed analysis of why MLLMs perform poorly on chart tasks and offers three interesting insights (Reviewer 1).
- Diversity of tasks covers a wide range of needs and reasonably evaluates predicted normal usage (Reviewer 2).
- Good mix of open and closed models (Reviewer 2).
- The pipeline data creation approach and new dataset will benefit future research (Reviewer 1).
- Thorough analyses assess impact of combinations of visual elements, chart types, and annotations on the same data (Reviewer 5).
- Highlights several limitations that can guide future LMM designs (Reviewer 5).
- Paper is well-organized and clearly written (Reviewer 1).
- Systematically answered three research questions (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- Heavy reliance on GPT for evaluation, especially in an area where LLMs are known to struggle, and lack of human evaluation (Reviewer 2).  
- Lack of verification of Vega-Lite outputs; some existing datasets have wrongly generated charts, potentially throwing subsequent steps into question (Reviewer 2).  
- Dataset details are vague; more comprehensive statistics would improve clarity (Reviewer 3).  
- Limited contribution: prior work (e.g., [1]) already includes similar descriptive tasks; a detailed comparison with other graphical perception benchmarks is needed to clarify core differences (Reviewer 3).  
- Lack of in-depth analysis:  
  * Section 4 states that adding redundant visual elements hurts performance – a counterintuitive observation needing more analysis (Reviewer 3).  
  * The paper underscores annotation impact but the role of visual elements remains unclear; analyzing samples accurately classified without annotation could provide insights (Reviewer 3).  
  * Should further explore the impact of reasoning about relationships among multiple visual elements vs. individual elements (Reviewer 1).  
- The proposed benchmark is toy-level and not diverse enough; only limited insights are gained (Reviewer 4).  
- All three findings are not surprising – many prior works (MMVP, Blink) have already revealed poor perception of LMMs, and reasoning in charts is even harder (Reviewer 4). Given that LMMs lack vision reasoning is obvious, the paper should propose methods to help LMMs (e.g., visual-COT for cross-reference); the paper does not attempt to solve any of the identified problems, limiting its contribution (Reviewer 4).  
- Only four LMMs tested, trained on different data and resolutions, making direct comparison impossible; should evaluate more LMMs to enhance reliability (Reviewer 5).  
- Section 5.1 lacks essential details: threshold for measuring LMM’s ability to identify important chart regions, how ground-truth regions are annotated, and how correctness is checked (IoU threshold?) (Reviewer 5). The method may have flaws – e.g., removing a label like “2010” may not harm understanding (Reviewer 5).  
- The paper gives far more claims than results can support. Example: line 289 claims lightweight LMMs have weaker generalization on charts lacking numerical cues, but only one lightweight model was tested (Reviewer 5).  
- Do MLLMs perform differently on similar tables across different domains (sports, news, etc.)? For the same table, do different instructions significantly impact understanding of visual elements? (Reviewer 1)  
- Any insights on how findings translate to real or more complex charts? (Reviewer 2)  
- Would results differ when using a more/less popular tool compared to Vega-Lite? (Reviewer 2)  
- How can position, length, and size be combined in a bar chart (line 319)? The toy chart seems similar to a bar chart using only position and length; no example in Figure C2 demonstrates a chart combining position, length, and size (Reviewer 5).  
- In Figure 5, the region containing 0.827 shows large correspondence with the response – please explain this phenomenon and whether it is consistent with your analyses (Reviewer 5).  
- Typo: line 179 “LLaVa” should be “LLaVA” (Reviewer 5).
