## Merged Review

### Summary
The reviews are mixed. Reviewers 1, 3, 4, and 5 rated the paper low (5 or 6) while Reviewer 2 gave a high score (8). All acknowledge that the paper studies a timely and under-explored topic—privacy risks of Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT). Reviewer 2 finds the evaluation extremely thorough, methodical, and valuable for practitioners. The other reviewers praise the breadth of experiments but raise significant concerns about presentation clarity, limited scope of privacy attacks, generalizability to larger models and different secrets, over-claiming the number of models evaluated, and insufficient theoretical grounding. Several concrete missing experiments, comparisons, and ablations are requested.

### Strengths
1. The paper addresses a timely and important research question about privacy vulnerabilities in PEFT, which is widely adopted for deploying LLMs (R1, R2, R3, R4, R5).
2. Extensive and detailed experiments covering 4 PEFT algorithms (LoRA, adapters, prompt-tuning, prefix-tuning), two base models (GPT-2 and GPT-2 XL), two datasets (Enron, MIMIC-III), and multiple configurations (number of tunable parameters, secret position, secret duplication) (R1, R2, R3, R5).
3. The main finding that PEFT methods offer significantly better privacy guarantees than full-parameter fine-tuning is a valuable insight for the ML community (R1, R4).
4. Reviewer 2 particularly appreciated the methodical approach: breaking down “what are the privacy risks of PEFTs” into modular components studied in a structured manner, with clear orthogonal metrics (Exposure, Perplexity, DP budget) and meaningful experimental variables.
5. Surprising findings are highlighted, e.g., increasing the number of tunable parameters does not necessarily increase memorization in PEFT (R2).
6. The integration of Differential Privacy within PEFT and the comparison of DP libraries (FastDP) in the appendix provide useful guidance for practitioners (R2, R3).
7. The analysis of the effect of secret position on memorization gives a unique perspective (R4).
8. The paper offers practical recommendations for different stakeholders (practitioners, developers, researchers) and can serve as a basis for developing privacy-preserving strategies (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
1. **Claimed number of language models is exaggerated.** The paper claims evaluation over “100 language models,” but only 2 base models are used (GPT-2, GPT-2 XL). The 100 variations (5 datasets × 4 methods × 5 seeds) do not constitute 100 distinct language models; they are variations under different fine-tuning conditions. R4 requests modifying the claim to specify the number of base models (2) and clarifying the 100 variations (excluding full fine-tuning, it should be 80). R4 also notes that repeating an experiment 5 times does not create a completely new language model.
2. **Limited scope of privacy attack.** The paper relies on only one type of data extraction attack (secret guessing with known context). To match the broad title, the authors should also evaluate membership inference or model inversion attacks, which are commonly used to assess language model vulnerabilities (R3). R5 similarly notes that focusing only on a “secret guess” task may not provide a sufficient basis for a comprehensive assessment; other scenarios like information retrieval could be explored.
3. **Generalizability concerns.** Reviewer 2 finds the secrets chosen arbitrarily; the variable “type of secret” is not studied with the same depth as other variables. For instance, the hypothesis that differences between Enron and MIMIC are due to secret types could be tested by using an email address in MIMIC and a name in Enron. Other secret types (e.g., less common names, longer secrets, SSN) should be examined. Reviewer 5 doubts findings generalize to larger models like LLaMA series, and the larger scale of PEFT parameters in such models could alter conclusions. R4 requests additional experiments with models such as Pythia, Gemma, or LLaMA3.
4. **Definitional issues.** The adaptation of Memorization in Definition 3.1 is unnecessary and confusing because all experiments use the Exposure metric from Carlini et al. 2019 without modification (R1, R4). R4 asks why a new definition is needed if the quantification metric is the same.
5. **Counterintuitive or missing explanations.**
   - R1 and R4 question the statement that “a slight increase in perplexity (0.01–0.15) … is too small to result in a significant increase in the exposure.” Since lower perplexity is better, they suggest the intended meaning is “increase in perplexity does not lead to an increase in exposure.” R1 requests an undertrained baseline matched to PEFT perplexity levels to validate that such performance loss does not affect exposure.
   - In Figure 3, Exposure with DP is worse than without DP; R1 asks for an explanation.
   - In Figure 3, why is Exposure higher with 1 insertion than with 500 insertions? (R1)
   - Table 1: R1 asks whether it is constructed using 1 insertion (likely), and requests the relationship between Exposure and intermediate insertion values (between 1 and 500).
   - Figure 5 has non-monotonic dependencies (e.g., r=32 unexpectedly low in top left, r=16 low in bottom left, pt=16 between 32 and 64 in bottom middle). R1 asks for hypotheses.
6. **Presentation quality issues.** 
   - In Figures 1, 4, 5 (and appendix figures), axis scaling choices cause most points to cluster in one corner, making interpretation difficult. For Figures 4 and 5, one high perplexity point obscures differences among others. R1 suggests using log-scale (i.e., Cross-Entropy Loss) instead of raw Perplexity.
   - Multiple plots in figures have differences only explained in captions, forcing readers to repeatedly refer to the description. R1 suggests placing key features (like epsilon values) as titles above individual plots.
   - The DP privacy-utility trade-off (Figure 4) would be more compelling as plots with multiple points per method (Perplexity vs. Exposure with different epsilons) rather than separate scatterplots per epsilon with different scales (R1).
   - Figure 4’s axes scales are very different; epsilon=0.1 is likely too strong; what about epsilon=1? (R2)
   - Reviewer 3 notes spelling errors (“measyured” in Section 4.4), reference inconsistencies (Table 1 cited as Table 3 in Sections 4.2 and 4.3), and missing data support for prompt-tuning analysis in Section 4.4 (no direct data or appendix reference).
   - Reviewer 4 notes typos: “confirmes” (line 163), “fllows” (line 184), “find” (line 242), and caption of Figure 5 should say “datasets” not “models.”
   - Reviewer 4 points out quotation marks around “PRIVACY” in the section title are confusing (R2).
7. **Missing experiments and ablations.**
   - DP experiments in Figure 3 are only shown for prefix-tuning. R4 and R2 request running the same experiments on prompt-tuning, LoRA, and adapters to fully support claims about DP and secret position.
   - Figure 12 in the appendix shows epsilon vs. memorization for GPT-2 XL with only two datapoints. R2 asks for GPT-2 results and more epsilon values.
   - R1 requests explicit examples of dataset records after secret insertion (e.g., in the appendix).
   - Hyperparameter selection (e.g., adapter rank 32, prompt/prefix tokens 32/16, LoRA rank 8) lacks justification. R4 asks whether these were chosen via optimization or based on prior works.
   - Reviewer 2’s hypothesis about LoRA’s rank acting as an information bottleneck: the paper does not evaluate how easily secrets can be memorized depending on closeness to pre-training data (e.g., compare when a name already appears in pre-training vs. not).
   - R2 asks for the value of delta in DP training and whether privacy unit is token-level or record-level.
8. **Utility metric over-reliance.** The paper primarily uses perplexity, which may not reflect performance on specific downstream tasks (e.g., question answering, sentiment analysis). R3 and R3’s Q2 suggest adding task-specific accuracy or F1 scores for a broader view of the privacy-utility trade-off.
9. **Obvious findings.** Some findings, such as that stronger privacy (smaller epsilon) reduces data extraction success, are obvious (R4). The statement about “even with a sufficiently small epsilon, data extraction can be rendered ineffective” is tautological unless the authors meant the opposite (R4 Q3).
10. **Lack of theoretical analysis.** The definitions of memorization and privacy risk lack theoretical depth. R5 requests more rigorous theoretical explanations and analyses.
11. **Dataset size.** The fine-tuning datasets are small (~14k records; Enron is 13,399 records). R2 wonders about the impact of dataset size (e.g., using full Enron with 600k records) and the rationale for making Enron exactly 13,399 to match MIMIC (line 184 incorrectly states “13,431”).
12. **Concurrent work.** R4 points to a concurrent work on privacy auditing of language models (noted as [b]) that should be acknowledged, though its focus is on full fine-tuning and membership inference.
