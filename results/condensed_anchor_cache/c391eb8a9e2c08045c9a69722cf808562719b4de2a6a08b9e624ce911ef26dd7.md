## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes FreeLM, a method that mixes supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data with pretraining data using a unified binary classification format and an iterative training scheme. The authors claim that this approach is “fine-tuning-free” and that a small GPT-2 sized model (0.3B parameters) outperforms much larger models like GPT‑3 and InstructGPT (175B) on several language understanding tasks. Reviewers strongly question these claims: they note the method still requires gradient updates on task training data (i.e., it is not truly fine-tuning-free), it lacks novelty (essentially multi-task fine‑tuning combined with pretraining), the evaluation is narrow and often inappropriate for decoder-only LMs, and the reported comparisons to GPT‑3/InstructGPT are unfair because those models did not have access to the same training splits. Reviewers’ confidence ranges from fairly certain to absolutely certain; scores are mixed (1, 3, 1, 3). One reviewer (R2) is skeptical of the SOTA claims; R1 and R3 are strongly critical, while R2 and R4 see some value in the idea but point to major missing baselines and analysis.

### Strengths
- The core motivation—improving general-purpose LMs beyond pretraining by leveraging small, high-quality datasets—is on the right direction (R1, R4). Merging pretraining and fine-tuning into a single stage could be a more efficient training approach (R3).
- The paper studies whether auto-regressive language modeling ability is impacted by adding SFT signals and finds minimal negative effect; this is an encouraging sign (R1). It also sets out to study cross‑task generalization, reporting an interesting effect on COPA (R1).
- Unifying NLP tasks into a common format may aid evaluation (R2), and the figures are appreciated (R2, R4). The paper is mostly well‑written with intensive experiments and analysis (R4); however, one reviewer (R3) finds the writing clarity poor and needing improvement.
- Injecting task bias into the pretraining stage is seen as an interesting idea (R4). The fact that FreeLM, despite being much smaller, surpasses larger models on GLUE downstream tasks is noted (R3) but later disputed as an unfair comparison.

### Weaknesses
- **“Fine-tuning-free” label is misleading.** The method still requires gradient steps on the training split of the evaluation tasks (or on very similar tasks), so it does not eliminate the need for a fine-tuning dataset. This makes comparisons to zero-shot/few-shot results on GPT‑3/InstructGPT unfair because those models did not have access to those training sets (R1, R2, R3). Even if training from scratch, the term “fine-tuning-free” is inaccurate as the model undergoes parameter updates (R2). Straightforward fine‑tuning of GPT‑2 on the same tasks likely outperforms FreeLM, which the authors fail to acknowledge as a limitation (R2).
- **Lack of novelty.** The approach is essentially multi‑task fine‑tuning with the pretraining task mixed in. No baseline is provided for: (a) using the vanilla fine‑tuning objective with the same pretraining/fine‑tuning scheduling, (b) multitask parallel training with both pretraining and vanilla fine‑tuning tasks, or (c) multitask fine‑tuning (learning‑free when applied to other tasks) (R1, R4). A much simpler approach—simply mixing pretraining data/task at fine‑tuning stage—might produce the same effect (R1). The “teacher iterative” method is not clearly distinguished from existing prompt‑based learning methods that also merge pretraining and prompt‑fine‑tuning (R3).
- **Narrow and inappropriate evaluation.** The GLUE benchmark is typically used for encoder‑only models (BERT‑like) or encoder‑decoder models; decoder‑only LMs generally score lower (e.g., ~80 on MNLI vs. >90 for state‑of‑the‑art encoder models). FreeLM should be evaluated on established zero‑shot benchmarks for LMs (e.g., T0‑Eval with 12 evaluation datasets) or on generative tasks (MathQA, HumanEval, MBPP, OpenBookQA) (R3). The evals are too narrow; for comparison with GPT‑3/InstructGPT, the authors should test few‑shot and zero‑shot performances on unseen tasks where no training split of a similar task is available (R1). The unified data formulation is classification‑heavy and may not capture generation‑heavy use cases (e.g., “write me a poem”) (R1).
- **Missing analysis on generation quality and scalability.** The paper does not provide perplexity scores or numerical comparisons on NLG benchmarks; only a few unconvincing examples are given, and human evaluation is needed to substantiate generation improvements (R2, R4). There is no comparison with in‑context learning as the model scales; in‑context learning is another training‑free method that benefits greatly from scaling, and FreeLM’s scalability is not addressed (R4).
- **Unfair comparison to GPT‑3/InstructGPT.** The comparisons lack details on how prompts were designed for these baselines; improper prompting can significantly impact performance (R2). A fairer baseline would be fine‑tuning an open‑source LM (e.g., LLaMa) on GLUE (R3) or creating a pretraining‑fine‑tuning baseline (R3, R4). Claims of SOTA are not accurate (R2).
- **Writing needs improvement.** The language is not always fluent or succinct, sometimes reading like a translation (R3). The paper uses embellished terms (“teacher signal”, “language signal”) which obscure the fact that the method is just co‑training (R2).
