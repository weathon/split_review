## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Tilted SAM (TSAM), a generalization of Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) that uses exponential tilting to reweight local minima. TSAM is parameterized by a tilt hyperparameter $t$ and reduces to SAM as $t\to\infty$. The authors prove that TSAM’s objective is smoother than SAM’s and explicitly favors flatter minima as $t$ increases. An algorithm inspired by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling with N=1 perturbation per step is proposed. Empirical results on CIFAR-100, ImageNet, DTD, and GLUE benchmarks (using ResNet18, Vision Transformers, DistilBert) show improved test performance and flatter minima compared to SAM and ERM. Reviewers agree the idea is intuitive and the paper is well-written, but they raise concerns about the clarity and strength of the theoretical generalization analysis, the practical cost (3–5× SAM’s cost plus hyperparameter tuning), sensitivity of $t$, lack of wall‑clock timing and ablation studies, and insufficient experimental scale and detail.

### Strengths
1. **Novel and flexible framework** – TSAM’s exponential‑tilting approach reweights multiple points in the neighborhood of local minima instead of only the worst‑case solution, allowing a trade‑off between worst‑case and average‑case scenarios.
2. **Theoretical support** – The paper proves that TSAM’s objective is smoother than SAM’s (easier to optimize) and that it explicitly favors flatter minima as $t$ increases. Generalization bounds under standard hypotheses are provided.
3. **Empirical gains** – TSAM achieves better test performance than SAM and ERM across a range of image and text tasks (CIFAR‑100, ImageNet, DTD, GLUE) and models (CNNs, Transformers). Competitors were tuned. The HMC‑inspired algorithm is a practical contribution.
4. **Clarity and presentation** – The paper is well‑written and easy to follow, with theory and experiments supporting the main claims.

### Weaknesses
1. **Conflicting explanations for TSAM’s advantage** – One claim is that TSAM improves generalization by considering a broader range of local solutions, while another is that it favors flatter minima as $t$ increases. Since TSAM converges to SAM as $t\to\infty$, this suggests SAM would ideally be best if optimization were perfect; TSAM’s practical improvement may stem from a trade‑off between flatness preference and optimization ease rather than from a fundamentally better objective. (Reviewer 1)
2. **Weak theoretical generalization guarantee** – The bounds in Section 3.3 show TSAM can have smaller upper bounds on linear population error, but (a) the bound for SAM is worse than for ERM, contradicting SAM’s known practical success, undermining the comparative claim; (b) the analysis relies on an upper‑bound argument and does not directly connect the tilted objective to improved generalization. The proof of Theorem 2 is a standard application of Hoeffding’s inequality and a lemma from Aminian et al. (2024). (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and lack of guidance** – The tilt parameter $t$ is sensitive (see Figure 3: flatter minima do not always improve generalization) and requires careful tuning per dataset. No clear strategy or intuition for selecting $t$ is given, making the method less practical (added grid‑search cost). (Reviewers 3, 4)
4. **High computational cost** – TSAM requires 3–5× the computational cost of SAM per iteration (multiple perturbation evaluations). The paper reports only training epochs, not wall‑clock time. The extra cost of tuning $t$ and the sampling parameter $s$ further reduces practicality. An analysis of runtime complexity and a comparison with baselines in wall‑clock time is missing. (Reviewers 1, 2, 3, 4)
5. **Marginal improvements on transformer tasks** – In the second table of Table 1 (Vision Transformers on DTD), TSAM’s improvement over SAM and ERM is marginal. The added cost might not be justified, especially compared with efficient SAM variants (e.g., ESAM). (Reviewer 1)
6. **Missing ablation for HMC sampling** – The algorithm sets N=1 for computational efficiency. An ablation study in the appendix examining larger N (e.g., N=5,10) would help validate this choice and reveal performance‑vs‑speed trade‑offs. (Reviewer 2)
7. **Inadequate experimental scale and detail** – No experiments on ImageNet‑1K (a standard benchmark in SAM‑related work). Multiple architectures per dataset are not compared. The noise level of Noisy CIFAR‑100 is not specified. Table 1 results lack indication of number of runs, mean/std, and statistical significance. (Reviewer 4)
8. **Unjustified choice of sharpness metric** – Section 5.2 uses $\mathrm{E}[L(\theta^\star + \epsilon)]$ and $\mathrm{var}[L(\theta^\star + \epsilon)]$ as sharpness measures without justification. Standard metrics like Hessian spectra, maximum eigenvalue, or $\lambda_1/\lambda_5$ (used in SAM and VASSO) are not reported, making comparison with existing work difficult. (Reviewer 4)
9. **Minor technical/clarity issues** – Meaning of “(1)” at line 281 is unclear. The role of $\hat{\theta}$ in Algorithm 2 (why not compute $\epsilon$ directly?) needs explanation. Line 475 may contain a typo: when $t=0$, TSAM should reduce to the average weight perturbation, not SAM. (Reviewer 4)
