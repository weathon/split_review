## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes an evaluation framework for generative robotic simulations (task sets with demonstrations) using three axes: quality (scene alignment and task completion scored by LLMs/VLMs), diversity (text embedding dissimilarity and world model prediction loss on trajectories), and generalization (zero-shot success rate of a diffusion policy trained on multiple generated tasks). The framework is applied to tasks from RoboGen, GenSim, and BBSEA, and the automatic evaluations are compared to human judgments.

### Strengths
*   The problem of systematically evaluating generative simulations is timely and under-explored, and the paper takes a sensible first step toward a quantitative evaluation standard (Reviewers 2,4).
*   The three proposed axes—quality, diversity, and generalization—are all relevant and important for assessing whether a generated task set is useful for robot learning (Reviewers 1,2,4). Reviewer 2 additionally notes that splitting into sub-criteria is pertinent.
*   The approach leverages LLMs/VLMs to automate evaluation, which is a practical way to reduce the high cost of manual assessment (Reviewer 3).
*   The authors conduct human evaluations and report correlations, which adds some evidence that automatic scores align with human judgment (Reviewers 2,3). Some VLM/LLM choices show promising correlation, though others show less alignment (Reviewer 3).
*   The analysis exposes concrete issues in existing generators, notably poor generalization in RoboGen and BBSEA data, highlighting a need for higher-quality data (Reviewer 2).
*   The task diversity evaluations, while less meaningful than other axes according to one reviewer (2), are still interesting.

### Weaknesses
*   **Sim-to-real gap not addressed.** Multiple reviewers (1,4) note that the framework omits evaluation of how well simulated tasks transfer to the real world. Reviewer 4 also raises the importance of the difficulty distribution of generated tasks for policy learning, which is not measured.
*   **Diversity metrics are incomplete or insufficiently justified.** Reviewer 1 points out that diversity only considers text and trajectory, ignoring visual input, which should be included. Reviewer 3 questions the use of world model prediction loss as a proxy for task diversity: high loss could stem from task complexity or model limitations rather than diversity, and a single task with varied dynamics (e.g., table rearrangement) could also yield high loss. The paper lacks rigorous justification for this metric.
*   **Generalization and diversity overlap conceptually.** Reviewers 1 and 3 question whether generalization is truly distinct from diversity. Reviewer 1 suggests investigating the correlation between diversity and generalization scores, and possibly unifying the two metrics. Reviewer 3 asks for a clear definition of “generalization” in the context of generative simulation.
*   **Inconsistent or weak correlation with human evaluations.** Reviewer 2 notes that Figure 3 does not show a consistent correlation between the proposed automatic metrics and human evaluations. Reviewer 4 asks for quantification of deviation (e.g., why scene alignment scores correlate better than task completion scores in Figures 3 vs 4).
*   **Calibration of VLM/LLM scores.** Reviewer 2 states that the alignment score given by mLLMs is not calibrated; some calibration would make results more reasonable.
*   **Generalization evaluation uses a questionable policy.** Reviewer 2 argues that using an unconditional diffusion policy is less convincing than a task-conditioned multi-task policy (e.g., BAKU). Reviewer 4 additionally notes that different task groups are used to measure generalization across baselines (Table 6), making it unfair to conclude that GenSim generalizes better—the variability in task group definitions affects conclusions.
*   **Scene alignment and realism claim are overreaching.** Reviewer 3 asks whether scene alignment is based on robot trajectory (lines 348-350) and why video is used instead of image, given that trajectory quality is not factored in. Reviewer 4 argues that scene alignment measures consistency between description and scene, not necessarily “realism,” and demands justification for the term.
*   **Lack of comparison to existing evaluation methods.** Reviewer 4 asks how this framework improves over prior work such as Self-BLEU or embedding similarity scores already used in RoboGen. The contribution is unclear if these earlier metrics already capture diversity.
*   **Human evaluation details are insufficient.** Reviewer 4 notes that only 10 tasks were evaluated; the number of evaluators, their expertise, and rating guidelines are missing, undermining reliability. Reviewer 3 also asks how humans performed the evaluation (e.g., 1-5 scale, ranking).
*   **Fairness across baselines is compromised.** Reviewer 3 observes that each baseline uses different tasks (lines 318-319), so intrinsic task biases may affect LLM/VLM scores; a standardized task set is needed for fair comparison. Reviewer 4 echoes this for generalization evaluation.
*   **Clarity and methodological detail.** Reviewer 4 calls for clearer definitions of “task quality,” “task diversity,” and “generalization ability,” and more detail on metric calculations. Specific ambiguities include: how text embedding $e_i$ is formally defined (line 264); whether the 8 images extracted from the trajectory (line 246) are random or evenly spaced and whether they include the final frame; which VLM/LLM is used in Section 4.1.2 (Figure 4) and whether results are consistent across models; and whether the VLM limitation “lack of access to top-view rendered data” (line 354) refers to GenSim’s VLM or the evaluator’s VLM.
*   **No new benchmark or dataset is proposed.** Reviewer 1 notes the paper only analyzes existing benchmarks but does not introduce a new one to resolve the identified issues.

**Minority positive view:** Reviewer 2 (score 8) is substantially more positive, finding the three axes “reasonable,” the task diversity evaluations “interesting,” and the human evaluation alignment beneficial. However, this reviewer also raises the above weaknesses on correlation, calibration, and policy choice. The other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 5) are more critical, particularly about conceptual overlap, missing comparisons, insufficient justification, and incomplete evaluation criteria.
