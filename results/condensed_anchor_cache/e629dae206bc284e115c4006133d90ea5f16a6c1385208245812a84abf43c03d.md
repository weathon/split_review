## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes two modifications to training algorithms based on Koopman mode decompositions of the weights: Koopman Gradient Acceleration (KGA), which projects gradients onto stable singular vectors of a DMD approximation of the Koopman operator, and Koopman Weight Masking (KWM), which sets components with negligible unstable Koopman components to zero. The methods are tested on fully connected and convolutional networks up to 75M parameters (ResNet-50), showing smaller training and test errors compared to vanilla SGD and other baselines. The approach is framed as modeling weight trajectories via DMD and using a look-ahead to speed convergence. However, there are substantial concerns about theoretical justification, cost versus performance, experimental design, and clarity of presentation.

### Strengths
- Focus on numerical experiments to demonstrate KGA and the speed-up obtained from KWM (R1).
- Draws connections between optimization and dynamical systems viewpoints (R1).
- Builds well on prior touchstone papers (e.g., Dogra and Redman 2020), showcasing both the usefulness and limits of those early insights (R2).
- Proposed method consistently outperforms on accuracy for the chosen tasks, supported by theoretical development (R2).
- Generic enough to scale to many problems (R2).
- Scale of experiments (75M parameters) compares very favorably to older works (~10k parameters) (R2).
- Proposed algorithms seem technically sound (R4 – minority positive view; score 5).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical justification.** The methods are heuristics; it is unclear whether they lead to convergence in non-convex settings, and they could introduce oscillations or suboptimal regions. There is no clear connection from the gradient projection to the underlying optimization landscape (R1).
- **Misuse of dynamical systems terminology.** Terms like “stable manifold” are used incorrectly; the demarcation into “stable”, “unstable”, and “neutral” Koopman modes is hand-wavy, and projecting onto a subset of singular vectors of a DMD approximation is arbitrary (R1).
- **DMD approximation issues.** The DMD matrix is only an approximation of the Koopman operator on L², dependent on snapshots and time window. Projecting onto its range introduces an unaccounted representation error, and the subsequent masking further alters the optimization dynamics in an uncontrolled manner (R1).
- **Cost vs. performance comparison insufficient/missing.** Figure 7d lacks the SGD curve (blue invisible) so cost cannot be assessed; the only relevant table (Table 1, A.3) is in the appendix instead of the main body. Memory footprint and running time are not fairly compared against SOTA optimizers (R2, R4).
- **Incomplete literature review.** Missing early works: [1] Dietrich, Thiem, Kevrekidis (2020) on Koopman operator of algorithms; [2] Dogra (2020) on dynamical systems and neural networks (R2). Also, the introduction and related works, rooted in dynamical systems, do not fit the actual focus on identifying important weight directions via SVD (R3).
- **Unsubstantiated assumptions.** Both KGA and KWM assume that important weight directions change slowly over iterations/epochs (U matrix reuse, mask reuse for an entire epoch). This requires theoretical or empirical validation (R3).
- **Limited baselines and unfair comparisons.** Only Adam, SGD, and variants of the proposed approach are compared; many other optimization algorithms and training techniques exist (R3). The proposed method is similar to using a higher learning rate for specific weight components, yet the same learning rate is used for baselines, potentially leading to an unfair comparison (R3). Adam is sometimes used as a fall-back when KGA does not help, and no consistent improvement over SOTA is demonstrated (R4).
- **Similarity to prior SVD/pruning methods not explored.** The method resembles training a pruned network (KWM) with higher learning rates (KGA). Comparison of ideas with prior works that apply SVD or eigen decomposition for pruning/interpretation is needed to clarify novelty (R3).
- **Unclear modeling of training dynamics.** The SVD is performed on weights of a single timestep, not across multiple timesteps, so the method may not capture training dynamics involving interactions over time (R3). Section 3.3 (KWM) is not clearly shown to outperform simpler baselines like Delta masking or Norm masking (R4).
- **Presentation issues.** Figure 3 is poorly formatted (symbols almost impossible to read on the circle; font sizes too small) (R2). Citation style differs from other papers (R3). Methods and experiments are not completely described; no code is provided (R4).
- **Unanswered questions that overlap with criticisms / missing experiments.** Why does gradient projection accelerate optimization given that the dynamics are completely changing? (R1) What is the justification for projection onto “stable” singular vectors? (R1) Do the different columns of matrix W_i correspond to time snapshots? (R1) What happens when training continues for more epochs with KGA? (R1) How does Figure 3 relate to Figure 12, and why does α=10 place a vector outside the unit circle? (R4) In Equation 14, how are λᵢ indexed and what are “steady” modes? (R4) Can the method be compared to the Hamiltonian-solver Koopman boosting approach of Dogra and Redman 2020? (R2)

**Note on reviewer disagreement:** One reviewer (R4) gave a score of 5 and found the algorithms technically sound, contrasting with the majority score of 3. Despite that positive assessment, R4 also noted missing descriptions, unfair comparisons, and unclear baselines.
