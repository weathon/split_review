## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper reformulates peer review as a multi-turn, long-context dialogue with author, reviewer, and decision maker roles. It constructs the ReviewMT dataset of 30,854 papers and 110,642 reviews from ICLR (since 2017) and NeurIPS (since 2021). The authors propose evaluation metrics and assess various LLMs under this setting.

### Strengths
- Timely and solid motivation; more automated peer review work is needed given the volume of submissions. (R1)
- The dataset is a great resource for future work; an unprecedented large-scale ML/AI review dataset; valuable for the research community. (R1, R2, R3)
- Extensive empirical studies on both open-source and closed-source language models (e.g., GPT-4, GLM, Qwen, Gemma, Yuan, Baichuan2) in peer review tasks. (R2)
- Framing the review of scientific papers as a multi-turn long-context dialogue is intriguing and mirrors real-world scenarios. (R3)

### Weaknesses
- **Dataset construction lacks technical novelty.** The dataset is simply downloaded and processed from OpenReview, with its format directly derived from ICLR/NeurIPS review documents. Fine-tuning uses the LLAMA-FACTOR library on models under 10B parameters. Deeper analysis and modeling for this task are missing. (R2)
- **Scope is narrow.** Only NeurIPS and ICLR conferences are used. The authors acknowledge this limitation, but it needs addressing (e.g., adding other venues). (R1)
- **Evaluation pipeline is unclear and incomplete.**  
  - Which model role-plays the author, which the reviewer, and which is the final judge? (R1)  
  - What does each model take as input? Raw text? With or without bibliography? Appendix? Are formulas, tables, and figures processed? Marker tools handle mathematical formulas poorly, and not all chosen models are multimodal, so figure information is likely missing. (R1, R2)  
  - The average token count in the ICLR 2024 training set exceeds 20,000, but used models have context limits (QWEN: 8192, Gemma/Gemma2: 8192, Yuan: 8192, Baichuan2: 4096) – it is unclear how they handle long texts during training/generation. (R2)  
  - The evaluation protocol is confused: it is not specified which responses are evaluated by the automatic metrics – those of authors, reviewers, or decision makers. If only reviewers are evaluated, why are other roles omitted? (R3)  
- **Evaluation metrics are unreliable and insufficiently diagnostic.**  
  - Metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, validity, human evaluation of consistency) only assess text similarity to reference reviews. ROUGE is especially problematic because verbose responses can have low ROUGE yet still be high quality. (R1, R2)  
  - The experiments do not reveal critical issues in LLM-generated reviews: exaggerated text, distorted score distributions, inability to judge novelty (overlooking existing papers), contradictions with paper conclusions, severe hallucination and bias. Conclusions are superficial (e.g., “fine-tuned models perform better than non-fine-tuned ones, GPT-4 performs well, GLM is second”). (R2)  
  - Human evaluation is positive but peer review is inherently subjective and costly, limiting scalability. (R3)  
- **Human evaluation details are missing.** Five expert reviewers were recruited, but who they are, how they were recruited, their evaluation process, and their expertise are not specified. The test set covers diverse ML topics, so it is uncertain whether these five reviewers are qualified to evaluate all directions. (R2)
- **Missing dataset analysis and baselines.**  
  - Train/test split details are not reported (in-domain vs out-of-domain, token and score distributions). (R1)  
  - Which models are more likely to accept a paper? Provide a baseline where sections from different papers are randomly combined to test whether LLMs can detect inconsistency or still assign an acceptable score. (R1)  
  - Prompts used for GPT-4 and other models in zero-shot are not shown. (R1)  
- **Dialogue formulation oversimplifies real-world review.** The task uses only four dialogue turns (review, rebuttal, final review, decision), while real OpenReview discussions involve many more utterances. The formulation should be extended to capture iterative exchanges. (R3)  
- **Presentation and writing issues.**  
  - Typo: double inverted commas at lines 211–214. (R1)  
  - Figure 2 should mask author names to anonymize the dataset. (R1)  
  - Figure 3c reports only the mean; variance or quartiles are needed. (R1)  
  - Figure 3d gives a gross accept/reject breakdown; a year-wise split should be provided in the appendix. (R1)  
  - Introduction line 53: “generating static reviews” is unclear – the dataset structure does not inherently capture “non-static” reviews. (R2)  
  - Introduction line 72 overemphasizes “a novel perspective on the complete peer-review process” – the ICLR/NeurIPS process already follows a multi-reviewer-with-AC structure. (R2)  
  - Model specifications inconsistent: ChatGLM3 described as “with a default selection of 6B to 9B parameter” (line 362) but “with 130 billion parameters” (line 1022). YUAN2 only has 2B, 51B, and 102B versions, not 6B–9B – affecting reproducibility. (R2)  
  - The first paragraph (lines 32–47) is not relevant to the research topic and can be deleted. (R3)  
  - Section 2.1 in Related Work is unrelated and should be removed. (R3)  
  - Figure 4 could be moved to the appendix. (R3)  
  - Prior works such as SEA (Automated Peer Reviewing in Paper SEA) should be discussed in Related Work. (R3)  
- **Reviewer disagreement.** Reviewer 2, while optimistic about potential impact, became more critical after discussion (rating dropped from 6 to 5.5), particularly questioning the validity of human evaluation and the limitations of the current experimental setup. Reviewer 3 gave low presentation (1) and contribution (2) scores. (R2, R3)
