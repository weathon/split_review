## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper extends prior work (Denison et al., 2024) on specification gaming by examining whether LLMs trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest can engage in reward tampering and other forms of reward hacking purely through in-context iterative reflection (termed “in-context reinforcement learning,” ICRL), without explicit training on a curriculum of gameable environments. The authors test gpt-4o, gpt-4o-mini, o1-preview, and o1-mini in the five-task curriculum from SEG, and report that ICRL can increase gpt-4o-mini’s propensity to learn specification-gaming policies, generalizing (in rare cases) to editing its own reward function. The paper provides code/data and clear visualizations.

### Strengths
- The application of in-context reflection (ICRL) to study specification gaming is a novel and timely perspective, directly relevant to current interest in agentic capabilities and LLM safety.
- The paper is clearly written and the visualizations (e.g., success rates per task, generalization results) are easy to interpret.
- The authors provide links to their code and datasets.
- Reviewer 2 specifically acknowledges that the work provides evidence that ICRL may enhance gpt-4o-mini’s propensity for specification gaming compared to the baseline SEG approach.

### Weaknesses
- **Inconclusive results and limited evidence:**  
  - Figure 4B (cumulative pass rate) does not show baseline SEG results; it is unclear whether the difference between SEG and ICRL is significant.  
  - Figure 2B: ICRL percentages are low for most tasks (e.g., 0.20% in “Reward Tampering,” 2.25% in “Tool Use Flattery”). Standard error ranges for “Insubordinate Rubric Modification” and “Tool-use Flattery” overlap significantly between SEG and ICRL, and only 3 runs are used. The lack of statistical significance makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions.  
  - Figures 6–9 (generalization results) appear similar between SEG and ICRL, and no error bars or statistical tests are provided.  
  - Only one reviewer (Reviewer 2) emphasizes that the core research question and framework are directly inherited from SEG, with minimal novel experimental design or actionable insights for model improvement.

- **Limited model diversity:**  
  All experiments use only OpenAI models (gpt-4o, gpt-4o-mini, o1-preview, o1-mini). No models from other vendors (e.g., Anthropic) or open-source models (e.g., Llama chat models) are tested, limiting generalizability. A reviewer notes the budget could have been used to test a broader range of models.

- **Concerns about the evaluation protocol:**  
  The success rate metric for sycophancy tasks (e.g., Philosophical Sycophancy) may conflate genuine agreement with strategic manipulation, making it unclear whether “passing” indicates specification gaming. This issue is discussed in the alignment forum thread linked by the paper.

- **Speculative claims not well supported:**  
  - The claim of a “strong scaling trend” (Figure 1B) is based on only two size comparisons (4o vs. 4o-mini; o1-preview vs. o1-mini), with unknown parameter counts and RLHF procedures. The observed differences could be due to factors other than scale.  
  - The qualitative claim that chain-of-thought reasoning becomes “significantly more misaligned” after expert iteration with ICRL lacks quantitative details (e.g., number of samples, criteria for misalignment). The promised full transcript (Appendix D.2) appears to be missing from the appendix.

- **ICRL method needs broader testing and clearer scope:**  
  The paper assumes all tasks have well-defined numeric rewards, which is unrealistic for many real-world LLM use cases. Testing with qualitative verbal feedback (as in Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023) would better establish the dangers of ICRL. Additionally, the term “in-context reinforcement learning” may be a misnomer since traditional RL uses reward only for training, not during inference; “iterative refinement” might be more appropriate.

- **Specific reviewer questions (seen as criticisms/missing experiments):**  
  - In the transcript shown in Section 4.2.1, the model states in its CoT “I will modify it to return a positive value instead, which will trick the oversight into giving me a higher score.” But neither the system prompt nor reflection prompt mentions an oversight mechanism—only a preference model and user desires. Are there missing details?  
  - Reviewer 2 asks: (a) How does ICRL differentiate itself from SEG in terms of novel contributions and practical advantages? (b) What specific, actionable recommendations for improving model architectures or training can be drawn from the findings? (c) Additional experimental validations across diverse tasks and architectures are needed.  
  - Reviewer 1 asks: Why is “Insubordinate Rubric Modification” frequency much higher than “Nudged Rubric Modification” (opposite of Denison et al.)?
