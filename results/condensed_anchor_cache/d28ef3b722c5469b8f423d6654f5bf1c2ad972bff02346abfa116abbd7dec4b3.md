## Merged Review

### Summary
Four reviewers rate the paper 6/10 and recommend acceptance. They acknowledge the paper’s clear exposition, novel combination of sparsity and misspecification in RL, and near‑matching upper/lower bounds. All note the computational intractability (exponential in sparsity \(k\)) as a major weakness, and several question the practical relevance and clarity of certain technical details. There is minor disagreement: one reviewer gives a contribution score of 3, while the other three give 2.

### Strengths
- Writing is clear; the novelty and differences from the prior misspecified bandit work (Dong & Yang, 2023) are explicitly stated. (R1)
- The lower bound construction is interesting: it first demonstrates a suboptimality gap with no samples using binary trees, then modifies the instance so that the reward signal is exponentially sparse, forcing brute‑force search. (R1, R4)
- The algorithm is simple, follows a traditional elimination scheme, and the proof sketch is easy to grasp (uses Hoeffding’s inequality). (R1)
- Results are fresh and counter‑intuitive; it is interesting that standard bandit algorithms cannot be extended to this RL setting. (R1)
- Nearly matching upper and lower bounds (up to polylog factors) are provided. (R1, R3, R4)
- The work makes a reasonable contribution to RL theory; the guarantees are non‑trivial and somewhat surprising. (R2)
- The lower bound also proves that the linear scaling with \(H\) is optimal. (R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Computational intractability.** The algorithm uses an \(\epsilon\)-net over candidate \(\theta_h\), searching over all possibilities; sample complexity and runtime scale as \((d/\epsilon)^k\), which is exponential in the sparsity \(k\). (R1, R3, R4)
- **Algorithm is essentially brute‑force.** It is structurally similar to OLIVE (Jiang et al., 2017) and eliminates only one parameter per level per iteration, raising concerns about practical efficiency in high‑dimensional settings. (R1, R4)
- **Niche problem setting.** The paper is likely only interesting to researchers in core RL theory; broader impact is unclear. (R2)
- **Hard instance uses exponentially large state space.** The paper does not discuss whether similar lower bounds hold with a smaller state space. (R3)
- **Error guarantee linear in \(H\).** While optimal, this linear dependence may be a significant limitation in long‑horizon tasks. (R4)
- **Upper bound techniques are narrow.** If the parameters were shared across steps \(h\), a simple \(\epsilon\)-net enumeration would suffice; the non‑trivial part is removing an exponential dependence on \(H\) when parameters are not shared. (R2)
- **Unclear definitions.** The definition of \(\Delta(\cdot)\) is missing; it is unclear why parameters are restricted to the sphere rather than the ball; how \(s_0\) is sampled is not specified. (R3)
- **Missing analysis of parameter choices.** The optimal sizes of \(\epsilon_{\text{net}}\) and \(\epsilon_{\text{stat}}\) are not given; the meaning of “\(\epsilon\)-optimal” (\[V^*-V(\pi) \le \epsilon\]) implies that to get error \(\epsilon'\) one must set \(\epsilon_{\text{stat}}=\epsilon'/H\), introducing an \(H^2\) factor in the sample complexity. (R1)
- **Confusion in sample complexity expression.** Line 438 writes \(H^2\cdot m\) including \(\epsilon_{\text{stat}}^2\) in the denominator; it should be \(H\cdot m\)? (R1)
- **Does the method exploit sparsity beyond the cardinality of the \(\epsilon\)-net?** (R3)
- **Is the exponential dependence on \(k\) necessary?** (R4) – an open question, but raised as a weakness.
- **Can similar guarantees be obtained beyond linear function approximation?** (R2) – not a criticism, but noted as a natural open question.
- **Minor disagreement in perceived contribution:** Three reviewers assign contribution score 2, while one assigns 3, reflecting somewhat different views on the significance of the algorithmic novelty. (R1 vs. R2, R3, R4)
