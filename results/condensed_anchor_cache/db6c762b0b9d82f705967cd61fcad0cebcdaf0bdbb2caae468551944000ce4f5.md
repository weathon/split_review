## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces the Trajectory Map (TM), a matrix of cosine similarities between parameter checkpoints across training steps, to study the directionality of neural network optimization trajectories. The analysis shows that directional movement saturates early in training, influenced by weight decay and momentum, and that subsequent training exhibits oscillatory micro-level behaviors. Based on this directional redundancy, the paper proposes a “decapacitation hypothesis”—that freezing most parameters after directional saturation can preserve performance—and provides some supporting experiments across image classification and language modeling.

### Strengths
- **Novelty of the Trajectory Map concept**: The TM provides a fresh perspective by translating high-dimensional parameter evolution into a tractable visualization and quantitative tool (R1, R2, R4). Several findings are novel, e.g. momentum and weight decay increasing rather than decreasing variability in optimization paths (R1).
- **Empirical breadth**: Experiments span multiple architectures (ResNet, VGG, Pythia LLMs) and hyperparameter configurations, including both vision and language domains, which strengthens the generality of the observations (R1, R2, R3). Inclusion of the Pythia language model is a good extension beyond the over-parameterized image classification setting (R3).
- **Potential practical impact**: The decapacitation hypothesis opens up avenues for reducing computational cost by freezing parameters after directional saturation, with a clear connection to methods like LoRA (R1, R2). The paper’s disambiguation between distance traveled in parameter space and straightness of the trajectory is a useful conceptual contribution (R1).
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is clearly laid out with helpful color choices and succinct writing (R1). The simple quantity chosen for analysis is well motivated and fleshed out (R3). The TM transforms extremely high-dimensional information into a manageable form suitable for visualization and statistical tools (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Dependence on learning rate schedule**: The observed saturation of directional movement coincides exactly with learning rate decay steps, raising the possibility that results are an artifact of step-wise decay rather than a general property of optimization. Additional experiments with smoother schedules (cosine annealing, constant LR) are needed to verify generalizability (R2, R3). Appendix results showing the effect of LR schedule do not fully resolve this (R3: “directional movement saturates exactly when LR is decayed – confounding factor”).
- **Lack of random walk baseline in main text**: The analysis would be significantly stronger if all similarity values were reported relative to a random-walk baseline with decaying step size. Currently, it is not clear how much added similarity/dissimilarity is present in training trajectories versus a random walk, and the non-relative TM for random walks is not shown for comparison (R1). The random walk baselines (Appendix C) should use the same empirically determined step sizes and consider all combinations of momentum/weight decay (R1).
- **Missing training/validation loss and accuracy**: The paper does not report any loss curves or accuracy values for the main experiments. Without these, it is difficult to interpret whether directional stability corresponds to a network that is successfully fitting the data or one that is stuck in a local minimum (R2, R3). A stronger connection between the proposed MDS metric and performance metrics (e.g., statistical correlation tests) is needed (R2, R3). The claim “Directional measures predict…” is too strong given the limited evidence (R3).
- **Weak connection to existing literature and loss landscape understanding**: The motivation for studying the TM is sparse; the paper should explicitly contrast its approach with prior trajectory analyses (e.g., linear interpolation studies, muP layer-specific evolution) and connect to practical generalization measures (R3). R4 states that the new definitions do not build strong connections with existing optimization knowledge, limiting their usefulness for motivating new methods. Contributions would be strengthened by discussing prior work on tuning only normalization layers in finetuning (e.g., arXiv:2312.11420) and providing a clearer contrast with other approaches.
- **Decapacitation hypothesis – naming and evidence**: The name “decapacitation” is odd and may mislead; the phenomenon is better described as “saturation of directionality” (R3). The empirical support in Section 6 is weak: patterns in Figure 8 do not align clearly between left and right panels and seem contingent on the percentage of iterations frozen (R4). No experiments with LLMs (Pythia) are shown for the decapacitation setting (R3).
- **Sampling rate issues**: The per-epoch sampling rate may significantly affect measurements of adjacent-step angles (Figure 4). For example, the measured angles for no momentum/no weight decay could differ if measured every iteration vs. every epoch. Higher-frequency sampling in the first few epochs would clarify these results (R1).
- **Theoretical simplifications in Section 4.2**: The model assumes a stable Hessian and uses Edge of Stability analysis for gradient descent, while the experiments use practical SGD. This discrepancy is not addressed, and the simplifications may not be justified (R3).
- **Missing figure references**: Figure references on lines L1090, L1120, and L1126 are absent (R2).
- **Limited hyperparameter exploration**: Only two settings each for momentum and weight decay are tested, making it difficult to generalise claims about their role (R3, R4). The impact of different optimizers (SGD vs. Adam) is not compared even in the LLM experiments, where Adam is used (R4).
- **Confounding of model size vs. performance in MDS analysis**: The correlation in Figure 7a between apex angle and model size is straightforward, but Figures 7b and 7c fail to capture a monotonic trend; the implications of this failure are unclear (R4). The complexity measure in Section 5 is not clearly defined (R4).
- **Generalizability concerns**: The primary finding of directional redundancy may be an artifact of projecting high-dimensional parameters into a low-dimensional cosine similarity metric, which could lose information about whether later iterations are truly redundant (R4). The paper does not provide theoretical support for the claim that redundancy implies the ability to freeze parameters (R4).
