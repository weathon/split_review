## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper applies equivariant graph neural networks (GNNs) to predict the stiffness tensor (fourth-order) of architected lattice metamaterials. The model builds on the MACE architecture and adds a positive semi-definite (PSD) layer to enforce conservation of energy and SE(3) equivariance. A dataset of structure-property relationships for strut-based lattices is generated and released. The model is compared against Crystal Graph Convolution (CGC) and NNConv baselines. An application to material design is demonstrated.

### Strengths
- There is less work focused on higher-order tensors beyond first-order tensors (e.g., coordinates, velocity, force); studying a fourth-order tensor represents a new application area. (R1)
- The positive semi-definite layer is an interesting new tool for physics-based machine learning. (R2)
- The paper has a clear motivation: designing a GNN that fulfills inductive biases for lattice architected metamaterials. (R3)
- The proposed approach is clean and easy to follow. (R3)
- The figures are pretty. (R1)
- The evaluation scheme is quite solid and the different training methods are sound. (R2)
- The paper contains all important information to follow the general physics background, even for non-material-design experts. (R2)

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: The model is a direct utilization of the MACE architecture with an added PSD layer. The claim of “high-order rotational equivariance” is not an original contribution but belongs to the original MACE model. (R1, R3, R4) Predicting a fourth-order tensor whose symmetry is already maintained by existing models (TFN, PAINN) does not constitute a theoretical innovation in equivariance. (R1)
- **Missing equivariance proofs for the PSD layer**: The steps of arranging \(n(n+1)/2\) entries into matrix \(M\) and the function \(\rho\) (eigenvalue decomposition and eigenvalue manipulation) lack formal proofs of equivariance. Different arrangements could affect transformation properties under rotation. The claim that “if the overall model had been equivariant with respect to vectors in \(U\), it will remain equivariant after eigenvalues are made positive” is not trivial and requires rigorous mathematical justification. (R1)
- **Potential reduction of representational capacity**: Even if the PSD layer is equivariant, the function \(\rho\) may not be injective (e.g., \((\pm \Lambda)^2\) giving the same result), leading to an unfaithful group representation. Increased errors of CGC+ve, NNConv+ve, and MACE+ve in Table 1 may be a manifestation of this decline in representation ability. (R1)
- **Lack of comparison with finite element (FE) methods**: The paper mentions FE modeling but does not provide direct comparisons of computational cost (wall time) or accuracy. Such comparisons are essential to demonstrate practical advantages. (R1, R2, R3) 
- **Insufficient baselines**: Experiments lack comparison with state-of-the-art geometric GNNs such as TFN, NequIP, SCN, GMN, and SE(3)-Transformer. (R1)
- **Missing runtime comparisons**: No runtime comparisons are reported, especially since e3nn-based models are known to be slow. (R2)
- **No out-of-distribution (OOD) experiments**: OOD testing is claimed as a reason for building models on physical principles but is not performed. (R2)
- **Important details relegated to appendix**: Definitions of stress and strain tensors, their relation to the stiffness tensor, why the stiffness tensor can be represented as a matrix, and how positive definiteness is ensured are only given in the appendix. (R2)
- **Unclear/improper use of some terms and citations**: “Edge shifts” is used without definition or reference. (R4) The suffix ‘lb’ in training methods is not defined. (R4) Clebsch–Gordan coefficients are mentioned without proper citation. (R4) Numbers in parentheses next to “Young’s modulus,” “shear modulus,” and “Poisson’s ratio” on page 17 are left unexplained. (R4)
- **Unclear illustrations and descriptions**: The claim that energy conservation equates to a PSD tensor is only in a footnote; it should be explained in the main text. (R4) The choice of the optimal ‘ve’ method (squaring matrix A) is mentioned late in the results section rather than in the methodology. (R4) Only absolute errors are reported without ground truth magnitudes or relative errors, making it hard to assess accuracy. (R4) It is unclear whether Figure 1b shows ground truth or predictions. (R4)
- **Background and related work is repetitive**. (R2)
- **Flaws in Finite Element Method (FEM) introduction**: The paper states FEM has “~10⁹ elements” without reporting computation time for that scale; GNNs cannot handle such scales on a single GPU, making the comparison unfair. (R4) The statement that FEM ensures force equilibrium is incorrect; it is the underlying PDE that ensures this. (R4) FEM itself does not ensure PSD properties; this is ensured by the constitutive model. (R4) The paper incorrectly claims that FEM is rotationally equivariant without special treatments. (R4)
- **Ablation studies missing**: The paper discusses limitations of previous Cholesky/eigenvalue decomposition methods for enforcing PSD but provides no experimental verification of this point. (R3)
- **Limited practical demonstration**: The paper could show how the model helps in other related tasks or downstream tasks beyond predicting the stiffness tensor. (R3)

**Minority contrast**: One reviewer (R2, score 8) is substantially more positive, viewing the application as novel and the evaluation as solid, while noting fewer weaknesses. In contrast, R1 and R4 (scores 5 and 6 after revision) find the contributions incremental and the paper less clear, with R4 initially recommending rejection but later raising the score to 6 after the rebuttal clarified motivation and writing.
