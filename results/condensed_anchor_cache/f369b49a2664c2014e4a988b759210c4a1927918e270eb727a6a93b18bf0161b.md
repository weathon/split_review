## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes the Stochasticity-controlled Diffusion Bridge (SDB) framework, which extends the design space of diffusion bridge models by introducing stochasticity control (SC) in three components: the transition kernel, the sampling SDEs, and the base distribution. SDB includes score reparameterization and a specially designed discretization scheme to mitigate singularities during training and sampling. Experiments on image-to-image translation tasks show that SDB achieves up to 5× faster sampling than DDBM, lower FID scores, and improved diversity (measured by a newly proposed Average Feature Distance metric). The paper claims that controlling stochasticity in the transition kernel improves image quality, in the sampling SDEs improves efficiency, and in the base distribution improves diversity.

### Strengths

1. **Generalized framework and identification of existing model shortcomings.** The SDB framework provides a more unified view of diffusion bridge models (DBMs) by explicitly identifying the impact of noise in sampling SDEs, transition kernels, and base distributions. The SC mechanism introduces flexibility and precise control over noise at various stages, addressing limitations of conventional diffusion models. *[R1, R4]*

2. **Mathematical soundness and stability improvements.** The paper ensures sampled distributions follow a mathematically sound process; proofs for Lemma 1 and Theorem 3 validate that stochastic control maintains desired marginal distributions over time. The proposed score reparameterization and discretization sampling scheme are designed to avoid singularity and instability during training and sampling, offering novel insights into stochastic diffusion processes. *[R1, R2, R4]*

3. **Strong empirical performance.** SDB outperforms previous DBM methods (e.g., DDBM, DBIM) in image quality (lower FID), sampling efficiency (up to 5× faster), and conditional diversity on image-to-image (I2I) tasks, as evaluated by several quantitative metrics. The method demonstrates high-fidelity outputs that preserve alignment and detail between source and target distributions. *[R1, R2, R3, R4]*

### Weaknesses

1. **Insufficient ablation and component analysis.** The paper lacks thorough ablation studies to isolate the contribution of each SC component:
   - No separate analysis of how varying the noise added to the base distribution (\(b\)), the transition kernel (\(\gamma_t\)), and the sampling SDEs (\(\epsilon_t\)) individually impacts FID, sampling speed, and diversity. *[R1, R4]*
   - No ablation on the effect of different \(\gamma_{\max}\) values. *[R1]*
   - No ablation on the proposed score reparameterization and discretization sampling algorithm; it is unclear how much gain each technique contributes. *[R1]*
   - No numerical stability analysis (e.g., gradient norms, singular value behavior) to verify that the reparameterization indeed mitigates singularity. *[R1]*

2. **Insufficient evaluation scope and qualitative comparisons.** The experimental evaluation is considered simplistic and does not fully support generalization claims:
   - Qualitative comparison is limited: only Fig. 4 compares SDB to DDBM; no qualitative or quantitative comparison to DBIM in the diversity table (Table 4). *[R1]*
   - The diversity metric (Average Feature Distance, AFD) lacks validation: no comparison with human judgments, no application to datasets with known diversity characteristics, no case studies showing insights missed by existing metrics. *[R4]*
   - No evaluation on more extensive or real-world benchmarks to strengthen results. *[R2]*
   - The claim of 5× faster sampling uses DDBM as the only baseline; the computational overhead of SC mechanisms (e.g., computing \(\alpha_t,\beta_t,\gamma_t,\epsilon_t\)) is not compared to other DBMs in terms of wall-clock time or memory. *[R4]*

3. **Lack of clear design guidelines and justification for stochasticity control parameters.** The SC introduces several hyperparameters (\(\alpha_t,\beta_t,\gamma_t,\epsilon_t\) and the noise added to the base distribution), but guidance for systematic selection is missing:
   - The value of \(\epsilon_t\) is largely determined empirically; no sensitivity analysis or grid search is provided. *[R4]*
   - The rationale for why specific design choices (e.g., convolving \(\pi_{\text{cond}}\) with a Gaussian vs. adding Gaussian noise, kernel size) is not explained. *[R1]*
   - Practical advantages of the SC mechanism over simpler alternatives are not sufficiently justified. *[R2]*

4. **Presentation and clarity issues.**
   - The presentation of stochastic control in Sections 3 and 4 mixes well-known stochastic process theory with the paper’s innovations, making it difficult to identify what is genuinely novel. *[R2]*
   - A diagram or figure illustrating how SC interacts with other components is missing; the purpose of Figure 1 (progressive noise addition) is unclear since I2I translation ultimately requires source-to-target transformation. *[R4]*
   - Related work: DBIM is not discussed in the related works section. *[R1]*
   - Typo: Line 212 incorrectly says “ODE” where “SDE” is meant. *[R1]*

5. **Inconsistency and missing experimental details.** Table 2 and Table 3 have mismatched settings: Table 2 shows \(\eta=0.3\) as best, but Table 3 uses \(\eta=1\); the quantitative scores for \(\eta=1\) on edges2handbags differ between the tables and are not explained. *[R1]*

6. **Limited scope of application.** The paper focuses only on 2D image-to-image translation; it does not explore extensions to other domains such as text-to-3D (e.g., using SDS loss), which would broaden its impact. *[R3]*

7. **Minor concerns.** The ability to convert the sampling process into an ODE process via the Fokker-Planck equation is not addressed. *[R2]* The paper does not analyze numerical stability directly (e.g., behavior of singular values during training) to confirm that reparameterization avoids singularities. *[R1]*
