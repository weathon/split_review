- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 6

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether end-to-end trained neural networks can recover latent variables (“motifs”) that determine an input–output mapping, using extreme activation sparsity as the inductive bias. It provides a theoretical Motif Identifiability Theorem with sufficient conditions and an error bound, and introduces the SPARLING algorithm to enforce extreme sparsity. Experiments on three synthetic domains (digitcircle, LaTeX-OCR, audiomnist) show >90% motif localization accuracy. The three reviewers (scores 3, 5, 6) agree the problem is interesting and the theoretical effort is valuable, but raise concerns about presentation clarity, missing baselines, practical relevance, and missing analyses. One reviewer (R3) finds the paper well‑written; another (R1) finds it unclear.

### Strengths
1.  **Interesting problem**: The question of whether neural networks can automatically identify latent motifs (intermediate state) from only end‑to‑end supervision is well‑motivated and important for interpretability (R1, R2, R3).
2.  **Theoretical contribution**: The paper formalizes sufficient conditions for motif identifiability and derives an upper bound on motif error in terms of end‑to‑end error. This theoretical effort is appreciated (R1, R2, R3). The guarantees are a core contribution (R3).
3.  **Clear motivation and goals** (R2): The motivation, contributions, and overall narrative are easy to follow.
4.  **Demonstrates both success and failure of sparsity**: The paper shows a scenario where interpretable latents are recovered via sparsity, and also provides evidence where the assumptions break (LaTeX‑OCR) (R2).
5.  **SPARLING algorithm**: Simple, efficient, and potentially applicable beyond the presented tasks (R2).
6.  **Originality**: The setting is original and opens a path toward harder problems like RNA splicing (R3).
7.  **Well‑written** according to one reviewer (R3) – contrasts with clarity issues noted by others (see Weaknesses).

### Weaknesses
1.  **Presentation and clarity**:  
    *   The definition of “motif” remains unclear even after revisions (Figure 1 still abstract); a concrete example showing the tensor shape and its relation to input/task is needed (R1).  
    *   Notation is confusing: square brackets are used inconsistently for sets vs. tensor dimensions (R2); the locality definition mixes spatial and channel indices and abuses notation for footprint function (R2); Section 3.2 definitions interchange \(v_{\hat{g}(x)}\) and \(v_{\hat{m}}\) and have a summation over \(i'\) not appearing in the summand (R2).  
    *   Equation typo: in \(v_{\hat{m}}(i)=\sum_{i’ \in p_2(i)} \mathbf{1}(\hat{m}[i] \neq 0)\), the right‑hand side should probably reference \(\hat{m}[i']\) (R1).  
    *   Missing equation and line numbers (R1).  
    (One reviewer, R3, found the paper well‑written; the others disagree.)

2.  **Lack of proper baselines**:  
    *   Results in Figure 3 are not convincing because no baseline is shown (R1, R2). The implicit comparison to a non‑sparse network is insufficient.  
    *   Needed: direct comparisons to a random mapping baseline, a network trained without the sparsity constraint, or to other existing methods (R1, R2). The current evidence only shows that a non‑sparse network does not produce motif‑like features, not that the proposed method is superior to other possible approaches (R1).

3.  **SPARLING novelty ambiguous**: The main text presents the SPARLING algorithm as a contribution, but Appendix A states it already exists in prior work (Gupta et al.). The paper should clarify whether the algorithms differ and what exactly is the contribution here (R2).

4.  **Confusing error metrics**: The definitions of error metrics in Section 3.2 and of \(\alpha\)-MOTIF-IMPORTANCE are hard to follow. An intuitive explanation through one of the synthetic tasks would help (R2).

5.  **Limited practical relevance**:  
    *   Tasks are highly synthetic and unrealistic; it is unclear whether the assumptions (input noise, locality, extreme sparsity) hold on common tasks like MNIST digit classification (R1, R3).  
    *   The locality constraint may be overly restrictive for capturing non‑local dependencies in real‑world data (R3).  
    *   The necessity of input noise is not justified (R1).  
    *   The paper should include a dedicated Limitations section to clarify the scope (R1).

6.  **Missing experiments and analyses**:  
    *   Sensitivity of motif recovery to the choice of latent layer’s dimensionality is not explored (R3).  
    *   Computational cost as input size/complexity increases is not analyzed (R3).  
    *   Would the method work with more complicated architectures (more layers)? How to select which layer should extract specific motifs? (R3)  
    *   No figure describing the network architecture used in Section 5.1 (R1).  
    *   Related work on “decision sparsity” (Enouen & Liu, Sun et al., Ren et al.) is not discussed (R1).  

7.  **Reviewer disagreement**: The reviewers have divergent opinions on the paper’s quality and impact. One (R1, score 3) finds presentation poor and contribution limited; another (R3, score 6) finds it well‑written and the problem important.
