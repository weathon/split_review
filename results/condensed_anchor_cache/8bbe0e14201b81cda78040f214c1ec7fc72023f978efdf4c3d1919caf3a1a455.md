## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper studies how optimal learning rate \(\eta\) and batch size \(B\) scale with total training steps \(T\) in LLM pretraining under \(\mu\)P. Experiments on 32M–354M parameter models (C4 dataset) identify three \(\eta\) scaling regimes (\(\eta \propto \sqrt{T}, 1, 1/\sqrt{T}\)) controlled by a critical batch size \(B_{\text{crit}} \propto T\). The optimal batch size is claimed to scale as \(B^* \propto \sqrt{T}\) when \(\eta\) is tuned. Sensitivity to \(\eta\) decreases with larger \(T\) but stays constant under \(\mu\)P model scaling. One reviewer (score 6) is more positive about the empirical patterns and their usefulness; three reviewers (scores 5) find the evidence insufficient to support the claimed scaling laws.

### Strengths
- Addresses an important problem: hyperparameter tuning is costly in LLM scaling, and understanding how optimal \(\eta\) and \(B\) change with data size goes beyond the compute-optimal paradigm.
- Well-organized experiments with multiple model sizes and training horizons.
- Identifies distinct scaling regimes for \(\eta\) depending on \(B\) relative to a time-varying critical batch size, and shows that optimal batch size is positively correlated with \(B_{\text{crit}}\).
- Provides a conjecture (Eq. 1) that aligns with existing theoretical models, offering a useful framework.
- The scaling of batch size in data (Fig 2b) and the sensitivity analysis of loss to \(\eta\) (Fig 3) are empirically intriguing and potentially impactful for practitioners.
- Demonstrates that optimal \(\eta\) and \(B\) dynamics are preserved under \(\mu\)P model scaling, challenging the conventional view that \(B_{\text{crit}}\) depends only on loss value.

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient empirical evidence for claimed scaling laws.**  
  - Figure 1 is noisy; the optimal LR fluctuates across \(T\) (e.g., inconsistent between \(T=500\) and \(T=1000\)), and the proposed scaling does not consistently capture these fluctuations. The lack of a clear monotonic trend makes the \(\eta\) scaling questionable (Reviewer 1).  
  - Figure 1a shows a non‑monotone pattern in batch size for a fixed \(T\) – what explains this? (Reviewer 2)  
  - Optimal batch size \(B^*\) (Fig 2a) changes only once (from \(2^{18}\) to \(2^{20}\)); deriving a \(\sqrt{T}\) scaling from two data points is not convincing (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).  
  - \(B_{\text{crit}} \propto T\) is claimed from a single bump in the data (a \(2^2\) budget increase out of \(2^5\)); extrapolating a general scaling from one step change is overclaiming (Reviewer 2).  
  - All scaling laws are derived from 6 batch sizes and 8 token budgets, with a “lost” early epoch effect; the origin of the three specific growth regimes (\(T^{-0.5}\), constant, \(T^{0.5}\)) is not justified by the empirics alone (Reviewer 2).

- **Lack of deeper analysis and theoretical justification.**  
  The paper reads as an experimental report that summarizes observed patterns without explaining *why* the learning rate can be much larger when \(T\) is large and batch size is large, or why the regimes occur. Understanding the underlying mechanisms is needed (Reviewer 3). The claims should be tempered to match the strength of the evidence, and Appendix A.2 content should be integrated into the main text (Reviewer 2).

- **Confusing and inconsistent definitions.**  
  - The definition of \(B_{\text{crit}}\) is unclear: line 107 defines it as the point where \(\eta \propto \sqrt{B}\) breaks (citing Shallue et al. 2019, but without a specific section), while lines 188 and 294 use it as the batch size that gives the best validation error for a given \(T\). This discrepancy needs clarification (Reviewers 3, 4).  
  - Notation: \(\epsilon = 1e^{-8}\) (line 122) should be \(10^{-8}\).  
  - The effective learning rate for Adam (line 428) lacks sufficient detail (Reviewer 3).

- **Limited generalizability.**  
  Experiments are conducted only on one dataset (C4) and only with \(\mu\)P‑parameterized models. No sanity checks on downstream tasks (e.g., does optimizing pre‑training loss under these rules also benefit downstream performance?). Additional results on non‑\(\mu\)P models are needed to understand broader applicability (Reviewers 1, 3).

- **Writing and presentation issues.**  
  The introduction overstates the contribution, making it sound like a theoretical paper, while the empirics are too weak to support the strong scaling claims. The paper’s claims should be presented as conjectures with appropriate caveats (Reviewer 2).
