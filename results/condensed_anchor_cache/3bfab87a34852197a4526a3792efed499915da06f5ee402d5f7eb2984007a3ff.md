## Merged Review

### Summary
Two reviewers rated the paper as solid (score 5) and one as weaker (score 3, lower confidence). The reviewers recognized the goal of a bounded, dataset-agnostic, label-aware similarity metric for multi-source learning that combines optimal transport, cosine similarity, and Hellinger distance. Strengths include novelty, clarity, and extensive experiments. Weaknesses center on: an unintuitive or insufficiently justified relationship between similarity and utility; missing explanation of key algorithmic components and experimental creation of non-iid datasets; insufficient treatment of the claimed privacy-preserving contribution (SMPC) with no security analysis or proof; modest empirical correlation strength in mid-similarity ranges; lack of exploration of non-iid learning approaches; unclear motivation for Hellinger distance over alternatives; theoretical analysis that uses a restrictive scenario and omits the Hellinger component; and the point that gradient similarity estimation without model training is not a distinguishing feature. The minority reviewer was substantially more critical, especially about the theoretical intuition and the explanation of experimental setup.

### Strengths
- The similarity metric is easy to compute and can be helpful for practitioners training models on multiple data sources.
- The metric is the first to simultaneously achieve several desired properties: boundedness, being dataset-agnostic, considering label information, and requiring no model training. It combines optimal transport, cosine distance, and Hellinger distance in a new way.
- The paper is mostly clear and well written: Algorithm 1 clearly shows the metric computation, notation is easy to follow, and there is a guide to interpretation (e.g., values > 0.5 induce negative learning).
- Sufficient related works and background are provided, allowing easy placement in the literature.
- Many experiments are conducted, including synthetic and real datasets covering regression, multi-class image classification, etc. Full details are given for reproducibility, and results show a correlation between the metric and learning performance under varying non-iid degrees.
- The paper studies a foundational problem in machine learning that may have broad impact.

### Weaknesses
- The relation between data similarity and model utility is counter-intuitive and not adequately justified. The paper suggests datasets should be as similar as possible (e.g., Fig 1), but intuition says that adding a too-similar dataset yields no improvement, while adding a too-different dataset degrades performance. The theoretical insight about cosine similarity close to zero implying minimal loss improvement requires clarification.
- Key concepts in the algorithm are not clearly explained: what μ and Σ represent (presumably Gaussian parameters, but unclear), and the notation “u” in equation (5) appears to be a typo for “μ”. The algorithm’s intuition would benefit from demonstrating similarity values for simple cases (e.g., D1 = D2, D1 subset of D2, fresh samples from the same distribution).
- The experimental setup for creating datasets with different levels of similarity is not described. It is important to examine whether the artificially created datasets reflect real scenarios where multiple institutions own slightly different datasets and want to jointly train a model.
- The claim of privacy-preserving computation is a major weakness. It is unclear what privacy guarantee is provided (DP, SMPC?) until page 4. The SMPC treatment is limited: no computation analysis, no security proof, and Algorithm 1 suggests SMPC is only applied to features, not labels, conflating the security guarantees. The claim of introducing no error is not supported (the linked proof is background in supplement S.1).
- The empirical correlation between the metric and learning performance is not very strong. It separates well IID (metric ~0.1 or lower) from heavily non-iid (0.3+), but there is large variation and high variance in intermediate ranges.
- There is lacking exploration with respect to non-iid learning approaches: these approaches often perform worse even in non-iid settings, which is counter to their design; more exploration is needed.
- The choice of Hellinger distance for label similarity is not well motivated; why not use Wasserstein distance?
- Theoretical insights (Section 6) need improvement. The scenario “any pair of vectors drawn from two random and independent datasets” may not be representative, as datasets can overlap. Also, the Hellinger distance is not discussed in the theoretical analysis.
- The claim of “estimating gradient similarity without model training” is not a distinguishing feature, as deriving a bound of gradient similarity from data similarity is straightforward for Lipschitz functions; any data similarity metric can estimate gradient similarity.
