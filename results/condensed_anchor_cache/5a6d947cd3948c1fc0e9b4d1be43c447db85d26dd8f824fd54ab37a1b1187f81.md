## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an end-to-end unsupervised approach for analyzing animal behavior by learning hierarchically organized discrete latent representations from pose time-series data using vector quantization (VQ-VAE with multi-level encoding). The method is applied to rodent datasets for behavioral motif discovery, motion synthesis, and cross-species mapping. Reviewers generally found the paper well-written and the application interesting, but raised significant concerns about technical novelty, insufficient comparisons to state-of-the-art models and baselines, lack of ablations, unclear architectural justifications, and missing details on the quantization process.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (R1, R2, R3), with clear technical details (R2) and relatively clear presentation (R3).
- The motivations are mostly clear and the applications are well explained (R2). The theoretical development is sound (R3).
- The method introduces a novel use of vector quantization and hierarchical encoding for behavioral analysis (R4), and experiments are conducted on multiple real datasets (R4).
- The experimental results support the authors' claims (R1), and the evaluations are clear (R2). The application to animal behavioral data is interesting and important (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Technical novelty and positioning**: One reviewer (R3) questioned whether the contribution is sufficient for the ICLR community, noting that applying VQ-VAE to animal behavioral data may be novel but lacks architectural or learning improvements beyond multi-level encoding, and may be better suited for a different venue. The other reviewers did not express this concern as strongly but also noted unclear novelty (R1) and the need to clearly state advantages over existing methods.
- **Insufficient positioning with related work**: The paper is not well positioned with existing work on learning movement primitives and behavioral decomposition in embodied and robotics domains (R3 lists Paraschos et al. 2013; Merel et al. 2019a, 2019b; Bohez et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2021; Rao et al. 2022). Alternative approaches using sequential latent variable models for time-series data are not adequately discussed, and architectural choices (e.g., encoding entire trajectories into a single latent code) are not justified in comparison (R3). The related work section is placed at the end, which reads like an afterthought; it should be moved earlier to provide context (R1, R3).
- **Insufficient comparisons**: The method is compared mostly to weak baselines (fully-connected MLP, GRU) and only one table of quantitative results (for motion synthesis) is provided (R3). There is no comparison with state-of-the-art behavioral analysis or motion synthesis methods (R1, R2, R3). Quantitative comparison against the KPMS benchmark is missing (R4). Additional applications and comparisons are needed (R2).
- **Lack of ablations**: The paper lacks ablation studies on the impact of the hierarchical structure, the vector quantization process itself, the size of the codebook (R1), and the effect of not using quantization on the proposed applications (R2). A more detailed ablation would help demonstrate the benefit of the proposed approach (R2).
- **Missing details on quantization**: How is the codebook initialized and updated? (R2, also indirectly R4 regarding equation (3)). The embedding and commitment terms in equation (3) are not intuitively explained (R4). The method's robustness to variations in the number and granularity of discrete codes is not analyzed, and it is unclear how to determine the number of codes at each level (R4).
- **Limited evaluation of learned representations**: Beyond motion synthesis, the analysis of the learned discrete behavioral motifs is limited (R3). It is unclear how well these motifs capture meaningful behavioral patterns and how they compare to existing methods for behavioral decomposition (R3). More thorough analysis and discussion of limitations and failure cases are needed (R2).
- **Minor issues**: There is a missing/broken citation in the first paragraph (R1, R2, R3). The novelty contributions are not clearly articulated (R1); the advantages of the method over existing approaches (e.g., improved interpretability, better performance, novel applications) should be made explicit.
