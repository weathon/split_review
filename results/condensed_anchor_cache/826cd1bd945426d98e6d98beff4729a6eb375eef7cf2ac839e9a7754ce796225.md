## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a multimodal architecture that combines graph neural networks (GCN, GIN, Graphformer) with a RoBERTa language model trained on tokenized ECFP fingerprints. Pre-training includes contrastive learning with graph augmentations (atom/edge masking). Evaluation is performed on MoleculeNet benchmarks. One reviewer (R2) strongly questions the fundamental suitability of this approach, while others (R1, R3) find aspects novel and the results promising. However, significant methodological and baseline concerns are raised across reviews.

### Strengths
- The paper addresses an important problem: learning effective molecular representations (R2).
- The combination of graph neural networks with an ECFP-based language model is novel (R1). One reviewer (R3) specifically praises the use of ECFP hashes as tokens as an elegant solution that introduces a much needed chemical inductive bias, with ECFP-BERT outperforming ChemBERTa in 8 out of 11 datasets (R3).
- The method outperforms other baselines including MolCLR (R1).
- The idea of masking only atom and edge types during graph pre-training is intuitive and an interesting incremental contribution (R3).
- The insight regarding GNN architecture choice depending on dataset size (GIN better for small datasets, Graphformer for larger datasets) is a useful finding (R3).
- The paper is clearly written and concise; analysis and discussion are well presented (R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient baselines for ECFP-BERT comparison:** Only ChemBERTa (an outdated LM) is used; more recent LMs such as ChemBERTa-2 and MolFormer-XL, and traditional ML with ECFP fingerprints alone (e.g., SVM, RF, LightGBM) should be included to demonstrate that the LM actively enhances performance compared to direct fingerprint use (R3). The DVMP paper is cited but not used as a baseline; since DVMP uses SMILES instead of ECFP for the LM, it should be compared to prove the necessity of ECFP over SMILES (R1).
- **Fundamental issue with ECFP for language models (strong criticism from R2):** ECFP fingerprints are unordered sets of hashed substructures, lacking sequential or topological information; language models assume sequential context, making them mismatched. BPE tokenization of ECFP hashes fragments compact representations into uninformative tokens. Masked token prediction is ineffective because neighboring ECFP hashes have no built-in relationship. *Contrast:* Reviewer R3 finds ECFP as tokens an elegant solution with chemical inductive bias, creating a clear disagreement on this point.
- **Graph augmentation and contrastive learning concerns (R2):** The contrastive learning approach may be ineffective because different molecules inherently have different graph structures, already producing large distances between negatives compared to positives, making the problem too easy. The paper lacks a clear description of the graph augmentation methods, raising validity questions. (R2)
- **Missing data splitting details:** No information is provided on how the MoleculeNet dataset was split for downstream tasks, compromising comparability and reliability (R2).
- **Pre-training dataset inconsistency:** The methods section states that RoBERTa was trained on ECFPs from PubChem and ZINC, but the experiments section says only PubChem (10M entries) was used (R3). Also, the text mentions ZINC but then notes PubChem as the largest dataset (line 214 contradicts ZINC’s scale >37B compounds) (R3).
- **Missing error bars/results variability:** Tables 1 and 2 should include measures of error across runs to assess training stability and statistical significance (R3).
- **Missing ablation studies:** The performance of the GNN architectures alone (without BERT) should be reported. Also, a transformer trained on SMILES in place of ECFP-BERT would help demonstrate the superiority of the ECFP approach (R3).
- **Missing ECFP radius and minor technical details:** The ECFP radius used for fingerprint calculation is not stated (question from R3). No dataset sizes are provided to support the claim that GIN is better for small datasets and Graphformer for large datasets (R3). Table 1 has a formatting issue: both BERT-GIN and BERT-GCN show 0.79 for Tox21, but only BERT-GIN is bolded, which is inconsistent (R3).
- **Typos in manuscript (R3):** Line 105: “we **it** implement” – remove “it”; Lines 140-141: “This sensible of surrounding environment representations” is unclear; Line 200: “ECFP connectives” should be “substructures” or “hashes”; Line 209: “-2^32 to 2^32” likely should be “0 to 2^32”; Line 214: calls PubChem the largest dataset but mentions ZINC.
