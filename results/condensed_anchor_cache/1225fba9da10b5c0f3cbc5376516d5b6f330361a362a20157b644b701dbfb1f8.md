- Scores: 6, 8, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes LDCQ (Latent Diffusion Constrained Q-learning), an offline RL algorithm that combines batch-constrained Q-learning (BCQ) with latent diffusion models. The method first trains a VAE to compress state-action trajectories into a latent space, then learns a latent diffusion model and a Q-function over latents. At inference, multiple candidate latents are sampled from the diffusion model and scored by the Q-function. The approach is designed to improve credit assignment and avoid extrapolation errors. Experiments on D4RL benchmarks show competitive or state-of-the-art results on several tasks, particularly long-horizon, sparse-reward environments.

### Strengths
- **Novel combination**: The idea of shifting diffusion into the latent space and separating policy auto-encoding from Q-learning and diffusion is novel. The method inherits strengths of both latent diffusion models (LDMs) and batch-constrained Q-learning (BCQ), addressing a gap in prior diffusion-based RL methods like Diffuser and Decision Diffuser (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2).
- **Analysis of multi-modality**: The paper provides clear analysis comparing the diffusion model to a VAE baseline, supporting the hypothesis that diffusion models better handle multi-modal latent distributions (Reviewer 2).
- **Strong empirical results on select benchmarks**: LDCQ achieves state-of-the-art or comparable performance on several challenging D4RL tasks, notably Franka kitchen-partial-v0, kitchen-mixed-v0, and antmaze-large-diverse-v2, with significant improvements in some cases (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 highlights these as the most notable achievements.
- **Source code provided**: The authors provide the source code, aiding reproducibility (Reviewer 2).
- **Clear exposition**: Figures and algorithms are clear and help understanding. The paper is well-organized and readable (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2). Reviewer 1 finds the overall writing clear, while Reviewer 2 notes minor clarity issues (addressed below).

### Weaknesses
#### Stretched claims and unsubstantiated intuitions
- **“Improves credit assignment” and “faster reward propagation”**: These claims are repeatedly stated but not empirically justified. The method does not explicitly modify reward structure or backpropagation; the intuition that latent space manipulation inherently yields better credit assignment is unsupported (Reviewer 2, strong concern).
- **“Powerful decoder” vs. “limited decoder capacity”**: The paper describes the decoder/policy as powerful and capable of handling high-frequency details (a property from vision LDMs), yet later acknowledges that limited decoder capacity likely causes performance saturation in Figure 4. This contradiction is not resolved, and the notion of a “powerful decoder” in RL (where action spaces are typically low-dimensional) is not substantiated with evidence (Reviewer 2). Reviewer 2 requests analysis of frequency content or other metrics.

#### Insufficient empirical evaluation
- **Omitted D4RL environments**:  
  * Maze2D (umaze, medium) tasks are not reported (Reviewer 2). These are simpler environments that often reveal subtle algorithmic differences and are important for evaluating robustness.  
  * Adroit “-expert” versions (pen, hammer, relocate) are silently omitted (Reviewer 2). These are standard for assessing behavior cloning capability, and their absence raises concerns about cherry-picking.  
  * The latter category of environments that do not require trajectory stitching (e.g., “-expert” datasets) are incompletely reported (Reviewer 2).  
- **Missing error bars**: Confidence intervals are absent from Figure 4 and from the Adroit results in Table 1 (Reviewer 2). This makes it difficult to assess statistical significance.
- **Not all results are state-of-the-art**: The claim of “state-of-the-art” should be qualified, specifying which environments and by how much (Reviewer 2). Some reported values are not superior to prior work.
- **Limited baselines for latent planning**: The paper should compare with methods that also perform planning over learned latent action spaces, such as OPAL (VAE-based), PLAS (VAE-based), and Flow to Control (normalizing flows) (Reviewer 1). Without these, it is unclear whether performance gains come from the diffusion model’s expressiveness or simply a better prior on the latent space (Reviewer 1). The ablation in Section 5.2 compares the diffusion prior to a VAE conditional prior, not to a learned latent policy (Reviewer 1).
- **Need for discrete action results**: The paper claims the method works for both continuous and discrete actions, but no discrete-action results are provided (Reviewer 3).
- **Qualitative trajectory visualization**: Visualizing trajectories at test time would help support the trajectory-stitching property (Reviewer 2). Visualization of latent space traversal during execution is also missing.
- **Appendix results not well integrated**: The appendix mentions CARLA and goal conditioning tasks, but these are not clearly discussed in the main paper; no quantitative results for CARLA are presented in the main text (Reviewer 3).

#### Latent quality and representation concerns
- **Dependence on Beta-VAE quality**: The Q-function is learned over latent skills \(z\) produced by a Beta-VAE. Prior work shows VAEs may not produce high-quality latents; the paper does not provide qualitative or quantitative evaluation of latent quality (e.g., TD errors, Q-value consistency) (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 also requests comparisons with other representation learning objectives (e.g., contrastive, flow-based) to assess whether a better representation would further improve performance.
- **How diffusion solves VAE limitations**: The paper argues for diffusion over VAE latents, but LDCQ still relies on a VAE embedding. It is unclear whether the advantage comes solely from the diffusion model’s ability to model multi-modal distributions more effectively (e.g., with less KL penalty) or from some other factor (Reviewer 1). A comparison with flow-based latent spaces (lossless representation) would clarify this (Reviewer 1).
- **Need for qualitative latent analysis**: Figure 2 shows latents are diverse across horizons, but it does not demonstrate that latents capture task-specific information. Reviewer 3 asks for quantitative evaluation of Q-function effectiveness over latents (e.g., TD errors) and qualitative analysis of latent structure.

#### Missing experiments and ablations
- **Impact of re-sampling \(z\) at each step**: The policy executes a fixed latent \(z\) for \(H\) steps. What would happen if \(z\) is re-sampled after every action (like MPC)? Would it improve performance at higher computational cost? (Reviewer 2, question overlapping with ablation suggestion).
- **Entropy-regularized objectives**: It is suggested that entropy-regularized objectives could be used to induce diverse skills from the diffusion prior, especially for discrete actions (Reviewer 3).

#### Clarity issues
- **BCQ acronym**: The acronym BCQ is used multiple times before being explained in Section 4.2. It should be introduced earlier in Section 3.2 (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing algorithm for first stage**: Algorithms 1 and 2 cover the second stage and execution, but there is no algorithm box for the first-stage VAE training. The policy \(\pi_\theta\) has subscript \(\theta\) that is not used elsewhere (Reviewer 2).
- **Experimental organization**: The main paper focuses on D4RL, but the appendix contains CARLA and goal conditioning results without clear takeaway. Figures and tables for those settings should be better integrated or explained (Reviewer 3).

#### Reviewer disagreement
Reviewer 1 is cautiously positive (score 6, update from 5) and finds the idea novel but questions the empirical justification. Reviewer 2 is strongly positive (score 8, high confidence) and considers the method significant, though notes stretched claims and empirical gaps. Reviewer 3 is more critical (score 5) and emphasizes concerns about latent quality, comparison to other representation objectives, and experimental clarity. The overall evaluation of the paper is mixed, with the most positive reviewer highlighting strong new results on challenging tasks, while others call for more rigorous baselines and analysis.
