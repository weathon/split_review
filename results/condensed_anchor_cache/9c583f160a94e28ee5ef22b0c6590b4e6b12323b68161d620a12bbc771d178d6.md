## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates node representation learning in Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) under weak/noisy labels. It first empirically studies the robustness of existing GCL methods to weak supervision, concluding that they are all affected by label noise. The authors then propose WSNet, a method that incorporates weak label signals into the contrastive objective by leveraging graph communities to identify positive sample pairs. Experiments on five benchmark graph datasets show that WSNet outperforms state-of-the-art GCL and noisy-label learning baselines, particularly under high noise (53% label noise). The paper also provides code for reproduction.

### Strengths
- **Novel method and combination**: The idea of combining weak labels with graph communities to construct positive pairs for GCL is novel (Reviewers 1, 3). The approach is formulated clearly (Reviewers 3, 4).
- **Extensive experimental evaluation**: The paper conducts experiments on multiple standard benchmark datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Texas, etc.) under different noise regimes (Low 10%, Medium 32%, High 53%), and compares against several GCL and noisy-label methods (Reviewers 1, 3, 4).
- **Good overall performance**: WSNet shows particularly strong gains in high-noise settings (Reviewer 2). The ablation studies demonstrate the necessity of combining the self-supervised loss (L_s) and the supervised contrastive loss (L_supcon) (Reviewer 4).
- **Clear presentation and reproducibility**: The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with thorough details on the problem setting, loss definitions, and experimental setup (Reviewers 3, 4). Code is provided (Reviewer 4).
- **Meaningful contribution to weak-supervision GCL**: The construction of positive pairs from nodes in the same community is a meaningful direction for graph contrastive learning under weak labels (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient analysis of RQ1 (robustness of existing GCL methods)**: Too much space is devoted to simply running baselines without deep analysis. The conclusion that neighborhood-based sampling performs better in high noise is noted but not explained (Reviewer 1). Further analysis (e.g., performance under different classifiers, identification of commonly robust samples) is lacking (Reviewer 4). The definition of robustness to label noise is confusing—whether the noise applies to true labels or generated weak labels; with 53% label noise, achieving high accuracy may be meaningless (Reviewer 3).
- **Weak label generation is unclear**: The paper does not specify which labeling functions (LFs) were used to derive the weak label matrix Λ (Reviewer 2). For citation datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed) that do not naturally come with weak labels, the generation method and accuracy control (47%, 68%, 90%) need elaboration (Reviewers 2, 3). A comparison of different labeling functions would strengthen the work (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing experiments and ablations**:
  - The effect of the community detection algorithm (e.g., Louvain vs. Leiden) and label aggregation method on results is not tested (Reviewer 1). Louvain produces exclusive communities, which may not reflect real-world overlapping memberships (Reviewer 2).
  - Sensitivity analysis of the negative sample size \(r\) is missing (Reviewer 1).
  - No analysis of the trade-off between self-supervised loss \(L_s\) and supervised loss \(L_supcon\); a hyperparameter to balance them is not explored (Reviewer 2).
  - The top-1 positive sampling strategy (highest similarity) is not compared to top-k sampling (Reviewer 1).
  - An MLP classifier is not considered (Reviewer 4).
- **Computational concerns and efficiency**: Identifying same-community nodes via community detection on the whole graph is computationally intensive; no efficiency analysis or scalability results are provided (Reviewer 2).
- **Theoretical analysis is insufficient** (Reviewer 2).
- **Limited dataset scale**: All experiments use small datasets (hundreds to thousands of nodes). The method should be evaluated on larger-scale graphs such as OGB (ogbn-arxiv, etc.) or Aminer-CS to verify real-world applicability and efficiency (Reviewers 1, 2, 3). The weak/noisy label setting appears confined to a limited context; generality is questionable (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing comparison with cluster-based GCL methods**: The idea of using cluster/community information as auxiliary supervision is similar to existing cluster-based graph contrastive learning approaches (e.g., [1] in Reviewer 3). Such baselines should be included (Reviewer 3).
- **Notation inconsistencies**: In Algorithm 1, \(\lambda_i\) looks like a scalar but Section 3 defines \(\lambda\) as a label function; it is unclear whether \(\Lambda_i\) and \(\Lambda[V_i]\) represent the same vector (Reviewer 1).
- **Presentation issues**:
  - Lack of diagrams or figures illustrating the architecture (Reviewer 2).
  - Table captions should appear above tables; text between two tables should be avoided (Reviewer 3).
  - Several typos and grammatical errors throughout the paper (Reviewer 2 provided a detailed list).
- **Unanswered questions that overlap with weaknesses** (treated as weaknesses above):
  - Why does WSNet perform better in High Noise than Medium Noise on Texas? (Reviewer 1)
  - How are ties resolved in majority voting for weak label aggregation? (Reviewer 2)
  - Why not use the Leiden algorithm (better-connected communities) instead of Louvain? (Reviewer 2)
  - How is the role of hard samples (especially negatives) accounted for in \(L_supcon\)? (Reviewer 2)
  - What is the rationale behind the specific noise ratios (53%, 32%, 10%) compared to the step sizes used in PI-GNN (0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8)? (Reviewer 2)
  - How does performance improve on non-homophilous graphs? Is it due to the community detection algorithm? (Reviewer 2)

**Reviewer disagreement note**: While all reviewers rate the paper fair (soundness 2, contribution 2, presentation 2–3), opinions on significance vary: Reviewers 1 and 3 (score 6) consider the approach novel and the experiments extensive, whereas Reviewers 2 and 4 (score 5) note insufficient innovation and limited generality. The weaknesses reflect both sets of concerns.
