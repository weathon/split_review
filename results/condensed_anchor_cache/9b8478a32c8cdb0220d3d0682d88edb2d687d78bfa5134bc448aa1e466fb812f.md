## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper experimentally examines the resilience of LLM-based multi-agent systems with different communication structures (linear, flat, hierarchical) when malicious agents are present. It introduces two methods to simulate malicious agents (AutoTransform and AutoInject) and two defense methods (Challenger and Inspector). Experiments across four tasks (code generation, math problems, translation, text evaluation) find that hierarchical structures are the most resilient, with the lowest average performance drop of 23.6%, compared to 46.4% and 49.8% for flat and linear structures. The paper also studies effects of error rates, error types (syntactic vs. semantic), and shows that defenses can improve resilience.

### Strengths

- **Importance and originality**: The research direction is interesting and of great significance. The hypothesis that multi-agent system structure is central to resilience is original and intriguing. The paper is resourceful in its methods for simulating malicious agents (R1, R2, R3).
- **Clear presentation**: Overall well-structured and easy to follow. The research questions are clearly stated. Given the complexity of the experiments, results are presented in a way that does not overwhelm (R1, R2, R3).
- **Well-designed experiments**: Reasonable choices of systems and tasks. The selection of downstream tasks covers a wide range. The experimental setup is robust with several architectures and tasks, using quantitative measures for detailed analysis (R3, R4).
- **Insightful findings**: The conclusion that hierarchical systems exhibit better resilience, supported by performance metrics, is valuable for practical MAS design. Non-trivial observations include that introduced errors can sometimes improve output in debate-based agent models (R1, R4, R5).
- **Contributions to LLM safety**: Exploration of malicious agent effects and defenses offers important insights for enhancing reliability in decentralized or unregulated environments (R4).
- **Interesting case studies**: Section 4.6 case studies are compelling; a more systematic assessment would be exciting (R2).

### Weaknesses

- **Malicious agent design is heuristic and lacks guarantees**: AutoTransform is trivial and provides minimal added insight over AutoInject; it is unclear why an LLM-based approach is necessary. The error-rate metrics (Pm, Pe) in AutoInject are questionable: injecting the same number of errors per line can lead to drastically different output behavior (e.g., changing `while b` to `while True` causes an infinite loop, while `while b>=0` does not). The proposed methods may not capture majority types of real-world attacks (R1, R1).
- **Research questions are shallow; limited depth and missing ablations**: The paper attempts too much with too many variables, each investigation limited in depth. There is insufficient systematic ablation or closer analysis. For example, in the study of defense methods, it is unknown how much the relation between defense and attack matters; one would like to see results of defenses even without attacks. The paper does not deepen insights into how to design better resilient systems (e.g., whether a linear architecture can be made more resilient by joining a defense method). The surprising observation of performance increase is discussed only up to reasons, not used to derive designing insights (R1, R2, R2).
- **Inconsistent and unclear experimental setup**:
  - It is not always clear which agent is malicious, which outputs the final result, and which task is used for which architecture. The number of agents is unclear, raising doubts about generalizability (R1).
  - “Vanilla” in Figure 3 and elsewhere is vague; Figure 3 y-axis shows absolute performance metrics, not percentage drop as the title suggests, and different tasks (with possibly different metrics) are plotted on the same scale (R1, R3).
  - The introduction of “Error Types” (syntactic vs. semantic) appears only for code generation tasks, adding inconsistency (R2).
  - In several figures (e.g., Figure 4 only evaluates MAD, Figure 7a does not include MAD, Figure 8 only Camel) and tables, it is unclear why certain configurations are tested and not others. For instance, Figure 5 simply says “selected downstream tasks” without specifying which (R3, R3).
  - No error bars, standard errors, or confidence intervals are reported anywhere, making statistical significance difficult to interpret (R2, R3).
- **Defense discussion is limited**: The paper does not provide definitive evidence that the proposed defense methods achieve consistent performance gain across scenarios, nor guarantee performance in realistic settings. The question of how well Challenger and Inspector fix the corresponding attack types is not systematically addressed (R1, R1 question).
- **Claims too strong and unaware of prior literature**: The paper claims to be “the first” to examine how different structures affect resilience, which is false in the context of general multi-agent/distributed systems (e.g., Byzantine Generals problem, fault-tolerance algorithms). The paper does not cite relevant literature from game theory, networking, or databases. The observation that hierarchical systems are more resilient because of replication is not new; the paper does not distinguish effects of replication and hierarchy. Related work also misses “Prompt Infection” (arXiv:2410.07283) (R3, R5, R5).
- **Observations may be artifacts of specific LLM versions**: The implementations are short prompts to ChatGPT; conclusions depend on the current version of GPT-3.5/GPT-4o and may not generalize to other LLMs or future versions. For example, the observation that malicious agents gradually lose track of the error-injection assignment is a limitation that could be fixed by periodic re-prompting—the paper does not consider implications if malicious agents were more persistent (R5, R5).
- **Limited model scope**: Experiments only test GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o; no comparison with other models (e.g., o1-mini, o1-preview) (R4).
- **Task selection and analysis gaps**:
  - The degree of agent specialization and its effect on resilience (e.g., math-focused vs. generalist) is not explored (R4).
  - The chosen tasks are discrete and objective; more nuanced real-world tasks (e.g., recommendation engines) might face different malicious behaviors (R4).
  - The analysis of error rates (Pm, Pe) is superficial; it lacks discussion of why those rates were chosen and how they apply to real-world scenarios (R4).
- **Specific methodological concerns**:
  - In Appendix B.2, the prompt for text evaluation tells the agent which model (ChatGPT or Vicuna-13B) generated the text; this may bias evaluation (R3).
  - The claim (line 156) that it is not possible to use LLMs to inject syntax errors in 20% of code lines is dubious; current SOTA models can follow such instructions with additional checks (R3).
  - Code generation is the most verifiable task (by running code), yet agents were apparently not allowed to verify—simple baselines like a single GPT-4o agent might outperform multi-agent setups (R2, question 4).
- **Minority reviewer contrast**: One reviewer (score 8, R4) is considerably more positive, praising solid experimentation and contributions to LLM safety, while another reviewer (score 3, R2) is the most critical, doubting the main claims and citing lack of focus and insufficient evidence. The merged weaknesses reflect the majority concerns, but note that R4 found the experimentation robust and findings insightful (R4 strengths).
