## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a contrastive explanation method for reward models (RMs) using two-stage LLM prompting: first identify words related to pre-defined high-level evaluation attributes, then perturb those words to generate counterfactual (CF) and semifactual (SF) examples that flip or preserve the preference judgment. The method is validated quantitatively (e.g., CF coverage, semantic/syntactic distance) and qualitatively (e.g., case studies comparing three open-source RMs, including Deberta-v2 trained on harmless data). The approach is presented as a flexible framework for RM explanation.

### Strengths
- **Novel and clean methodology.** The two-stage prompting to generate CF/SF examples for RM explanation is novel (Reviewers 1,2,3,4). The method is general enough to work with any black-box RM and does not require training data (Reviewer 3). The pipeline—attribute extraction, textual perturbation, CF/SF categorization—is well motivated and easy to follow (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 3 explicitly states the technique is "clean and simple" and draws appropriately from existing XAI literature.
- **Experimental design validates the approach.** Quantitative experiments demonstrate improved CF coverage compared to baselines (Reviewer 1), and the method produces perturbations with lower syntactic and semantic distance (Reviewers 1,2). The case study in Section 4.1 is particularly clever: because Deberta-v2 was the only RM trained on the harmless dataset, its higher sensitivity to the "harmless" attribute serves as a ground-truth validation that the method identifies the correct behavior (Reviewer 3). The overall experiment design is appropriate and tests the right things (Reviewer 2).
- **Strong qualitative demonstration of usefulness.** The case studies effectively show how the generated CFs/SFs can be used to interpret RM behavior, including global sensitivity analysis and behavioral comparisons across RMs (Reviewers 3,4). The authors demonstrate that the method can provide insights even when the RM is a black box (Reviewer 3).
- **Relevance and significance.** The problem of RM interpretability is important for trustworthy LLM alignment (Reviewers 2,4). The topic and proposal meet the significance threshold for a major conference (Reviewer 2). Reviewers 3 and 4 gave scores of 8, indicating strong positive reception.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and impact on policy model transparency is not clearly argued.** The claim that "more transparent RMs would enable improved trust" is mostly intuitive and not sufficiently developed. Even if RMs become fully transparent, the policy model remains a black box. The paper does not explain how RM transparency translates into improved policy transparency or trust (Reviewer 1). One reviewer found the existing qualitative findings "trivial and superficial" and lacking deeper insights to improve trust in reward or policy models (Reviewer 1).
- **The finite, pre-defined attribute list limits explanatory scope and introduces potential biases.** The method relies on a subjectively defined set of high-level evaluation attributes, which may miss other important factors that RMs use but humans do not think of (Reviewer 1). For instance, a completion might be preferred because of a clever metaphor that does not fit any listed attribute, and a dynamic or wildcard attribute would be better (Reviewer 2). Furthermore, the attributes are elicited from the test set, which grounds perturbations to "expected" variations and may not uncover surprising biases (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 questions whether the LLM gives consistent attribute labels (e.g., exactly "avoid-to-answer" every time) and whether a frequent attribute like "complexity" always means the same thing (positive vs. negative). The sensitivity of the method to the chosen attribute distribution is not explored, but would be important to understand (Reviewer 3).
- **Unclear minimal/maximal perturbation justification.** The method does not clearly reflect how examples are "minimally perturbed" or "maximally perturbed" as claimed. Did the authors encourage the LLM to increase or decrease perturbation ratio until it cannot anymore? (Reviewer 1). Also, in the qualitative example (Fig. 4), some responses appear almost completely rewritten, raising doubts about whether the LLM faithfully follows the prompt to modify only identified words (Reviewer 4).
- **Why use binary preference flips instead of cardinal rewards?** The authors use Preference Flip Rate (PFR, an ordinal binary measure) rather than the cardinal reward differences. No justification is given for preferring this over a cardinal approach (e.g., normalized reward gaps). A cardinal approach seems strictly superior and would allow more fine-grained analysis (Reviewer 2).
- **Tested RMs are too small to demonstrate significance.** The reward models evaluated are extremely small/poor by current standards. Modern 7–8B RMs are much more relevant and easily runnable on a single 24GB GPU, so compute is not a barrier. It is unclear whether the positive results will carry over to stronger, practically used RMs (Reviewer 2). The authors are strongly encouraged to include experiments with more capable RMs.
- **Incomplete related work coverage and baseline justification.** Several related works on perturbation-based CF explanation for RMs are mentioned in lines 179–181 but not expanded; the baselines in lines 277–284 seem unrelated to those works, leaving doubt about how the method fits into the existing literature (Reviewer 2). Additionally, prior work (Zeng et al. 2024, Park et al. 2024) that uses directed perturbations based on specific attributes is cited, yet the paper claims "no XAI techniques have been explored for reward models" (L.59), which contradicts this (Reviewer 3).
- **Need for ablation on the LLM used for generation.** The method relies on GPT-4o to generate perturbations. Its sensitivity to the choice of LLM (e.g., less powerful or open-weight models) is not tested. Since the main contribution is the analysis technique, its robustness to different generation LLMs must be demonstrated, as not all practitioners have access to GPT-4o (Reviewer 3).
- **Limited evaluation granularity.** The paper reports only the fraction of test comparisons where at least one valid CF/SF is found. Differentiating cases with multiple valid CFs would better capture RM brittleness (Reviewer 3). Also, there is a notable drop in CF/SF coverage when perturbing both responses (chosen and rejected) vs. only one (Table 1), which is not explained (Reviewer 3).
- **Additional methodological concerns.** 
  - The step 2 prompt requires the modification to be both better/worse overall and better/worse on the targeted attribute. The necessity of the first condition is not justified and may be redundant (Reviewer 4).
  - The method would benefit from introducing "control" attributes that are not expected to flip the label, to provide context and identify biases when the RM does flip on them (Reviewer 3).
  - Several reviewers asked for more compelling evidence of usefulness. For example, applying the method to reward models with known issues (e.g., length bias) to see if the generated CFs/SFs correctly identify that bias (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 also noted the existing qualitative findings lack actionable insights.
- **Presentation nits.** Figure 4 caption could be significantly improved (e.g., explain dashed lines for black-and-white readers) (Reviewer 2). It is unclear whether the global/local ranking comparison mentioned at L.419–420 actually produces results or is just for Figure 4 (Reviewer 2).
