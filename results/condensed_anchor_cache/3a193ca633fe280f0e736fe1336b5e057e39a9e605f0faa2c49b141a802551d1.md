## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Goal Achievement Guided Exploration (GAGE) to mitigate premature convergence in RL. GAGE maintains an estimate of the optimal performance level and compares it to the current performance to control exploration vs. exploitation. Goal achievement is defined as the ratio of the current policy's expected return to the optimal policy's expected return (computed via Monte Carlo estimates divided by a heuristic total maximum reward). The approach adaptively tunes exploration noise (action smoothing for discrete actions, policy variance for continuous actions) based on this ratio. Experiments are conducted in five continuous control tasks (with PPO backbone), MiniGrid hard-exploration tasks, and a "Game Console" problem. The method is easy to implement and improves performance in several tasks.

### Strengths
- The core idea of using goal achievement as a dynamic criterion to balance exploration and exploitation is intuitive, simple, yet non-trivial, and directly targets the important but under-studied problem of premature convergence.
- The discussion of the "Game Console" problem in exploration is valuable and clearly illustrates the failure mode that GAGE addresses.
- The paper is well-written overall, with strong contextualization and motivation of the premature convergence issue, and a comprehensive review of related works.
- The approach is applicable to both continuous and discrete action spaces, with appropriately designed solutions for each.
- The algorithm is straightforward to implement and shows substantial performance improvements in many tasks.
- The priors required (human estimate of maximum achievable reward per term) are reasonable for many RL domains and not overly restrictive.
- Experimental validation is reasonably thorough, covering multiple domains and including ablation-like comparisons (GAGE-50, GAGE-75, GAGE-100).

### Weaknesses
- **Assumptions and limitations of goal achievement definition**: 
  - The approach requires explicit knowledge of the reward function and its individual terms, which is a strong assumption not needed by most exploration methods. This needs thorough discussion and motivation.
  - The “optimal” reward is unknown; the method uses a hyperparameter threshold. This introduces two limitations: (1) the goal setting heavily influences learning—if set too high the algorithm may fail to converge, if too low it may still lead to premature convergence; (2) the goal is highly task-specific, requiring prior knowledge.
  - The minimum-over-terms formulation can cause excessive exploration if a single reward term is very hard (far from optimal), even when total reward is high, potentially hindering improvement of other terms that require small noise.
  - When the learned policy achieves higher rewards than the set target (goal achievement >1), the formula leads to a negative lower bound for policy variance (Eq. 4). The paper reports that the agent can still optimize beyond the target (e.g., 5m/s target → 7m/s learned), but the mechanism is not explained. 
  - The impact of a bad reward upper bound estimate (e.g., V_star set to 1 or 99) is not tested; stress tests breaking performance are missing.
- **Theoretical and empirical justification**: 
  - Figure 2 and the main text claim that entropy maximization “changes the order of action probabilities” or “elevates the least promising action.” This is incorrect for both discrete (target entropy does not reorder probabilities; Shannon entropy is concave and pushes toward uniform without reordering) and continuous (the entropy bonus is concave) cases. The motivation from this claim is unsupported.
  - The action smoothing procedure for discrete actions (Section 3.2, steps 1–4) contains several computations with no theoretical or empirical evidence justifying them. Ablations comparing this complex temperature schedule to simpler alternatives (e.g., mixing with uniform) are missing.
  - There is a lack of theoretical discussion on why adjusting noise via goal achievement yields improvement.
  - Evidence for the main claim that GAGE enhances exploration over state-of-the-art is insufficient; stronger theoretical or empirical evidence is needed.
- **Experimental weaknesses and missing baselines**:
  - The continuous control tasks only compare GAGE against the backbone PPO. Missing baselines include: methods that explicitly avoid local optima while using intrinsic motivation (e.g., Chen et al., 2022), pre-defined entropy schedules (linearly decreasing, constant, constant+linear), curiosity-driven exploration, novelty-based exploration, and other intrinsic reward approaches.
  - The five continuous control tasks do not clearly reflect the four factors of premature convergence identified in Section 2.1; the motivation for selecting these tasks and how they demonstrate addressing premature convergence should be explained.
  - Standard continuous action benchmarks requiring exploration (e.g., AntMaze from hierarchical RL literature) are not used.
  - The “Game Console” problem is interesting, but the relationship of GAGE to methods that do not use intrinsic rewards (since GAGE also modifies exploration) is unclear.
  - In Figure 3, some GAGE variants still converge to local optima (e.g., Ant Acrobatics: GAGE-75 and GAGE-100; Humanoid Pole: GAGE-100; Humanoid Tightrope: GAGE-50 and GAGE-100), indicating the local optima issue may not be fully addressed.
  - For Figure 5, baseline performance on the non-game version of the task is not shown; it would strengthen the point to confirm all algorithms learn the task successfully without the game console.
  - The differences between GAGE-50 and GAGE-100 are not discussed.
- **Presentation and clarity issues**:
  - Writing is verbose in places (Section 2); the proposed method does not appear until page 4.
  - Section 4.2 suffers from a sudden drop in writing quality (odd phrasing, grammatical errors).
  - Figure 3 and its legends are too small to read.
  - Figure 4’s captioning and plot spacing are confusing; the right two plots should be closer together.
  - Equation 7 uses brackets [] that are not explained.
  - Equation 4 seems to assume identical variances for all action dimensions; this should be clarified.
  - Figure 2 needs a clear definition of what “entropy maximization” means; for a discrete distribution, maximum entropy yields a uniform distribution, which differs from Figure 2b.
  - The explanation of “entropy loss can not maintain the distribution shape, i.e., the order of actions’ probabilities of the learned policy” for discrete actions needs more detail.
- **Reviewer disagreement**:
  - One reviewer (score 8) is very positive, describing the work as “a worthwhile step forward” with “real implications” and recommending acceptance, while another reviewer (score 3) is highly critical, questioning the approach’s assumptions, the accuracy of the motivation, the experimental baselines, and the claimed evidence, and recommending rejection. Two other reviewers give scores 6 and 5. This significant divergence should be carefully considered.
