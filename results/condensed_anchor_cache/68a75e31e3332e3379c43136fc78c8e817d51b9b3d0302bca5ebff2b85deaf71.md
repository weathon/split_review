## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CVXQ, a mixed-precision weight-only quantization framework for LLMs that uses convex optimization to assign bit depths to row or column clusters within weight matrices, then refines step sizes and biases. Experiments on OPT and LLaMA models report perplexity (WikiText) and GSM8K. The method is shown to outperform GPTQ, AWQ, and OWQ on perplexity for various OPT sizes. Reviewers note strong theoretical grounding but raise significant concerns about insufficient comparisons, lack of hardware acceleration discussion, missing evaluation on downstream tasks, and issues with the convex optimization formulation.

### Strengths

- Demonstrates that companded quantization reduces mean square error of weights more effectively than uniform quantization (R1).
- Introduces novel row-wise and column-wise clustering (partitioning) for weight-only quantization (R1, R4).
- Formulates bit-depth assignment as a Lagrangian convex optimization problem and solves it to find the optimal bit assignment under a constrained average bit depth (R1, R3).
- Shows that the proposed partitioning methods can achieve greater bit-depth savings compared to non-partitioned methods (R1).
- Provides mathematical formulations assuming weight distributions (Normal or Laplace) and illustrates distribution fits with figures (R2).
- Overall framework appears logically sound and justifiable (R3).

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient comparisons to existing methods:**
  - Missing comparison with mixed-precision LLM quantization methods such as FlexRound and QuIP (R1).
  - Comparisons are primarily against uniform-precision quantization; paper should survey and compare with other mixed-precision approaches to provide stronger context (R3).
  - No ablation study comparing the proposed step-size determination method with standard techniques (Min-Max, MMSE, Hessian-based) (R3). Separating the evaluation of bit-precision allocation from the quantization algorithm would clarify each component’s contribution (R3).
  - Overall, the paper lacks comparative analysis to underscore relative advantages of CVXQ (R3).

- **Missing hardware acceleration and practical implementation details:**
  - No discussion on how the proposed mixed-precision (varying bit depths within a matrix) can be accelerated on existing GPUs (R1, R2). Well-established methods like AWQ and OWQ provide kernel implementations and latency improvements, making comparisons unfair without addressing hardware constraints (R2). Utility of intra-matrix mixed precision is unclear and would require complex, specific hardware design, limiting broad application; most mixed-precision quantization occurs between layers, not within a matrix (R4).
  - The paper lacks details on kernel implementation, expected speedups, and how varying bit depths affect matrix-multiplication kernels as batch size increases (R1, R2). The authors note hardware acceleration will be addressed in “Part 2,” but reviewers consider this essential for evaluating practical benefits (R1, R2).

- **Incomplete evaluation:**
  - Evaluation relies heavily on perplexity; insufficient use of downstream-task metrics such as MMLU and AlpacaEval (R1). Testing on a wider range of models and benchmarks is needed to validate generalizability (R3).
  - Tables 1 and 2 do not report the average bit depth actually achieved by CVXQ, making it impossible to assess true compression ratio (R1). Since the convex optimization may not exactly match the user-specified target, actual bit rates could differ (R1).

- **Scalability and formulation concerns:**
  - The quantization time may increase exponentially with iterations (as shown in Figure 5), which could be a significant limitation for large models (R1). The paper claims fast quantization but lacks experimental or theoretical evidence to support this (R3).
  - The convex optimization formulation may be flawed: for instance, in equation (3), f(X) is not convex, questioning the validity of the entire problem (R4). (Note: Other reviewers did not raise this issue; R4 is the sole critic.)
  - The paper’s contribution is not distinct: mixed-precision quantization and group quantization are well-established; the paper should clearly highlight what is novel (R4). The definition of “Part 1” in the title is unclear, and many descriptions in the text are ambiguous (R4).

- **Clarity and details:**
  - The meaning of “row” and “column” in row/column partitioning (Figure 3) and the units used for clustering (Tables 1 and 2) are not defined (R1).
  - No discussion on whether CVXQ can be extended to activation quantization, especially given the known importance of activation outliers (R1).
  - The effect of calibration set size on CVXQ performance is not studied (R1).
