## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends the recurrent switching linear dynamical system (rSLDS) by replacing the exchangeable Chinese Restaurant Process prior with a distance-dependent CRP (dist-CRP) whose sufficient statistics are approximated via the heat equation and finite-difference methods. The resulting infinite recurrent SLDS (irSLDS) avoids fixing the number of discrete states a priori, introduces geometry on the discrete state space, and retains input-driven recurrence. The method is validated on a synthetic NASCAR benchmark (both standard and extended with non-stationary dynamics) and on Neuropixel recordings from the International Brain Laboratory (IBL) mice decision-making task. Comparisons against rSLDS and SLDS show competitive or improved test log-likelihood, state discovery, and interpretability.

### Strengths
- **Novel contribution to SLDS literature**: The paper removes the exchangeability assumption of the CRP while preserving input recurrence, enabling flexible state cardinality and fewer parameters compared to standard HMM-based approaches (Reviewers 1, 4). The use of the heat equation to express deterministic sufficient statistics for the dist-CRP is considered novel and leads to a relatively straightforward implementation (Reviewer 4).
- **Geometric structure on discrete states**: The influence-function formulation introduces a spatial smoothness prior that constrains state duration and organizes states in a latent geometry, allowing the continuous state to guide discrete switches (Reviewers 2, 3).
- **Performance gains on non-stationary data**: irSLDS better captures trial-varying dynamics and task-relevant state switches on both simulated and real neural data, uncovering non-stationary processes that standard rSLDS misses (Reviewers 2, 3, 5). Visualizations of flow fields and switching states are strong and illustrative (Reviewer 1).
- **Clear exposition**: The paper is well written, with a well-motivated build-up from rSLDS to irSLDS, and the code in the appendix is appreciated (Reviewers 3, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited and inconclusive comparisons**: Most experiments only compare irSLDS against rSLDS at small state sizes (4–8); the improvements in test log-likelihood are modest and not obviously practically significant (Reviewers 1, 4, 5). Reviewers note that a fairer baseline would include rSLDS with many states or a grid search over state sizes – and if those baselines are cheaper to run, irSLDS’s advantage is unclear (Reviewer 4). One reviewer argues that the comparison is inappropriate because rSLDS assumes stationary dynamics, making its inferiority on non-stationary data expected (Reviewer 5). Additional datasets or a more extreme NASCAR (more states, longer trials) are needed to demonstrate the method’s novelty and practical edge (Reviewers 1, 4). Missing plots (e.g., rSLDS continuous states in Fig. 2E, rSLDS discrete state sequence in the NASCAR experiment) weaken the comparison (Reviewers 3, 4). MSE for the extended NASCAR task is not reported in Table 1 (Reviewer 5).

- **Theoretical and computational concerns**: The claim of similar time complexity to rSLDS is unsupported; no empirical runtime comparison is provided (Reviewer 4). The parallel scan efficiency is questionable: unlike S4D/DSS/S5, the transition matrices are not diagonalized, so the approach still incurs cubic cost for matrix operations (Reviewer 1). A reviewer also flags an error in the complexity formulas (sequential scan should be O(K²T), not O(K³T)) and notes that the parallel scan benefit depends on state size (Reviewer 4). The overall computational cost is conceded to be “considerably more expensive” (Reviewer 3).

- **Insufficient justification of the heat-equation prior**: It is not explained why the heat equation is the best choice among possible infinite priors; ablation studies with no smoothness or alternative smoothness formulations are missing (Reviewers 1, 2). The prior’s sensitivity to time is questioned: for dynamics with long-duration states or extreme time-dependence (e.g., Lorenz attractor), the method’s behavior is unclear (Reviewer 2). A reviewer also asks how the upper bound K is set when the model is called “infinite” and whether it can be made arbitrarily large (Reviewer 2). The discovery of a state that is never used (state 2 in Fig. 3F lower) suggests the model may overestimate state cardinality (Reviewer 3).

- **Missing analyses and clarity issues**: The motivation for moving from the Polya-gamma augmentation (Section 3.1) to the heat-equation sufficient statistic (Section 3.2) is not fully explained in the main text; readers are directed to the appendix (Reviewer 4). The paper does not discuss limitations for Poisson observations, only Gaussian observation models are considered (Reviewer 3). The trajectory in the extended NASCAR example appears non-smooth; it is unclear whether this is due to model bias (Reviewer 5). The paper would benefit from citing relevant work on infinite switching states (e.g., arxiv 2111.01256) (Reviewer 4). Minor typos occur (e.g., first sentence of Conclusion, missing ‘s’ in Section 4.2) and the caption of Figure 1 could link terms to the main text (Reviewers 2, 3).

- **Disagreement among reviewers**: While some reviewers find the results novel and well-supported (ratings 8), others consider the improvements marginal and the experimental design unconvincing (ratings 5, 6). One reviewer (rating 5) questions the overall novelty and the computational claims, while another (rating 8) praises the theoretical integration. This divergence underscores the need for stronger empirical evidence and clearer justification of modeling choices.
