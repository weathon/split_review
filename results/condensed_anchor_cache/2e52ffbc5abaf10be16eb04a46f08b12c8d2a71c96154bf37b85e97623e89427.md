## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes an enhancement to adversarial debiasing that handles multiple sensitive attributes under differential privacy. The approach combines adversarial debiasing (Zhang et al. 2018) with DP-SGD (via Jagielski et al. 2019) and multi-attribute debiasing (Kang et al. 2022). Experiments on Adult, COMPAS, and a hate speech dataset show results under different fairness and privacy settings.

### Strengths

1. **Timely and important problem**: The intersection of privacy, fairness, and utility is a relevant and growing research area. Extending fairness from single to multiple sensitive attributes is practical and necessary. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4, 5)
2. **Clear motivation and exposition**: The paper is well motivated and some parts are clear to read. (Reviewers 1, 4) Presentation quality is variable, however (see Weaknesses).
3. **Extensive empirical evaluation**: Experiments use two tabular datasets (Adult, COMPAS) with two sensitive attributes (race, sex) and a natural language dataset (hate speech) with four sensitive attributes (age, gender, ethnicity, country). Standard fairness metrics (Equality of Odds) are employed. (Reviewers 3, 5) One reviewer (2) finds the experiments weak; another (4) notes only EoD is evaluated.
4. **Author knowledge**: The authors appear knowledgeable about the background and related work. (Reviewer 2)

Despite these strengths, the contribution was rated low (1) by two reviewers (1, 5) and fair (2) by two (3, 4), with only one reviewer (2) giving a good rating (3). Presentation ratings range from poor (1) to good (3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Limited novelty and incremental contribution**: The method is a straightforward combination of existing techniques (Kang et al. 2022 + DPSGD from Jagielski et al. 2019, building on Zhang et al. 2018). It does not introduce significant new ideas beyond merging prior work. (All reviewers)
2. **No fairness guarantees**: The method only minimizes the success of a particular adversary; it does not satisfy any formal fairness guarantee (e.g., provable Equality of Odds). This is an inherent limitation of adversarial approaches. (Reviewers 4, 5)
3. **Unclear privacy guarantee**: The paper does not state a formal privacy guarantee for Algorithm 1. There is no theoretical analysis of the privacy loss, and it is unclear how the perturbation, clipping, and projection steps collectively satisfy DP (e.g., per-example vs. batch, effect of projections on sensitivity). The neighbourhood relation for DP is not explicitly defined. (Reviewers 2, 3, 4, 5)
4. **Weak and incomplete experimental evaluation**:
   - Only three datasets evaluated; only one privacy budget (ε) per dataset, making cross-dataset comparison difficult. How were ε values chosen? (Reviewers 2, 4, 5) The justification that large ε is acceptable because others use even larger values is not widely accepted. (Reviewer 4)
   - The only baseline is a basic classification model; missing comparisons with existing DP+fairness methods (e.g., from the survey arXiv:2305.06969). (Reviewers 1, 5)
   - Inconsistent and unexplained results: DP+debiasing performs better than DP-only on Adult and COMPAS (Tables 2, 3) but worse on the hate speech dataset (Table 4). Similarly, debiasing improves over the non-private baseline for hate speech but hurts for the other datasets. No explanation is provided. (Reviewers 2, 4, 5)
   - Overclaim: Abstract states the method “meet[s] various fairness criteria,” but only Equality of Odds is evaluated. (Reviewer 5)
5. **Ambiguity in algorithm and method details**:
   - Algorithm 1 is vaguely described; the pseudo-code is not implementable as given (e.g., missing details on projection, clipping, noising, whether steps are per-example). (Reviewers 2, 4, 5)
   - Why are there two terms for L_a in Eq. 2? What is the privacy risk from the adversarial component (U)? (Reviewer 3)
   - The paper claims the method ensures “unbiased learning and individual data protection” without providing evidence beyond empirical results. (Reviewer 4)
   - It is unclear where Equality of Odds is enforced in the method; the description of metrics in Sec. 4.5 is insufficient. (Reviewer 4)
   - Assumptions (e.g., group vs. individual fairness) are not justified. (Reviewer 5)
6. **Missing in-depth analysis and ablation studies**: No investigation of how hyperparameters (e.g., the weight of the fairness loss) affect the utility-fairness trade-off. Sensitivity of results to the chosen privacy budget is not studied. (Reviewers 1, 5)
7. **High computational cost**: The method requires per-example gradients for both predictor and adversary, which likely increases training time and memory. No computational cost is reported. (Reviewer 5)
8. **Presentation issues**:
   - Writing is often high-level, obscuring technical details. (Reviewer 2) One reviewer rated presentation as poor (1). (Reviewer 3)
   - Typos and missing references: “Debasing” in Figure 1 caption; “following advantages: reasons:” in Section 6; undefined “??” references at end of Section 6.1 and elsewhere. (Reviewers 1, 2, 4)
   - Figure 1 is not referenced in the text. (Reviewer 5)
   - Conclusion mentions four datasets but only three are described. (Reviewer 2)
   - Section 6.2 claims supplementary material is provided, but none was found. (Reviewer 4)
   - Claim about Jagielski et al. only noising the Auditor misrepresents their setting (they assume the learner does not have sensitive features). (Reviewer 4, smaller comment)
