## Merged Review

### Summary
One reviewer (score 5, confidence 3) praised the clear motivation, methodological rigor, comprehensive experiments (vision, tabular, noise robustness, missing labels), and practical utility. The other three reviewers (score 3, confidence 3) found the paper to be a formalization of an existing problem with limited novelty, described as more of a survey of known techniques (data valuation, dataset distillation). They raised significant concerns about experimental framing (e.g., lack of Dhard size, evaluation only on Dhard subset) and missing experiments on privacy protection. All reviewers agreed the problem is relevant but diverged on the contribution's strength.

### Strengths
- **Clear motivation**: The paper addresses a real and relevant problem in machine learning where data sharing is constrained by privacy or budget.
- **Methodological rigor**: Mycroft leverages both gradient-based functional similarity and feature similarity, supported by mathematical modeling and empirical evidence.
- **Comprehensive experiments**: Tests across multiple domains (vision, tabular) and scenarios (noise robustness, missing labels) show Mycroft approaches full-information baseline with small data budgets (Table 1, Figure 1).
- **Practical utility**: Data-efficient solution potentially valuable for smaller entities or restricted-access applications.
- **Novel problem formulation**: Equation (1) formalizes the data-sharing problem in a seemingly new way.
- **Regularization properties**: Section 5 suggests additional regularization benefits beyond subset selection.
- **Interesting observation**: Section 4.3 notes that an earlier checkpoint is better for gradient matching.
- **Easy to follow**: The paper is well-structured and readable.
- **FuncFeat algorithm**: Provides a simple and effective way to combine two commonly used data valuation techniques (feature similarity and gradient matching).
- **Collaboration framing**: Places the often-discussed problem of external data augmentation on formal ground, demonstrating how existing solutions can be applied.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty**: Core techniques (gradient matching, feature similarity) are adaptations of existing methods; the combination is not sufficiently novel for a conference publication (R1, R4). The paper reads more as a formalization and survey of known techniques (R4) and feels similar to dataset condensation/distillation (R3).
- **Missing real-world scenarios**: The motivation (privacy protection) lacks concrete real-life applications (R3, R4). If privacy is the key challenge (Section 2.1), why would data owners be willing to share even a small amount of data? Privacy concerns remain, making the problem similar to federated learning (R3). The problem does not seem to address a genuine need (R3).
- **No privacy-preservation experiments**: Despite Appendix G mentioning privacy techniques, no experiments evaluate privacy protection. Since privacy is a core concern, it is impossible to judge the method’s efficacy in realistic settings (R2, R2 question).
- **Experimental framing issues**:
  - Section 4 does not specify the size of Dhard, making results hard to interpret (R2, R2 question).
  - Evaluation in Sections 4 and 5 is only on a subset of Dhard (the held-out portion?), not on the full validation/test set. Thus it is unclear whether the method overfits to Dhard or how additional data affects overall performance (R2, R2 question).
  - Unclear which experiments evaluate on the held-out portion vs. the whole Dhard (R2).
  - No comparison with dataset distillation methods, where data owners could distill their dataset into a representative subset (R3).
- **Algorithm/proof ambiguity**:
  - RetrieveTopK function in Algorithm 3 is confusing; the textual explanation calls it a "greedy heuristic" but does not clarify how it ensures coverage of Dhard (R2).
  - Algorithms 4 and 5 are hard to understand without textual description (R2).
  - The inequality below Equation (5) in the proof of Theorem 3.1 does not match the definition of eλ (Equation (1)); the proof suggests the first gradient-matching term is squared (R2).
  - Unclear how sparsity implies the inequality below Equation (5) (R2).
  - Definition of fλ in the proof does not match the definition in Section 3 (R2).
  - Description of OMP from Elenberg et al. (2016, Algorithm 3) does not result in Algorithm 2 because Algorithm 2 does not account for the gradient of the feature-regularization term ||Ψw||² (R2).
  - Why does Algorithm 2 differ from Elenberg et al. Algorithm 3? (R2 question)
- **Scalability concerns**: Gradient-based data selection can be computationally expensive for high-dimensional datasets; the paper does not adequately address this (R1, R1 question 3).
- **Vulnerability to strategic data owners**: The paper only briefly mentions malicious owners but does not explore how Mycroft handles deliberately misleading data subsets (R1, R1 question 2).
- **Unclear handling of edge cases**:
  - How does Mycroft perform when Dhard is not well-defined or when the model trainer cannot easily identify misclassified examples? (R1 question 1)
  - Could data owners strategically provide non-informative samples to exploit the protocol? (R1 question 2)
  - The assumption that MT has learned needed information (but not good performance) fails when new categories or large domain gaps exist—what happens then? (R3 question)
- **Dataset selection**: No rationale is given for the choice of datasets in Section 4, and no reference to relevant work using them (R2 question).
- **Relation to data valuation literature**: The paper does not clearly differentiate its formalized problem and approach from existing data valuation methods (R4 question).
