## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces an attack-agnostic augmentation method that constrains existing poisoning attacks to a subnetwork (a "pill") rather than modifying all model parameters. The method consists of three stages: pill construction (identifying critical parameters), pill poisoning (applying existing attacks to only those parameters), and pill injection (calibrating the update to evade detection). Experiments on four poisoning attacks, nine defenses, and three datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10) show that the augmented attacks can bypass defenses and achieve up to 7× error rate increases on both IID and non-IID data in cross-silo and cross-device settings.

### Strengths
- The method is attack-agnostic and can be combined with various poisoning attacks; the core idea of using a subnetwork for stealth is interesting and novel.
- Extensive experimental evaluation across 9 defenses, 3 datasets, and both IID and non-IID partitions. The method considers both stealthiness and effectiveness simultaneously.
- The augmentation can incorporate both cross-silo and cross-device configurations.
- It can make non-SOTA poisoning attacks perform as well as SOTA model poisoning attacks.
- The appendix demonstrates that even with only 10% compromised clients, the augmented attacks can evade detection when other attacks fail.
- For non-IID data, the method outperforms existing attacks in 23 out of 24 cases, achieving high error rates even in heterogeneous environments.
- The paper even sketches a potential defense against the proposed method, showing awareness of the arms race.
- (Reviewer 4 was particularly positive, emphasizing generality and practical effectiveness.)

### Weaknesses
- **Writing and clarity issues:** The method description relies on oral explanations and lacks formal equations; the intuition and contribution of each sub-component (e.g., “Designing Pill Blueprint”), how the most important parameters are determined, and what SimAdjust/DisAdjust in Algorithm 1 contribute are not clearly stated. The ‘Dynamic Pill Search’ algorithm is key but relegated to the appendix and should be in the main text.
- **Missing ablation studies:** The paper does not ablate:
  - Percentage of data held by the adversary per total client data.
  - Effect of SimAdjust vs. DisAdjust.
  - Impact of different initialization strategies for the pill search.
  - How the starting point (first neuron on first layer) affects error propagation and overall performance.
- **Unconvincing baseline results:** In Table 2, baseline attacks (except Sign-Flipping) show very low error rates (10–20%) even with FedAvg (no defense); FLD defense sometimes increases error rates. This contradicts prior work (e.g., Fang et al. 2020), which reports up to 80% error rates. Explanation required.
- **Missing comparisons to related stealthy attacks:** The paper does not compare or discuss PDG, Model Replacement, Constrain-and-Scale, or Neurotoxin.
- **Computational overhead:** The dynamic pill search and injection stages may introduce significant overhead for deep/complex models. The paper does not analyze time complexity or quantify resources (e.g., compared to a single client’s training time), raising concerns about practicality and potential server suspicion due to long waiting times.
- **Scalability and threat model limitations:**
  - Experiments use a small number of clients; it is unclear whether the method works with hundreds or thousands of clients and smaller attacker data fractions.
  - 20% malicious clients is a strong assumption; practical attacks often use 1–5%. The paper should show performance with smaller attacker numbers and dynamic numbers of compromised clients (e.g., decreasing over time).
  - The threat model assumes complete control over compromised clients, with no exploration of scenarios where control fluctuates.
- **Unclear synchronization and update frequency:** The paper does not specify whether compromised and benign clients upload updates simultaneously or with variable delays, nor how frequently updates are sent. This affects attack stealth and effectiveness.
- **Parameter estimation process for non-averaging defenses:** The estimation (using the mean of compromised model weights) is only described for FedAvg. For Median, FLTrust, and Flame, which use non-averaging rules (median selection, trust scores, gradient-based filtering), the paper does not explain how the estimation adapts or why it still works. A proof or detailed justification is needed.
- **Limited model architectures and attack types:** Experiments use only simple CNNs; generalizability to transformers, ResNet, or VGG is untested. The method should also be evaluated on targeted (backdoor) attacks, not just untargeted.
- **Contradictory design in Pill Poisoning:** The “Pill Poisoning” uses an extra-trained model update as a reference to make the malicious update less divergent from the global model, which seems to contradict the original attack’s goal of maximizing divergence. The paper does not explain why this approach outperforms original attacks even under FedAvg.
