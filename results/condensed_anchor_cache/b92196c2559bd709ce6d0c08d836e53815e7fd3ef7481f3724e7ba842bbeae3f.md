## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes an adversarial training paradigm that incorporates OOD detection to improve GNN robustness against poisoning and evasion attacks, motivated by showing that standard adversarial training can cause models to learn incorrect information. An ensemble of OOD detectors is trained on PGD-generated adversarial edges to identify and remove perturbed edges at inference. Experiments on over 25,000 perturbed graphs evaluate against adaptive and non-adaptive attacks. Reviewers agree the idea is novel and results are strong (R1, R2, R4: scores 6,6,8), but one reviewer is highly critical (R3: score 3), doubting the fundamental soundness of the OOD detection approach and noting the lack of comparison with certified defenses. Concerns about generality of the OOD assumption, specialization to PGD attacks, trivial-defense reduction, and narrow evaluation benchmarks are raised across reviewers.

### Strengths
- **Idea and motivation**: The OOD perspective on adversarial edges is interesting and novel (R1, R2). Showing that standard AT leads to incorrect learning (R3) and motivating OOD detection is well done (R4).
- **Theoretical analysis**: The theoretical part is sound (R2), though some reviewers disagree on the rigor of Proposition 1 (R3, R4: see Weaknesses).
- **Experimental scope**: Extensive experiments over 25,000 perturbed graphs (R3). The defense is tested against both non-adaptive and adaptive attacks, including scenarios where the attacker has full knowledge of the detectors (R2). Results are compelling on a diverse robustness benchmark (R4) and outperform existing baseline defenses (R1).
- **Settings considered**: Wide range of settings: transductive evasion/poisoning and inductive attacks (R4).
- **Clarity**: The approach is clear and well-motivated (R4); paper is easy to follow (R2).  
  *Contrast*: R3 finds the paper hard to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Generality of the OOD assumption** (R1, R3):  
  - Hypothesis 1: attacks can be successful without being significantly OOD (e.g., subtle edge changes). The OOD detector may miss such perturbations (R1).  
  - Showing adversarial edges are OOD is straightforward and not a deep insight (R3).  
  - R3 doubts an OOD detector can distinguish adversarial edges from in-distribution edges under strong white-box attacks, noting that previous empirical defenses based on adversarial edge detection have failed; adversarial edges are more stealthy than typical OOD edges (R3).
- **Reliance on PGD-generated samples** (R1):  
  - The OOD detector is trained only on PGD attacks, making it overly specialized. Robustness against other attack types or adaptive attackers targeting the detector is not explored (R1).  
  - R2 asks: how would the ensemble detector work against an EoT adversary? The authors should test this scenario (R2).
- **Trivial perfect defense concern** (R4):  
  - For transductive evasion, the proposed defense may reduce to the trivial perfect defense of memorizing the clean graph (Gosch et al. 2023). Under unique node attributes, the perfect version of the OOD ensemble would ignore the perturbed graph entirely (R4).  
  - Relatedly, Gosch et al. 2023 note that a too-loose perturbation set definition leads to this issue. That work proposes local constraints; how does that affect Proposition 1 in this paper? (R4).
- **Proposition 1 issues** (R3, R4):  
  - The proposition assumes many nodes’ local structures change; under strong white-box attacks this may not hold on many nodes. The “proof” is not a rigorous proof—it is an unverified observation (R3, R4).  
  - The proposition highlights that the perturbation set definition is too loose, rather than showing a fundamental problem with adversarial training for graphs (R4).  
- **Narrow model/dataset evaluation** (R4):  
  - While the range of settings is wide, the set of models and datasets considered is quite narrow.
- **Lack of comparison with certified defenses** (R3):  
  - The paper tests only empirical defenses that are already known to be broken by adaptive attacks. Certified defenses against graph evasion/poisoning exist (post-2021). The authors should compare with and discuss why certified defenses are not evaluated (R3).  
  - R3 specifically asks: Why choose baselines that are vulnerable to adaptive attacks? (Figure 2).  
- **Poisoning scope limitations** (R1):  
  - The poisoning scenario assumes perturbations happen only in training nodes. An attacker could target nodes outside the training set, which the framework does not account for.
- **Implementation details** (R4, R3):  
  - How is the threshold \( t \) determined? Is it the same across all OOD detectors? (R4).  
  - Why does the ensemble flag an edge as adversarial if *any one* detector flags it, rather than using majority vote? (R4).  
  - Can the OOD detector ensemble transfer to a different GNN instance? (R4).  
  - Notations are hard to understand (R3).  
- **Minority negative view** (R3):  
  - Overall, R3 finds the empirical defense is only a delta improvement and doubts its soundness, especially the feasibility of the adversarial edge detector. The other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) are more positive but still note these weaknesses.
