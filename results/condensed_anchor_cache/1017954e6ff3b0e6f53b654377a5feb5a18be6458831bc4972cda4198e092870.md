## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes using three existing pointwise information (PI) measures—pointwise mutual information (PMI), pointwise \(\mathcal{V}\)-information (PVI), and pointwise sliced mutual information (PSI)—as post-hoc confidence estimators for deep neural networks. It provides theoretical analyses of their invariance, geometric properties (e.g., sensitivity to margin), and convergence rates, and conducts experiments on misclassification detection, selective prediction, and calibration error using image classification datasets. The authors claim that PVI outperforms PMI and PSI, as well as baselines including softmax, margin, max logit, and negative entropy, for failure prediction and confidence calibration. Reviewers find the topic interesting and the exposition clear, but raise major concerns about the post-hoc claim, experimental design, contradictory theoretical vs. empirical results, limited novelty, and insufficient comparisons. One reviewer (score 3) is particularly negative, questioning the experimental fairness and the strength of the claims.

### Strengths
- The paper offers a comprehensive theoretical and empirical analysis of three pointwise information measures (PMI, PVI, PSI) for confidence estimation, covering misclassification detection, selective prediction, and calibration error. (R1, R4)
- It is well-written and clear, with detailed explanations and code availability. (R2, R4)
- The information-theoretic perspective provides an interesting new angle for confidence estimation, and the benchmark results are useful for the community. (R2, R4, R5)
- Theoretical results on invariance, geometric properties, and convergence rates are solid, with clear mathematical notation and proofs; theory is partially confirmed by experiments (e.g., Fig. 1, Table 2). (R4)
- The approach does not require modifying model architecture or retraining the base classifier, though it still requires training additional estimation models. (R1, R5)

### Weaknesses
- **Post-hoc claim and practicality.**  
  The term “post-hoc” is misleading because PMI, PSI, and PVI require training additional neural network models (e.g., conditional density estimators), not just a simple recalibration on a hold-out set. This limits applicability to scenarios where one has access to training data and can train extra models. (R1, R5)  
  Specifically, PVI uses temperature scaling as part of its estimator, raising the question whether its superior performance stems from the measure itself or from temperature scaling. An ablation of PVI without temperature scaling and a comparison to softmax with temperature scaling is needed. (R1, R4, R5)

- **Experimental design and baselines.**  
  - Missing comparisons with established post-hoc confidence calibration methods: Temperature Scaling (TS), Ensemble Temperature Scaling (ETS) [Zhang et al. 2020], isotonic regression, and regularization-based approaches. (R1, R5)  
  - Missing comparisons with other uncertainty quantification methods: MC Dropout, MCMC, Laplace approximation, non-pointwise information measures (e.g., MINE), sharpness score, and predictive entropy. (R3, R4)  
  - Only small-scale datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10) are used; the claim of scalability is unsupported. Experiments on ImageNet, TinyImageNet, and with modern architectures (ViT, DeiT) are required. (R2, R3, R5)  
  - For calibration, only two simplistic baselines are compared, and results in Table 3 are often within each other’s margin of error, making the claim of “outperforming all existing baselines” unjustified. (R5)  
  - The PI measures are softmaxed and temperature-scaled before benchmarking, while baselines like logits are not subjected to the same processing. A comparison with raw (unscaled) PI measures is needed to isolate the contribution of the measures from the post-processing. (R5)  
  - No ROC curves or ECE diagrams are provided; visualizations (saliency maps, calibration plots) are insufficient. (R3)  
  - Only features from the last layers are used without clear rationale. (R5)  
  - The estimation methods for the information measures and the choice of squashing functions (softmax, temperature scaling) are not systematically justified; they appear to be selected based on hold-out performance. (R5)

- **Contradiction between theory and experiments.**  
  The theoretical analysis shows that PMI has the best invariance properties, PSI has the best geometric (margin sensitivity) and convergence properties, yet experiments consistently show PVI is the best performer. This contradiction is not reconciled. (R5)  
  For example, PSI correlates better with margin (Table 1) but PVI performs better in failure prediction and calibration. (R1)  
  The connection between theoretical properties (invariance, margin sensitivity, convergence) and the observed confidence estimation performance is not clearly established. (R2, R5)

- **Weak novelty and reliance on prior work.**  
  PMI, PSI, and PVI, as well as many of the theoretical analyses (invariance in Goldfeld et al. 2021, convergence in Goldfeld et al. 2021 and Xu et al. 2020), are already proposed. The paper’s contribution is mainly in applying them to confidence estimation, but the experiments are unconvincing. (R4, R5)  
  One reviewer (R2) finds the insights novel, but this view is not shared by others.

- **Uncertainty interpretation issues.**  
  - Remark 1 invokes invariance to input transformations as a desirable property for uncertainty, but it is unclear what kind of uncertainty (aleatoric vs. epistemic) is being addressed, and the argument that confidence should be invariant to \(g(X)\) when \(g(X) \neq X\) is not well justified. (R4, R5)  
  - Remark 2 relates confidence to margin, but ignores data atypicality and out-of-distribution samples; in well-separated classes maximum confidence everywhere would be desired, contradicting the margin intuition. (R5)  
  - The motivation connecting PI measures to probabilistic causation is mentioned but never examined experimentally, and the claim that PI measures are optimal for explainability is unsupported. (R5)

- **Specific methodological gaps.**  
  - The definition of “sample-wise margin” is not mathematically given. (R5)  
  - In Table 2, the values of \(n\) differ between PMI and PSI, raising questions about comparability of convergence results. (R2)  
  - For PVI, no guidance is given on how to model \(\mathcal{V}\) to achieve estimation error comparable to the PMI bound in Prop. 6. (R2)  
  - The application of the estimation error bound from Jiang et al. 2017 (on \(P(X)\)) to \(P(X|Y)\) in Prop. 6 is not justified. (R4)  
  - The paper does not discuss extension to other data modalities (text, audio, video). (R4)

- **Computational overhead.**  
  All three measures require training additional models (e.g., density networks), making them less efficient than simple baselines (softmax, max logit). (R4, R5)  
  PMI and PSI in particular underperform softmax despite this overhead, raising doubts about their practical utility. (R1, R5)
