## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Laplacian sparsification to scale spectral GNNs, enabling end-to-end training unlike precomputation-based approaches. Experiments on small to very large datasets, including Ogbn-papers100M, are provided. Reviewers note that the theory largely applies prior spectral sparsification work (Cheng et al. 2015) and that the method is clearly explained. However, significant concerns are raised about applicability to non‑linear/multi‑layer GNNs, missing efficiency benchmarks, limited comparisons with other scalable GNN methods, and unencouraging experimental results on standard datasets.

### Strengths
- The paper tackles a relevant scalability issue of spectral GNNs with a clear and strong motivation.  
- Scalability is demonstrated on the large‑scale dataset Ogbn‑papers100M.  
- The idea of leveraging Laplacian sparsification for GNN scalability is interesting.  
- Rigorous mathematical proofs are provided (though largely based on prior work).  
- The method is clearly explained and supported by theoretical analysis.  
- Experiments cover datasets of various sizes, including very large graphs.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited applicability to realistic spectral GNN architectures.**  
  The formulation assumes a linear‑in‑parameters polynomial filter and does not encompass common designs like a 2‑layer GCN with non‑linearities. It remains unclear how the sparsification interacts with non‑linear activations and multiple stacked spectral layers. One reviewer specifically asks: *“Does the approach apply to stacked spectral filters interleaved with ReLU?”*

- **Missing efficiency and complexity evaluation.**  
  Experiments measure only predictive performance. The paper should report wall‑clock time, memory footprint (original vs. sparsified graphs), training/inference time, and error bars.  
  – One reviewer questions: *“What is the reduction in overall GNN training time?”*  
  – For sparse graphs (edges O(n)), direct computation of a K‑degree polynomial costs O(Kn) operations, while the proposed construction takes O(K²n); the method’s benefit for sparse graphs is unclear and needs complexity analysis.  
  – The memory footprint of the sparsification step itself may be large; this should be quantified.

- **Insufficient comparisons with other scalable GNN methods.**  
  The paper does not compare against sampling‑based approaches (FastGCN, ClusterGCN), precomputation‑based methods (SIGN), thresholding for spectral approaches (Gasteiger et al. NeurIPS 2019), or node‑sampling methods with better asymptotic guarantees (e.g., Keriven et al. – O(1/ε²) sampled nodes independent of degree).  
  – The authors need to explain what is gained on account of a potentially worse dependency on graph degree (linear in degree vs. degree‑independent node sampling).  
  – Comparisons with the “detached” precomputation paradigm are also missing.

- **Theoretical contribution is incremental and not tied to GNN performance.**  
  The theory is a direct application of Cheng et al. (2015) spectral sparsification of random‑walk matrix polynomials; only the propagation matrix is approximated, not the final GNN output after non‑linearities and multiple layers.  
  – One reviewer notes Theorem 4.1 seems an imprecise version of Cheng et al. – the random variable is not clearly identified.  
  – The proof in Appendix A.1 is unclear and lacks a rigorous proof environment; the extension from Cheng et al. is not clearly stated.  
  – Effective resistance is introduced without definition.

- **Experimental results are not consistently positive.**  
  One reviewer (score 3, high confidence) finds the gains marginal on heterophilic datasets and performance degraded on well‑known datasets; another notes improvements may fall within standard deviation.  
  – The paper claims “slight performance advantage” on heterophilic datasets but provides little discussion or explanation of why sparsification helps there.  
  – Standard deviations are not reported.

- **Many hyperparameters may hinder practical use.**  
  The framework introduces several tunable parameters (spectral similarity ε, sampling numbers, etc.), making it potentially impractical.

- **Writing and presentation issues.**  
  – Notation inconsistencies (small x vs. large X; w used for both polynomial coefficients and edge weights).  
  – Missing references (e.g., “O(n log n/ε²)” on page 7).  
  – Overclaim (“first work tackling scalability of spectral GNNs” – prior scalable spectral GNNs exist).  
  – Equation (2) first approximation is incorrect for K=1 (P does not approximate L); powers of L should use k, not K.  
  – “Desiring matrix” should be “desired matrix”.  
  – Definition 3.1 should read “positive semi‑definite”.  
  – “The proposed bound is much tighter than what is practically required” likely means “much higher”.  
  – Section 3.1 overlaps substantially with the introduction.

- **Questions from reviewers that should be addressed as missing experiments/ablation.**  
  – Is the sparsification applied once before training or at each forward pass?  
  – What is the improvement from the node‑wise sampling method (Section 3.3)?  
  – What percentage of edges are retained after sparsification?  
  – How is the spectral similarity ε determined, and how does it affect final accuracy?  
  – Comparison with Gasteiger et al. (thresholding) needed.  
  – Numerical comparison with detached approaches (e.g., SIGN) missing.
