## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Adaptive Curvature Step Size (ACSS), a method that dynamically sets the step size equal to the normalized radius of curvature computed from consecutive gradients: \( r_t = \|g_t\| / \| g_t - g'_t \| \), where \( g_t \) is the gradient at the current iterate and \( g'_t \) is the gradient at a tentative step \( w_t - \eta g_t \). ACSS is integrated into optimizers such as SGD, Adam, AdaGrad, and RMSProp. Empirical evaluations span 20 datasets and 12 base optimizers, primarily comparing training loss after a fixed number of epochs.

### Strengths
- **Novel and simple idea**: Using path curvature to adapt step size is intuitive and requires no additional memory for second-order information (R1: “I like the simplicity”; R2: “novel use of path geometry”; R4: “novel idea of automatically rescaling step size based on local geometry”).
- **Theoretical analysis for strongly convex, smooth functions**: ACSS is shown to achieve linear convergence (Theorems 2/9) and to be scale-invariant (Theorem 4). R1 describes the analysis as “clear and thorough”; R3 notes it demonstrates linear convergence under standard assumptions.
- **Extensive empirical evaluation**: Tests across 12 optimizers and 20 datasets with consistent training loss improvements. R1: “very thorough”; R5: “effort in validation” and “large collection of comparisons”.
- **Scale invariance**: Theorem 4 shows the effective step size is (near-)invariant to rescaling, which R4 acknowledges “brings a benefit in practice by reducing hyperparameter tuning”.
- **Clarity and presentation**: The paper is well written and easy to follow (R1: “excellent presentation”; R5: “clarity of the idea”).

### Weaknesses
- **Incorrect theory (minority reviewer R4 strongly contests)**:  
  - The paper claims GD-ACSS converges linearly on strongly convex smooth functions (Theorem 2), but R4 provides a counterexample on \( f(w) = \tfrac{L}{2} w^2 \) showing non-convergence: iterates oscillate at distance \( \min\{r, Q-r\} > 0 \) from the optimum when the effective step size is lower-bounded by \( Q > 0 \).  
  - R4 argues the proof of Theorem 2 is flawed: it derives a lower bound on the contraction factor instead of an upper bound.  
  - Theorem 1’s lower bound on the effective step size should be \( \min\{1/L, \eta r_{\max}\} \), not simply \( 1/L \), and the proof of Theorem 5 is incorrect when \( r_{\max} < 2/(\mu+L) \).  
  - Lemma 1 requires twice-differentiability, which is not implied by L-smoothness or strong convexity.  
  - Theorem 3 (stability under gradient perturbation) has a suspect proof, and its purpose is unclear.  
  - No pointers to proofs in the main text.  
  - Other reviewers (R1, R3, R5) also note the theory is limited to strongly convex smooth deterministic settings and does not clearly establish superiority over standard methods (R3: “same convergence rate as gradient methods”; R1, R5 question extension to non-convex and stochastic cases).

- **PyTorch implementation is wrong (R4)**:  
  - The implemented ACSS optimizers do **not** compute the forward/backward pass at \( w_t - \eta g_t \); instead they compute gradients at \( w_t \) twice, so \( g_t = g'_t \) and the curvature radius becomes undefined. This explains NaN when `epsilon=0`.  
  - For all optimizers except SimpleSGD, NAG, and HeavyBall, the code lacks the clipping radius \( r_{\max} \) and instead uses a modified formula with an `epsilon` parameter: \( r_t = \|g_t\| / \max\{\|g_t - g'_t\|, \epsilon\} \). This removes the positive lower bound on step size, contradicting the theoretical claim and allowing convergence only due to `epsilon`.  
  - The paper does not explain this discrepancy, so the successful convergences in Figures 1, 7, 8 may rely on `epsilon` rather than the claimed algorithm.

- **Unfair comparison due to extra computation and fixed epochs**:  
  - ACSS requires two forward/backward passes per iteration (R2, R4). Comparing methods after the same number of epochs penalizes base optimizers because ACSS effectively uses twice the gradient computations per epoch. R4 shows that if one compares AdamW at epoch 4 (0.499 loss) with AdamW-ACSS at epoch 2 (0.589 loss), the base optimizer actually performs better.  
  - R3 notes the experimental setup lacks hyperparameter tuning details, raising concerns about fairness.  
  - R5 and R1 observe that improvements on realistic datasets are slight for industrial optimizers (e.g., Adam, AdamW) and that Figures 5 and 7 do not clearly show superiority.

- **Missing test/generalization performance**:  
  - ACSS is evaluated only on training loss, but deep learning practice cares about test/validation error. R2: “It would be valuable … to explore whether ACSS similarly improves test performance.” R4: “What really matters in the DL context is to reduce test error and achieve successful generalization.” Missing results makes it hard to judge practical utility.  
  - R2 suggests comparing with Sharpness-Aware Minimization, a geometry-inspired method that improves generalization.

- **No sensitivity analysis of ACSS hyperparameters** (R2): The paper does not study the robustness of \( r_t \) and \( r_{\max} \) (or `epsilon`) choices, leaving practitioners without guidance. R2 asks how to interpret and set these values.

- **Figures are unclear or misleading** (R1, R3, R5):  
  - Figure 2 and 3 use inconsistent colors (blue vs. green for improvement).  
  - Figure 5 has too many cells, making comparison difficult; average rankings would help (R3).  
  - Figure 7 shows both methods reach the minimum, just with different path characteristics (R1, R5).

- **Additional theoretical and conceptual concerns**:  
  - The step size cancellation effect in Equations (2)–(3) and Theorem 4 raises questions (R3: “effect of step size is canceled out … can authors provide further justification?”).  
  - The lower bound on gradient norm in Theorem 3 is not justified (R3).  
  - R4 questions whether ACSS can escape sharp local minima because it takes small steps in high-curvature regions.  
  - The curvature definition \( r_t \) ignores directional changes in gradients (e.g., \( g'_t = 3g_t \) vs. \( g'_t = -g_t \) give same \( r_t \)); R4 suggests incorporating directional information could improve local geometry estimation.  
  - R5 asks about the approximation in Equation (593) and notes the local-Lipschitz constant depends on step size \( \eta \) as well.

- **Minor writing and formatting issues** (R1, R4, R5): Abstract repeats identical sentence (“Our results consistently show that ACSS provides performance benefits”), missing pointer to Appendix C in Section 2.1, duplicate word ‘through’ in citation, formatting of $L$-smooth, inconsistent numbering in Proposition B.1.
