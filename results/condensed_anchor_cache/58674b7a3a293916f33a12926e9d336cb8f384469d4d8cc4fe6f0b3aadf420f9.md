## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Consistent In-Context Editing (ICE), a regularization loss that augments standard fine-tuning for knowledge editing. ICE minimizes the KL divergence between the model’s output distribution when conditioned on a context that states the new fact and its output distribution without that context. The context is generated by GPT-4 from the fact’s prompt and target. Experiments on the KnowEdit dataset (and others) evaluate accuracy, locality, generalization, and linguistic quality. ICE shows strong performance, particularly in the continual editing setting, and maintains lower model degradation after many edits.

### Strengths
- The idea of using the model’s own in-context ability to produce a distributional target for fine-tuning is novel for knowledge editing; it combines fine-tuning with a distillation from the in-context behavior (R1, R2, R3).
- The method is clearly motivated and well-written (R1).
- ICE is the only method capable of effective continual knowledge editing across multiple edits (R1).
- Ablation studies demonstrate the importance of both the dynamic target distribution (updating the teacher during training) and the context itself; dynamic targets lead to better convergence (R2).
- As the model is edited repeatedly, ICE shows less degradation than other baselines (R2).
- Evaluation is performed on several datasets (KnowEdit, etc.) with multiple metrics covering accuracy, locality, generalization, and perplexity (R4).
- One reviewer (R3, score 8) highlighted that the method’s reliance on the base model’s in-context ability suggests stronger models should benefit even more, making this a useful contribution with potential for future work.

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and relation to prior work**: The method is technically similar to context distillation (Snell et al. 2022) and gisting (Mu et al. 2023); the paper does not draw clear connections or differentiate itself beyond the application to fact updating (R2, R4). The author response did not present specific differences from Snell et al. (R4).
- **Missing baseline**: A crucial baseline is missing: training the same model with the same layers (5 layers, as used for ICE) using only the fine-tuning loss (λ=0 for the ICE term). While FT-M and FT-L are reported, they update only one layer. The paper does not clearly show that the improvement is due to the ICE loss rather than just fine-tuning the same set of layers (R4). After discussion, the authors claimed this baseline exists, but it was unclear which baseline corresponds to λ=0 (R4).
- **Role of context vs. distillation objective**: It is unclear whether the “context” (GPT-4-generated statements) is what makes ICE effective, or whether the distillation-like objective (matching distributions) alone is sufficient. Ablation rows with “x” in the Context column (i.e., using the distillation-like objective without context) appear competitive or better than baselines in Table 3, raising the question of what context adds (R2). An “upper bound” experiment measuring the model’s performance when using the context at test time (p_θ(x | c, q)) before and after training would help isolate this (R2).
- **Reliance on GPT-4**: The context generation requires GPT-4, which itself can hallucinate or contain outdated information, potentially degrading the optimization. The paper does not discuss mitigation strategies or alternative context generation approaches. A Limitations section addressing this risk is needed (R1).
- **Pipeline cost**: The method requires sampling from the model at every optimization step and querying GPT-4 for context, making it computationally heavy (R1). The training time and prompting cost relative to FT-M should be reported (R2).
- **Incomplete baseline set**: The comparisons omit strong baselines like MEND and SERAC, which achieve results only slightly worse than FT-M. Without them, ICE appears to be tied with FT-M, whereas in reality ROME/MEMIT are relatively weak baselines (R2). The paper says one baseline “demonstrated nearly the best performance” in a survey but does not include that best model (R4).
- **Unclear formalism**: 
  - Equation 7 uses θ_s and θ_{s+1} in a way that is inconsistent with the description; if the target distribution is frozen, both subscripts should be θ_s (with no gradient for the left term) (R2).
  - The rationale for conditioning the context-distribution sampling on [c, q, x^*] (including the target) is not explained; sampling without the target (only on [c, q]) would better match the KL formulation in Eq. 4 (R1).
  - Algorithm 1 shows only L_ICE; if L_FT is also used, the algorithm should be updated (R1).
  - The variable “temperature” on line 431 is not defined (R1).
- **Limited model scope**: Only one base model (GPT-2 XL) is used. Results may not generalize to other model sizes or architectures; in particular, the base in-context learning capability likely affects the success of ICE (R3).
- **Missing details in main paper**: The number of layers updated by ICE (same as MEMIT, 5 layers) is only reported in the appendix; it should be in the main paper for fair comparison with FT-L and FT-M (R4). The value of λ used and whether it differs per dataset is not stated (R4).
- **Metric and evaluation questions**:
  - Perplexity is reported, but it is unclear whether it is the perplexity of the trained model itself or of a reference model on generated text (R4).
  - The WikiBio dataset is missing the Portability score; the reason is not explained (R4).
  - How are end-of-sequence tokens controlled in generation? The fluency metric may be affected by unrelated text generated after the correct answer (R2).
- **Minor presentation issues**: Typos in Figure 1 (FT loss should be p_θ_s(x | q) not p_θ_s(x)) (R1). Quotation marks should be typeset correctly (R2). The abbreviation ICE conflicts with prior work (Cohen et al. 2023); CICE might be clearer (R2). The example about US elections should be updated given its imminence (R4). Mention of GPT-2 in the main text should be moved to appendix (R2).
