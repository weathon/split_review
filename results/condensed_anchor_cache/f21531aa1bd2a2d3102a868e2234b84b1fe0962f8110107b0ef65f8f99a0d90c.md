## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes Differential Model Scaling (DMS), a fully differentiable method for searching optimal width and depth in neural networks. DMS uses a novel differentiable top‑k operator with a learnable threshold and a resource loss to balance performance and capacity. The method is evaluated across image classification (ImageNet), object detection (COCO), and language modeling (Llama‑7B), showing improvements over NAS baselines, including a 0.7% top‑1 accuracy gain over ZiCo with the same search time (0.4 GPU days). Reviewers mostly find the approach novel and well‑executed, but raise concerns about experimental fairness, clarity, and missing comparisons.

### Strengths
- The differentiable top‑k operator is a novel, skillful, and well‑motivated way to jointly optimize width and depth, addressing a key limitation of prior nondifferentiable NAS methods (R1, R3, R4).
- The DMS framework merges task loss and cost loss under the guidance of a resource constraint, enabling efficient search (R3).
- Experimental evaluation is exhaustive across diverse tasks (vision, NLP) and architectures (CNNs, Transformers), with search time comparisons showing compute savings (R1).
- The paper is clearly written and broadly accessible (R1, R4).
- The method scales NAS to realistic search spaces, which is a significant contribution (R1).
- Empirical results outperform ZiCo by 0.7% top‑1 on ImageNet while matching search time (R2).
- The core idea–a differentiable and direct way to control element count–is a clear advantage over prior differentiable selection methods that fix the number of elements (R2).
- The method is sound and straightforward in concept (R5).

### Weaknesses
- **Search time fairness and initialization** (R1, R2): It is unclear whether the cost of pretrained initialization (observed to be highly beneficial in Sec. 5) is included in the reported search times. The initialization scheme used in Tables 1,2,3 should be clarified. Table 4 (pretrained vs. scratch comparison) is confusing and appears to mix search and retraining stages in a way that may not be resource‑neutral.
- **Comparison to existing NAS search spaces** (R1): The paper compares against OFA and AutoFormer on different search spaces. A head‑to‑head evaluation on the exact OFA or AutoFormer search space is needed to isolate the gains of the search algorithm from those of the search space itself.
- **Pareto‑front scalability** (R1): Unlike methods such as OFA, DMS must be rerun for each resource constraint; obtaining a full Pareto front is expensive.
- **Missing comparison to structured pruning methods** (R2): The method follows a pipeline similar to training‑based pruning, but experiments compare only to NAS methods (e.g., JointPruning). A comparison to state‑of‑the‑art structured pruning approaches (e.g., RIGL) would strengthen the results, along with a clear distinction between structured and unstructured pruning.
- **Gradient vanishing in mask** (R2): The sigmoid used to generate the mask saturates outside the active region, potentially leading to vanishing gradients. An analysis of gradient flow through the mask and whether this occurs during training is missing.
- **Clarity of resource‑to‑threshold relationship** (R2): How the resource constraint directly influences the learnable threshold (and thus the top‑k selection) is explained only in the appendix; it should be in the main text.
- **Misleading use of “top‑k”** (R2): The method does not select a fixed number of elements (k). The mask is threshold‑based and the number of selected elements is a consequence, not a direct parameter. The term “top‑k” is therefore misleading.
- **Sensitivity of importance measure \(c\)** (R2, R3): The importance measure is a moving average of Taylor importance with a specific hyperparameter. Its stability directly affects the gradient estimate of the threshold \(a\). No ablation explores alternative importance measures (e.g., gradient magnitude, activation‑based scores). This is crucial to demonstrate robustness.
- **Minor empirical gains** (R2, R5): The gains over prior NAS methods are relatively small (e.g., 0.7% on ImageNet). Table 6 shows only marginal improvements. One reviewer questions the practical significance of such gains.
- **Cost losses limited to FLOPs/parameters** (R3): Only FLOPs and parameter count are used; latency (a more practical measure) and memory cost are not explored. Incorporating latency would increase real‑world relevance.
- **Inadequate justification of new differentiable top‑k** (R3): The paper does not explain why Gumbel‑softmax or other differentiable selection mechanisms are insufficient for jointly optimizing width and depth. A detailed comparison is needed.
- **Missing code/reproducibility** (R3, R4): The code and implementation details are not provided, hindering verification and adoption.
- **Joint optimization of layerwise and channelwise masks** (R4): How the layerwise mask and channelwise mask interact and whether they can be jointly optimized efficiently is not explained.
- **Lack of proper control experiment** (R4): No ablation isolates the effect of the differentiable top‑k from other factors (network architecture, learning rate, data augmentation). Improvements could stem from these rather than the method itself.
- **Excessive hyperparameters with no analysis** (R4): The method introduces hyperparameters (temperature, sparsity ratio, regularization coefficient) without a systematic study of their impact on performance and stability, nor guidance for choosing them on new tasks/architectures.
- **Incomplete ablation** (R4): Comparison is only to uniform scaling; compound scaling and progressive scaling are not included. The method is not studied on different layer types (convolutional vs. attention).
- **Unclear motivation for moving average in layerwise mask** (R4): The reason and effect of the moving average update (Sec. 3.1.3) on convergence and stability are not explained.
- **Choice of L1‑norm for channelwise mask** (R4): Why L1‑norm is chosen over L2 or entropy regularization for the channelwise mask is not justified.
- **Weak ImageNet baselines** (R5): The baselines used for image classification are too low; they should include models with 90%+ top‑1 accuracy.
- **Minor formatting issue** (R3): On p.5, “larger” should be “smaller” when describing the portion of importance values relative to a given element.
