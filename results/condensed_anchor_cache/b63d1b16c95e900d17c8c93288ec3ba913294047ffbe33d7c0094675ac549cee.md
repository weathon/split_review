## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies emergent capabilities in transformers by training small GPT-2-sized models from scratch on a context-sensitive formal language (PCFG). Three tasks are used: left-to-right language modeling, an unscrambling task (convert a set of words into a valid string), and a conditional generation task (generate a sentence containing specified words). The authors track grammaticality (PCFG acceptance) and type-constraint satisfaction, identifying three learning phases that coincide with sudden improvements in downstream performance (exact match for unscrambling, loss for LM). They then model the acquisition of entity-type relationships as percolation on a bipartite graph, deriving a theoretical phase transition that predicts the point at which the model transitions from memorization to generalization. The empirical scaling law of that point matches the theoretical scaling of bond percolation, and the authors argue that emergence can be predicted by understanding data regularities.

### Strengths
- The paper is extremely well written, a pleasure to read, and focuses on clearly understanding the phenomenon of emergence in a tractable setting. It is well placed in the context of prior and current related work on emergence.
- The topic (emergence of capabilities with scale, and the transition from memorization to generalization) is of both scientific and societal importance. The toy setup is similar enough to realistic settings to be interesting, yet simple enough to isolate and study these phenomena.
- The exploration of a new setting – learning entity-type relationships as percolation on a bipartite graph – is novel. The theoretical explanation using bond percolation is insightful and deserving of follow-up work. (Note: while reviewers 1 and 3 found this connection unclear, reviewer 2 considered it a strength.)
- The paper provides a clear definition of emergence, which is more formal and general than some prior definitions, and may help further research into measurement of emergence (reviewer 3). (Reviewers 2 and 4 also note the definition provides a nice background, though reviewer 2 questions its necessity if structure claims are removed.)
- The findings are well supported by experiments, and the appendix contains detailed additional evidence, including attention map analysis (Appendix F.6).
- The paper builds a bridge between LLM learning dynamics and physics/complex systems theory, using phase transitions from complex system theory.
- The results are potentially generalizable to other neural network models beyond transformers trained on formal languages.

### Weaknesses
**Justification of “structures learned by the model”**  
- The claim that the model acquires “structures” (grammar, context-sensitivity regularities) is not well supported by the evidence presented. The main body only examines performance metrics; the attention map analysis in Appendix F.6 is qualitative and weak – it would be more convincing if quantified and placed in the main text.  
- The definition of “structure” (Definition 1) seems almost tautological, as it is defined by performance on downstream tasks that rely on that regularity. A more parsimonious definition would be a discontinuous improvement in those tasks, but this makes the claim redundant.  
- Better tests for grammar acquisition exist, e.g., minimal pairs (e.g., *The man who likes apples is here* vs. *The man who likes apples are here*), which should be used instead of relying on generation accuracy.  
- *Suggestion*: If the evidence for structure learning is weak, these claims should be removed or significantly de-emphasized.

**Clarity and necessity of the percolation model**  
- The analogy between learning type constraints over training time and bond percolation on a bipartite graph (a function of data properties |E| and |K|) is not clearly explained. Several reviewers found the connection unintuitive.  
- The paper does not provide a clear mechanistic explanation of *how* the percolation model captures the observed emergence, nor does it discuss alternative graph-theoretic models (e.g., submodular functions) that might also explain the results.  
- The limitations of using percolation as an analogy are not addressed. (Reviewer 1 was willing to increase score if the analogy is clarified; reviewer 3 echoed these concerns.)

**Novelty relative to prior work**  
- Several related papers have similar conclusions: Murty et al. (2024) also train transformers on formal languages and find emergence of the correct learning rule and tree-structures with extended training. Chen et al. use a similar setting with masked language models and show grammar is abruptly acquired, with a causal relationship to downstream performance. Allen-Zhu et al. also study transformers on formal languages and find learnability under constraints.  
- Missing from related work: Singh et al. on phase transitions of learning subcircuits for in-context learning, and Tigges et al. on how known circuits evolve in the Pythia suite over training (noted by reviewer 4).  
- The paper does not adequately address how its findings reconcile with claims that apparent emergence is an artifact of discontinuous evaluation metrics (e.g., Schaeffer et al.). If the definition differs from prior ones, the justification for the new definition as a proper approximation should be provided.

**Generalizability beyond formal languages**  
- The formal language setup is popular but may not capture the complexities of natural language (less rigid, more ambiguous structure). The paper does not justify why findings should generalize, nor does it discuss how the specific grammar and type system influence the observed emergence.  
- It is unclear whether similar phase transitions would occur with different formal language structures or with noisy/less structured real-world data.  
- The study only examines emergence over training data scaling, but the introduction mentions other axes (compute, parameter size). It is unclear whether the results hold for those axes – a more general claim requires investigation.

**Presentation and exposition**  
- Some definitions are hard to follow: Definition 2 could be clearer (“S” is a non-terminal, start from S, and treat non-terminal mapping as normal, not a special case); Definition 3 (entities with unique identifiers for subject/object) would benefit from plain language and an explicit example.  
- The paper sometimes gets bogged down in formal details (e.g., Definition 2) when a plain-language explanation with simple examples would be more accessible. Conversely, some sections are too rambling (e.g., Section 5.1 Phase 3) and should use bullet points or short headings.  
- Minor: “nonlinear” in Definition 1 may be confusing (quadratics are nonlinear but gradual); “discontinuous” might be more precise. Inconsistent capitalization “Humans” vs. “humans” (line 227/228). Appendix C definitions appear to swap N/NT and Sigma/T. For the indicator variable (line 260), consider using \mathbbm{1} instead of \delta.

**Questions that overlap with missing experiments or justifications**  
- How is the specific task (free generation / unscrambling / conditional generation) communicated to the model? Is a special token used?  
- For the unscrambling task, is the solution guaranteed to be unique? If not, what justifies using exact match / average probability of valid tokens as evaluation?  
- Do similar phase transitions occur with smaller or larger models? Do architecture tweaks change the dynamics in non-trivial ways?  
- The paper mentions “order parameters” early but only returns to them in the conclusion. How do these differ from/relate to “progress measures” (Barak et al. 2022, Nanda et al. 2023) – a question raised by reviewer 4?
