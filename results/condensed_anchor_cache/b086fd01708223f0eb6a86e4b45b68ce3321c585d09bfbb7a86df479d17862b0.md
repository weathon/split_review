## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper proposes Sequence Reinforcement Learning (SRL), a model-based RL algorithm that produces a sequence of actions from a single state, enabling effective control at lower decision frequencies than the environment MDP. SRL uses a learned environment model and an actor-critic architecture operating at different temporal scales, with a “temporal recall” mechanism where the critic uses the model to estimate intermediate states and provide learning signals for each action in the sequence. After training, the actor generates action sequences without the model, achieving model-free control at slow frequencies. The authors introduce a Frequency-Averaged Score (FAS) metric to evaluate performance across varying decision frequencies. Experiments on continuous control tasks (MuJoCo Gym-v2) show SRL achieves performance comparable to Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) while significantly improving FAS, and also outperforms model-based online planning on FAS. The paper draws biological parallels to action chunking in the basal ganglia.

### Strengths

- The research motivation – comparing decision patterns and frequencies between RL and humans, and relating real-world RL challenges to neuroscience – is compelling and creative.
- The main idea of an RL algorithm inspired by biological principles, where each component operates at different frequencies, is novel. The storyline leading to experiments is smooth and easy to follow.
- The SRL method is explained clearly and is easy to understand and implement.
- Experimental evaluation demonstrates significant improvement in the introduced FAS metric. The usefulness of FAS is adequately demonstrated.
- The method is evaluated across several environments (10 tasks) with 5 seeds against the strong SAC baseline. Learning curves are provided.
- The approach is well motivated using recent neuroscientific discoveries, with a comprehensive comparison to structures in the mammalian brain.
- The issue of high computational demands of modern RL algorithms in robotics is quite relevant.
- The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses

- **Insufficient baselines and comparisons (multiple reviewers).** The experiments include only SAC (2018) as a baseline. This is insufficient to assess the method’s effectiveness. Several works on frame-skipping, action repetition, long-horizon exploration, action correlations, and temporal credit assignment (e.g., [1,2,3,4] from reviewer 1, FiGAR, TempoRL, D4PG, model-based agents, hierarchical RL) are highly relevant. At a minimum, SAC with action repetition (SAC-J) should be shown to demonstrate whether SRL offers an advantage. TempoRL appears in the appendix but not in the main text. The field of hierarchical RL, where a manager runs at low frequency and a worker at high frequency, is only mentioned in passing and deserves discussion.
- **Evaluation of SAC is unfair.** SAC was trained without action repetition but then evaluated at substantially lower frequencies (e.g., for SRL-J with large J). This cannot work and is not a relevant baseline. Comparisons with SAC trained with randomized time steps, action repeats, or methods like FiGAR and TempoRL are needed.
- **Marginal and inconsistent performance vs. SAC.** SRL only outperforms SAC in 6 out of 10 tasks. The improvement is marginal and not convincing. More significant and consistent gains are needed.
- **Contradiction with model-based online planning.** In 5 out of 10 tasks (InvPendulum, Hopper, InvDPendulum, Reacher, Swimmer), Online Planning outperforms SRL, contradicting the caption claim that SRL “clearly outperforms” online planning. This discrepancy undermines the claim.
- **Claims not fully substantiated (multiple reviewers).**
  - The claim (line 055) that “When RL agents are constrained to human-like reaction times, even state-of-the-art algorithms struggle” lacks citation or experimental backing.
  - The claim (line 197) that existing methods cannot deal well with low control frequencies in continuous control with multiple action dimensions is unsubstantiated; FiGAR and TempoRL include such experiments.
  - The claim (line 516) that “in deterministic environments, a capable agent should achieve infinite horizon control for tasks like walking from a single state” is unrealistic due to compounding errors.
  - The claim (line 150) that online planning is not feasible in robotics or the brain is challenged by reviewer 4; TD-MPC2 and MPC over skills [1] counter this.
  - The claim (line 169) that MPC is limited to accurately modeled systems ignores learned-model approaches like TD-MPC2.
  - The paper states (line 098) that SRL learns open-loop control “utilizing slow hardware and low attention, hence low energy” – reviewer 4 questions why this should be true.
- **Missing evidence and ablation (multiple reviewers).**
  - Actual inference times are not reported. The abstract motivates reducing action production time, but it is unclear why computing the first action of a macro action should be faster than SAC, or how the algorithm offers an advantage in real-time fixed-frequency scenarios.
  - An ablation with ground-truth dynamics would help determine how much performance loss is due to model learning.
  - Sim-to-real transfer experiments would add quality.
  - Visualizing SRL action sequences versus SAC would be informative.
  - Training curves in the appendix are plotted such that some algorithms are nearly indistinguishable.
- **Technical concerns.**
  - The explanation of how SAC’s maximum entropy (line 252–259) automatically lowers entropy for deeper actions further from the observation lacks a clear connection between entropy regularization and the challenges of action sequence generation. Reviewer 3 asks: how does SAC lower the entropy of “deeper” actions?
  - The paper claims (line 292–296) to address credit assignment for action sequences but does not clearly distinguish from existing works (e.g., [2,5]).
  - The paper lists issues in line 93 (sparse rewards, jerky control, high compute cost, catastrophic failure due to missing inputs) but does not clearly show which are addressed by SRL. That line should be either substantiated or removed.
  - The policy’s use of GRUs for sequence generation is very similar to [4]; this similarity should be discussed.
  - The claim (line 160) that SRL has zero model complexity after training is true for many model-based RL algorithms (e.g., those cited in lines 138–143); this should be clarified.
  - Reviewer 2 asks for clarification on the biological plausibility of batched experience replay and the meaning of α in eq. 3.
  - Reviewer 3 asks: which SRL variations are in “all policies” (line 374) for the randomized timestep experiment? For example, if SRL-2 is included, how does it provide open-loop actions for 16 steps?
  - Reviewer 3 asks about the performance delta of SRL-8/16 vs. SAC in Figure 2 (leftmost action sequence length resembles MPC): is this due to poor model fit?
  - Reviewer 4 notes that the < sign in the last row of Table 1 should be >.
- **The paper disproportionately occupies space with biological concepts (reviewer 1).** Section 6 should be removed or moved to the appendix, as it detracts from the technical core. Other reviewers find the biological discussion a strength, creating a disagreement; however, the motivation shift from robotics to biological plausibility is not well aligned (reviewer 4). For instance, backpropagation through time in SRL is not biologically plausible.
- **Minor issues.**
  - Two “Table 3” captions appear (pages 8 and 9; likely a table numbering error).
  - Use different symbols for macro action and per-step action in Equation 1 (both use a_{t'}).
  - Layout of figures and text is low quality; e.g., Section 6 title is oddly placed.
  - Sentence repetition in abstract lines 17–19.
  - Typos: line 104 “demonstrates” → “demonstrate”; line 376 “in when” → “when”; line 535 “introduces” → “introduce”; line 054 “the shows” redundant; line 49 missing period; line 265 model not subscripted.
  - Reviewer 2 disagrees with the statement that “simple tasks like walking can be performed without input states if learned properly”; biological agents also use sensory input for gait.
  - Reviewer 3 partially agrees with the statement (line 334) that SRL-J is at a disadvantage, but notes the agent still uses J=1 information via model/critic training, so learning curves are comparable.
  - Reviewer 4 questions why Gym-v2 versions were chosen over v3 or newer versions.
