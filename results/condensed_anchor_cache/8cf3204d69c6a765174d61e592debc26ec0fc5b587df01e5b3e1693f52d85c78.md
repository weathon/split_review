## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper extends Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to handle tied preferences by replacing the Bradley-Terry model with Rao-Kupper (DPO-RK) and Davidson (DPO-D) extensions. Experiments on neural machine translation (BLOOMZ-mt-7b) and summarization show that including ties in training does not degrade task performance (as standard DPO does) and provides stronger regularization via KL divergence. The evaluations rely on BLEURT and reward model win rates. While the motivation is clear and the theoretical discussion is sound, reviewers agree the empirical evaluation is limited: only two tasks, a single base model, and no comparisons to other preference optimization algorithms (e.g., IPO, KTO) or to standard DPO without ties. One reviewer considers handling ties a minor problem, questioning whether DPO cannot already tolerate near-ties. Additional concerns include the reliability of automatic tie construction, missing ablation on tie proportion, lack of human evaluation, and unexplored impact of the new parameter (υ).

### Strengths
- The motivation is straightforward and addresses a known limitation of DPO: the inability to handle ties, which forces discarding of ambiguous pairs. (R1, R2, R4)
- The proposed DPO-RK and DPO-D variants are well-motivated with clear mathematical derivations and a nice explanation of the different assumptions in the Rao-Kupper vs. Davidson models. (R1, R4)
- Including ties yields better policies than discarding ties (R1), or maintains similar task performance while mitigating the degradation standard DPO suffers when ties are injected (R2, R4), making the approach more data-efficient.
- The experimental analysis includes statistical measures and visualizations of training dynamics (reward margins, gradient scale factors) that add depth to the assessment. (R4)
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow. (R4, R2 for background)

### Weaknesses
- **Weak empirical evaluation**: Experiments are limited to machine translation (only BLOOMZ-mt-7b, a relatively weak model) and summarization, without testing on common alignment benchmarks (e.g., HH-RLHF) or more complex domains (open-ended generation like AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, MT-Bench; mathematical reasoning). (R1, R2, R4). One reviewer notes that stronger base LLMs (e.g., TowerLLM for MT) and multiple base models should be used. (R1)
- **Limited evaluation metrics**: Only BLEURT and RM win rate are reported—metrics that may not correlate well with human judgment. Results on additional automatic metrics (COMET, BLEU, ChrF) are missing, as are GPT-4 and human evaluations. (R1, R2)
- **No comparison to standard DPO without ties**: The paper does not directly compare DPO-RK/DPO-D to standard DPO on the same task performance metrics; one reviewer explicitly asks whether performance will be lower than DPO. (R3) Also missing comparisons to other preference optimization algorithms (KTO, IPO). (R1)
- **Limited novelty**: The work essentially substitutes the Bradley-Terry model with pre-existing Rao-Kupper and Davidson extensions. One reviewer considers handling ties a minor problem, arguing DPO can already tolerate very close pairs. (R3, R4)
- **Unreliable construction of tie pairs**: Ties are defined via an automatic distance-based metric (e.g., BLEURT score thresholds), but the paper lacks evaluation of how reliable this approach is for obtaining genuine ties. (R2)
- **Insufficient analysis of the new parameter υ**: The Rao-Kupper and Davidson models introduce a probability adjustment factor υ, but the paper does not discuss its impact, tuning, or sensitivity. (R4)
- **Missing ablations on tie proportion**: The proportion of ties vs. winning pairs is not varied; exploring this could inform what kinds of preference datasets make the proposed methods work well. (R1)
- **One reviewer recommends reporting case studies** to qualitatively illustrate the effect of the proposed methods. (R2)
- **Subjective comparison method**: The evaluation relies on relative subjective methods (reward model win rates) rather than classification or generation benchmarks. (R3)
