## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper presents WizardMath, a framework that enhances mathematical reasoning in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning from Evol-Instruct Feedback (RLEIF). It first applies supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on evol-instruct data to generate diverse math problems, then trains an Instruction Reward Model (IRM) and a Process Reward Model (PRM), finally using PPO for reinforcement learning. Experiments on GSM8k and MATH show that WizardMath surpasses open-source LLMs by a substantial margin and outperforms several proprietary models including GPT-3.5, Claude, PaLM-2, and Minerva on GSM8k, as well as Text-davinci-002, PaLM-1, and GPT-3 on MATH.

### Strengths
- **Strong results**: Improvements are often more than 10 points. The model outperforms strong proprietary models, which is impressive and likely to be quickly adopted by the community. (R1, R4)
- **Thorough experiments and baselines**: Experiments span scales from 100M to 70B, with different base models (Llama-3, Mistral, Mathstral, Qwen2.5). Comparisons include multiple math-specific models (Mammoth, Math-Shepherd, etc.) and ablations are provided (Section 4.4). (R1, R2, R3)
- **Novel integration of IRM and PRM**: The Instruction Reward Model is a novel and useful contribution; combining it with the Process Reward Model improves model reliability and accuracy. (R2, R4)
- **Scalable and automated method**: The whole pipeline (including data generation) is fully automated, making it scalable and potentially adaptable to other process-intensive reasoning domains (e.g., coding). (R1, R4)
- **Data efficiency and impact**: The method is data efficient. The synthetic data and process supervision paradigm have had a great impact on the community. (R3, R4)
- **One reviewer (R3) found no weaknesses**, reflecting a highly positive assessment. Conversely, R1 emphasized the strength of the results to justify a high overall rating.

### Weaknesses
- **Unfair comparison of baselines (major concern)**: The paper compares models that have undergone both SFT and RL against baselines that have only undergone SFT (e.g., MetaMath, DartMath). This is unfair because those SFT-only models could also be improved with RL. The effects of SFT and RL should be isolated. In Table 1, the base scores for some model variants (e.g., WizardMath-Mathstral, WizardMath-Qwen2.5) are missing, so improvement magnitudes are unclear. (R1, R2)
- **SFT component underperforms**: WizardMath-SFT (LLaMA2-7B) scores 35.6 on MATH, which lags behind other SFT methods like XwinMath and Skywork. This suggests the main contribution lies in the RL part, but the paper does not clearly emphasize that. (R2)
- **Table 7 lacks adequate baselines**: Missing comparisons with LLaMA-2-7B trained on DartMath training data. (R2)
- **Dependence on GPT-4 for PRM labels and synthetic data**: Using GPT-4 to annotate step-level labels (PRM) and to generate synthetic evolutionary instructions may limit scalability for researchers without access to such models. The paper does not explore alternative generation methods. There is also a tension: the paper criticizes hallucination from teacher models but then uses GPT-4 for step annotations, which may introduce incorrect step labels. (R1, R2, R4)
- **Marginal novelty claim**: Evol-Instruct and PRM both existed previously; this paper primarily combines them. A more comprehensive contribution would have included other domains like code. (R1)
- **Presentation issues (mixed opinions)**: 
  - R1 found the paper unclear, assuming prior familiarity with Evol-Instruct without adequate introduction; the narrative is messy (e.g., concepts like “grade school” and “high school” questions introduced once and not revisited); Figure 1 is confusing (pyramid, pie chart, cube not clearly explained); multiple typos and poorly worded sentences.
  - R2 and R3 considered the paper well-written and rich in detail. However, R2 still gave Presentation 3, noting the need for better organization to emphasize the RL contribution. R1 gave Presentation 2.
- **Missing experimental details and ablations**:
  - In Equation (1), how is the parameter \( m \) set? How is the IRM trained? (R2)
  - Lines 256–258: the method retains solutions with incorrect answers. How might that influence results? Consider using IRM to filter low-quality examples for SFT. (R2)
  - During PPO, two reward models are used, which can be time-consuming and computationally expensive. What strategies mitigate this? (R2)
  - The claim that the method addresses the “False-Positive issue in the problem-solving process” (L89-90) should be validated by comparing false-positive rates on a test set. (R2)
- **Limited domain scope**: RLEIF has only been evaluated on math reasoning. It would be valuable to test on other reasoning domains (logical deduction, causal inference) to assess generalizability. (R4)
