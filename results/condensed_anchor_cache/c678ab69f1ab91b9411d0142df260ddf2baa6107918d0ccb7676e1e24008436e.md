## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes NEDAG-GP, a nonparametric continuous DAG learning method based on NOTEARS-MLP that uses Gaussian Process (GP) partial derivatives to quantify edge strengths and can incorporate expert prior knowledge. Evaluations on synthetic and real-world (Sachs) data show improved structure accuracy over NOTEARS-MLP.

### Strengths
- The application of GP partial derivatives to compute weighted adjacency matrices is novel and addresses interpretability of edge strengths (R2, R3).
- The paper provides an intuitive, well-structured introduction to continuous structure learning and the challenges of expert knowledge integration (R1).
- The nonparametric nature of GPs makes the method appealing for complex nonlinear data, and the capacity to incorporate expert knowledge is attractive (R4).
- Experimental results on both synthetic and real-world datasets (Sachs) are insightful and visually intuitive, demonstrating practical applicability (R1, R3).
- The discussion around different characterizations of edge strength is insightful (R4).  
- One reviewer (R3) rated the contribution highly (score 5) and found the work well-situated in the literature, whereas others considered the novelty limited (R1, R2, R4) – a clear contrast in reviewer opinion.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited novelty and insufficient comparison.**  
  - The use of GPs and GP-based DAG methods is already known (e.g., Aglietti et al., Wilson et al.); NEDAG-GP mainly sets up the weighted adjacency matrix differently, which is a minor variation (R2, R4). Only one baseline (NOTEARS-MLP) is compared; DAGMA, which has superseded NOTEARS in this area, is missing (R4). Classic combinatorial search algorithms (PC, GES) are not mentioned or compared, and the claim that combinatorial methods lack global visibility is false – GES explicitly addresses this (R1, R2).  
- **Unclear motivation and missing theoretical guarantees.**  
  - The paper does not clarify whether it addresses causal DAG learning or merely dependency modeling; if causal, a discussion on identifiability (unique DAG vs. equivalence class) is absent (R1). Identifiability or consistency guarantees for the proposed method are not provided (R2). The motivation for preferring GPs over neural networks is not fully justified beyond “nonparametric”; similarities and differences (e.g., “Deep neural networks as Gaussian processes”) are not explored (R2).  
- **Expert knowledge incorporation is not novel or clearly explained.**  
  - Expressing prior knowledge (forbidden edges, edge weights, topological order) via regularization or constraints is straightforward in existing continuous learning frameworks; Section 4.2 does not explain how constraints are actually enforced, and representing knowledge as exact numerical values seems harder, not easier (R2, R4). There is no ablation study comparing NEDAG-GP against NOTEARS augmented with nonparametric estimation or expert knowledge separately (R3).  
- **Experimental evidence is limited or narrow.**  
  - Figure 3 evaluates methods on only a 3-point coarse grid and suggests that NEDAG-GP’s good performance relies on the ground truth being a GP; evaluations on a wider range of functions are needed (R4). No uncertainty quantification results are provided despite using GPs, which is a missed strength (R4). The number of replications for Table 2 is not reported (R4).  
- **Presentation and writing issues.**  
  - Several imprecise statements: the claim that a single weight matrix can reveal full causal relationships ignores distributional assumptions (L65, R2); the claim about combinatorial search lacking global visibility is false (R2). Notation is unclear: Equation 4 uses \(H^1\) without definition, Equation 5 bolds \(x_k\) inconsistently, \(\sigma\) and \(\ell\) in Section 4.1 are not indexed by \(j,k\), and \(\theta\) in Appendix B.1 is unexplained (R3, R4). A pseudocode of the algorithm is missing (R3). None of the in-text or reference list citations include years (R4). Figure 2 is not referenced in the text, and Section 5 is not substantive enough to stand alone (R4). The Balancing Scoring Function in Appendix B.5 lacks a mathematical definition (R4).
