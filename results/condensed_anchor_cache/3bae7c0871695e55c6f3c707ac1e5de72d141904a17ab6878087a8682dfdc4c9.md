## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes TAMO, which treats tables as an independent modality by encoding them with a hypergraph neural network (nodes = cells, hyperedges = headers) and injecting the learned embeddings into LLMs. It also introduces StructQA, a synthetic benchmark for evaluating robustness to row/column permutations. Experiments on HiTab, WikiTQ, WikiSQL, FeTaQA, and StructQA show a 42.65% average relative gain across multiple LLMs (Llama2, TableLlama, Mistral) and training protocols (SFT, LoRA). Reviewers find the direction promising but raise concerns about novelty (prior graph-based table encoders exist, e.g., HyTrel), insufficient comparisons, unclear training details, limited experimental scope, and clarity issues. Reviewer 1 is notably more positive (rating 8) and open to raising the score after clarifications; the other three reviewers give 5. The paper’s clarity is contested: Reviewer 4 calls it well-written, while Reviewer 3 identifies significant terminology misuse and grammatical errors.

### Strengths
- **Novel direction**: Treating tables as an independent modality using hypergraphs to capture structural relationships (both simple and complex schemas) is a fresh approach beyond plain serialization (R1, R3, R4).
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: Experiments cover four standard TableQA datasets (HiTab, WikiTQ, WikiSQL, FeTaQA) plus the new StructQA, across three LLM backbones (Llama2-7b, TableLlama, Mistral) and two training modes (SFT, LoRA) (R1, R3).
- **Valuable benchmark**: StructQA provides a focused test for table structure understanding and robustness to permutations, contributing to future research (R1, R2, R4).
- **Strong empirical gains**: TAMO achieves an average relative improvement of 42.65% over baselines, demonstrating effectiveness (R1, R4).
- **Addresses a critical problem**: The paper rightly identifies that existing LLM serialization loses structural information and hinders reasoning (R2, R3).
- **Minority positive opinion**: Reviewer 1 considers the paper good and is willing to increase the score if key clarifications are provided; Reviewer 4 finds the paper well-written and easy to follow (contrasting with Reviewer 3’s clarity critique).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns**: Several reviewers note that encoding tables with graphs (including hypergraphs) has been done before (e.g., HyTrel: Hypergraph-enhanced Tabular Data Representation Learning, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11049; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07692). Although those works used smaller models, simply substituting them with LLMs is seen as incremental (R2, R4).
- **Insufficient comparisons**: The paper does not compare hypergraph encoding with simpler alternatives such as 2D positional embeddings or data augmentation for permutation invariance. It beats naïve baselines but does not prove superiority over other trainable methods of similar or lower complexity (R3). There is also no baseline or discussion of potential errors when injecting hypergraph embeddings into LLMs (R1).
- **Missing ablation and analysis**: No ablation studies isolate the impact of individual components (R3). The learned hypergraph representation itself is not analyzed (e.g., predicting table structure from the embedding) (R1). No experiment justifies why the textual serialization is still needed alongside the hypergraph embeddings (R2 Q1).
- **Unclear training protocol**: It is not specified whether TAMO is trained once generically or fine-tuned separately for each task, nor whether the training data is StructQA, the in-domain training sets, or a mix (R1 Q2, R2 Q3, R3 Q1).
- **Limited experimental scope**: All experiments use a context length of 1024 and single, short tables; scalability to huge tables (millions of rows/columns) or multi-table scenarios is not demonstrated (R4 W3). Only Llama2-7b is used as backbone; more advanced open-source LLMs like Llama3.1-8b could have given stronger baselines (R2 Q4).
- **Computational overhead**: Encoding every cell/row/column via hypergraph introduces significant time and memory costs, especially for large tables; the paper lacks a complexity analysis (R4 W2).
- **Real-world applicability**: The method assumes tables are already parsed into a structured format, but in practice tables are often embedded in unstructured text. This limits TAMO’s applicability (R3). Additionally, because the method prepends embeddings to the input, it can only be used with open-source LLMs, not closed models like GPT-4 (R4 W4).
- **Clarity and writing (disagreement among reviewers)**: Reviewer 3 identifies frequent misuse of terminology (e.g., confusing “tokens” with “embeddings”) and broken sentences (e.g., “TAMO+_SFT is competitive with specialist SOTA methods and the superiority of using hypergraphs…”, “This approach incorporates… can perfectly model…”), which obscure the technical content. In contrast, Reviewer 4 states the paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- **Repetition and overstatement**: The paper repeatedly emphasizes the limitations of serialization and the novelty of the modality approach, making it longer than necessary (R3).
- **Additional missing analysis**: Questions about other types of order permutations beyond row/column shuffling (R4 Q1) and whether training can be done purely on synthetic data like StructQA (R1 Q2) remain unanswered.
