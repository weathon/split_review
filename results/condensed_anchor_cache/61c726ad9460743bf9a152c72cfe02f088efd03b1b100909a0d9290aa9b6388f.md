## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper investigates how diffusion models generalize beyond the training set, proposing a “generalization through variance” phenomenon: the inherent noise in the denoising score matching (DSM) objective (whose target is only equal to the true score in expectation) strongly influences inductive biases. Using a physics-inspired path integral approach, the authors derive closed-form expressions for the sampling distribution of a diffusion model, captured by a V‑kernel that depends on the covariance of the noisy score proxy. They analyze three cases—memorization, a linear model, and a neural tangent kernel (NTK) model—and conclude that generalization occurs if and only if the V‑kernel is nonzero.

### Strengths
- Addresses a timely and central question: understanding how diffusion models generate novel samples similar to the training set.
- The analysis is dedicated to the generative problem and does not import generalization measures from other tasks.
- Intricate details of the diffusion models are clearly discussed, and the paper is well written, conveying ideas interestingly.
- The analytic stochastic path‑integral technique is novel and yields closed‑form expressions for the sampling distribution, with a focus on covariance structure.
- The approach is exemplified on two (three) analyzable architectures: memorization, linear model, and NTK model, demonstrating the V‑kernel as a useful tool.
- The theoretical framework represents a significant leap in understanding the mechanics behind diffusion model generalization.
- The theoretical analysis is of high quality, and the writing is clear.
- Expressing the PF‑ODE in terms of a path integral provides the key insight that generalization occurs iff the V‑kernel is nonzero.
- The paper provides a comprehensive study of three distinct cases through the lens of the V‑kernel.

### Weaknesses
1. **Insufficient characterization of meaningful generalization.**  Multiple reviewers note that the condition “generalization iff V‑kernel nonzero” only captures deviation from the training data but does not ensure that generated samples are *meaningfully* different (e.g., share similar features, lie on a reasonable manifold, are diverse and high‑quality). The paper does not discuss what properties of the V‑kernel lead to desirable generation; the argument that samples are different is insufficient without showing why they are not simply random noise or noisy duplicates of training points. (R1 Weakness 1, R3 Weakness i)

2. **Lack of interpretability and practical implications of the V‑kernel expressions.**  The closed‑form equations (Proposition 5.1‑5.2; equations (9), (13), (17)) are presented without sufficient explanation of how they should be interpreted, how easy they are to compute, or how their structure influences the reverse sampling process. The paper does not clarify how different terms relate to data properties, model architecture, or feature‑related inductive biases, nor does it discuss the implications for sample diversity and quality. A more intuitive breakdown and practical analysis are needed. (R1 Weakness 2, R3 Weakness ii)

3. **Lack of empirical validation and connection to practical improvements.**  The theoretical claims are not supported by simulations or experiments. Concrete examples illustrating how the predicted covariance structure directly affects the generative process—and how this effect compares with other factors such as model architecture or training set size—are absent. Without such validation, the theory remains abstract and its practical relevance is unclear. The paper also offers no concrete guidance on how the understanding of the V‑kernel could be used to design better architectures, training procedures, or sampling strategies for real‑world diffusion models. (R2 Weakness 1, R3 Weakness iii, R2 Question 4)

4. **Missing theoretical link between the V‑kernel and quantitative generalization metrics.**  The paper introduces the V‑kernel but does not provide a clear mathematical connection to measures of generalization error (e.g., expected generalization error or its bounds). A framework that explicitly relates V‑kernel properties to such metrics would allow a more quantitative assessment of generalization. (R2 Weakness 2, R2 Question 3)

5. **Unclear or insufficiently justified definitions, claims, and positioning.**  
   - “Boundary regions” (line 67) are used repeatedly without a precise definition. (R1 Q1)  
   - The claim that models generalize better when “somewhat underparameterized” (line 75) appears to conflict with the (Karras et al., 2024) paper cited earlier; no reconciliation is provided. (R1 Q2)  
   - Interpolation is called “non‑trivial generalization” (line 78) without justification. (R1 Q3)  
   - The operator 𝒟 in equation (6) is not defined. (R1 Q5)  
   - The assertion at the bottom of page 1 that “there is arguably no theory” overlooking existing work on diffusion‑model generalization (e.g., Li et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024). (R2 Q1)

6. **Unaddressed assumptions about Gaussianity and the role of the starting point.**  
   - The role of the noisy starting point \(X_T\)—which itself is generated from training data in a noisy manner—in contributing to generalization is not discussed. (R1 Q6)  
   - After neglecting higher‑order terms in equation (7), the estimator distribution is approximated as Gaussian. This may tacitly assume the data distribution is Gaussian; if the data were Gaussian, would the result simply reduce to the approximated integral? The paper does not address whether this assumption limits the generality of the analysis. (R1 Q7)
