## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces ToolEmu, a framework that uses an LM to emulate tool execution and an LM-based automatic safety evaluator to test LM agents across a diverse set of toolkits and scenarios. The goal is to identify risks (e.g., data leakage, financial loss) in a scalable manner. The paper demonstrates that 68.8% of failures identified by ToolEmu are validated as real-world agent failures via human evaluation, and that failure generation takes under 15 minutes versus 8 hours for a real bash sandbox. A benchmark with 9 toolkits and 144 test cases is used to risk-analyze current LM agents. Reviewers acknowledge the importance of the problem and the novelty of using LMs as both emulator and evaluator, but raise concerns about methodology reliability, sample size, evaluation rigor, clarity of severity criteria, and the generalizability of emulated failures to real-world plugins. One reviewer is notably more critical, giving a rating of 6 (fair soundness), while the other two rate 8 (good soundness).

### Strengths
- **Important and timely problem**: Evaluating LM agents integrated with tools/plugins is critical as these systems become widely deployed; conventional testing does not scale.
- **Automatic Safety Evaluator**: Development of an LM-based automatic evaluator for agent failures and risk quantification is a significant contribution.
- **Comprehensive test cases**: Inclusion of 9 high-stakes toolkits and 144 test scenarios, some not previously evaluated in other benchmarks, is valuable.
- **Human validation of failures**: 68.8% of failures identified by ToolEmu were confirmed as valid real-world failures by human annotators.
- **Efficiency**: ToolEmu generates failures in under 15 minutes, a dramatic improvement over the 8 hours required by a real bash terminal sandbox.
- **Reduced manual labor**: Using LLM intelligence to emulate tools reduces the need to implement real tool environments for every test scenario.
- **Well-motivated toolkit design**: The curated toolkit covers a broad range of risk scenarios.
- **Well-organized paper**: One reviewer noted the paper is well-organized and has extensive supporting materials in the appendix (though another reviewer suggested reorganization).

### Weaknesses
- **Reliability of LM-based evaluation**: Using LMs to evaluate other LMs raises concerns about validity. One reviewer emphasized that it is unclear how biases or limitations in the evaluating LM might affect the evaluation of the agent under test, and whether ToolEmu itself could incur risks. Another reviewer noted that not all identified failures are true failures, either because of invalid emulator behavior or invalid classification. The paper’s own discussion acknowledges that LM-based emulators and evaluators might occasionally produce critical issues or incorrect evaluations.
- **Sample size and validation sufficiency**: The benchmark uses only 144 test cases, and only 100 of those were used for human validation of the evaluator. One reviewer questioned whether this sample size provides sufficient coverage and whether the validation is robust, especially given that the Cohen’s κ between human annotators was less than 0.5, raising doubts about whether human annotations can be considered ground truth. Another reviewer echoed that the 6 out of 7 severe failures instantiated on a real bash terminal lack statistical context and may not be representative.
- **Unclear criteria for severity**: The paper does not provide clear criteria for what constitutes a “severe” failure. The example in the introduction (7 severe failures, 6 instantiated) is interesting but lacks a principled definition and statistical grounding.
- **Time measurement ambiguity**: It is not clear whether the reported times (8 hours vs. under 15 minutes) are averages or one-time measurements, and whether the process to instantiate failures was straightforward or involved complexities.
- **Generalizability to real-world plugins**: One reviewer questioned whether the range of plugin behaviors emulated by ToolEmu matches the diversity of real-world software plugins, and whether the failures identified are representative of real deployments. The paper does not address how ToolEmu could be scaled to scenarios with multiple interacting plugins, which may introduce new risk scenarios.
- **Insufficient interpretability**: One reviewer noted that the contribution could be improved by providing more interpretability of the failures and the evaluator’s decisions.
- **Sensitivity to prompting**: It is unclear how sensitive ToolEmu’s findings are to minor variations in agent prompts or emulated plugin responses (e.g., word choice, punctuation). The paper does not discuss this robustness.
- **Lack of debugging insights**: The paper does not show how ToolEmu’s results can provide actionable insights for debugging and fixing agent problems, nor how it might be used to assess relative risk of real-world scenarios.
- **Paper organization**: One reviewer noted that frequent transitions between main text and appendices (due to space constraints) can be confusing and suggested optimization. (Another reviewer considered the presentation excellent, so this is a minor point of disagreement.)
