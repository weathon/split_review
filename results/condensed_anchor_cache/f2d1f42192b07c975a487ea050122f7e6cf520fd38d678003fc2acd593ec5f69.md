## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Neural Field Classifier (NFC), which formulates neural field methods as classification rather than regression by encoding continuous targets (e.g., RGB) into high‑dimensional discrete encodings (e.g., 8‑bit binary vectors) and optimizing a binary cross‑entropy loss. Experiments on novel view synthesis (NeRF synthetic, LLFF, Replica) and neural surface reconstruction show that this classification variant consistently outperforms its regression counterpart at negligible extra computational cost.

### Strengths
- **Clear motivation and writing.** The paper is easy to follow and well‑structured (R1, R3, R4).
- **Novel and general idea.** To the reviewers’ knowledge, this is the first investigation of classification as an objective for neural fields. The approach is simple, effective, and architecture‑agnostic, making it applicable to many neural field tasks (R2, R3, R4).
- **Thorough experiments.** Extensive comparisons across multiple datasets (NeRF synthetic, LLFF, Replica) with an array of NeRF variants (DVGO, etc.) demonstrate consistent improvements in metrics. The paper also evaluates robustness to sparse inputs, corrupted images, and dynamic scenes (R1, R2, R3, R4).
- **Informative visualizations.** Figures clearly convey the main ideas and show marked qualitative improvement over regression baselines (R2, R3).

### Weaknesses
- **Outdated baselines and missing state‑of‑the‑art comparisons.** The compared methods are from 2020‑2022 (e.g., DVGO). DVGO is designed for acceleration, not accuracy, making accuracy comparisons unfair. Comparisons with current state‑of‑the‑art NeRF accuracy methods are missing (R1, R4).
- **Missing computational cost analysis.** The paper does not report FLOPs or wall‑clock time comparisons with DVGO (R1).
- **Insufficient ablation studies.**
    - The regression loss is still included in the total loss (Eq. 6). An ablation that removes the regression loss entirely is needed to isolate the impact of the classification loss and determine whether the regression loss is necessary or negligible (R4).
    - The target‑encoding (text encoding) ablation is performed on only two scenes of one dataset. A comprehensive study across datasets and scenes is required to assess whether the encoding is consistently beneficial and whether it is essential (R4).
- **Lack of theoretical analysis.** No explanation is provided for *why* classification outperforms regression or why it is more robust to corruption and sparse inputs (R3).
- **Unclear method details.**
    - How is density (sigma) output? Is it still regressed, and if so, why not also treat it as a classification problem? (R2)
    - How is volume rendering performed with the binary classification output? How are the bits integrated in the standard volume rendering equation? (R2)
    - The number of training views used for the Replica scenes is not specified, making the qualitative results for regression (Fig. 3) appear unreasonably poor and raising concerns about fair comparison (R2).
- **Questionable qualitative comparisons.** The regression baseline results in the figures look worse than expected for a vanilla NeRF with a reasonable number of views, suggesting possible unfairness in visual comparisons (R2).
- **Missing reference to related work.** A relevant prior work [a] that uses CNN encoders is cited; the paper should discuss how the proposed classifier differs from this approach (R1).
- **Domain scope.** Experiments are primarily restricted to novel view synthesis. While some neural surface reconstruction is included, broader generalization to other neural field applications is not demonstrated (R2; note R4 acknowledges the surface experiments but agrees the scope is limited).
