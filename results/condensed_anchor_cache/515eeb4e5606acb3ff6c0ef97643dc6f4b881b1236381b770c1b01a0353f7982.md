## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a cross-modal knowledge distillation method that decouples features into low-frequency (modality-generic) and high-frequency (modality-specific) components using Fourier transforms. The authors apply MSE loss on low-frequency features and a logarithmic MSE loss on high-frequency features, plus feature scale alignment and a shared classifier (feature space alignment) to handle distributional shifts. Experiments on audio-visual, image-text, and depth-RGB tasks show improvements over prior cross-modal KD methods. The idea of decoupling by frequency is interesting, but reviewers raised concerns about the motivation and evidence for the frequency claim, insufficient differentiation from related works, and some experimental issues.

### Strengths
- The observation that low-frequency features carry modality-generic information and high-frequency features carry modality-specific information is interesting and novel in cross-modal distillation.
- Decoupling features via Fourier transform is a sensible approach to treat different frequency components differently.
- The method achieves significant improvements (a few points) across a wide range of datasets spanning audio-visual (CREMA-D, AVE, VGGSound), image-text (MMIMDB, Food-101, NUS-WIDE), and depth-RGB (NYUDv2, SUN-RGBD) tasks.
- The paper provides diverse and extensive experiments, including classification and semantic segmentation, as well as adequate ablation studies on the main components.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow (noted by some, though others found presentation basic).
- It is reasonable to explore using a pretrained model from a related modality as teacher, and the frequency perspective is fresh in the literature.

### Weaknesses
1. **CKA evidence for frequency-modality correlation is weak.** The CKA scores between modalities are very small (e.g., 0.3 vs. 0.28 on AVE), and the difference between low-frequency and original features is marginal. It is unclear whether such small differences are statistically significant and can justify the claimed correlation or the observed performance gains.
2. **Insufficient differentiation from prior frequency-based knowledge distillation works.** The core contribution overlaps with FreeKD (semantic frequency prompt) and "Extracting Low-/High- Frequency Knowledge from GNN to MLP." The paper does not clearly explain how the proposed method differs from these works.
3. **The feature space alignment module (shared classifier) is similar to prior work.** The use of a shared classifier between teacher and student is very similar to the method in "Knowledge distillation via softmax regression representation learning" (ICLR 2021) and lacks clear novelty or justification.
4. **Experimental results may not be reproducible as claimed.** The paper states it follows the settings of (Huo et al., 2024) (C2KD), but the reported results in Table 1 & 2 differ from the corresponding tables in C2KD paper. This discrepancy needs clarification.
5. **Frequency decomposition can be harmful in some cases.** In Table 3, on CREMA-D with audio modality, the proposed frequency decomposition hurts performance (60.9 → 60.8). Also, without the LogMSE loss, the method achieves the same score as DIST baseline, indicating the performance gain may come from a common trick.
6. **Motivation for logarithmic MSE is unclear.** The use of LogMSE for high-frequency features is not well justified; alternative approaches (e.g., normalizing features before loss, or simply weighting the high-frequency loss less) could achieve the same effect. An ablation comparing these alternatives is missing.
7. **The need for the feature scale alignment module is not demonstrated.** The paper does not provide an ablation where frequency decomposition, scale loss, and LogMSE are used but alignment is turned off, so the actual contribution of alignment is unknown. Its motivation is also insufficiently explained.
8. **Missing visual examples to qualitatively understand high/low-frequency features.** The paper would benefit from showing what these frequency-decoupled features look like across modalities to support the claim that low-frequency captures modality-generic information.
9. **Some equations are unnecessary.** Basic definitions of MSE, feature standardization, and normalization (Eqs. 4, 7, 8, 9) can be removed without harming readability.
10. **Unclear setting for cross-modal distillation and when it helps.** The paper assumes only one modality is available for the student, but the mention of "vision-to-language distillation" suggests both text and vision could be used. It is not discussed whether uni-modal (same-modality) distillation should serve as an upper bound, or when cross-modal distillation actually helps or hurts.
11. **Missing discussion on modality bias.** Table 1 shows visual modality is favorable in CREMA-D while audio dominates in AVE and VGGSound; a discussion of modality bias across datasets could provide deeper insights into cross-modal distillation.
