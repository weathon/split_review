## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper proposes a new adversarial attack vector for LLMs by eliciting confidence scores under black-box access. The authors argue that in closed-source models only the final output is available, preventing soft-label attacks. They show that prompting LLMs for confidence ratings (e.g., via a template) yields calibrated estimates that can guide word-substitution perturbations. Experiments on LLaMA 3-8B Instruct and Mistral-7B Instruct-v0.3 across SST2, AG-News, and StrategyQA achieve state-of-the-art attack success rates compared to hard-label black-box baselines (Self-Fool Word Sub, SSPAttack). The method is presented with diagrams, equations, pseudo-code, and qualitative analysis. The code is public.

### Strengths
- **Novelty**: First paper to apply confidence elicitation to construct adversarial examples for LLMs, addressing the unachievable soft-label issue in black-box attacks.
- **Simplicity and efficiency**: The attack is easy to conduct and requires fewer queries than existing black-box adversarial attacks while maintaining higher semantic similarity in perturbed samples.
- **Presentation**: Clear figures, examples, equations, and pseudo-code make the method and related work easily understandable.
- **Experimental thoroughness**: Multiple datasets (SST2, AG-News, StrategyQA), two models (LLaMA 3, Mistral V0.3), comparison against hard-label baselines (Self-Fool Word Sub, SSPAttack), and sensitivity analyses. The authors also test the trustworthiness of confidence elicitation (Figure 3), showing it is calibrated (not hallucinated).
- **Interpretability**: The attacks are relatively interpretable and provide a starting point for understanding how LLMs perform classification tasks.

### Weaknesses
- **Limited task scope**: The attack is evaluated only on classification tasks, while LLMs are primarily used for generation. The relevance to real-world threats (e.g., jailbreaks, harmful request rejection) is unclear. In harmful scenarios, the probability of a productive completion may be tiny, and CEAttack might not outperform hard-label methods. One reviewer (Reviewer 2) notes that classification is a minor part of LLM usage and has limited impact.
- **Confidence estimation not properly validated**: The elicited confidence scores are never measured for calibration against simple baselines (e.g., multiple queries at temperature 1). No comparison to simpler confidence estimation techniques is provided. The paper mentions Expected Calibration Error (ECE) but lacks a clear explanation of how the calculation ensures the reliability of the estimates. This makes it hard to assess the validity of the calibration claims.
- **No evaluation on actual closed-source models**: Experiments use open-source instruction-tuned 8B models. It is unclear if the method works on true black-box APIs (e.g., GPT-4 or Gemini). Smaller models may not respond to the confidence-eliciting prompt template. One reviewer notes that closed-source models should be more resilient to non-semantic input perturbations and may be better at confidence elicitation, so testing them is necessary.
- **Missing baselines from prompt optimization**: The paper does not compare against prompt optimization methods like Tree of Attacks (ToA) or TAP, which already use adherence signals and achieve higher performance in realistic jailbreak scenarios.
- **Attack quality concerns**: The absolute attack success rate is low, and many generated examples change the meaning of the sentence or make it less meaningful (e.g., “Did the Wehrmacht affect the outcome of the War to End All Wars?” → “Did the Wehrmacht impacting the outcome of the War to Conclude All Wars?” on StrategyQA). This raises doubts about whether the attacks achieve the claimed semantic similarity.
- **Computational complexity**: The synonym replacement method grows exponentially with input length, leading to prolonged processing times for longer sentences. The paper does not report total attack time or average number of calls per adversarial example. It is unclear whether the attack takes tens of hours or tens of minutes.
- **Missing defense discussion**: No exploration or discussion of potential defenses against CEAttack, which would be valuable for understanding its practical impact.
- **Clarity issues**: It is unclear whether the optimized question is evaluated inside or outside the prompt template used for confidence elicitation. The adversarial objective should distinguish whether flipping the confidence score within the template is equivalent to flipping the final prediction. Additionally, the text is vague about the Mistral model version (v0.2 or v0.3), the abstract does not state the model size, and on line 267 the ASR acronym is defined incorrectly as “after success rate.” Table text is too small.
- **Generalizability of confidence values**: The assumption that elicited confidence is well-calibrated across different LLMs and linguistic contexts is insufficiently explored. It is unknown whether most mainstream LLMs even support confidence elicitation.

### Reviewer Disagreement
Reviewer 2 (score 5) is significantly more critical of the paper’s scope and impact, emphasizing the limited relevance of classification attacks and the missing comparison to prompt optimization methods. Reviewers 3 and 4 (scores 8 each) praise the novelty and presentation but also raise serious concerns about the evaluation of confidence estimation, real-world applicability, and threat model relevance. Reviewer 1 (score 6) provides balanced criticism, especially regarding task scope, complexity, and calibration details.
