## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces Generative Adversarial Equilibrium Solvers (GAES), a GAN-based method that learns to map pseudo-games (where players’ actions affect both payoffs and feasible action spaces) to generalized Nash equilibria (GNE) or competitive equilibria (CE) from a sample of problem instances. Exploitability is used as the loss function, with a discriminator approximating best responses and a generator outputting equilibrium candidates. Theoretical guarantees (convergence to stationary points, sample complexity bounds) are provided, and experiments cover Nash equilibria in normal-form games, CE in Arrow-Debreu exchange economies, and GNE in an environmental model of the Kyoto mechanism. Results show state-of-the-art performance in several benchmarks.

### Strengths
- **Novel and scalable method**: GAES provides a simple, efficient, and scalable algorithm for training generic GNE solvers, overcoming previous challenges of supervised learning (e.g., gradient intractability, non-Lipschitz exploitability, unbounded gradients). The GAN formulation is novel and strongly backed by theory. (Reviewers 1,2,4)
- **Strong theoretical backing**: Convergence to a stationary point of exploitability and generalization bounds are given. (Reviewers 1,2) [Note: Reviewer 3 questions the link between stationary points and GNE; see Weaknesses.]
- **Impressive empirical results**: Experiments on non-trivial, non-monotone/non-jointly-convex games (Arrow-Debreu economies, Kyoto mechanism) demonstrate effectiveness and outperform prior methods. Results are “on average better than at least 99% of action profiles.” (Reviewers 1,2,3)
- **Generality of pseudo-games**: The method applies to a wide range of game-theoretic models, including normal-form games, competitive economies, and environmental economics. (Reviewers 1,4)
- **Innovative approach**: Using neural networks as function approximators for equilibrium computation is innovative and could accelerate practical equilibrium computation. (Reviewer 4)

### Weaknesses
- **Assumption 1 (strong concavity) is violated in key settings**: The assumption that the utility function is strongly concave is not satisfied in the Arrow-Debreu competitive economy (or other practical settings). A small discussion about regularization is needed. This gap between theory and practice is significant. (Reviewers 2,3) [Reviewer 3: “It should be the case that a ‘small’ regularizer can always be incorporated without affecting the equilibria by much, but I think this should be discussed in more detail.”]
- **No guarantee that a stationary point corresponds to a GNE**: Theorem 4.1 guarantees convergence to a stationary point, but it is not shown that such points are necessarily GNE. The paper’s narrative repeatedly claims the method maps pseudo-games to GNE, which may not be theoretically sound. (Reviewer 3)
- **Algorithmic closeness to prior work (Exploitability minimization in games and beyond)**: The approach is very close to that paper, limiting the algorithmic contribution. The authors must highlight differences in more detail. (Reviewer 3)
- **Different network architectures used in experiments**: The two experimental settings use different network architectures, which undermines the claim of GAES as a general solver for GNE. (Reviewer 2)
- **No performance guarantee on the training set**: The theoretical analysis does not provide a guarantee for performance (e.g., exploitability) on the training set itself. (Reviewer 2)
- **State-of-the-art complexity claim is unclear and may be incorrect**: Footnote 4 claims O(1/ε³) complexity, a major improvement over O(1/ε⁶), but the class of problems this applies to is not explained precisely. Several recent papers (e.g., “Faster single-loop algorithms for minimax optimization…”, “faster stochastic algorithms… under Polyak-Łojasiewicz Conditions”, “Doubly smoothed GDA…” ) appear to obtain much better dependency. (Reviewer 3)
- **Missing discussion of equilibrium selection**: The paper focuses on finding a single equilibrium, but many games have multiple equilibria. (Reviewer 4)
- **Presentation issues**: The default template title is still in the PDF. Figures are small and blurry (not vector graphics). References need polishing (arXiv-only citations for old papers, inconsistent URLs, formatting issues, overfull equations in appendix). (Reviewers 3,4)
- **Questions overlapping with criticisms/missing experiments**:
  - Can GAES scale to modern games with multiple neural networks (e.g., GANs, multi-agent RL)? What are the main technical challenges? (Reviewer 1)
  - Is there a guarantee when the utility function only satisfies convexity (not strong concavity)? (Reviewer 2)
  - How does performance degrade with approximate discriminators? Is there a way to quantify required discriminator accuracy? (Reviewer 4)
  - Could GAES be applied to other solution concepts like correlated equilibria? How would the formulation need to change? (Reviewer 4)

### Reviewer contrast
Reviewer 1 is extremely positive (score 1, confidence 5), seeing no special weakness and praising the framework as novel, well-backed, and empirically strong. Reviewers 2, 3, and 4 are moderately positive (scores 5,6,6) but raise significant concerns about theoretical assumptions, experimental generality, algorithmic novelty, presentation, and missing analyses. While all reviewers acknowledge the novelty and potential impact, the majority have reservations that lower their confidence.
