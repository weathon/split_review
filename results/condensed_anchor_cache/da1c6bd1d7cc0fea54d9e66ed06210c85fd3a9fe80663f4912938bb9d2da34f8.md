## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces FAMMA, an open-source benchmark for financial multilingual multimodal question answering, containing 1,758 QA pairs from university textbooks and exams covering eight finance subfields. Questions mix text with charts, tables, and diagrams, and include English, Chinese, and French. Evaluations show even advanced models like GPT-4o achieve only 42.85% accuracy. The authors also explore retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, and perform error analysis. One reviewer was more critical (score 5), particularly regarding data quality assurance and copyright concerns, while others praised the thoroughness and the error analysis.

### Strengths
1. **Novelty and relevance** — FAMMA is a timely, innovative benchmark tailored to the financial domain with a unique focus on both multilingual and multimodal question answering, addressing a gap as many existing benchmarks become saturated.
2. **High-quality data sourcing** — The data is sourced from university finance courses, class quizzes, and exams, with annotations and cross-verification by domain experts, enhancing reliability (R2, R3).
3. **Excellent error analysis** — The paper includes a detailed error analysis on GPT-4o outputs, identifying categories such as lack of financial domain knowledge and numerical inaccuracies, which helps understand remaining challenges (R2).
4. **Sound evaluation set** — Evaluation covers a well-balanced mix of closed and open-source vision-language models (including GPT-4o, two versions of Claude-Sonnet, and Qwen2-VL-70B), with average of three runs reported, strengthening conclusions about LLM shortcomings (R2).
5. **Results highlight gap** — The finding that even recent powerful LLMs struggle significantly (42% accuracy vs. 56% human) demonstrates the benchmark’s difficulty and potential to drive research in expert financial QA (R3).

### Weaknesses
1. **Data quality assurance lacking** — The paper does not provide quantitative analysis of the two-stage data cleaning process (initial cleaning and difficulty-level annotation). A breakdown with quantitative results is needed to assess reliability and consistency (R1). Additionally, the reported human performance of 56% (L270) is relatively low, raising concerns about dataset quality. Reasons for low human performance (e.g., test-takers unfamiliar with all subdomains, question ambiguity) are not explained, and no inter-annotator agreement rate is reported for the cross-verification step where each question is checked by 2-3 annotators (R2: L218).
2. **Copyright and usage rights** — All data appears derived from textbooks, exams, and educational materials that may have restricted research use. The paper must explicitly address copyright concerns by providing evidence of permissions or free-use provisions. One reviewer flagged this as a significant legal risk, and a separate reviewer echoed the question of copyright for public release (R1, R3).
3. **Potential data contamination** — The paper does not discuss the possibility that some questions sourced from online resources may appear in LLM pretraining corpora, which could affect evaluation validity (R3).
4. **Chain-of-Thought results undermine the benchmark’s claimed difficulty** — CoT prompting on a subset of 100 errors corrected 44 errors. If extrapolated, this would yield ~68% accuracy on the full test set, surpassing the human 56% reported in Table 2. This contradicts the claim that FAMMA poses a significant challenge for LLMs. The authors should report full test-set performance for RAG and CoT instead of only a subset. Moreover, it is unclear whether RAG/CoT negatively impact questions already answered correctly by the vanilla approach (R2, R3).
5. **Incomplete RAG and inference-time method details** — The RAG setup is confusing: the embedding model, prompt used, and whether a commercial offering is used are not specified. Additionally, other inference-time methods (self-consistency, program-of-thoughts) were not evaluated. The o1-preview model’s performance is also not reported, and the “o1-style reasoning” description should cite the original Chain-of-Thought paper (R2, R3).
6. **Limited evaluation scope** — The set of evaluated multimodal LLMs is small (primarily two proprietary models and one open-source model). A broader range of multimodal models would provide a more comprehensive assessment and better validate the benchmark’s utility (R1). Also, only one open-source model is tested.
7. **Small size of non-English subsets** — The numbers of Chinese and French questions are limited, reducing interpretability of multilingual results. The authors could augment these subsets by translating English questions using GPT-4o with human verification (R3).
8. **Lack of multimodal ablation** — The paper does not show performance with text-only input, nor does it ablate the impact of displaying tables as images versus converting them to text. This would help assess how critical multimodal input is for the benchmark (R3).
9. **Minor issues** — Short-form answers (multiple choice or short numbers) make automatic evaluation easy, but models may arrive at the correct answer for wrong reasons; requiring models to show work for some questions could address this. The paper’s table formatting includes excessive empty space. The split between validation and test sets is heavily unbalanced, and the rationale is not explained. The LLM-powered evaluation (L261) differs little from exact match for most answers, and the specific GPT-4 version (date) used is not stated (R2). The distribution of modalities (charts, tables, diagrams) across the dataset is not provided (R3).
