## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces Masked Cell Modeling (MCM) for one-class anomaly detection in tabular data. Normal samples are used to train a mask generator that produces multiple soft masks (via sigmoid), each applied to the input before an autoencoder reconstructs the original. A diversity loss (log‑sum‑exp, Eq. 5) prevents mask collapse. Anomalies are detected by high reconstruction error. Experiments are conducted on 20 benchmark datasets against 9 baselines, with extensive ablations. One reviewer (2, score 8) was distinctly more positive than the other two (scores 6 and 6), particularly about the method’s novelty and empirical strength.

### Strengths
- The proposed masked modeling framework for tabular anomaly detection is sensible and novel.
- Results are encouraging and comprehensive, with extensive ablation studies (Tables 2‑3, Figs 5‑6).
- The data‑dependent masking strategy outperforms more naive masking approaches.
- The paper is well‑written and easy to follow.
- Masked modeling for tabular data makes conceptual sense.
- MLM for anomaly detection is relatively under‑explored, and adapting it to tabular data is a novel avenue.
- The idea of learning masking matrices may be applicable to other domains.
- Reviewer 2 (score 8) is notably more positive about the method’s novelty and empirical strength.

### Weaknesses
- Table 2: The ensemble of masks contributes little to performance; an ablation with ensemble turned off for the Task E variant would be informative.
- Table 3: The impact of hyperparameter λ on overall performance is not studied.
- Fig. 5: The mask generated for normal data should also be shown, along with the variety of masks produced by the generator.
- Fig. 6: The x‑axis is not clearly labeled.
- Eq. 5: The motivation for the specific log‑sum‑exp form of the diversity loss is not explained; an ablation comparing alternative diversity‑loss designs is missing.
- Scalability: Computational cost (matrix operations) and model latency/training‑inference time are not investigated; the method’s scalability to very high‑dimensional tabular data is unclear.
- Batch size is very large, which affects the dimensionality of the mask matrix M. How does the method perform with a smaller batch size? Can batch size differ between training and test time?
- Why use soft masks instead of binary masks? The soft‑mask transformation might turn a normal sample into a synthetic anomaly during training, potentially hindering model learning.
- Why is learnable masking important for anomaly detection when random masks suffice for other tasks (e.g., language modeling)?
- Model parameters and latency compared with baseline methods are not provided.
- The method’s limitations (e.g., when would it fail?) are not discussed (question from reviewer 2).
