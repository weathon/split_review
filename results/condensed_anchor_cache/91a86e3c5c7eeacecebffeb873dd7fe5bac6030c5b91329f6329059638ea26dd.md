## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces MMKE-Bench, a multimodal knowledge editing benchmark for large multimodal models (LMMs). It uses free-form natural language to represent knowledge and defines three editing tasks: visual entity editing, visual semantic editing, and user-specific editing. The dataset comprises 2,940 knowledge items and 7,229 images across 110 fine-grained types, generated by LLMs and human-verified. Five editing methods (FT-LLM, KE, MEND, SERAC, IKE) are evaluated on three LMMs (BLIP-2, MiniGPT-4, LLaVA-1.5).

### Strengths
1. **Free-form natural language representation** – All reviewers highlight that using free-form natural language instead of traditional triple-based representations better captures real-world complexity and makes the benchmark more flexible and applicable.
2. **Diverse task setup covering three editing types** – Reviewers agree the inclusion of visual entity editing, visual semantic editing, and user-specific editing provides a comprehensive evaluation tool. R2 and R4 note it supports both individual and sequential editing.
3. **Detailed dataset construction process** – R1 and R2 praise the thorough description of data collection and structuring, with R4 noting the process is robust.
4. **Extensive evaluation across multiple methods and models** – R1 and R2 commend the evaluation of five editing methods on three prominent LMMs, providing a systematic performance baseline. R2 specifically notes the analysis of method differences across tasks.
5. **Clarity of writing and broad data sources** – R3 and R4 find the paper well-written and the benchmark spans diverse entity types and sources.

### Weaknesses
1. **Task division lacks clarity and overlap between visual entity and visual semantic editing** – R3 and R4 argue both tasks require image comprehension and text-based knowledge retention, essentially falling under visual question answering. R3 says the boundary is blurred. R4 adds that user-specific editing is an application scenario, not a distinct task type.
2. **User-specific editing scenario practicality questioned** – R3 and R4 note that in real-world applications, database or memory-based search might be more effective than editing the model for each user. R3 doubts its practicality.
3. **T-Loc test is too easy** – R3 and R4 observe that results are near 100 for methods like SERAC, FT-Alignment (FT-LLM?), and MEND, suggesting the randomly sampled T-Loc questions are too simple. They recommend introducing more challenging cases (e.g., using GPT to generate harder examples and verifying them).
4. **Dataset size may be insufficient for training-based methods** – R3 and R4 question whether the modest amount of training data (2,940 knowledge items) is adequate for methods requiring training, such as SERAC and MEND. R1 does not raise this concern.
5. **Missing theoretical analysis on why methods perform poorly** – R1 (score 8) notes the paper lacks in-depth analysis explaining why existing editing methods underperform on multimodal tasks, and suggests adding theoretical or technical contributions to strengthen the paper for a venue like ICLR.
6. **Insufficient evidence of the benchmark’s necessity** – R1 requests comparative analyses with existing benchmarks (e.g., MC-MKE, VLKEB) and examples of real-world scenarios where MMKE-Bench is more valuable. R2 also asks for a comparison of visual entity editing data quality with prior datasets.
7. **Figures need improvement** – R2 finds Figure 1’s soccer example not universally understandable and Figure 2’s four construction steps visually indistinguishable. R4 notes Figure 1’s user-specific editing example shows the same original and editing knowledge, causing confusion; Figure 2 has a “G-Rel.” label that should be “T-Rel”, two identical questions in the last box, and confusing placement of “hint”.
8. **Counterfactual data limitations** – R2 points out the counterfactual construction may create a distribution that differs from real-world data, and LLM-generated counterfactuals may introduce biases or inconsistencies. R2 suggests comparing model performance on both counterfactual and real-world data.
9. **Lack of empirical analysis on human verification impact** – R2 notes the paper does not provide examples or quantitative comparisons showing how human verification improved consistency, e.g., types and frequency of errors corrected.
10. **IKE adaptation to LMMs not explained** – R3 and R4 ask how IKE is adapted to LMM editing, which examples are used in context, and whether image-based examples are included. R1 asks why IKE has better generalization/transferability and what new perspective the benchmark provides beyond prior IKE results.
11. **Gaps in sequential editing analysis** – R4 notes the paper explains SERAC’s behavior in sequential editing but leaves out MEND. R3 and R4 ask for clarification on “user number” and “allowed maximum items” (Section 5.2.2). R3 finds the gaps in visual editing tasks (1, 3, 6, 10) too small compared to prior work (LLM, VLKEB). R4 says the gaps are not substantial enough.
12. **Task generalization evaluation is weak** – R4 argues that using only one case to prove task generalization is not persuasive and calls for more comprehensive experiments.
13. **Ambiguous or unclear statements** – R4 notes: line 450 “visual knowledge” is ambiguous and reliability not necessarily lower as stated; line 466 the conclusion that “parameter-based methods are better at applying edited knowledge to new contexts” is not well justified. R1 questions the definition of “counterfactual editing” in line 776–781.
14. **Typos and writing inconsistencies** – R3 and R4 report:
    - Table 2: “Entity Entity Editing”
    - Table 6: missing decimal point in SERAC results, GAP 3 T-Rel column
    - Figure 2 bottom-right: “G-Rel.” (should be “T-Rel”)
    - Line 355: “MLLMs” vs. “LMMs” elsewhere
    - Line 324–327: incorrect left quotation marks in LaTeX
    - Figure 1: user-specific editing original and editing knowledge identical (confusing)
    - Figure 2: two questions in last box are the same; “hint” placement confusing.
15. **(R3 minority concern) LLaVA-1.5 size claim** – R3 questions the statement that LLaVA-1.5 outperforms MiniGPT-4 due to larger model size, noting both use 7B LLMs and MiniGPT-4’s vision encoder is larger. Also states the model comparison is not directly related to the benchmark’s core focus.
