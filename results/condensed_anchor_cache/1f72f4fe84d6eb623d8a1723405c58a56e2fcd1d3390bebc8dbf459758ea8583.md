## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces AC-PKAN, a Physics-Informed Neural Network framework that integrates Chebyshev Type-I polynomial-based Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) with internal/external attention mechanisms, including a Residual Gradient Attention (RGA) mechanism for dynamic loss-weighting and wavelet-activated MLPs. The method is evaluated on five benchmark tasks across three domains. Reviewer opinions range from highly positive (score 8) to moderately positive (6,6) to critical (5), with the main disagreements concerning the challenge level of the benchmarks, the explanation of architectural choices, and the depth of experimental analysis.

### Strengths
- The proposed Chebyshev Type-I polynomial layers (replacing B-splines) and the RGA adaptive reweighting mechanism are novel (R1, R4). The architecture addresses known limitations of PINNs and KANs: gradient stiffness, limited interpretability, scalability, and convergence stability (R2).
- Theoretical proof that the network has non-zero derivatives of all orders is provided (R1).
- Ablation studies show the necessity of each module (R1, R3, R4). The importance of the linear layer is highlighted (R2, question 4).
- The paper includes pseudocodes, mean values of GRA/RBA weights over epochs (demonstrating balanced attention), loss landscape analyses, and detailed experiment settings, aiding reproducibility (R2).
- Empirical performance is promising across all tested benchmarks, including complex PDEs and non-trivial domains (R1, R2, R3).
- Presentation and logical flow are clear (R2, R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- The connection between the RGA mechanism and the Cheby1KAN layer feels disjointed; the paper lacks a theoretical explanation for why Cheby1KAN is more effective with RGA compared to other network layers (R3).
- Missing intuition for architectural choices: why Chebyshev Type-I polynomials improve over B-splines in performance and memory cost (R1), and the role of the wavelet activation function and how it helps performance (R1).
- No running time comparison across methods (KAN, Cheby2KAN, MLP, Transformer), leaving the accuracy-efficiency trade-off unclear (R2).
- Ablation study only reports overall performance drops; it does not analyze how each component (e.g., RGA/RBA attention, Chebyshev KAN layer) could individually improve other algorithms like MLP-based PINNs (R2).
- Proposition 1 is vague; “positive integer N” requires checking the appendix to be understood (R2).
- Lack of experiments on higher-order/higher-dimensional PDEs, despite the claim that AC-PKAN can approximate such problems. All tested PDEs are 1-D or simple 2-D cases (R2). The 1D-wave case: PINN (Wang 2022, fig.6) achieved a relative l2 norm of 1.7e-3, the same order as AC-PKAN, contradicting the claim of outperforming PINNsFormer (which scored 0.32) (R4). The other PDEs (2D Poisson) are smooth, simple, and solvable by traditional finite element methods; challenging problems like the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation (chaotic), lid-driven cavity (Re=3200), or Navier–Stokes flow around a cylinder are not included (R4).
- Missing comparisons with FNO-related methods (R3).
- The design choices for the RGA loss weights (Equation 8) are not fully justified: scaling factors are set to 1, and a logarithmic transformation is applied; the effect of removing the log or using other scales is not demonstrated (R2).
- The method introduction (pages 3–7) is overly expatiatory; the main text should concentrate the method and include more theoretical insights and experiments (R4).
- Reviewer questions that overlap with missing experiments/explanations: (a) Can RGA be applied with Adam/AdamW? (R2). (b) How does AC-PKAN handle the curse of dimensionality for multi-dimensional PDEs? (R2). (c) Can a simpler architecture like MLP with sinusoidal activations also guarantee non-zero derivatives? (R2). (d) Why does removing the linear layer have the most significant impact on AC-PKAN’s performance, while other PINN methods do not require similar linear projections? (R2). (e) If the dataset is trained in batches (i.e., mini-batch SGD), how is the maximum loss over the entire training data computed in Equation 4? (R3).
