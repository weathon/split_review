## Merged Review

### Summary

This paper presents Generative Visual Pretraining (GVP), a unified probabilistic framework that combines autoregressive (AR) and masked image modeling (MIM) objectives for self-supervised visual representation learning. Borrowing the two-stream attention mechanism from XLNet, GVP processes content and query tokens with causal masks over random permutations of input patches, predicting tokens in groups. Experiments are conducted on both pixel and discrete token spaces, evaluating via linear probing, fine-tuning on ImageNet-1K, and transfer learning to 9 downstream tasks. The framework claims improvements over prior AR and MIM methods.

### Strengths

- Simple yet principled idea to unify AR and MIM pretraining within a single probabilistic framework, revealing the difference between sequential dependency (AR) and independence assumption (MIM). This provides a useful starting point for studying generative visual pretraining. [R2, R3, R4]
- Evaluation covers many existing methods across both pixel and discrete input/output spaces, with benchmarks including linear probing, fine-tuning, and transfer learning. [R1, R3]
- Clear and well-structured write-up that bridges the gap between AR and MIM paradigms, making the connection easy to follow. [R3]
- Extensive exploration of different formulations (pixel vs. discrete, linear vs. fine-tune evaluation), demonstrating thorough experimental design. [R3]
- Provides mathematical analysis from a probabilistic perspective, and flexible generation capability due to the integration of both modeling types. [R4]

### Weaknesses

- **Training cost and efficiency not addressed.** The method processes each token twice (once as content and once as query), leading to at least 2× training cost and increased memory compared to standard MIM (e.g., MAE). No training time, inference time, or GPU usage comparisons with baselines (including SAIM and RandSAC) are provided. The use of large batch sizes is not justified or ablated. This omission makes it unclear whether performance gains justify the added complexity. [R1, R4]
- **Unfair or insufficient comparisons in the main table (Table 1).** All baselines are compared after only 200 pretraining epochs, while MAE is typically trained for 800+ epochs (and shows notable improvement from 800 to 1600). Comparisons should be based on total compute or total data consumed, not simply number of epochs. It is unclear whether GVP has a higher upper bound or merely converges faster. [R3]
- **Motivation for combining AR with MIM is not clearly established.** The paper states that the two approaches “complement each other,” but does not articulate why autoregressive prediction benefits visual representation learning (e.g., what property the sequential dependency adds beyond masked modeling). The experimental ablations (fixed vs. random ordering, group size) only show that strict sequential ordering is harmful—consistent with MIM—but fail to isolate any benefit specifically from the AR component. [R2, R1]
- **Lack of step-by-step ablation from a pure MIM baseline.** To identify which component drives improvement, the authors should start with a pure MIM version (no grouping, no causal masks, no two-stream attention) and incrementally add each modification, reporting performance at each step. The existing ablations (patch ordering and auto-regressivity) do not reveal the key factor behind the gains. [R2]
- **Missing comparisons with closely related autoregressive self-supervised methods.** The paper does not compare against SAIM and RandSAC, both contemporaneous works exploring AR for vision (patch-level and segment-level, respectively). It is possible GVP simply combines ideas from these two methods; the claimed advantages over them are not demonstrated. [R4]
- **Insufficient validation on detection and segmentation tasks.** Only ADE20K segmentation is reported. Object detection results on COCO are missing, which limits the assessment of semantic effectiveness in multi-object scenes. The paper should include COCO detection/segmentation results to strengthen the claim of general applicability. [R2, R4] (One reviewer also notes a lack of discussion on semantic effectiveness in multi-object scenes overall.) [R4]
- **Notation is chaotic and undefined.** Equation (2) has inconsistent font styles and subscripts for x; m is not introduced; on page 6, M is not defined. Equation (3) has a typo: z should be ≥ m, not t. [R2, R3]
- **Group segmentation distribution is not described.** How are groups formed and sampled during training? The paper does not specify the distribution over group sizes or permutations. [R2]
- **Source of numbers in Table 1 and hyperparameter tuning not disclosed.** It is unclear whether numbers are cited from original papers or reproduced; how hyperparameters were tuned for each method is not explained. [R1]
- **Added sampling flexibility not leveraged downstream.** While GVP supports arbitrary prediction orders at inference, the paper provides no experiments or ideas on how this flexibility could be useful for downstream tasks; it reads as “another more complex way to obtain very similar representations.” [R1]
- **No code release announced.** Open-sourcing would promote reproducibility and broader use. [R3]
