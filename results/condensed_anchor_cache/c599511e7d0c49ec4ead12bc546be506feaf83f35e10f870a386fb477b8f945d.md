## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies catastrophic forgetting in LLMs by constructing an \(M \times N\) matrix \(Z\) of log-perplexity increases for \(N\) upstream examples after fine-tuning on \(M\) downstream tasks. It finds that \(Z\) exhibits low-rank patterns often approximable by multiplicative scalar effects. Leveraging this, the paper predicts forgetting for unseen tasks via matrix completion (e.g., kNN over SVD) and selects upstream examples for replay to mitigate forgetting. The approach is compared against similarity-based baselines and shows improved perplexity retention. One reviewer (score 6) was notably more positive, praising the paper’s clarity, extensive studies, and effective mitigation; other reviewers (scores 3, 5, 3, 3) raised substantial concerns about practicality, experimental design, and clarity.

### Strengths
- **Timely and important problem**: Understanding fine-grained associations between downstream tasks and catastrophic forgetting has great potential for knowledge retention in LLMs (R1, R4, R5).
- **Clear presentation**: The paper is well-written, with helpful figures (e.g., Figure 1) and easy-to-follow analysis (R1, R2, R5).
- **Extensive studies**: Provides well-designed quantitative and qualitative analyses of the correlation between forgetting and task similarity (R2).
- **Distinguishes sensitivity from similarity**: Highlights that task importance/similarity is a different notion from sensitivity to forgetting (R2).
- **Novel replay method**: The predict-then-upweight replay strategy effectively mitigates upstream forgetting in terms of perplexity (R2, R3). The mitigation case study is a good idea to test the utility of predictions (R4).
- **Interesting low‑rank representation**: Demonstrating predictable forgetting patterns via low‑rank structure is thought‑provoking and connects previous work on task similarity to the LLM setting (R3, R4).
- **Original and significant**: Takes a simple approach to understanding example forgetting and produces an effective mitigation algorithm (R5).

### Weaknesses
1. **Practicality limitations**  
   - Requires fine-tuning the base LLM on each task to gather training data for matrix completion, incurring high computational cost. It is unclear whether the method is robust with a small number of tasks (R1 W1).  
   - Requires full access to upstream data for replay, which may be unavailable due to data updates or sharing policies; the matrix completion formulation does not naturally handle this scenario (R1 W2).  
   - The matrix completion approach itself requires forgetting measurements on the test tasks (i.e., fine-tuning on those tasks for a seed set), severely limiting practical utility. Ground truth forgetting can be measured directly via the initial fine-tuning run, so the savings (inference cost on remaining upstream examples) are minimal (R4 weakness, R5 Q5).

2. **Unconvincing experimental setup**  
   - The mitigation pipeline runs two fine‑tuning rounds: first without replay to gather forgetting statistics, then with replay. It is unclear whether statistics gathered *after* the first round hold during the second round with replay (R1 W3).  
   - Performance gains are insufficient; all methods remain far from pre‑finetuning performance, raising concerns about the effectiveness of replay‑based mitigation (R1 W4). The random baseline sometimes outperforms more complex baselines (R3, Figure 6).  
   - The fine-tuning tasks used for the mitigation experiments (Section 4.2) are not reported, making the section impossible to evaluate (R4 Q5).

3. **Clarity and justification of low‑rank findings**  
   - The claim of “simple structure” is not clearly supported: the pattern in Figure 2 is not obviously neat, and more analysis on what specific knowledge is forgotten (e.g., topic correlation) is needed (R3 weakness, R4 weakness).  
   - The \(R^2\) scores for the multiplicative model vary significantly across models (e.g., OLMo‑7B vs. MPT‑7B, OLMo‑1B vs. OLMo‑7B), questioning robustness. Why do OLMo‑7B‑Instruct’s associations appear more complicated? (R4 weakness, R4 Q1, R2 Q3).  
   - The takeaway from low‑rank structure is unclear: what should the null hypothesis be? The optimal predictor used for mitigation is kNN over SVD, not SVD directly, reducing the importance of the SVD analysis (R2 weakness 2, R5 weakness 1). Why does kNN outperform SVD? (R5 Q3).  
   - SVD components in Figure 4 do not add information beyond the full matrix; forgetting from fine‑tuning on TruthfulQA is already evident in Figure 2c (R4 weakness).  
   - More insights are needed on which tasks and which examples cause more forgetting (e.g., data amount, OOD, noisy/higher‑loss examples) and on interpreting \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\) from the multiplicative model (R5 Q2).

4. **Missing downstream task evaluation**  
   - Forgetting is measured only by log‑perplexity increase. It is not shown whether forgetting hurts model capabilities on benchmarks (e.g., accuracy on TruthfulQA) or whether reducing perplexity brings practical benefits or trade‑offs (R2 weakness 1, R5 weakness 2, R2 Q4).  
   - Perplexity increases on certain examples may not degrade LM performance, especially for noisy or rare examples; the impact on downstream task accuracy should be evaluated (R5 Q4).

5. **Missing baselines and analyses**  
   - Figure 6 should include a baseline using actual forgetting values for all data (R3 weakness). The choice of kNN over additive linear or SVD methods for mitigation is not justified; if these were tested, they must be reported and included in multiple comparisons correction (R4 Q4).  
   - A baseline in matrix completion using only the average over tasks (rather than fitting each task separately) would strengthen results (R4 Q2).  
   - Significance testing details are missing: how were multiple comparisons corrected? (R4 Q3).  
   - Why was Spearman correlation chosen over Pearson? Also, alternatives to TF‑IDF (e.g., cosine similarity of last‑token representations) should be explored (R2 Q2).

6. **Language and clarity**  
   - The paper needs significant language improvements: numerous sentences are ungrammatical or unclear (e.g., “There has been a growing need for long‑term usability of LLMs”, “We perform statistics of forgetting”, “As a main comparator”). The vague term “associations” should be replaced with precise terms like “sample forgetting matrix” (R4 weakness). The abstract should be restructured to motivate the problem more clearly before detailing the matrix construction (R4 minor).  
   - Lack of substantive discussion of limitations beyond replay (R4 weakness).
