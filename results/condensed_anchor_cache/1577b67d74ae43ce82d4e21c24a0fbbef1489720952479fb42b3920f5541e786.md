## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper introduces a novel task: generating concurrent co-speech gestures for two interacting characters from conversational audio. The authors construct a large-scale dataset GES-Inter (over 7M frames, ~70 hours) from talk show videos, with careful curation (12-step filtering from over 1000 hours, including speaker separation, text recognition, and manual review). They propose Co³Gesture, a framework with bilateral cooperative diffusion branches, a Temporal Interaction Module (TIM) for cross-attention between audio and motion features of both speakers, and a mutual attention mechanism. Experimental results on GES-Inter show improvements over single-person baselines and two-person methods like InterGen and InterX, with quantitative, qualitative, and user study evaluations.

### Strengths
- **Novel task**: Generating concurrent, two-person co-speech gestures from conversational audio, naturally extending single-person co-speech gesture generation and moving the field forward.
- **Large-scale dataset (GES-Inter)** : Collected from in-the-wild talk show videos, containing diverse two-person interactive posture sequences (~70 hours, >7M frames) including body and finger movements and facial expressions. The data processing is thorough, with speaker occurrence rules and a 12-step filtering pipeline ensuring quality; the audio is separated for each speaker, text recognized, and participants invited to review the data.
- **Technically sound model**: Co³Gesture uses bilateral cooperative diffusion branches, a Temporal Interaction Module (TIM) to model temporal associations between speakers’ gestures, and a mutual attention mechanism to enhance learning of concurrent interactive motions. The design is novel yet simple, making it easy to reproduce and extend.
- **Effective modules**: TIM captures selective features refined from single-speaker or mixed audio, aligning with the physical nature of conversational gestures; mutual attention boosts coordination.
- **Strong baselines**: Outperforms recent single-speaker and conversational gesture generation methods (including InterGen, InterX) on GES-Inter, with quantitative metrics, ablation studies, and a user study confirming advantage.

### Weaknesses
- **Spatial assumptions**: Model does not account for spatial relationships between speakers; Speaker A always appears on the left, both always seated in chairs. Root joint appears fixed (no global translation/orientation). The rationale for fixing root position is unclear, and the implications for realism and future work (e.g., standing/walking interactions) are not discussed.
- **Dataset quality concerns**: Examples show hand self-penetration. Frame rate is only 15 FPS (lower than mainstream motion datasets), limiting compatibility. All subjects share the same body shape parameters and fixed root position; preprocessing/post-processing steps and motivations for these decisions are not sufficiently explained. More comprehensive example videos, potential FPS enhancement tools, and clarification of why shape and root are fixed are needed.
- **Missing dataset comparison**: No quantitative comparison with a prior two-person gesture dataset (reference [2] in the paper lacks a table comparing size, capture equipment, diversity, quality metrics).
- **Baseline adaptation unclear**: The baseline methods compared (Sec. 4.2) are originally single-person methods; how they were adapted for two-person generation is not specified, which may affect performance. Also missing quantitative comparisons with relevant two-person methods (InterGen, InterX) in some sections (though InterGen is mentioned in user study marginal improvement, and the paper does compare with them in the main results).
- **Pre-trained model concern**: For some baselines, pre-trained models were used without retraining on GES-Inter, which is problematic because those models are not expected to produce realistic interactive gestures.
- **Ablation study incomplete**: Ablation only reports metrics without TIM and mutual attention. Should include a simple fusion module (e.g., MLP or concatenation) to demonstrate the advantage of the proposed modules; qualitative results for ablated models are also missing.
- **User study details missing**: Number of videos per participant, mean/variance of video lengths, fractions of frames with motion per person, standard deviation of responses, and statistical significance of differences across methods (scores appear close for InterX, InterGen, and proposed) are not reported.
- **Interaction metrics weak**: Current metrics (FGD, BC, Diversity) are insufficient for evaluating interactive motions. Need interaction-specific evaluation criteria that capture correspondence between speech and gestures for both speakers; typical interaction types should be categorized.
- **Audio processing issues**: Using mixed audio (C_mix) as input to the Audio Encoder without special handling for overlapping speech (when both talk simultaneously) is not addressed. Rationale for using C_mix instead of separate C_a and C_b to get interaction embeddings (Line 266) is unclear; is C_mix = C_a + C_b? Need mathematical relationship, visual comparison, or ablation to understand the role of C_mix vs. separate streams. Also, the effectiveness of speech separation and text recognition alignment should be demonstrated with more examples.
- **Foot contact loss questionable**: Only upper body motion is generated (Line 244), yet a foot contact loss (Eqn. 4) is used. Why? What is the benefit? An ablation quantifying its effect on upper body motion is needed; the lower body pose during loss calculation is unspecified.
- **Visual results limited**: Most generated clips show one person idle, are only 3–4 seconds long, and one longer example (10+ sec) exhibits jittery motion. Motion balance between persons is not quantified; sources of jitter and potential mitigation steps are not investigated.
- **Figure 3 ambiguous**: The figure shows c_a, c_b, and C_mix as separate inputs to the Audio Encoder, but the text says the input is C_mix = {c_a, c_b}. The representation is misleading; clarify figure or text.
- **Mutual attention unclear**: The explanation for exchanging input order in the mutual attention module (Lines 280–282) is confusing. Does "order" refer to swapping the speaker roles (e.g., question-answer order)? This affects understanding of the module’s design motivation.
- **Minor typos**: Line 258 “pheromones” should be “phonemes”; Table 3 “Attetion” should be “Attention”.
- **Missing input specification**: Dimensions and composition of inputs (e.g., audio c_a, motion x_a) are not clearly stated. Which joints are used for upper body? Does x_a include facial expressions and shape parameters? Why only upper body – is lower body data insufficient in the dataset?
