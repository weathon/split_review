## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a task-gram language model built from n-gram co-occurrences in pretraining data (Pile) to measure the correlation between LLM output probabilities and task-specific data frequency, defining distributional memorization via Spearman correlation. Using Pythia models of varying sizes, it evaluates four tasks: machine translation (WMT-09), factual question answering (TriviaQA), world knowledge (MMLU), and math reasoning (MMLU reasoning subset). It finds that memorization is strongest in factual QA, and that as model size increases, only factual QA shows increased memorization, while translation and reasoning tasks show more generalization (novel outputs). The paper also includes a gradient-based verification method. Scores: 3, 8, 6, 3. Reviewer 2 (score 8) was notably more positive than the other reviewers (scores 3, 3, 6).

### Strengths
- The task-gram model provides a concrete, clearly defined metric to connect LLM output distributions directly with pretraining data, offering an alternative approach for analyzing memorization vs. generalization at scale (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).
- The work is exceptionally well-written, structured, and easy to read (Reviewer 2, 3).
- The methodological contribution is strong and likely extendable to multiple tasks, leveraging prior work on ∞-gram and WIMBD (Reviewer 2).
- The choice of downstream tasks (translation, factual, math) is good and covers a range of task complexity (Reviewer 2, 4).
- The authors confirm findings using a gradient-based method and check for cross-contamination of training data, adding robustness (Reviewer 3).
- Experimental results reveal interesting trends about how model size influences memorization vs. generalization across tasks, useful for understanding scaling (Reviewer 1, 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Insufficient novelty**: The finding that factual question answering relies on memorization and memorization increases with model size is not new. Prior work extensively demonstrates factual knowledge storage in FFNs (e.g., [1,2,3,4,5] listed by Reviewer 4) and memorization is already discussed in previous works ([1] cited by Reviewer 1). The overall conclusion that tasks exhibit varying dependence on memorization is unsurprising (Reviewer 1, 4).
- **Task-gram model limitations for complex tasks**: The n-gram approach is insufficient for generative tasks with short input descriptions that require abstraction and creativity. The authors should evaluate on more complex tasks like code generation (HumanEval) and math reasoning (GSM8K) (Reviewer 1). MMLU is not a good benchmark for reasoning; datasets like MGSM are more appropriate (Reviewer 4). Additionally, MMLU has known annotation issues and may not reflect true generalization (Reviewer 2). Using GSM8K would better disentangle memorization vs. reasoning, given recent evidence of memorization issues (Reviewer 2).
- **Spearman correlation conflates memorization with necessity**: High correlation in translation could reflect accurate language pairing rather than memorization, misinterpreting necessary task patterns as memorization (Reviewer 1). Relying solely on Spearman correlation may not capture nuances of memorization and generalization; additional experiments or mathematical justification are needed (Reviewer 4).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity not assessed**: The metric depends on many hyperparameters (embedding model, cosine similarity thresholds, choice of dataset) but sensitivity is not measured. Different 5-gram threshold values are used for WMT vs. TriviaQA/MMLU without justification (Reviewer 3). The selection of thresholds across tasks is unclear and sensitivity of results to these parameters is unknown (Reviewer 1).
- **Distributional generalization definition is insufficient**: The criterion that generalization is divergence between LLM output and pretraining data distribution is necessary but not sufficient. Smaller LLMs may fall into degenerate/repetitive distributions not conforming to natural language syntax, which would artificially obtain high generalization scores. Fluency measurements or qualitative discussion would help (Reviewer 3).
- **Generalizability concerns**: Results are conditional on the Pile dataset and Pythia models, limiting generalizability to other data and architectures (Reviewer 2, 4). It is unclear if similar trends hold for models trained on different corpora (Reviewer 2). WMT-09 is outdated and raises data contamination concerns; more recent datasets like WMT23 or WMT24 should be used (Reviewer 4). The MMLU dataset has annotation issues that may bias results (Reviewer 2).
- **Practical implications unclear**: The paper does not clarify how its findings can be used to improve LLM training or deployment strategies (Reviewer 4). Reviewer 1 asks for potential methods to actively balance memorization and generalization.
- **Missing analysis on data specifics**:
  - Rare or absent n-grams: The handling of n-grams with low frequency or absence in the denominator is not explained; the percentage of dropped n-grams per dataset should be reported (Reviewer 2).
  - Overlapping n-gram counting: In sentences with multiple task-related n-grams, it is unclear how overlapping n-grams are counted and whether this affects interpretation for longer sentences (Reviewer 1).
  - Synonyms: The approach does not account for synonyms, which may confound results, especially for smaller datasets (Reviewer 2).
  - Use of both ∞-gram and WIMBD may introduce bias; results using only one should be shown (Reviewer 2).
- **Cross-task memorization not addressed**: Some tasks share similar patterns or data structures (e.g., QA and factual retrieval), and memorization from one task could inadvertently support generalization in another. The task-specific n-gram approach may not capture these cross-task effects (Reviewer 1).
- **Qualitative insights missing**: Smaller Pythia models obtain ~10% accuracy on MMLU (below random chance) when the number of n-grams in training is 150; no qualitative explanation is given (Reviewer 3).
- **Data contamination checks**: Overlap between Pile and test data was checked using exact n-gram matches with n > 14, but this value is large (especially if stop words are removed) and not justified (Reviewer 3).
- **Presentation issues**: Figure 3 uses different scales for MMLU knowledge and reasoning subplots, which is hard to see; the scales should be visually highlighted (Reviewer 2). Section 2 could include a small schematic plot of expected memorization vs. generalization trends (Reviewer 2). There is an editing mistake in Definition 3: "we define the extent of an LLM distributional memorize the pretraining corpus" (Reviewer 3).
