## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes PFGuard, a framework that combines importance sampling for fairness with PATE/PTEL-based private generation, aiming to simultaneously satisfy fairness, differential privacy, and utility. The reviewers are split: three (R2, R3, R5) found the idea and contributions valuable (scores 6,8,8), while two (R1, R4) raised fundamental concerns about privacy accounting, novelty, and experimental setup (scores 6,3). Overall, the paper is well-written and the problem is timely, but serious methodological gaps and overclaimed novelty are highlighted.

### Strengths
- The problem of conflicts between privacy and fairness in generative models is interesting and timely; the detailed discussion of how the two objectives can counteract each other (e.g., fairness modifying sensitivity, privacy clipping worsening fairness) is appreciated (R1, R3, R5). Some reviewers note this recognition is not entirely new but still a useful explicit treatment (R1, R4, R5).
- The proposed PFGuard framework – isolating fair training via balanced (importance-resampled) teachers and then privately distilling – is a reasonable approach and could be extended to other downstream applications requiring privacy (R5). The use of importance resampling for balanced data is a sensible way to inject fairness (R3).
- The presentation is clear, and Figures 1, 2, and 3 effectively communicate the challenges (R1, R4). The writing has no obvious grammatical errors (R4).
- Experiments are extensive, covering MNIST, FashionMNIST, and CelebA, with comparisons against several baselines including fair variants of DP-SGD (R2). The results in Tables 3 and 4 are well-presented and show improved utility and fairness under a fixed privacy budget (R3, R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Privacy leakage from importance sampling (R1, echoed by R4 Q3):** The paper claims that privacy follows from data disjointness alone, but PATE’s guarantee also requires random partitioning of the private data. The importance sampling step is inherently non-random (it depends on sensitive groups), introducing an additional privacy cost that is not accounted for. The authors must acknowledge and formally characterize how importance sampling does not break the PATE privacy guarantee (R1, R4). Prior work (Kulynych et al. 2021) addresses this by accounting for the cost of sampling; the paper does not cite or follow that approach.
- **Overclaiming novelty (R1, R4, mild from R5):** Remark 1 claims the paper is the first to reveal that fairness and privacy techniques can counteract each other. Several prior works (Yaghini et al. 2023, Tran et al. 2021, Bullwinkel et al., Rosenblatt et al., Abroshan et al., Cheng et al.) make the same observation, often for classification under PATE. The paper’s argument that these works use a different DP definition (sensitive attribute DP) is not fully correct: Lowy et al. (DP-FERMI) and Kulynych et al. consider central DP, and Tran et al. and Yaghini et al. use PATE classification, very close to the PTEL setting except for generation. The authors must soften the claim and properly distinguish their contribution.
- **Unrealistic assumption about balanced sensitive attributes (R1):** Assumption 2 (line 256) states that the balanced distribution \(p_{\text{bal}}\) is uniformly distributed over \(s\). In real-world data, minority groups exist by definition; this assumption is unrealistic and would require repeated resampling of minority samples, increasing privacy cost. The paper does not justify this or discuss its feasibility.
- **Lack of fairness guarantees (R5):** The method provides no formal fairness guarantee. The link between balanced minibatches and teacher fairness is intuitive but unproven, and the effect of the private distillation step on fairness is unknown. The heuristic of giving each teacher probabilistically one minority sample is insufficient to guarantee teacher-level fairness.
- **Insufficient analysis of privacy budget and utility-fairness tradeoff (R2, R3):** Only a limited range of epsilon values is tested, making it hard to understand the effect of privacy budget on utility and fairness (R2). Experiments fix the privacy budget and only examine utility-fairness tradeoffs, which does not align with the paper’s stated goal of studying privacy-fairness conflicts (R3). R3 requests Pareto front results covering all three metrics.
- **Apples-to-oranges experimental comparison (R4):** PFGuard assumes access to a public reference dataset (10–100% of \(|D|\)) for teacher training and threshold estimation, but baselines like DP-SGD are not given the same advantage. If such a public reference is available, why not simply train on it? The paper does not clarify label missingness in the public sample, and no experiments test the impact of a biased public reference. R4 requests equalizing access or providing holdout experiments.
- **Vague and incomplete Section 3 (R5):** The section “Adding Fairness Can Worsen Privacy” lacks concrete examples; the following subsection actually discusses both directions. The paper should split the examples and elaborate more on the concrete mechanisms (e.g., how fairness modifications to sensitivity affect privacy, and how clipping affects fairness adjustments).
- **Missing relevant baselines and prior work (R1):** The paper omits comparisons with Kulynych et al. 2021 (importance sampling for DP-SGD), Lowy et al. 2023 (DP-FERMI), and does not adequately address why accounting for the privacy cost of adjusting the clipping bound \(C\) makes those works unsuitable as baselines. R1 requests inclusion of at least one such baseline.
- **Sequential procedure not convincing for tradeoff (R3):** The framework applies fairness first (importance sampling) then privacy (PATE), so it is not clear how the two are “traded off” rather than separately enforced. R5 asks: why apply “method + PFGuard” in figures? If PFGuard is end-to-end, the unique impact of the fairness component should be isolated. R3 would like a study of how fairness degradation from teacher to generator depends on the distillation budget.
- **Limited dataset scope and fairness notion (R2):** The experiments use only image datasets; the paper should include tabular datasets (as in Tran et al. 2021b) and consider alternative fairness notions (e.g., Rawlsian Max-Min) for contexts where group fairness is not applicable.
- **Downsampling for fairness may degrade quality (R3):** Resampling to obtain balanced data reduces the effective training size, which can hurt generation quality. The paper does not discuss this tradeoff.
- **Unclear when minority samples are downweighted for DP (R3):** The paper states that fairness adjustments can increase sensitivity, but does not explain in what scenarios minority samples should be downweighted to control sensitivity.
- **Figure 3 inconsistency (R3):** “Reweighted” and “Rewerighting” appear to be the same; privacy budget is fixed, limiting analysis.
- **Focus on GANs rather than modern diffusion models lessens impact (R5, note),** but this is not a fatal weakness.
