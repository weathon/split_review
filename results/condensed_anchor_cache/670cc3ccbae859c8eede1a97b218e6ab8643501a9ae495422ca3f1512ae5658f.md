## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a physics-in-the-loop hybrid scheme for real-time architectural structure design (bar systems). A neural network (MLP) encoder maps a target shape to mechanical properties (bar stiffnesses); a differentiable mechanics simulator (decoder) then produces a physically feasible shape under equilibrium, guaranteeing mechanical constraints. The encoder is trained end-to-end with the simulator. Validation on masonry shells and cable-net towers shows better accuracy and generalization than pure neural baselines (MLP, PINN) and accuracy comparable to direct optimization but in real time. A physical prototype (Figure 6) is fabricated. Scores are 6,6,6,8. One reviewer (score 8) is notably more positive, praising extensive experiments and a real-life sample; others raise concerns about generalizability, training overhead, and simplicity of the case studies.

### Strengths
- Combining physics (differentiable mechanics) with ML removes the need for costly training labels; the model learns by integrating the physics model with inexpensive loss functions.
- Out-of-distribution performance is interesting, reducing the need for extensive variability in training data (Reviewer 1, confidence 4).
- Paper is nicely written, well structured; figures are clean and easy to understand (Reviewers 2,3,4).
- Method sound; architecture leveraging JAX makes intuitive sense (Reviewer 2, confidence 5).
- Implementation of combining a differentiable simulator as decoder with a neural network encoder is nicely demonstrated (Reviewer 3, confidence 2).
- Building a physical prototype (Figure 6) based on the approach is highly unusual and should be commended (Reviewers 3 and 4).
- Results successfully highlight that the simulator ensures the final design results in a pin-jointed structure with resolved forces at equilibrium (Reviewer 3).
- Experimental results are extensive; real-life sample illustrates practical potential (Reviewer 4).
- Motivation is clear and method justified well (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Simplistic case studies and basic physics model**: The case studies (masonry shells, cable-net towers) are relatively simplistic with low degrees of freedom (DOFs) and do not reflect real-world complexity. The use of a linear Finite Difference Method (FDM) restricts applicability to scenarios where material and geometric nonlinearities are negligible. Many real-world architectural structures exhibit complex behaviors not captured by linear models. The method’s scalability to higher DOFs or inapplicability of linear FDM (e.g., dynamic scenarios) is not discussed (Reviewer 1, confidence 4; Reviewer 3 echoes concern about low DOFs).
- **Lack of generalizability across structural configurations**: Each trained model is closely tied to the underlying structure (number of bars \(M\) and nodes \(N\)); changing them likely requires retraining. The MLP encoder inherently ties the model to a specific input size, limiting its suitability for varying parameters. This significant limitation should be more explicitly addressed (Reviewers 1 and 2, both confidence 4 and 5). Reviewer 2 emphasizes that the architecture ties the model to specific input size, making it unsuitable for varying numbers of bars or nodes without retraining.
- **Training time and amortization cost not reported**: The hybrid approach runs the physics model during inference, increasing training costs. Training times for the proposed method and baselines (MLP, PINN) are not reported. Without this, assessing real-world viability is difficult; if training significantly exceeds several direct optimizations, direct optimization may be preferable (Reviewer 1, confidence 4). Reviewer 3 (confidence 2) adds: the cost of amortization (e.g., ~100 direct optimization runs for the training set) is not mentioned as a limitation. In practice, each direct optimization already runs in <2 seconds (cable-net) or <6 seconds (masonry shell); the paper should explicitly state whether wall-clock time is a problem in practice and how often the function is called.
- **Decoder not discussed in main body**: The decoder (differentiable simulator) is mostly described in appendices; this absence makes it difficult to fully understand the model’s architecture and working principles. The paper should discuss the decoder in the main text (Reviewer 2, confidence 5).
- **Runtime measurements have large variances**: The reported runtime measurements have such large variances that they are not meaningful. Benchmarking on macOS (with many background processes) is not appropriate; the authors should use a dedicated environment and better runtime measurement libraries. The variances make it impossible to draw reliable conclusions about computational efficiency (Reviewer 2, confidence 5).
- **Parameterization lacks flexibility**: The proposed parameterization requires retraining whenever the design representation changes. This is a major drawback for iterative architectural design. A generalizable parameterization strategy is missing (Reviewer 1, confidence 4).
- **Missing discussion of \(\tau\) (minimal stiffness parameter)** : In Eq. (3), \(\tau\) specifies minimal stiffness, but there is no follow-up discussion on how it influences optimization or resulting designs. The implications of different \(\tau\) values should be explored further (Reviewer 2, confidence 5). Reviewer 3 asks: what mechanism prevents the model from providing excessively large stiffness values for all bars? (This overlaps with stiffness regularization.)
- **Motivation and pipeline clarity**: How does a designer come up with a target shape \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\)? How does the simulator require only \(\mathbf{p}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) for shape prediction? The pipeline seems to miss information to build a shell with \(N\) pins (Reviewer 3, confidence 2). Also, notation confusion: \(p\) for norm and \(\mathbf{p}\) for loads (minor).
- **Initialization from NN for direct optimization**: From Fig. 9, optimization initialized with the proposed method converges quickly. Including results from NN-initialized direct optimization (which would guarantee local minima and avoid physics overhead during training) would enhance the contribution (Reviewer 1, confidence 4).
- **Variation in MLP and PINN inference times**: Since input sizes are constant, inference times for fully trained networks would be expected to be similar. The paper should comment on this discrepancy (Reviewer 1, confidence 4).
- **Figure clarity issues**: In Figure 1, it appears three figures are simultaneously provided to the encoder, but text indicates only one shape; clarify. Also, describe the dimension of matrix \(K\). In Figure 3, it is hard to see how the \(x\) vectors are updated; zooming in and adding text would help (Reviewer 4, confidence 3).
- **Questions that require clarification or additional experiments**: 
  - Does “fabrication requirements” imply each bar has additional constraints? (Reviewer 2)
  - Will the code be released? (Reviewer 3)
  - Does the chosen bar stiffness affect loading on the design (e.g., stiffer bar might lead to heavier design)? (Reviewer 3)
  - How is stress direction determined a-priori? (Reviewer 3)
  - When training, how does the loss account for permutation invariance with locations? (Reviewer 3)
  - Why is the masonry shell modeled as a pin-jointed structure but physically built as a shell? How are the two related? (Reviewer 3)
  - Define “mechanical integrity” and “mechanical efficiency” earlier. (Reviewer 2, minor)
  - Use “test-time inference” rather than “inference” to avoid confusion with parameter inference. (Reviewer 3, minor)
