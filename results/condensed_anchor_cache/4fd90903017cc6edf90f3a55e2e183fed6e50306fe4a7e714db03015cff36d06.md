## Merged Review

### Summary
Reviewers found the adjoint-based PDE discovery approach to have some merits but raised significant concerns regarding novelty, experimental scope, computational cost, and presentation quality. The method is compared only to PDE-FIND (2017) on a few simple 1D examples (heat, Burgers, wave) with few parameters. The theoretical derivations are sound but lack rigor in defining functional spaces and boundary conditions. The idea of using adjoint equations is considered interesting by some (R1, R4) but incremental by others (R2, R3), who note that adjoint-based parameter estimation for PDEs is well-established in inverse problems and design optimization (e.g., Jameson 2003). Writing quality is inconsistent: some found it clear (R2), others found it poorly written and confusing (R1, R3). Experiments include relevant settings (coarse time, noise, ill-posed tasks) but are insufficient to demonstrate scalability or robustness.

### Strengths
- The topic is interesting (R1).
- The idea of using adjoint equations for PDE discovery appears new in this context (R4) and provides a straightforward gradient descent algorithm (R3).
- Theoretical derivations are sound and clear (R2, R4). The adjoint approach enables analytic gradients.
- The method achieves good results on the considered PDEs (heat, Burgers, wave) compared to PDE-FIND (R3).
- Experiments cover relevant settings: coarse temporal resolution, noisy data, and ill-posed tasks (R3).
- The method can be effective in low-data regimes by discretizing the PDE on a finer mesh via adjoint equations (R4).
- The paper is generally well-written with clear explanations (R2). [Note: R1 and R3 disagree; see Weaknesses.]

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and Contribution**: Adjoint-based parameter estimation for PDEs is already well-established in inverse problems and design optimization (e.g., Jameson 2003). The paper does not clearly articulate what is new or how it differs from existing adjoint methods (R2, R3). The approach is incremental (R2). In contrast, R4 considers the idea new. The paper should include an explicit discussion of novelty and a more detailed comparison with existing methods in PDE-constrained optimization (R2, R3).
- **Experimental Scope**: Limited to very simple 1D PDEs (heat with D=1, Burgers, wave) with very few parameters; no tests on higher-dimensional, complex, or coupled nonlinear PDEs (R2, R3, R4). The method’s ability to handle a large number of candidate terms (large \(p\) or \(d\), cross-terms, mixed partials) is not examined (R4). Scalability to 3D fluid flow or systems of coupled PDEs is not demonstrated (R2).
- **Baseline Comparisons**: Only compared against PDE-FIND (2017), which is already somewhat dated; many newer methods (e.g., PINN-SR, Bayesian discovery methods) are not included (R3, R4). A comparison with PINN-SR is specifically needed as the framework closely resembles it (R4). Bayesian equation discovery algorithms (e.g., [1-3]) should be discussed and compared (R4). No comparison of computational cost, convergence rate, or sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning is provided (R2, R3).
- **Computational Complexity**: The method requires solving the forward PDE (1) and the adjoint PDE (7), both of which can be challenging and time-consuming (R3). No complexity analysis or runtime comparison with PDE-FIND is given (R3). The reliance on forward solvers may become prohibitive for large datasets (e.g., Fig. 1(d) should show computational time increasing with training samples; this is not addressed) (R4). The choice of solution algorithm could affect performance, but this is not discussed (R3).
- **Writing and Presentation**: The manuscript is not well-written; motivation unclear (R1). There are several typos (“Eq. equation” in lines 201, 215, 376, 482, 1172) and odd language (“Here, $x(k)$ is coordinates”) (R3). Terms are undefined (e.g., “semi-discrete total variation of C”, line 176) (R3). The structure of experiments is confusing: Section 3 “Results” is followed by Sections 4–6 also containing experimental results (R3). Notation issues (e.g., missing comma in line 135) (R4). Some reviewers found the writing clear (R2), but R1 and R3 found it lacking.
- **Theoretical Rigor**: Derivations of Eqs. (4) and (6) are only informally explained; functional spaces for the solution \(f\) of (1) are not defined, making it impossible to judge correctness (R3). Conditions such as \(\lambda(x,t) \to 0\) for \(x \in \partial\Omega\) are not justified, and the differentiability of \(\lambda\) is assumed without rigorous justification (R3). The statement “clearly, we are assuming that all considered derivatives of \(\lambda\) exist” is not rigorous, especially since an overly smooth \(\lambda\) imposes restrictions on the space of weak solutions (R3).
- **Regularization and Sparsity**: The method uses \(\ell_2\) regularization with a thresholding heuristic; \(\ell_2\) is not well-suited for sparse selection (e.g., Brunton et al. 2016 use \(\ell_1\)) (R3). The relation to iterative hard thresholding (IHT) methods from compressed sensing is not mentioned (R3). The selection of the threshold \(\sigma\) is critical: for small coefficients (e.g., viscosity \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-5}\)), the algorithm may discard relevant terms (R4).
- **Applicability Limitations**: The method is only demonstrated with zero boundary conditions (line 189); unclear if it works for other boundary types (R4). It is not tested on time-independent systems (e.g., Darcy, Poisson equations); if applied, how averaging of gradients in Algorithm 2 works without a time component is unclear (R4). The method does not handle irregular observations in time and space (R4).
- **Experiment Details and Questions**: In Fig. 1(c), the error increases with an increase in data for both methods, which is intriguing and not explained (R4). For ill-posed problems (Eqs. 13–14), the paper should provide error estimation and visual solution fields before concluding accuracy (R4). The noise variance is specified as a percentage without reference (e.g., is it SNR?) (R3). The normalization of the integral in (3) is not justified given a regular grid (R3). The authors state that the gradient is most sensitive to highest-order terms but do not explain why (R3). The definition of \(f^*\) (line 121) should map to \(\mathbb{R}^N\) on the grid \(\mathcal{G}\); consistency with coarser time grids is unclear (R3). The method should be evaluated with higher-order derivatives and polynomials (including cross-terms and mixed partials) to check accuracy and computational efficiency (R4). A posteriori analysis of learning performance with respect to data or PDE type, and well-posedness of the parametrization (e.g., performance for similar PDEs), is missing (R2).
