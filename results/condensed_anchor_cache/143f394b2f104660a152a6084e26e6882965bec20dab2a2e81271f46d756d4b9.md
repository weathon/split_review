## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper introduces FLanS, a free-form language-based segmentation model for medical images, trained on over 100k images from 7 public datasets covering 24 organ categories. It uses a RAG-based text prompt generator and a symmetry-aware canonicalization module. The model handles anatomy-informed, position-driven, and size-driven prompts. All reviewers note the underutilization of language in medical segmentation as a positive direction, and the RAG approach is seen as interesting. However, confidence in the method’s practical utility and novelty is divided. One reviewer (score 5) finds the contributions strong; another (score 1) questions the core motivation and novelty; two reviewers (score 3) express skepticism about real-world applicability and the sufficiency of the comparison. All reviewers agree on the need for stronger baselines, theoretical analysis, and greater dataset transparency.

### Strengths

- The paper explores how text description variability affects segmentation performance, which is interesting.
- Adapting RAG to generate diverse, realistic text descriptions is an interesting approach to improve generalization.
- Language inclusion in medical image segmentation is currently underutilized; the RAG-based creation of additional text reports is a plus.
- The use of position to steer segmentation predictions is novel.
- The paper is well-written and the core idea is easy to follow.
- FLanS is evaluated on diverse organ segmentation datasets (FLARE22, WORD, RAOS, in-domain and out-of-domain).
- The method is trained on a large-scale dataset of over 100k medical images from 7 public datasets.
- Key contributions: the RAG-based text prompt generator, the FLanS model for text-driven segmentation, and the symmetry-aware canonicalization module.

### Weaknesses

- **Missing supervised baselines (2D and 3D):** The paper lacks comparisons with current 2D state-of-the-art supervised models such as nn-UNet and Swin-UNet. A supervised performance reference (e.g., a 3D model like TotalSegmentator) is needed to assess relative performance.
- **Orientation canonicalization is not novel:** Changing image orientation to a consistent framing (e.g., RAS) is a standard preprocessing step. The paper does not justify why this is a clinical problem requiring a machine-learning module, nor does it quantify how often metadata is missing in practice. Several reviewers noted simpler established methods suffice.
- **Practical utility of anatomy-agnostic prompts is questionable:** The method operates on 2D slices, but in real 3D clinical volumes the “largest” or “leftmost” organ is inconsistent across slices. This makes the anatomy-agnostic setting impractical—users would need to adjust prompts per slice. It remains unclear how prompts like “segment the leftmost organ” should be applied across the whole volume.
- **Added benefit over direct organ selection is unclear:** For anatomy-informed prompts, a user could simply state the organ name; the method adds an unnecessary layer of indirection. A reviewer asked why this approach is preferable over using a supervised multi-organ segmenter (e.g., TotalSegmentator) and discarding unwanted masks, or over using a language model to map a description to a label and then retrieving a conventional segmentation.
- **Closed-set evaluation limits generalization claims:** The model is evaluated only on the same 24 organs seen in training, and test prompts are similar to training prompts. Experiments on novel or unseen classes are needed to demonstrate that language guidance enables true generalization beyond supervised fixed-organ segmentation.
- **Comparison to referring expression segmentation (RES) baselines is insufficient:** The paper claims superior performance over SOTA baselines like GRES, but it lacks direct visual examples, error analysis, and a detailed breakdown of performance across different prompt types. The cited work should be explicitly compared: Liu, Chang, et al. “GRES: Generalized referring expression segmentation.” CVPR 2023.
- **Lack of theoretical analysis:** As an ICLR submission, the paper does not provide formal analysis of the RAG-based generator or the symmetry-aware canonicalization module. A theoretical framework would strengthen the understanding of the model’s behavior and limitations.
- **Insufficient dataset construction details:** The description of how the free-form text prompts were generated, annotation guidelines, quality control measures, and potential biases is lacking. More transparency is needed to assess reliability and reproducibility.
- **Small organs and all organs not adequately handled:** The method’s ability to distinguish small or adjacent organs (e.g., overlapping organs in the abdomen) is not experimentally addressed; it is unclear whether the RAG generator or segmentation model can cover all organs in a region equally.
- **Visualization is incomplete:** The t-SNE plot of text embeddings should be perfectly separable for different organs; a more meaningful visualization—such as an attention map showing correlation between text and image—is needed to demonstrate semantic grounding.

Contrast: One reviewer (score 5) considers the contributions strong, while another (score 1) finds the core motivation and technical challenges insufficiently justified. The remaining reviewers (score 3) are skeptical of the method’s practical advantage over simpler alternatives.
