## Merged Review

### Summary
GODD is a diffusion-based generative framework for 3D molecules that addresses out-of-distribution (OOD) generation where distribution shifts arise from structural variations (e.g., rare scaffolds/rings). It employs an asymmetric equivariant autoencoder to capture "distributional physical priors" – learned latent representations of molecular fragments – and conditions the denoising process on these priors. The model is trained on data-abundant distributions and tested on OOD sparse regions without further exposure to those molecules. Experiments on QM9, GEOM-DRUG, and GEOM-LINK report improved success rate (up to 65.6% over baselines) and adaptation to fragment-based drug design.

### Strengths
- Tackles a challenging and poorly explored problem: generating 3D molecules in data-sparse regions due to structural shifts (scaffold/ring OOD). (R1, R2, R4)
- Promising results on multiple benchmarks with comprehensive analysis and useful evaluation metrics. (R1, R2, R4; R3 agrees performance beats baselines)
- Well-written paper with clear theoretical analysis demonstrating SE(3)-equivariance of physical priors and novel formulation of distributional physical priors from molecular fragments. (R2)
- Provided well-organized, ready-to-run code. (R4)
- The framework can be adapted to fragment-based drug design tasks. (R1, R2, R4)

### Weaknesses
- **Presentation and clarity (multiple reviewers)**: The method is presented in an overly complicated and pompous manner, obscuring the fact that it is essentially a conditional generation/inpainting task with a learned embedding; terms like "physical prior" are misleading—the prior is learned from 3D structure, not from physics. (R3 major; R1 minor)
- **Ambiguity in method details**: The integration of the learned latent representation (\(f_x\), \(f_h\)) into the noise-prediction model is unclear; Figure 1 is confusing (physical prior shown as global but is actually point-wise latent). (R4)
- **Unfair or questionable comparisons**: Baselines like EDM, GeoLDM, EquiFM are unconditional, so their low percentage of OOD generation is expected; C-EDM conditions only on a scalar, while GODD conditions on geometric elements (the very target structures); evaluating the partition of geometry elements in the output when these are given as input may not be fair. (R4) – Related: the model may simply modify the given scaffold locally rather than truly transferring knowledge from rich domains. (R1)
- **Performance insufficient for practical utility (minority reviewer, score 3)**: Molecular stability on QM9 barely exceeds 50%; on GEOM-DRUG, atom stability is near 0% (Table 3), suggesting generated molecules are likely not even valid, making comparisons nearly meaningless. Unconditional benchmarks (e.g., EDM) achieve ~80–92% atom stability on standard tasks. The method is far from useful in fragment-based drug discovery. (R3) – Note: other reviewers (R1, R2, R4) find results promising; this contrast is preserved.
- **Limited applicability**: Requires a predefined scaffold structure, which is a limitation for real-world scenarios where the desired scaffold is unknown or randomly generated. (R1, R2) – Also may not address OOD with entirely novel core structures. (R2)
- **Equivariance proofs are not novel**: The proofs of SE(3)-equivariance are simple extensions of well-known results (e.g., Kohler et al., ICML 2020), but the paper presents them as new without proper references. (R3)
- **Potential issue with scaffold editing during generation**: Since the scaffold is only a condition, not explicitly enforced, the model sometimes edits the scaffold (changes atom types/coordinates). This raises questions about how proportion and coverage are calculated in such cases. (R1)
- **Missing ablations and clarifications** (posed as questions/weaknesses):
  - For molecules with many rings, how is the input scaffold selected? (R1)
  - For linker design with disconnected fragments, can the scaffold be defined? Can the method handle incomplete scaffolds? (R1)
  - Expressiveness of the atom-level latent \(f_h\) (1 dimension) may be insufficient; would higher dimensionality help? (R1)
  - How is the atom number determined when sampling latent noise? (R1)
  - The weight defined in Section 2.2 (line 160) is not used anywhere. (R3)
- **Minor**: "Physical prior" naming is non-standard; it's a learned embedding from 3D fragment structure, not a physical constraint. (R1, R3)
