## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies whether recurrent (GRU, LSTM) and Transformer neural networks develop symbol-like number variables when trained on next-token prediction numeric equivalence tasks (repeat a token as many times as it occurred in the prompt). Using Distributed Alignment Search (DAS), the authors test alignment with three hypothesized symbolic programs: Up-Down (counting down from target quantity to zero), Up-Up (counting up from zero to target), and Ctx-Distr (summing context tokens). RNNs align with symbolic counting (Up-Down), while Transformers recompute context step-by-step without cumulative counter states. Larger models show less graded alignment. Task variants affect alignment; the Same-Object task yields no aligned symbolic algorithm.

### Strengths
- The paper furthers interpretability of transformers on mathematical/symbolic tasks and is well-written with clear motivation. (R1, R3, R4)
- Novel application of DAS to study symbolic counting behaviors in RNNs and Transformers; experiments present interesting results verifying the hypothesis. (R2)
- The experimental design is reasonable and thorough, identifying different characteristics across architectures and analyzing the impact of model size and intervention magnitude on interchange intervention accuracy (IIA). One reviewer, despite being less familiar with the field, found the paper accessible and appreciated its clarity. (R3)
- The approach of using causal methods (DAS) and training with next-token prediction (without explicit number-variable supervision) is well-motivated and clearly articulated. Core claims appear well-supported. (R4)
- The study offers insights into how RNNs simulate symbolic counting (incrementing/decrementing a variable) and how Transformers compute context step-by-step. (R2, R1)

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty and task simplicity:** The counting task is very simple (repeating a token the same number of times as in the prompt). Similar or more complex analyses exist in prior work on modular addition, addition, induction, relational reasoning, and grammatical counting (e.g., [1-6] cited in reviews). The paper applies DAS without contributing new methodological insights. The findings are low-hanging fruit and do not advance the Pareto frontier of interpretability. The lack of more complex tasks (nested operations, conditional counting) weakens the broader claim of discovering “symbol-like variables.” Even the more positive reviewer (R3) noted the lack of ambition; the most negative reviewer (R1) emphasized that the paper does not advance beyond existing work. (R1, R2, R3, R4)
- **Limited causal interventions:** DAS interventions are only within-distribution (target quantities held out within the observed integer range). The paper does not test generalization to numbers outside the training range (e.g., 30) or apply more aggressive interventions like flipping the phase variable multiple times within a sequence. Such tests are hallmarks of systematicity in symbolic programs and are strongly recommended. (R3, R4)
- **Lack of verification that alignment implies true implementation:** The paper only shows alignment between hypothesized programs and neural representations but does not rule out spurious correlation. No method is provided to distinguish whether the model actually implements the proposed counting mechanism or merely behaves as if it does. Additional validation (e.g., advanced alignment analyses, larger model evaluations) is needed. (R2)
- **Superficial analysis of Transformers:** The analysis of Transformers is shallow; it does not delve into attention weights, MLP hidden states, or positional encodings. DAS is only applied between the two Transformer layers, not within MLP or attention heads. More thorough investigation of how information is processed is missing. (R2, R4)
- **Unexplained result for Same-Object task:** The Same-Object task yields models that do not align with any symbolic algorithm, contrary to expectations. This is not explained, yet it is critical for the paper’s mechanistic interpretability claims. (R3, R4)
- **Clarity of DAS explanation:** The explanation of Distributed Alignment Search is unclear and would benefit from a figure and more intuition. Readers unfamiliar with DAS may find it hard to follow. This is important given DAS’s centrality to the results; even the more positive reviewer (R3) noted this issue. (R3, R4, R2)
- **Figure 3 right panel uninformative:** The panel showing model size vs. task performance and symbolic alignment is trivial (it is obvious that larger models perform better). The results in Section 4.2 are sufficiently conveyed by Figure 3 left panel alone. (R3, R4)
- **Missing experiments and ablations:**
  - No study of why Up-Down is learned over alternatives (e.g., inductive biases). (R1)
  - No exploration of multiple program variables (e.g., both count and phase in Up-Down). (R3, R4)
  - No analysis of the impact of the hyperparameter *D* (non-zero dimensions) on results. (R3)
  - No evidence supporting the claim that larger numbers are less precise; the paper could check if task performance is worse at larger numbers, especially earlier in training. (R4)
  - Cited work [7] suggests additional experimental evidence through advanced alignment analyses or larger model evaluations would be valuable. (R2)
- **Minor issues:** Some ungrammatical sentences and typos (e.g., lines 169, 190, 246). The description of the “Phase variable to track quantity at each step” is unclear (what does “quantity” refer to?); the text should explicitly indicate that this variable tracks whether the ‘T’ token has been encountered. (R3, R4)
