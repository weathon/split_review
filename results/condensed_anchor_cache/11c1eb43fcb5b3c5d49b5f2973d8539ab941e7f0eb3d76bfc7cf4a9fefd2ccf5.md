## Merged Review

### Summary

The paper proposes a new loss landscape sharpness metric (inspired by Keskar et al. 2017, adapted for autoregressive decoder-only models by computing on the last token’s logits) as a computationally cheap proxy to detect training instability induced by reduced-precision floating-point representations (FP8). It investigates the stability of FP8 LLM training by simulating incremental bit reductions in mantissa and exponent. The authors conclude that FP8 training is not yet robust enough (compared to BF16) to be cost‑effective, as it severely limits hyperparameter ranges. Experiments use TinyLlama and nanoGPT as proxies for larger models. Reviewer scores are 5, 6, 5, 3; one reviewer (R4, score 3, confidence 4) is substantially more critical than the others.

### Strengths

1. **Novel, computationally cheap stability metric** – The sharpness metric computed on the last token’s logits (rather than on inputs) reduces computational cost because it needs to be measured only once per checkpoint. The metric detects training divergence before it manifests in the loss curve, as shown in Figure 8 and Table 1 for E8M5 and other precisions (R1, R2, R3). R1 calls it an “early‑warning system”. R2 emphasises its effectiveness and cheapness. R3 describes the metric as “good” and a beneficial analytical framework. R4 acknowledges the idea but insists that comparison to prior sharpness methods is a must.

2. **Confirms greater sensitivity to exponent bits than mantissa bits** – The paper’s ablation results agree with previous findings that exponent reduction is more destabilising than mantissa reduction (R2).

3. **Practical scale and multi‑dimensional bit analysis** – Unlike many studies limited to small models, this work evaluates FP8 on full‑size LLMs (TinyLlama, nanoGPT). It uniquely simulates bit reductions on *both* exponent and mantissa, while most prior work only varies mantissa bits, giving a more complete picture of precision effects (R1).

4. **Real‑world hyperparameter sensitivity demonstration** – The paper shows experimentally that FP8 narrows the range of usable learning rates compared to BF16, a cost‑saving limitation not extensively examined before (R1).

5. **Motivation for FP8 stability research** – The central concern about FP8 stability relative to FP16/BF16 is compelling, and the analysis can benefit the research community (R3).

### Weaknesses

1. **Lack of comparison to prior or alternative stability‑detection methods** – The paper does not compare its sharpness metric against the original Keskar et al. (2017) metric or other state‑of‑the‑art approaches (e.g., metrics based on gradients or internal activations) in terms of computational cost and early‑detection performance. A direct comparison is needed to demonstrate superiority for autoregressive models (R2, R4).

2. **Contradiction with successful FP8 training results in prior work** – The paper claims FP8 cannot match BF16 baselines, yet two prior works (Microsoft MS-AMP, arXiv:2310.18313, tested on 7B–175B models; Intel, arXiv:2409.12517) show FP8 can converge to FP16/BF16 loss levels. The authors attribute the divergence in their own experiments to the use of an open‑source dataset, but the Intel paper also used open‑source datasets and achieved convergence. Furthermore, the Intel paper attributed divergence to the SwiGLU activation, which is not used in nanoGPT (which uses GeLU). This discrepancy is not adequately explained, and the paper does not replicate or account for the experimental settings of those successful FP8 trainings (R3, R4). R4 considers this the “weaker part of the paper” and maintains a low score after the rebuttal.

3. **Limited scale and generalisability** – Experiments rely on smaller models (TinyLlama, nanoGPT) to approximate larger ones. Recent work suggests that robustness to low precision improves with model size and data quality (as seen in post‑training quantization), so results may not transfer to models like GPT‑3 (R1, R3). R3 expresses low confidence that observed effects persist in larger models.

4. **Unclear experimental details** – The dataset used in key experiments (e.g., Figure 6) is not clearly specified. Training steps are given without an intuitive sense of how many tokens were trained. The experimental setup for hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate schedules) is described only for one setting, and no alternative schedules are tested (R2, R3).

5. **Bit‑reduction implementation concerns** –  
   - For exponent‑bit reduction, the paper uses clipping; for mantissa reduction, it uses a bitmask that is acknowledged as an “imperfect approximation”. The potential bias introduced by this masking (especially in the backward pass) is not quantified, and its impact on conclusions is unclear (R4).  
   - Scaling is not applied when reducing exponent bits; adding scaling could substantially alter the results (R4).

6. **Missing experiments and analyses** –  
   - The paper does not report results for failures with very low exponent bits (e.g., E7‑like configurations) (R2).  
   - The sharpness trends in Table 1 (Llama 7B at various precisions) are not linked to specific thresholds that clearly predict instability; it is unclear what sharpness value signals imminent divergence (R1).  
   - The observed performance degradation in Figure 6 when applying MS-AMP with/without the LM head in FP8 is not explained, nor are loss trends compared to BF16 using exponential moving averages (R1).  
   - The paper does not investigate the two suggestions from the Discussion (high‑precision initial training stages, higher precision for sensitive layers) as potential stabilization techniques (R2).

7. **Limited scope of training hyperparameters** – The reported experiments are conducted only with one learning rate schedule and one set of initial conditions; verifying consistency across more schedules (e.g., different initial learning rates, warmup strategies) is necessary (R2).

8. **No task‑level performance evaluation** – The analysis focuses solely on training metrics (loss sharpness, divergence times) without assessing whether models trained in FP8 achieve competitive performance on downstream NLP benchmarks (e.g., perplexity, GLUE). This narrow scope limits the practical applicability of the findings (R1).
