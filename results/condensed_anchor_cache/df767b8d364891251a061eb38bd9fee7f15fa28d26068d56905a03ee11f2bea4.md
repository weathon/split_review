## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies identifiability of hidden causal variables and latent factors from multiple distributions (environments). Under sparsity constraints on the Markov network over latent variables and a sufficient-change condition across environments, the moralized causal DAG can be recovered up to simple indeterminacies. Separately, under a modular-change condition, the latent factors governing mechanism shifts can be recovered up to component-wise invertible transformations. The theoretical results rely on a characterization of conditional independence via second-order derivatives of the log-density (Lin, 1997). Experiments on small synthetic datasets are provided for the Markov network recovery.

### Strengths
- The problem of identifying latent causal structure is highly relevant.
- The paper studies a very general model (Eq. 1) and establishes fundamental identifiability conditions.
- Theoretical contributions include applying a proof technique from Lin (1997) using second-order derivatives of the log-density to characterize conditional independence.
- The connection between Markov networks and causal DAGs is interesting.
- The use of density derivatives for identification is of interest.
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.

### Weaknesses
- **Sufficient Changes Assumption (Assumption 1) is very strong and not discussed in practical terms.** It requires independence of conditional densities for every point \(z\), and the number of environments must be at least as large as the number of latent variables. No real-world examples or case studies are provided to justify when this holds in practice. Reviewers comment that the method cannot be used for a single environment, and the paper misleads by positioning itself as a generalization of single-environment methods.
- **The model assumes \(g\) is invertible and deterministic, forcing \(\dim(X) = \dim(Z)\) for smooth maps.** This is unrealistic. The change-of-variables formula used in Appendix A requires a square Jacobian; for non-square cases the argument would be different (see [3] suggested by Reviewer 1). The paper does not discuss how to relax invertibility or handle different dimensions.
- **The number of latent variables must be known in advance.** The paper does not address how to infer this in practice or adapt to unknown dimensionality.
- **Key definitions are missing or unclear in the main text.** “Modular changes” in Theorem 5, “sparsity constraint” in Theorems 2 and 3, and notations like \(\tilde{Z}\), \(h(\cdot)\), \(q(\cdot)\), \(m\) in Theorem 1, whether \(\theta\) is one-dimensional, and \(u\) in Eq. (3) are not properly introduced. This makes it hard to evaluate the claims.
- **Proof of Theorem 1 is problematic.** It appears to rely on Assumption 3, which is not stated in the theorem itself – a critical error. The significance of the linearly independent vectors (bullet 2) is not explained. The proof is difficult to follow.
- **Potential mathematical error regarding invertibility when dimensions differ.** The argument in Appendix A assumes a square Jacobian, but if \(\dim(X) \neq \dim(Z)\), a different change-of-variables formula (e.g., for manifolds) would be needed.
- **Proof of Theorem 5 is unclear.** The last line’s connection to the conclusion is not explained, and the role of the modular-change assumption is not explicitly stated. The zero-product condition on partial derivatives is not clearly linked to component-wise identifiability.
- **Insufficient connection to existing literature.** The identification technique using density derivatives is standard in causal discovery (e.g., additive noise, post-nonlinear models); the paper does not clarify how its results relate to or generalize these. Significant related work on multiple environments and causality is not cited. The neural network architectures are minor modifications of known normalizing flow architectures for causal discovery (proper references missing).
- **Experimental evaluation is limited and lacks critical details.** Experiments only validate Theorem 3 (Markov network recovery); no experiments for Theorem 5 (latent factor identification). Data generation (e.g., what are “noises” and “biases” in Sec. 5.3), how synthetic data satisfy assumptions (sufficient environments, invertibility of MLP), and the relationship between number of environments and latent variables are not specified. No baseline comparisons. Source code is not provided.
- **Evaluation in Figures 2 and 3 is ambiguous.** The scatter plots do not clearly show that hidden structure is recovered; their interpretation is unexplained.
- **The claim that Gaussian noise leads to mixing aligns with Theorem 3 is unsupported.** Theorem 3 does not distinguish between Gaussian and non-Gaussian noise.
- **Practical implications of identifying latent factors \(\theta\) up to component-wise invertible transformations are not discussed.** It is unclear what downstream tasks this enables or what “identifying potential shifts” means.
- **Reviewers questioned the modeling choice.** Why not directly model an SCM for \(X\) without assuming a latent SCM and invertible mixing function? A discussion is missing.
- **Additional minor issues:** On page 3, should it read \(Z_j \to X_i\) if and only if \(Z_j \in PA(X_i)\)? How is \((\hat{g}, \hat{f}, p_{\hat{Z}})\) learned to achieve Eq. (2)? In Eq. (7), what does dividing by \(S^{(u)}\) mean? The relation between modular change and the independence of causal mechanisms (ICM) is not clarified, including whether inequality constraints affect the result.
- **One reviewer (score 5) was more positive, noting the paper is well-written and the theory interesting, but still raised many of the same shortcomings.** The other three reviewers (scores 3, 3, 3) had strong reservations about assumptions, rigor, and experimental validation.
