- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 3, 5, 5, 5

## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Deep Anti-Regularized Ensembles (DARE) for improving uncertainty quantification on out-of-distribution data. It introduces an anti-regularization term that penalizes small weights, combined with a control process that maintains in-distribution loss below a threshold. Reviewers found the core idea novel, intuitive, and well-motivated, with promising toy experiments and a real-world CityCam application. However, one reviewer (score 3) was substantially more critical, citing significant presentation issues, computational overhead, and lack of convincing empirical evidence. The other three reviewers (scores 5) were positive overall but raised several concerns about the theoretical analysis, experimental confounds, loss function choices, and missing ablations.

### Strengths
- The idea of increasing ensemble diversity by maximizing weight magnitude while preserving in-distribution accuracy is creative, simple, and intuitively appealing (Reviewers 1,2,3,4).
- The control process for balancing anti-regularization with a validation-loss threshold provides a principled way to manage the trade-off (Reviewers 3,4).
- The theoretical motivation and analysis (Sec 2.3) are appreciated, although clarity issues were noted (Reviewers 1,2,3).
- Results on toy datasets (Section 4.1) demonstrate uncertainty quantification behavior comparable to distance-aware models like Gaussian processes (Reviewer 2).
- The CityCam application (Section 4.3) shows usefulness in a realistic distribution-shift scenario (Reviewer 2).
- Experiment settings are described in detail, supporting reproducibility (Reviewer 2); code is provided (Reviewer 3).
- The coverage of related work is reasonable (Reviewer 3).

### Weaknesses
- **Theoretical analysis (Sec 2.3) is unclear and unconvincing in its current form.**  
  - The definition and labeling of assumptions are missing (e.g., “Assumption 4” is referenced but not defined; Equations 4–6 are unclear) (Reviewers 1,4).  
  - The claim before Corollary 1.1 that “we see the importance of choosing a strictly concave function like the logarithm” is unsupported by any prior demonstration (Reviewers 1,4).  
  - The variance calculation (Equation 7 and preceding text) requires input features to be mean-centered, which is not stated (Reviewers 1,4).  
  - The connection to Theorem 1 is not explicit; the notation mixes scalars and vectors without clear distinction; several statements (e.g., “θ centered in θ*”) are ambiguous (Reviewer 3).  
  - Reviewer 3 recommends rewriting this section entirely to make the argument more clear.

- **Experimental design contains too many moving parts; causal conclusions are not isolated.**  
  - The conformalization procedure (Section 4.1) adjusts intervals using a calibration set, so performance gains could stem from conformal quantile regression rather than the proposed method (Reviewer 3).  
  - In classification experiments, a pretrained ResNet is used and only the last linear layer is ensembled (multiple heads). This undermines the random-initialization aspect that the method’s motivation relies on and contradicts the “ensemble of full networks” claim (Reviewers 2,3).  
  - The method is evaluated only on fully-connected layer models (or last linear layer); no experiments test deeper architectures or networks with normalization layers (e.g., BatchNorm) where the approach may be less effective due to scale-invariance (Reviewer 2).  
  - High variance on OOD data could arise from degenerate predictions per ensemble member rather than meaningful uncertainty; no metric for OOD error or in-distribution test error is provided to rule this out (Reviewer 3).  
  - Table 2 presentation is confusing: column labels do not clearly distinguish AUROC from accuracy; captions need to state explicitly that columns 1,2,4,5 are AUROC and columns 3,6 are accuracy (Reviewers 1,2,4).  
  - Figure 2 top row: red and green markers are unlabeled, making interpretation impossible (Reviewers 1,4).

- **OOD detection results are not consistently superior.**  
  For CIFAR-10 as in-distribution, DARE improves AUROC on SVHN but performs worse on CIFAR100 compared to baselines. It is unclear whether the method is beneficial for problems of CIFAR-10 complexity (Reviewer 2).

- **Hyperparameter τ introduces significant computational overhead and ambiguous learning.**  
  Setting τ based on the validation loss of a standard trained model requires a complete training run beforehand that cannot be parallelized, reducing the gains from the method’s parallelizability (Reviewers 1,4). Only 5 runs of 5 networks each are performed (Section before 5); it is unclear how τ is learned from these runs (Reviewers 1,4).

- **Custom loss function (Equation 10) raises multiple concerns.**  
  - The MSE with one-hot targets scaled by K (number of classes) appears arbitrary; scaling by K could make outputs indistinguishable when K is large (Reviewers 1,4).  
  - Standard softmax was found ineffective, but the paper does not justify why this alternative is better or provide ablation comparing it to softmax with the same ensemble (Reviewers 2,4).  
  - According to the decomposition of Krogh & Vedelsby (1994), Equation 10 (addition of the second term) actually *minimizes* diversity on in-distribution data, contradicting the paper’s goal of encouraging diversity (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 3 notes that recent work suggests discouraging diversity may be beneficial, but this needs clarification.

- **Missing related works and comparisons.**  
  - The paper should cite and discuss Lee, Yao & Finn (2022, ICLR) which introduces a similar diversity-encouraging objective for OOD robustness (Reviewer 3).  
  - The connection between ensemble performance and diversity was formalized by Krogh & Vedelsby (1994, NIPS), an earlier work than the cited Pagliardini et al. (Reviewer 3).  
  - Methods that adjust weight variance according to feature covariance structure (e.g., Izmailov et al. 2021, Wang & Aitchison 2022) are relevant and should be discussed (Reviewer 2).

- **Method name “anti-regularization” is a misnomer.**  
  The approach actually imposes an inductive bias (maximizing weights associated with OOD-frequent neurons), which is a form of regularization, not the opposite. A more accurate name would be “anti-shrinkage” (Reviewer 3).

- **Claim of parallel optimization is inconsistent with the loss function.**  
  Equation 10 includes a term that depends on the ensemble average \( \bar{h}(x) \), which would require the entire ensemble for backpropagation, contradicting the claim that each network is trained independently (Reviewer 3).

- **Activation function choice may be suboptimal.**  
  ReLU can cause dead neurons; a smoother activation like GELU might allow more weights to receive gradients and grow (Reviewer 4). This is not discussed or ablated.

- **Abstract clarity:** The term “large weights network” is used without first explaining that regularization reduces weights, making the motivation unclear at the start (Reviewers 1,4).

- **Reviewer 1 (score 3) was significantly more critical** than the other three reviewers (scores 5). The lower-rated reviewer emphasized presentation flaws, experimental overhead, and the need for larger-scale experiments to convincingly demonstrate efficacy. The other reviewers, while generally positive, collectively raised all the substantive issues listed above.
