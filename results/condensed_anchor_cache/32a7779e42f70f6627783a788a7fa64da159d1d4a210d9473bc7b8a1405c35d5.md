## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes a Follow-the-Perturbed-Leader (FTPL) algorithm for adversarial bandits with heavy-tailed, possibly negative and unbounded losses. The algorithm uses a skipping mechanism to ignore large losses and achieves near-optimal worst-case regret. The paper applies the algorithm to two settings: adversarial bandits with Huber contamination (losses corrupted by an arbitrary distribution) and with local differential privacy (LDP) guarantees. Reviewers generally find the paper well-written and clear, and the contributions interesting. One reviewer (Score 5) is more critical, arguing the improvement over FTRL is trivial; other reviewers (Scores 6,8,8) consider the work significant.

### Strengths
- **Clarity and exposition:** The paper is well-written, easy to follow, and clearly describes the algorithm, challenges, analysis, and applications (Reviewers 1,2,3,4). The proof sketch and appendix are well-organized and helpful (Reviewers 2,3).
- **Regret bounds and improvements:** The proposed FTPL algorithm achieves (nearly) optimal worst-case regret for heavy-tailed adversarial bandits, improving over FTRL-based methods (Reviewers 1,2,4). The improvement is particularly notable for the two applications: for Huber contamination, the algorithm matches the lower bound up to $\polylog(K)$ factors; for LDP, it improves the best-known regret by a $\polylog(T)$ factor (Reviewers 2,4). These results are new and better than prior work (Reviewer 2).
- **Algorithmic simplicity:** The FTPL algorithm has a more streamlined design than state-of-the-art FTRL algorithms, eliminating the need for additional explicit exploration and solely maintaining the absolute value of loss estimates below a predetermined threshold (Reviewer 1). It also avoids an undesired assumption (Assumption 2) required by FTRL (Reviewer 4).
- **Applications:** The two applications (Huber contamination and LDP) are interesting, and the results are either near-optimal or state-of-the-art (Reviewers 2,4).

### Weaknesses
- **Novelty concerns:** One reviewer (Score 5) argues that using FTRL (e.g., Wei et al., 2018) with a similar skipping reduction to bounded losses trivially yields the same regret bound up to logarithmic terms, providing a detailed derivation. This reviewer believes the claimed improvement is overstated. Another reviewer (Score 8) notes that the “breakthrough” relies on a known property (FTPL with Laplace perturbation) and the combination with Skipping is rather obvious, though they acknowledge that tuning parameters (e.g., $L_t$) is non-trivial (Reviewer 3). Reviewer 4 notes that claiming Lemma 1 as a key observation is an overstatement, as it appears in previous work; the authors should clarify what is new.
- **Geometric Resampling necessity unclear:** Reviewer 2 argues that standard importance weighting might suffice, but FTPL requires knowledge of the weights $w_t$, which are not directly accessible; the authors do not clearly explain why Geometric Resampling (GR) is needed and whether it is only for the DP application. This point is critical and not addressed.
- **Lack of empirical evaluation:** The paper is purely theoretical; several reviewers suggest including empirical analysis, e.g., running experiments with the FTRL algorithm of Huang et al. (2022) in this setting, or real-world examples (Reviewers 1,3).
- **Practical motivation and intuition:** Reviewer 3 questions the practical motivation for modeling losses as negative and heavy-tailed, arguing that the Huber contamination setting is valuable only if the contamination parameter $\beta$ is significantly small. Reviewer 4 asks for more intuition on why skipping large losses is effective, since heavy-tailed losses can be informative; ignoring them may discard useful signal.
- **Parameter knowledge requirement:** The algorithm requires knowledge of the heavy-tail parameters $\sigma$ and $\alpha$ (Assumption 1) to deterministically tune the step-size, skipping threshold, and $L_t$. Reviewers ask whether near-optimal regret can be achieved without this knowledge (Reviewers 1,3), and whether the skipping threshold $r$ can be set adaptively (Reviewer 3).
- **Computational cost:** The While-Loop runs up to $L_t$ rounds at each step, which may be computationally expensive. Reviewer 3 asks if one could design a more efficient sampling scheme that checks multiple perturbed leaders simultaneously.
- **Adaptive adversaries:** Reviewer 4 asks whether the results extend to settings with an adaptive adversary (the paper assumes an oblivious adversary); this is a missing extension.
- **Stochastic setting (best-of-both-worlds):** Reviewer 1 notes that FTRL is best-of-both-worlds (Huang et al., 2022) and asks whether the proposed FTPL algorithm can also achieve optimal regret in the stochastic setting; this is not discussed.
- **Applicability of DP result:** Reviewer 3 asks whether Corollary 1 (the LDP result) is specific to the $\epsilon$-differential privacy Laplace mechanism or generalizes to other privacy mechanisms that still guarantee heavy-tailed losses.
- **Polylogarithmic factors:** Reviewer 2 asks about the presence of $\polylog(K)$ factors in the regret and whether they can be removed entirely; no intuition is given for their necessity.
- **Necessity of skipping:** Reviewer 4 asks whether skipping large losses is necessary or whether a more clever analysis could remove this step. This is related to the intuition point but deserves explicit consideration.
