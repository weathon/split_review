## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper investigates whether in-context learning (ICL) with Transformers can generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks. Through synthetic experiments (e.g., learning mathematical functions, retrieval mappings) and analyses with GPT-2 and Llama2-7b, the authors find that ICL primarily implements functions from the pretraining hypothesis space rather than learning new functions. The ability to handle abstract labels in context is attributed to retrieval mechanisms learned during training, not to true OOD generalization. The paper also introduces a theoretical “low-test-error preference” mechanism: when faced with OOD tasks, the model selects the pretraining function that achieves the lowest test error on the given context. Results are supported by numerical experiments and a theoretical analysis that builds on prior work.

### Strengths
1. **Novel theoretical insight**: The paper provides a formal characterization of the “low-test-error preference” mechanism, explaining how models select among pretraining functions when presented with OOD in-context examples (Reviewer 1, 2).
2. **Well-designed experiments**: The experimental setups are carefully constructed to test specific hypotheses, e.g., the abstract-label classification experiments (Fig. 4c) cleanly demonstrate that this ability relies on retrieval rather than new-task learning (Reviewer 1, 2, 3).
3. **Significant problem**: Understanding OOD generalization of ICL is an important and timely question (Reviewer 2, 3).
4. **Interesting OOD tasks**: The synthetic word classification task in Section 3.3 is a simple yet meaningful test that could not have appeared by accident in pretraining (Reviewer 4). The use of Llama2-7b adds realism (Reviewer 1).
5. **Good contextualization**: The paper effectively references and distinguishes itself from existing related work (Reviewer 3).
6. **Originality**: To the reviewers’ knowledge, the behavior of ICL on OOD tasks has not been carefully demonstrated before (Reviewer 2).

### Weaknesses
1. **Presentation issues**: 
   - Typos throughout, e.g., subsection title “3.3 REAL-WORLD LLMS MAT NOT NECESSARILY IN-CONTEXT LEARN NEW TASKS” (Reviewer 4).
   - Equation 1 appears to supervise the prediction with \(f(x_i)\) instead of \(f(x_{I+1})\) (Reviewer 1).
   - Figure 2 notation is unclear: what is \(y_i^{\text{id}}\)? Is it the same as \(I_{y_i}\)? (Reviewer 1).
   - Caption of Figure 3 uses quotation marks incorrectly (Reviewer 1).
   - The paper needs a thorough proofread (Reviewer 4).

2. **Methodology concerns**:
   - **Training set size in Section 3.1**: When the training range is larger (e.g., \(y_i^{\text{id}} \in [50, 455]\)), test-time performance improves, but the number of training tasks may not be controlled. Better performance might simply be due to more data; the authors should clarify the number of tasks per range and ensure fair comparison (Reviewer 1).
   - **Motivation for experimental designs**: 
     - The first and last experiments in Section 3 map index embeddings to new indices – the motivation is unclear and no citation to prior work using such designs is provided (Reviewer 1).
     - The experiment in Figure 5 (OOD mapping task) is presented without a clear hypothesis or connection to real-world use cases; it “seems like a really whacky OOD task” that may not be representative (Reviewer 2).
   - **Assumptions about OOD for pre-trained models**: In Section 3.3, the authors claim the synthetic word classification task is OOD for Llama2-7b, but provide no evidence that it is far from the pretraining distribution (Reviewer 1). A control experiment training a model explicitly on this ICL task would verify that it is solvable when in-distribution (Reviewer 3).
   - **Limited evaluation setups**: All conclusions are drawn from simple curve-fitting or rule-based mappings; these may be insufficient to convincingly characterize the behavior of large models (Reviewer 1). One reviewer notes that the results are “unsurprising” – Transformers are deep neural networks and are not expected to generalize OOD without the right inductive biases; e.g., no one would genuinely expect a model trained on linear functions to generalize to quadratic ones (Reviewer 4). This contrasts with the originality claim (Reviewer 2).

3. **Theoretical and interpretive gaps**:
   - **No engagement with Bayes-optimal theory**: The paper does not discuss the dominant theory of ICL as performing Bayes-optimal prediction, which could account for all presented results (Reviewer 4). For Section 2, the model learns a prior over pretraining tasks; for Section 4, the posterior over task classes is updated by the context. The paper should explain which findings cannot be explained by this framework (Reviewer 4).
   - **Unclear distinction between existing knowledge and new contributions**: Sections 2 and 4 could be merged; it is hard to identify which findings are novel to this paper (Reviewer 2, Reviewer 3). The theoretical results heavily borrow from prior work [1], though the paper is transparent about this (Reviewer 3).
   - **Overclaimed conclusion about abstract labels**: The claim that “ICL’s ability to perform abstract label classification may not serve as evidence of learning new tasks” is partially contradicted by Figure 4c – when pretrained on enough diversity, models *can* classify OOD labels (green line). This suggests that, in a limited context, transformers *can* learn new OOD tasks (Reviewer 2).

4. **Clarity of specific sections**:
   - Section 4.2 (and Figures 8, 9) is hard to follow. The last rows of those figures show high losses throughout, and it is unclear why they do not match the lowest test-error pretraining task as expected (Reviewer 3).
   - The explanation of the retrieval-based version of the Llama2-7b task is difficult to understand (Reviewer 2).
   - Loss plots (e.g., Figure 7) should be shown on a log scale, especially when values include double-descent peaks that obscure small loss differences. The y-ticklabels in Figure 7b+c suggest the loss is ~10x higher than in 7a; the authors should explain why (Reviewer 3).

5. **Contrasting reviewer confidence**: Reviewers 2 and 4 express high confidence (4), with Reviewer 2 praising the originality and quality, while Reviewer 4 finds the results unsurprising and the contribution low (score 5). The paper clearly provoked disagreement on the novelty and significance of its empirical findings.
