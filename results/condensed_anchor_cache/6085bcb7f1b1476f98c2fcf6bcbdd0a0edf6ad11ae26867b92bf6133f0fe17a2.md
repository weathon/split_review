## Merged Review

### Summary
This paper addresses error slice discovery—identifying semantically coherent subsets where a model underperforms. It proposes *manifold compactness*, a coherence metric that does not rely on predefined slice labels or metadata, and presents the MCSD algorithm that directly optimizes risk and compactness. Experiments on several datasets are provided.

### Strengths
- Proposed a new coherence metric (manifold compactness) that does not require predefined slice labels or metadata (Reviewers 1, 2, 4).
- The MCSD algorithm directly treats risk and coherence as optimization objectives (Reviewer 4).
- The field is relatively new and interesting, meriting further exploration (Reviewer 2).
- Extensive experiments and case studies support the views, with new metrics and algorithms (Reviewers 2, 3, 4). “The experimental data is thorough and demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method across multiple datasets” (Reviewer 4).
- The paper provides a different perspective for measuring error slices (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Lack of theoretical analysis**: “Although extensive case studies support the authors' viewpoints, the lack of theoretical analysis is regrettable” (Reviewer 2).
- **Missing complexity analysis**: No analysis of time and space complexity. Constructing a kNN graph costs \(O(n^2)\) and solving Eq. 2 costs \(O(n^3)\); efficiency is a drawback as seen in Table 8. The authors should explain why this efficiency loss is worthwhile (Reviewer 2).
- **Metric concerns and insufficient justification**:
  - The example on page 4 used to argue for monotonicity as a criterion for a good coherence measure is not convincing. An arbitrary measure (e.g., difference between cluster size and total size) could also satisfy monotonicity. The authors need to further clarify the example, e.g., provide step-by-step details on how Figure 2c is generated and clarify the relationship between optimization variables \(w\) and \(g\) (Reviewer 3).
  - “The paper suggests that Euclidean distance is inferior to manifold, but this may be misleading. Constructing the k-nearest neighbors graph still depends on Euclidean distance. Additionally, because distance variance lacks bounds, it is more sensitive to samples, especially if the sample features are not normalized. Therefore, the superiority of manifold compactness over variance may not be fully convincing. Consider adding a new distance-based measure. Different manifold constructions and distance calculations are also anticipated” (Reviewer 2).
  - Reviewer 4 questions: “The expected trend in the experimental data is that as ACC increases, the Comp result should also increase. Why do Tables 2, 3, and 5 not follow this trend? Perhaps I am missing something—do the authors provide any further explanation?” (Reviewer 4).
- **Missing key details and unclear optimization**:
  - The dimensions of all variables are not provided clearly, making the paper difficult to understand (Reviewer 1).
  - “What’s the physical meaning of the second term in (2)? What’s the optimization solution for (2)?” (Reviewer 1).
  - The variables in the optimization objective appear to be \(w\) and the binary classifier \(g\); how does this generate a new space? How is Figure 2c generated? (Reviewer 3).
  - “The author mentions in line 273 that the \(w\) obtained by solving Eq. 2 is used to train the slice function \(g\). Are the training samples obtained using \(w\) already the desired samples? Does this result in a meaningless slice function \(g\)?” (Reviewer 3).
  - “Does the slicing function obtained by the authors give the same output results from the same input data in each slicing? If so, does multiple slices require re-solving Eq. 2 and re-training the slice function \(g\)? I think the desired slicing result can already be obtained before training the slicing function \(g\), does this mean that training the slicing function is meaningless?” (Reviewer 3).
  - “What is the difference between Eq. 2 and a clustering method? Equation 2 seems to be a clustering method as well. (e.g. Discuss how the proposed method specifically addresses the goals of error slice discovery in ways that clustering alone may not)” (Reviewer 3).
  - “In line 71, the authors describe the coherence method of past methods as only indirectly joining the optimization process. However, the authors' method also seems to be a two-step optimization process: i.e., first optimize the clustering and then optimize the slicing function \(g\).” (Reviewer 3).
  - The algorithm is divided into multiple parts; an end-to-end model would be more desirable (Reviewer 2).
- **Insufficient comparisons and unexplained results**:
  - “There are not enough comparison methods. What are the main advantages of the proposed method compared with the existing works?” (Reviewer 1).
  - “Why does Table 4 only compare the MCSD method with the Spotlight method?” (Reviewer 4).
  - “Why does the ACC of the error slices identified by MCSD outperform the SOTA methods by such a large margin [in Figure 4 on CELEBA]? Besides this specific similar feature, what other factors could lead to poor performance of the sliced subsets?” (Reviewer 4).
- **Problem definition notation issue**: “In the first paragraph of the problem definition on page 2: Does \(\mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y}\) represent the Cartesian product of two sets? If so, one should represent the predicted label, and the other should represent the true label. Why are both symbols \(\mathcal{Y}\)?” (Reviewer 4).
- **Contrasting readability**: While Reviewer 2 finds “the organization and writing of this paper [are] reader-friendly”, Reviewer 1 states “The paper is difficult to understand and follow as the source code is not provided.” (Reviewers 1, 2)
- **Weak motivation**: “The authors need to clarify further in the Section 1 what is the reason for proposing this method. The current motivation of the authors for proposing the method seems to be somewhat weak.” (Reviewer 3)
