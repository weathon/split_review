## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper proposes Training-time Neuron Alignment by Partially Fixing Neurons (TNA-PFN), a method that freezes a fraction of network parameters at initialization and trains the rest, aiming to reduce permutation symmetry and improve Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC). A preliminary theorem bounds gradient and curvature by the number of learned parameters, and experiments on MNIST/CIFAR10 show lower LMC barriers compared to vanilla training. Extensions to federated learning (FedPFN, FedPNU) are also presented. Reviewers agree the problem is important and the method is simple, but disagree on novelty, theoretical strength, and practical significance. Two reviewers (R1, R4) rate the method positively (6), citing extensive experiments and FL potential; two (R2, R3) are more critical (5), questioning novelty, the gap between theory and results, and the marginal improvement over existing post-hoc alignment.

### Strengths
- Training-time alignment avoids expensive post-hoc permutation search; not a post-processing step (R1).
- Simplicity of the method; clean and easy-to-follow writing (R2, R3).
- Extensive experiments and ablation studies covering width, depth, mask ratio, and multiple datasets (R1, R3).
- Theoretical support (Theorem 3.2) linking lower learned dimension to lower barriers; R3 finds the hypothesis convincing, though other reviewers consider it weak (R1, R2).
- Applications to federated learning (FedPFN, FedPNU) are interesting and potentially impactful; performance is similar or better than baselines in FL settings (R1, R3, R4).
- TNA-PFN outperforms vanilla pruning in Figure 2 (R4).

### Weaknesses
- **Mask-based scheme is not novel**: it resembles dropout/pruning with a fixed mask; re-initializing masked weights does not sufficiently differentiate from existing regularization (R2). R4 notes the method is compared only to vanilla training and vanilla pruning, not to other LMC-improving methods (e.g., Git Re-Basin post-hoc).
- **Theoretical justification is weak**: Theorem 3.2 only states that gradient/curvature bounds depend on dimension d, implying reducing dimension lowers barriers, but does not explain why this specific masking approach is better than other dimension-reducing methods (R1, R2). R3 adds that the theory contradicts empirical behavior in wide networks: MNIST results (Figure 3, second plot) and Entezari et al. show barrier _decreases_ with increasing width, while the theorem’s dimension-reduction argument would predict the opposite. This discrepancy raises concerns about generalizability.
- **Absolute LMC barriers remain high**: Even with TNA-PFN, the accuracy barrier is non-negligible in many experiments, questioning whether the reduction is practically sufficient for model merging or ensembling (R3). R3 notes the marginal improvement over vanilla training may not be enough.
- **TNA-PFN + weight matching (WM) offers minimal gain over WM alone**: The reduction in WM iterations is modest, and the extra hyperparameter (mask ratio ρ) adds tuning cost without clear benefit (R3).
- **Lack of large-scale experiments**: No results on ImageNet or similarly large datasets; results are limited to MNIST and CIFAR10 (R3, R4). Behavior on larger datasets may differ (R4).
- **Missing quantitative analysis of width/depth trade-off**: The method is conjectured to work better for wider networks and worse for deeper ones, but no plots with varying width and depth are provided (R4).
- **Unaddressed questions about mask ratio**: ρ is set to 0.4 in experiments; it is unclear whether this was tuned or has theoretical motivation (R3). The definition of “mask ratio” is not precise (R2).
- **Figure 1 does not report accuracy**; the metric shown is unclear (R4).
- **Pruning at mask ratio 0.7 causes model collapse to random guessing** (Figure 2); authors should explain why and whether similar collapse is expected on larger datasets (R4).
- **Permutation equivalence to reinitialization**: Because Gaussian initialization yields permutation-invariant distributions, the uncountable search space of permutations raises questions about how all permutations can be considered (R2).
- **LoRA section in Appendix C.1** is unclear in its purpose (R3).
- **Can Theorem 3.2 be expressed in terms of loss/accuracy barrier?** (R2).
