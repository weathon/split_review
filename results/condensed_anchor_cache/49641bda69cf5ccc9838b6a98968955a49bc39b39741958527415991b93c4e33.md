## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper introduces “support spectrums” to explain model predictions by constructing a support set of training points in the predicted class that lie between the test point and other classes, then ranking them using a global-to-local importance measure derived from influence functions or representer points. The method is evaluated with case studies on MNIST image classification and text generation (RedPajama/Wikipedia subset). Reviewer scores are 3, 3, 5, 1, indicating strong disagreement: one reviewer considers the method novel and reproducible (score 5), while another finds fatal empirical and scholarly flaws (score 1).

### Strengths
- Novel explanation type: a support spectrum that provides a transition from global to local importance for individual test points (Reviewers 1, 3).
- Correctly identifies that existing sample-based methods (e.g., influence functions, representer points) are often too global and skewed by outliers (Reviewers 1, 4).
- Derivation from linear classifier theory is technically correct and well-motivated; the method is relatively simple to understand (Reviewers 3, 4).
- The support spectrum of a point appears to be a good proxy for how hard the sample is for the model or how likely it was mislabeled (Reviewer 3).
- Qualitative examples are presented well, interesting, and include a case of dataset debugging (detecting spurious correlations in text generation) (Reviewers 3, 4).
- Code was reproducible on the reviewer’s machine with minor tweaks (Reviewer 3).
- Evaluation covers both image and text tasks (Reviewer 2).
- The problem of obtaining more tailored explanations is interesting (Reviewer 2).
- Presentation is clear and nicely illustrated with figures (Reviewer 4).
- The discussion of samples in experiments reveals clever uses of the method (Reviewer 4).

### Weaknesses
- **Limited significance**: The support set from optimization (1)–(3) does not guarantee the absence of outliers (e.g., mislabeled points or points near the decision boundary); if outliers remain, the method loses its advantage. Conversely, simply removing outliers before applying existing methods (influence functions, representer points) would cover the stated drawbacks, reducing the contribution (Reviewer 1).
- **Purely anecdotal evaluation**: No quantitative or qualitative evaluation beyond a few case studies; the empirical evidence is extremely weak (Reviewers 2, 4). A user study would have been much more convincing (Reviewer 4). For text generation, the method seems an unnatural fit (adjustments to remove relative scores and treat next-word prediction as classification) and the anecdotal results are only partially convincing (Reviewer 4).
- **No baselines compared**: The paper entirely omits comparison to existing sample-based methods (influence functions, representer points) or to other approaches for identifying hard/mislabeled samples (e.g., Dataset Cartography [Swayamdipta et al. 2020], Confident Learning [Northcutt et al. 2021]). This is a fatal flaw (Reviewers 2, 3, 4).
- The claim that existing methods are skewed by outliers is only demonstrated on a toy example, which is insufficient to criticize current approaches (Reviewer 3). The paper then uses those same criticized methods in the derivation, but does not empirically show how the proposed method overcomes their shortcomings (Reviewer 3).
- **Missing computational cost analysis**: Generating a support spectrum for deep models seems non-trivial; this is not examined (Reviewer 3).
- **Incomplete and under-cited**: Hardly any citations in the introduction, despite claims that need backing; the bibliography is severely limited, ignoring most recent explainability literature (e.g., Madsen et al. review) (Reviewer 4). Related work is discussed only in a late section and is limited; the method’s relation to counterfactual explanations is not clarified (Reviewer 2).
- **Similarity to counterfactual explanations not addressed**: The support set serves a similar purpose to counterfactual explanations, except that counterfactuals are in the opposite class. Why is the support set better, or is it just an alternative? Why must the examples be in the predicted class? (Reviewer 2)
- **Text generation scalability**: Generating support spectrums for each token may become impractical for longer sequences or large vocabularies; the paper does not comment on how this was handled in experiments (Reviewer 1).
- **Paper difficult to follow**: Some explanations (e.g., “outlier” defined inaccurately as “mislabeled point” or “points close to the decision boundary”) are unclear and imprecise (Reviewer 1).
- **Minor issues**:
  - L231: The claim “Empirical results show that using Wc − Wk and bc − bk … yields a better spectrum” is not demonstrated anywhere (Reviewer 3).
  - L158: “show the general spectrums (as points along the paths)” – it is unclear what the paths are (likely varying γ); the term “path” is used only here (Reviewer 3).
  - L192: “(b) the green class?” – unclear (Reviewer 3).
  - L288: “row 6” might be clearer as “row corresponding to k=5” (Reviewer 3).
  - L314: “TD-IDF” is used without spelling out Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (Reviewer 3).
  - L511: “the global to local support spectrums” – typo (Reviewer 3).
- **Lack of substantial step beyond prior work**: The method can be summarized as taking points descending by inner product with the test point and, among them, the one with lowest prediction score for the test point’s class; this does not strike the reviewer as a substantial advance (Reviewer 4).
