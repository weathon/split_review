## Merged Review

### Summary
The paper studies the generalization of gradient-based saliency maps for deep networks. It shows that interpretation generalization requires more training data than standard supervised learning, and that SmoothGrad’s Gaussian smoothing acts as a regularization mechanism. Theoretical bounds are derived using the analysis of Bartlett et al. (2017) for Simple Gradient, Integrated Gradients, and SmoothGrad (where the bound decreases linearly with the noise standard deviation). Experiments on various architectures and datasets support the theoretical results, and spectral normalization is shown to improve interpretation generalization. The topic is novel and previously unexplored. Two reviewers (score 3) raised fundamental concerns about the problem motivation and significance, while one reviewer (score 6) found the work interesting and the bounds important, with only minor presentation issues.

### Strengths
- The paper is well-written and investigates a previously unexplored topic: the generalization of gradient-based interpretation methods.
- The derived generalization bounds are non-trivial and important, demonstrating that interpretation requires more training data than standard supervised learning, and that SmoothGrad’s Gaussian smoothing acts as a regularizer (error decreases linearly with noise standard deviation).
- The analysis incorporates intuitive signals for human observers and yields insights into how neural network interpretations work.
- The paper shows that interpretation generalization can be improved with spectral normalization.
- The empirical experiments match the theoretical results.
- The paper leverages known theoretical machinery (Bartlett et al. 2017) to obtain new results for specific interpretation methods.

### Weaknesses
- **Motivation and significance concerns (raised by two reviewers):**  
  – The standard goal of post-hoc explanations is to explain a given network’s prediction on a given input; it is unclear why one should study how input gradients generalize from the training set to the test set in expectation (i.e., the MSE between the current network and the optimal network, Eq. (6)).  
  – The claim that a generalization condition is necessary for proper interpretation is not convincingly justified; it is not clear that a “satisfactory interpretation” corresponds to the saliency map of the optimal population-risk minimizer f*. This perspective seems of little interest for understanding why a given trained network makes a particular decision.  
  – If multiple optimal classifiers exist (solutions to Eq. (5)), they may have identical test performance but produce different saliency maps. Then the loss in Eq. (6) depends on which f* is chosen (Loss_I(f*,f,x)), making the defined generalization error ill-posed.  
  – Given such multiplicity, it is unclear whether sensitivity of saliency maps to training data matters; there is no guarantee that different f* produce identical interpretation outputs for the same x.  
  – The paper’s update proposes f*(x) = E_{f∼Unif(F*)} f(x), but the key question remains: is E_{f∼Unif(F*)}||I(f,x)−I(f*,x)||_2 small? If the optimal set has large variance, why does variation in saliency maps with training data matter?

- **Reliability of saliency maps (Reviewer 1):** Post-hoc explanation methods (including simple gradients and integrated gradients) have been criticized for being unreliable in explaining deep network representations (Adebayo et al. 2018, Shah et al. 2021, Adebayo et al. 2021). Even if one had the exact same saliency map as f*, these methods may not faithfully reflect what the network is doing, which questions the utility of studying their generalization.

- **Implication of bounds when training performance cannot be guaranteed (Reviewer 3):** In standard generalization, an upper bound guides minimizing empirical risk while controlling bound quantities. For interpretation, even if standard training yields f_w → ĥf* (the empirical minimizer), Loss_I(ĥf*, x) can still be large. It is unclear whether interpretation methods overfit to training data in the sense of Eq. (5), making a discussion of regularization premature.

- **Overparameterization context (Reviewer 3):** Overparameterized networks generalize well without requiring more data than parameters. If Simple Gradient and Integrated Gradient need more data to be reliable (Theorem 1), does this imply they are ineffective for interpreting deep neural networks?

- **Limited theoretical novelty (Reviewer 3):** A substantial part of the analysis is directly derived from Bartlett et al. (2017), with the primary change being the definition of “loss” and “error” for interpretations.

- **Clarity of experimental section (Reviewers 1 and 2):**  
  – Figure 2: The caption says “model pairs generate increasingly consistent interpretations,” but it is unclear what is being compared; f* is never accessible, so what is the baseline?  
  – Training procedure ambiguity: The text states “train for 200 epochs” and then “use pre-trained ImageNet weights and fine-tune for 50 epochs.” It is not clear whether this means starting from pre-trained weights and training for 250 total epochs, or something else.  
  – The description “normalized Spearman correlation of network interpretations against softmax predictions” is vague: are softmax predictions the argmax or the full k-dimensional vector? Between which two quantities is the correlation computed?  
  – Figure 1: Text and legend are too small; the production of lines and the meaning of each point are unclear. The lines do not seem to represent the data. Consider adding points from multiple random seeds.

- **Missing reference (Reviewer 2):** Galanti, T., Galanti, L., & Ben-Shaul, I. (2023). Comparative Generalization Bounds for Deep Neural Networks. *TMLR*. Should be cited.
